2. The second part of the proof follows and is taken from verse 13 immediately
following. For when verse 12 had said that the right to be made sons of God
was given to those who received Christ, verse 13 immediately describes them
as those who were born of God. He wishes the former words about receiving
Christ to be explained by these words—not, that is, of receiving Christ’s
obedience for righteousness in justification, but of receiving Christ himself
in adoption, so that, united with him, we may be sons of God, which occurs
when we are born of God.

The very learned man’s diffuse manner of writing makes it difficult to
understand the force of the argument readily, or at least to reduce it to
form. Its sum comes to this: after John had said, “As many as received him, he
gave this right, that they might be sons of God,” he immediately adds by way
of interpretation, “who were born of God.” It therefore follows from this
that the expression τὸ ἔλαβον, that receiving of Christ, must be referred
to adoption, not to justification.

I answer that it certainly does not follow. So far as I can consider the
matter seriously, no ground for this consequence appears to me. Rather, this
seems to be the genuine sense and construction of the passage: having
mentioned sonship through adoption in the former clause, the evangelist quite
appropriately rises in the following verse to that sonship which occurs
through regeneration. He does so for two reasons:

1. To show that we are reckoned among God’s sons under a twofold title, namely,
not only by adoption but also by regeneration.

2. To indicate the proximate foundation and origin of adoption, namely,
regeneration, according to the renowned Beza on this passage: “The Son,
excluded by most of his own and acknowledged by a certain few, by his power
regenerated them and admitted them to the common dignity of sonship.”
Piscator agrees in his *Analysis*, which reads as follows: “He expounds both
the effect of that faith, namely, the right of the sons of God, or
υἱοθεσίαν—that is, adoption—and also its efficient cause, which is
regeneration through the Spirit of God.” For those whom God regards as sons
in adoption he previously regenerated by the Spirit in effectual calling. But
this says nothing to prevent his doing so through the intervening reception
of Christ in justification. I gladly acknowledge that through faith we
receive Christ equally in adoption and in justification. Yet nothing here
compels us to understand the whole of this as the reception which occurs in
adoption, to the exclusion of that reception which occurred previously in
the order of nature in justification. Indeed, it does not even prove it
plausibly.

Let me add this as well. Just as this interpretation of the very learned man
excludes justification, so it appears to confuse regeneration and adoption,
which are distinct in their own nature. For although, as he says, when we are
born of God we are made sons of God, we are not so in precisely the same
manner. By adoption we are such only νομικῶς, legally, and imputatively; by
regeneration, however, we are such really and, if it is permissible to speak
in this manner of these divine things, in a physical manner.

How much more correctly, fully, and suitably to the divine economy
(οἰκονομίᾳ) could we say that these words contain and express three
things—regeneration, justification, and adoption—so that, first regenerated
in calling, we are said through faith to receive Christ in

[1679 printed p. 78 | IA leaf 86]

justification, and thus, first freed from the guilt of sins, are finally
regarded and treated as sons in adoption.

Thus much concerning the learned man’s first response to this passage: namely,
that this reception of Christ belongs to adoption, not to justification.

The second response follows. It concerns the benefit bestowed on those who
received Christ in the words ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς ἐξουσίαν τέκνα Θεοῦ γενέσθαι,
“He gave them power to become sons of God.” Here he distinguishes between
sonship itself and the sons’ ἐξουσίαν, or between a son with respect to
sonship itself and the status, and with respect to the prerogatives of
sonship. Just as these two are distinguished in nature, so they are sometimes
widely separated in time. “As long as a son is under guardians, he is a son,”
but for that time he does not enter into a son’s authority, power, and
inheritance, Galatians 4:1–2. We are even now sons of God, but “it has not yet
been manifested what we shall be,” 1 John 3:2. In this respect, therefore, we
are said still to await our adoption, Romans 8:23.

He accordingly says that two works are distinguished here. One is the work
of God the Father in the words “those who received Christ.” Since the reception
of Christ is the same as effectual calling and adoption, these are operations
of the Father, and consequently sonship also is the Father’s work. The other
is the distinct work of Christ the Son, contained in the words ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς
ἐξουσίαν τέκνα Θεοῦ γενέσθαι, “He gave them power to become sons of
God.” This, however, designates not sonship itself but the prerogatives of the
sons, such as justification, sanctification, and all the remaining things
which follow adoption. The sum is that these words posit two things. 1. God
the Father grants that we first become sons insofar as he draws us to Christ
through effectual calling and adoption, John 6:44. 2. When we have thus
already been made sons, Christ the Son leads us to the Father, John 14:6;
1 Peter 3:18, and communicates to us all the sons’ prerogatives, among which
justification is one. Thus, especially from this passage, sonship and adoption
will be prior to justification.

Response.

I confess that these things are beautiful and exceedingly well arranged. I
fear, however, that they are not spoken as truly as they are learnedly and
ingeniously. Several points, in my judgment, require examination.

1. He always goes wrong on the same string when he determines that adoption
is the same thing as effectual calling. For there can never be any
communication of Christ’s righteousness, which occurs in justification,
before the reception of Christ, approach to him, and union with him which
first occur in effectual calling. Since he confuses adoption with calling, it
therefore follows that he attributes this reception of Christ and union with
him to adoption, and consequently determines that adoption is prior to
justification in nature and order.

We, on the contrary, grant and freely profess that the first drawing and
approach to Christ, the first reception of him, and finally the first union
with him occur in effectual calling, which is first of all, and precede
justification in the order of nature. Yet we say that all these things are
wrongly attributed in the first instance to adoption, and that adoption is
wrongly placed before justification on that basis, unless he first proves
that calling and adoption are the same things. We have already demonstrated
sufficiently that this is false and that they differ in their whole genus.
Thus, although we believe calling, justification, and adoption to be
simultaneous in time, we nevertheless say that the order and method of the
divine economy (οἰκονομίας) in conferring these things is

[Marginalia: Chamier, however, wishes justification to be first of all, and
prior in Christ to creation itself. Volume 3, book 21, chapter 4, § 22.]

[1679 printed p. 79 | IA leaf 87]

that, first approaching Christ through faith in calling, receiving him, and
being united with him, we are first regarded as righteous instead of guilty
in justification through the free pardon of sins. Then at last, once
hostilities have been destroyed and abolished, we are raised to the high
rank of sons. His confusion of effectual calling with adoption is therefore
the πρῶτον ψεῦδος, the first falsehood.

2. He so restricts adoption to God the Father alone that he removes from
Christ the Son every part in conferring it. We sufficiently refuted this in
the answer to the first argument. He does indeed concede, in chapter 18 of
his treatise, that Christ the Son is the medium through whom God the Father
graciously gives all these benefits, including adoption. Yet he does so in
such a way that, with respect to justification, sanctification, and
glorification—which he calls the prerogatives of sons—Christ is both their
medium and their Lord and giver. With respect to calling, regeneration, and
adoption, however, Christ is merely their medium, while God the Father alone
is their Lord and giver. But how ἀνόλογον, how unreasonable, this is,
since Christ—to speak with the very learned Cameron—is “the procurer and
dispenser of all the goods which proceed to us from the kindness of the
Father himself, reconciled by his blood.” For if we consider Christ,

Either as the Son, the second person in the most holy Trinity, then, since he
is equally God with the Father, all these external works are equally common
to him with the Father;

Or if we consider him as the mediator and Θεάνθρωπον, God-man, then he is
the procurer of them all not only with respect to merit and the medium of
conferring them, but also their dispenser with respect to ἐξουσίαν,
authority, and dominion. He is the heir of all things, Hebrews 1:2, and Lord,
Matthew 28:18, to whom the Father gave all judgment, John 5:22, and who
presides over God’s house not as a servant but as a Son, Hebrews 3:5–6. Thus
he also dispenses and administers by right of command everything he procured
by merit. In this respect there is no difference between calling and adoption
on the one side and justification and sanctification on the other. He both
merited and confers all these things. No one on either side doubts the merit,
and the actual conferral is no less certain. The very learned adversary
acknowledges it concerning justification and sanctification, and rightly so,
for:

As concerns justification, it is ῥητῶς, expressly, attributed to Christ:
“By his knowledge shall my righteous servant justify many,” Isaiah 53:11; “so
that you may know that the Son of Man has power on earth to forgive sins,”
Matthew 9:6, which in the apostle’s sense is to justify, Romans 4:6–8.

As concerns sanctification, these words are plain: ἡγιασμένοις ἐν Χριστῷ
Ἰησοῦ, “those sanctified in Christ Jesus,” 1 Corinthians 1:2. “Christ
loved the church that he might sanctify it,” Ephesians 5:25–26. “Both he who
sanctifies and those who are sanctified are all of one,” Hebrews 2:11. For he
sends the sanctifying Spirit to perform this work; he is the head from whom
sanctifying grace flows into the members.

These matters are not in dispute. The only question is whether calling and
adoption are works so proper to God the Father that they are not likewise
common to Christ. The very learned man asserts that here, but sacred
Scripture does not.

Scripture indeed frequently attributes calling to God taken essentially and
also to God the Father. Yet it does not withhold calling from Christ but
assigns it to him: κλητοῖς Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, “called by Jesus Christ,”
Romans 1:6. Indeed, if they ascribe sanctification to him, as they do, how
will they deny to him calling, which is sanctification begun?

[Marginalia: On Matthew 16:18, p. 16; Beza; Piscator.]

[1679 printed p. 80 | IA leaf 88]

Perhaps the reasoning is thought to be different for adoption. Since adoption
is the adoption of sons, it will be proper to the Father, who is the
correlative of a son. We have answered this already. Though he is the Father’s
Son, he is nevertheless also to us אב עולם, the Father of eternity, Isaiah
9:6. Moreover, insofar as he is the Son, he is the firstborn. Made
participants in his sonship equally by his favor and his merit, we are both
his brothers and his sons, Hebrews 2:11, 12, 14. The distinguished passage
which we are now treating proclaims this very clearly when it says of Christ,
ἐξουσίαν ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς τέκνα Θεοῦ γενέσθαι, “he gave them authority to
become sons of God.”

3. The third point to observe is that these words speak of sonship itself,
not merely of the sons’ privileges, as the very learned man wishes. According
to his opinion, their sense would be this: to those whom the Father had first
regenerated by calling and made sons by adopting, Christ subsequently gave
the following privileges and inheritance of sons—namely, justification,
holiness, and glory. But the words sound something very different: not the
rights of sons, but the very right to sonship, ἐξουσίαν τέκνα Θεοῦ γενέσθαι,
“this right that they should be made sons of God,” as Beza renders it. Or, as
the Vulgate has it, “he gave power”—not so that those first made sons by God
the Father might later enter the sons’ inheritance through Christ’s gift, but
“to become sons of God.” This gift, then, is sonship itself. This ἐξουσία is
the primary privilege from which the other privileges and finally the
eternal inheritance follow. The learned man wrongly interprets the words of
these latter privileges alone.

4. My fourth observation is that in this matter, departing from our
interpreters, he approaches more nearly to the interpretation of the Roman
Catholics—namely, that of Maldonatus, with whom Grotius also agrees.
Maldonatus says that “sons of God” here means not those who have now been born
of God but those who enjoy the sons’ inheritance and are in heaven as if in
God’s house. Thus the sense would be: to those born of God who received him,
he gave a right to the future enjoyment of the inheritance in heaven. This
interpretation was first that of Theophylact. For he writes: οὐκ εἶπεν ὅτι ὅσοι
ἔλαβον, αὐτοὶ ἐποίησαν ἑαυτοὺς τέκνα Θεοῦ, ἀλλ’ ἐξουσίαν ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς τέκνα
Θεοῦ γενέσθαι, τουτέστιν, ἐν τῷ μέλλοντι αἰῶνι ἀπολαῦσαι πάντων τῶν γερῶν,
“He did not say that as many as received him made themselves children of
God, but that he gave them power to become children of God—that is, in the
age to come to enjoy all the rewards.”

I would not wish this interpretation to be attacked merely because it is
Theophylact’s, nor indeed rejected because it belongs to some Roman Catholic,
or to Maldonatus, the most abusive of the lot, provided that it is true and
genuine. Gomarus, in his examination of this passage, has sufficiently
explained how far it is from that. I acknowledge that the future
glorification on the last day is called adoption in Romans 8:23, but not
properly, as the Jesuit wishes; it is used tropically for the manifestation
of adoption. Here, however, the subject is sonship itself, and that sonship
not only to occur in the future—as the Vulgate interpreter’s reading, “he gave
them power to become sons of God,” namely, in the future, can be made to
imply—but already completed as soon as they believed and received Christ.
Hence Beza more correctly renders it, “he gave them this right, that they
might be made sons of God.” Maldonatus himself admits that the word γενέσθαι
can equally signify this, so that the sense is that he bestowed this honor
and dignity upon those who believed in his name, with the result not that
they would later become sons of God, but that they were sons already. By his
own admission, this sense once pleased him. But what happened at last? Hear
the Jesuit and the Jesuitical temper, especially that of Maldonatus. For he
adds, “It afterward displeased me when I considered it more closely and read
these things in a heretical interpreter”—he means Beza. See how perversely,
how characteristically Jesuitical! It is customary for Jesuits, and for
Maldonatus above the rest, that although they sometimes see the truth,

[1679 printed p. 81 | IA leaf 89]

they nevertheless begrudge it to us and reject it merely because it has been
explained by our writers. Although Maldonatus certainly deserved it, I would
not wish to repay him in kind under the law of retaliation by rejecting this
interpretation of the words simply because it is Maldonatus’s, but because it
does not fit the sense of the passage very well. Yet on both grounds the very
learned man—who is utterly removed from a Jesuit in both malice and
heterodoxy—nevertheless shares Maldonatus’s opinion, at least in his
exposition of this passage in this respect. For he wishes ἐξουσίαν τέκνα Θεοῦ
γενέσθαι to be explained not of sonship itself but only of the subsequent
prerogatives and inheritance, in order to serve his hypothesis that
justification is posterior to adoption. He therefore holds that this right
to sonship, which follows here from the reception of Christ that occurs in
justification, denotes not adoption itself but the privileges of those
already adopted.

Thus at last the controversy concerning the priority of justification over
adoption has been fully treated, and a comparison of justification with
adoption has been made.

It remains only to see what difference there is between justification and
effectual calling, regeneration, and sanctification. We have joined these
three—calling, regeneration, and sanctification—not because they are in every
respect and altogether the same, but because they agree in the one respect
in which they differ from justification.

Effectual calling and regeneration do indeed speak of the same thing, but
under a different manner and conception of speaking. Each signifies the
first infusion of grace into the soul.

Insofar as it regards God externally addressing and calling us effectually
through the word and internally through the Spirit, it is passively called
*calling*, 2 Timothy 1:9.

Insofar as in it we are converted from sin to God, it is *conversion*,
תשובה, Ezekiel 18; τὴν ἐπιστροφήν, Acts 15:3.

Insofar as from the seed of the word and the Spirit it connotes the generation
of a new spiritual life, it is ἀναγεγεννημένοι, “having been born
again,” 1 Peter 1:23; παλιγγενεσία, “regeneration,” Titus 3:5;
μεταμόρφωσις, “transformation,” and ἀνακαίνωσις, “renewal,” Romans
12:2; ἀνανεοῦσθαι, “to be renewed,” Ephesians 4:23; καινὴ κτίσις, “new
creation,” 2 Corinthians 5:17; regeneration, renewal, new creation, and similar
expressions.

The word *sanctification* extends more widely. It includes not only the
generation of spiritual life, in which sense it is the same as effectual
calling and regeneration, but also its increase, progress, and finally its
perfection and consummation. The same can be said of conversion. It denotes
not only the first act of converting, as in Acts 26:18, “that you may turn
them from darkness to light and from the power of Satan to God,” but extends
to the whole course of piety, from the starting barrier to the goal, from the
first calling to the final consummation of all in glorification. This whole
course, I say—the journey from its beginning all the way to its completion—
can rightly be called conversion, after the apostle’s words τὸν δρόμον τετέλεκα,
“I have finished the course,” and that for two reasons.

1. With respect to our original turning away from God, which is so great
that not only in our first conversion but even after the greatest advances
on this most blessed journey, as long as we are still travelers, we are only
in the process of returning. Here to advance is to go back; as we advance, we
return. Not τὸ σκοπεῖν, τὸ βραβεῖον τῆς ἄνω κλήσεως, “the looking to the
goal, the prize of the upward calling”: we have not yet fully reached God,
from whom we had turned away. Hence, concerning the conversion of the
holiest persons,

[Marginalia: Calling; regeneration; sanctification; *Teschubah*; Conversion,
2 Timothy 4:8.]

[1679 printed p. 82 | IA leaf 90]

we read in Isaiah 44:22, and even in Jeremiah 15:19, “if you return,” that is,
“if you continue as you have begun,” as Zanchius interprets it.

2. With respect to the detours in the return itself. For while returning, we
frequently turn aside from the right path, so that renewed conversion is
needed daily. To this belongs the Savior’s statement to Peter: “When you have
turned again”—that is, after your fall—“strengthen your brothers,” Luke 22:32.
To this may also belong the passage we have just adduced from Jeremiah: “if
you return”—that is, from your distrust, faintheartedness, and impatience—“I
will restore you.”

But I myself should return from this detour. My present point is that just as
conversion signifies not only the first act of returning but the whole
progress which follows, so sanctification signifies not only the first
infusion of grace but also its increase and progress until it is perfected
in glory. Thus Martinius correctly writes under the word *sanctification*:
“Sanctification is regeneration and the direction of the regenerate toward
the pursuit of good works. It contains new life and new obedience in itself.”

It is *begun*. Thus the Gentiles are said to be sanctified in their first
conversion, Romans 15:16.

It is *advanced*. Thus Revelation 22:11: “Let him who is holy be sanctified
still.”

Finally, it is *perfected*. Thus 2 Corinthians 7:1: “perfecting
sanctification.” “May the God of peace sanctify you wholly,” ὁλοτελεῖς ἢ
ὁλόκληρον ὑμῶν τὸ πνεῦμα, “or your whole spirit entire,” and the rest
which follows, 1 Thessalonians 5:23.

With these points premised, since we now know from what has been said what
calling, regeneration, and sanctification are in themselves and how they
differ from one another, the presently disputed difference of them all from
justification, which we are now treating, will readily be evident. It is
explained very well and sufficiently accurately in our Larger Catechism, to
which we need add nothing.

Let us therefore note very briefly some heads of this difference.

The first concerns the order of nature, which stands as follows.
Justification is posterior to effectual calling, regeneration, and also
sanctification insofar as the latter is the same as regeneration and
signifies the first infusion of grace. The apostle assigns justification this
place in his golden chain, Romans 8:30: “Those whom he called, he also
justified.” Reason also demands it. Since we are justified through faith as
an instrument, Romans 5:1, faith must stand antecedently to actual
justification. We are not now disputing the justification of the Antinomians
from eternity, or from the protoevangelium announced to our first parents, as
some have thought, or from the death of Christ, as others hold. Perhaps this
will be done later. But since faith is antecedently required for actual
justification, the infusion of grace in regeneration and begun
sanctification is antecedently required for faith itself, of which faith is
the primary part. Justification, which presupposes for its production actual
faith and even an act of faith, must therefore be posterior to calling and
regeneration, which produces that faith, and thus to begun sanctification. If,
however, we consider advanced or perfected sanctification, insofar as it
denotes the strength and increase of this habit and the internal and external
works of sanctification and new obedience, justification is altogether prior
to this sanctification. It grafts us into Christ as the root, “from whom alone
all our fruit is found,” Hosea 14:8;

[Marginalia: Jeremiah 3:2; C. à Lapide.]

[1679 printed p. 83 | IA leaf 91]

John 15:4. Therefore in the first calling he was made for us, first, wisdom;
second, righteousness in justification; third, sanctification; and finally,
last, redemption—that is, full glorification—1 Corinthians 1:30. Likewise,
at the last link of that golden chain—“those whom he justified” as antecedent,
“he glorified” as consequent—this sanctification is contained under
glorification, of which it is the begun degree, and is accordingly posterior
to justification. It is both the fruit and, whatever the Antinomians prattle
to the contrary, the mark of justification, according to the apostle’s words
in 1 John 3:7: “he who practices righteousness is righteous.”

2. The first difference between these matters concerned the order of nature;
the second regards their very nature in itself. Justification, like adoption,
is entirely σχετικὴ, relational, and relative in itself. Effectual calling,
regeneration, and sanctification, however, posit something absolute, simply
positive, and inherent. In justification there is a change of moral status;
in sanctification the change is physical. The former is a change of relative
condition with respect to God; the latter, of internal qualities within
ourselves. Since Forbes wished effectual calling to be the same thing as
adoption, he necessarily had to assert that calling was entirely σχετικὴ,
relational, just like adoption. But this effectual calling, taken passively
insofar as a person responds by approaching the God who calls, speaks of an
infusion of grace, regeneration, and begun sanctification. Here God gives his
people a new heart and puts a new Spirit within them, Ezekiel 36:26. But this
plainly declares something positive, inherent, and indwelling, arising from
God’s effectual inward operation, not from bare accounting, as occurs in
justification. Who would say that someone is regenerated, called, or
sanctified by imputation alone? Yet it can very properly be said, and must
altogether be said, that someone is justified in this manner, Romans 4.

3. In justification sin is not imputed, Romans 4:6–7. In sanctification it is
brought under the yoke, crucified, and finally abolished, Romans 6:6. Let the
example be a sick thief. Suppose someone is guilty of theft or murder and is
also very ill. In order to be ὁλόκληρος, whole and completely sound, he
needs both a pardon by which his guilt is removed—this justification
provides—and medicine which can heal the disease or wound—this sanctification
confers.

4. Hence, in the fourth place, the justification of all believers is equal,
but sanctification is not. When I say that the justification of all is equal,
I do not mean with respect to the sins forgiven, which are more numerous and
greater in some and fewer and smaller in others. Thus much is forgiven to
one, little to another, Luke 7:47. I mean with respect to remission, because,
however many or great the sins may be, all are equally forgiven all—עון ופשע
וחטאה, “iniquity and transgression and sin,” Exodus 34:7—to Manasseh as much
as to Josiah. It is also equal with respect to the same Christ, whom the
person weak in faith receives and holds in his entirety for full
justification just as much as one who, like Abraham, is greatly strengthened
in faith. Hence it is called ἰσότιμος πίστις, “faith of equal honor,”
2 Peter 1:1. The situation is far otherwise with sanctification. Although one
and the same Spirit does all these things, he distributes to each as he
wills. “Each has his own gift,” ὃς μὲν οὕτως, ὃς δὲ οὕτως, “one in this
manner and another in that,” 1 Corinthians 7:7. Thus, according to the
varying communication of the Holy Spirit and πνευματικῆς χάριτος, spiritual
grace, the activity of faith (ἐνεργείᾳ), and finally the age, disposition,
and diligence of believers, some are weak in faith and others strengthened;
some are little children, others young men, and still others fathers,
1 John 2:12–14.

[Marginalia: Romans 8:30; A moral, not a physical, change. Wotton; See chapter
19; *Gnavon Vapeshang Vechataah*; Romans 4:20; 1 Corinthians 12:11.]

[1679 printed p. 84 | IA leaf 92]

5. Justification can rightly be said in a certain sense to be accomplished
all at once and once for all. Sanctification, however, unless glorification
immediately follows first regeneration, as occurred to the thief converted
on the cross, grows little by little and by degrees toward perfection. Τὰ μὲν
ὀπίσω ἐπιλανθανόμενος, τοῖς δὲ ἔμπροσθεν ἐπεκτεινόμενος κατὰ σκοπὸν διώκω,
“forgetting the things behind and reaching forward to the things ahead, I
press on toward the goal,” Philippians 3:13–14. Here is αὔξησις, increase,
Philippians 1:25, and ἐπιτελοῦντες ἁγιωσύνην, “perfecting holiness,”
2 Corinthians 7:1.

I said that justification is accomplished in a certain sense all at once
and once for all, namely, insofar as the justifying sentence is considered
with respect to one’s state. From his first regeneration, every believer is
so justified that he will never afterward come into condemnation, Romans
8:1, 33–34. All past sins are so forgiven that future sins do not cause him
to fall wholly and completely from that state. If, however, we consider the
assurance of this justification and remission, it indeed grows and makes the
same advances as sanctification itself. If we consider the particular and
actual pardon of future sins and the application of that first justifying
sentence, this must be expected only after those sins have been committed,
through renewed acts of faith. There will be a fuller occasion to speak of
this matter later.

6. Finally, it follows from this that justification, with respect to that
justifying sentence and insofar as it regards one’s state, is perfect in this
life while we are travelers. We nevertheless limp and still sin, 1 John
1:8–9. Death alone is ἁμαρτίας ἐγκοπή, the interruption of sin, the burial
of sins. The spirits of the righteous made perfect do not breathe here. The
righteous do indeed aspire to this perfection, finally to be obtained in
heaven.

Thus at last we have completed the first question: what justification is in
itself and in what sense it is taken in Paul’s disputation concerning it. To
this was annexed a comparison of justification with the other saving gifts
of grace.

Second Question.

The second preliminary question which we proposed was this: What are those
works by which our justification is denied in sacred Scripture?

In sacred Scripture, especially in the Pauline writings, when our election,
calling, acceptance, or justification unto salvation and eternal life is
treated, our works, the works of the law, the law, and the law of works are
banished from meriting, producing, or procuring these things.

Thus, as concerns salvation: “By grace you have been saved through faith, not
from works, lest anyone should boast,” Ephesians 2:8–9; and Titus 3:5: “not
from righteous works which we did, but according to his mercy he saved us.”

As concerns calling: “He saved us and called us with a holy calling, not
according to our works, but according to his purpose and grace,” 2 Timothy
1:9.

As concerns justification, finally, the express statement (ῥητῶς) stands in
Galatians 2:16: εἰδότες ὅτι, and so forth: “knowing that a man is not
justified by works of the law, but through faith in Jesus Christ, even we
have believed in Jesus Christ that we might be justified by faith in Christ
and not by works of the law, because by works of the law no flesh will be
justified.” Here, knowingly and pointedly, justification is emphatically
denied to works of the law by a threefold negation.

It is accordingly called “righteousness apart from works,” Romans 4:6. The
Jews are said not to have attained it because they pursued it not by faith
but as if by works of the law, Romans 9:31–32.

Indeed, the same apostle does not hesitate to affirm in general, ὅσοι ἐξ ἔργων
νόμου εἰσὶν, ὑπὸ κατάραν εἰσίν, “As many as are of works of the law are
under a curse,” Galatians 3:10.

Since, therefore, these passages and many others frequently and expressly
remove from the causality of our justification: 1. works spoken of simply
and absolutely, as in Romans 9:11 and Ephesians 2:7–8; 2. “our works,”
2 Timothy 1:9, and “which we did,” Titus 3:5; 3. “works of the law,” Romans
3:20; 9:31–32; Galatians 2:16; 3:10, and also “the law of works,” Romans
3:27—in order to cast light on the whole of our future disputation concerning
justification, it will be worthwhile, indeed almost necessary, to inquire
more deeply and carefully what our works finally are, the works of which law,
and what law of works, from all of which the apostle so precisely removes
every merit or efficacy in our being absolved in justification.

There have been, and still are, various opinions on this matter.

1. The Roman Catholics, or at least some of them, first thought that these
statements should be restricted to the ceremonial law alone and its works.
For after the gospel had arisen, the Jews, the Judaizing pseudo-apostles, and
their followers, hybrid Christians, especially and insistently urged the
ceremonies of the law, and among them circumcision, which was their first and
chief ceremony, as is clear from Acts 15:1 and frequently elsewhere. The
apostle accordingly often mentions circumcision in his Epistle to the Romans
and in that to the Galatians, where he disputes concerning justification, and
always denies justification to it. They therefore thought that it alone was
treated and that it alone was removed from the business of justification in
this whole disputation, because it was the principal question, rather than
the moral law and its works, about which there appears to have been no
controversy with them.

Alphonsus a Castro especially follows this opinion in *Against Heresies*,
under the word *Faith*, heresy 2, where he says that “Paul speaks of works of
the old law, such as ceremonies and other rites, which had all been destroyed
by Christ’s death and possessed no power at all, and therefore could justify
no one. He was not speaking of the works of the other precepts of the
Decalogue.” He tries to prove this from the fact that the apostle immediately
added, “Is he the God of the Jews only? Is he not also of the Gentiles?”
Peter Lombard preceded him in this opinion, and Daventria, Virvesius, and
others also followed it. Although Dominicus a Soto does not approve it, he
nevertheless tries to make it at least plausible by several arguments, of
which the following two are principal.

First, after the apostle had denied justification to works of the law in
Romans 3, he immediately says in chapter 4 that Abraham was justified before
circumcision. It is as though he had treated only the law of circumcision,
that is, the ceremonial law. They also commonly adduce that the apostle so
often mentions circumcision in this disputation and repeatedly sets it forth
as an example of the law and its works.

Second, in Galatians 2, when Peter had been somewhat too indulgent toward the
ceremonies, Paul reproved him with this argument: “a man is not justi-

[Marginalia: Folio 210; C. à Lapide says that the works of the law from which
the apostle asserts no one to be justified are purifications, sprinklings,
and sacrifices, all of which were ceremonial; and our Hammond speaks in
nearly the same way.]

[1679 printed p. 86 | IA leaf 94]

fied by works of the law.” That would have been entirely ἀπροσδιόνυσον,
irrelevant to the matter, unless it were to be understood of the ceremonial
law.

But even these arguments of Dominicus could not persuade him to embrace this
opinion. Alphonsus himself, although he boasts that it is the exposition of
Theophylact, Ambrose, and Jerome, nevertheless did not trust it so fully and
added an alternative. He grants that it can be understood of the works of
the moral law—indeed, that it must be so understood according to the opinion
of Thomas, Dominicus a Soto, many Roman Catholic interpreters, and Bellarmine
himself. In *On Justification*, book 1, chapter 19, Bellarmine grants that
these words of the apostle exclude from justification not only the observance
of legal ceremonies but also that of moral precepts. This is no wonder, since
St. Augustine had so expressly declared his opinion on this matter. For in
*On the Spirit and the Letter*, chapter 7, he writes: “Lest anyone think that
the apostle said that no one is justified by that law which contains many
figurative precepts in the old sacraments, he immediately added and indicated
which law he meant when he said, ‘For by the law is the knowledge of sin.’ It
is therefore that law of which he later says, ‘I did not know sin except
through the law.’” And again in chapter 14: “He wishes the law by which he
says no one is justified to be understood not only of those sacraments which
had figures of things promised, but also of those works which, if anyone does
them, he lives righteously.”

The matter therefore stands well: by the admission of the Roman Catholics
themselves, the apostle’s explicit words deny justification not only to
ceremonial works but also to moral works. Has not the Roman Catholic doctrine
of justification by works therefore been entirely done away? Jesuitical
subtlety is needed here so that by a distinction they may evade what the
apostle said of all works universally, and thus escape more successfully.

2. They therefore say, secondly, that although in the apostle’s judgment not
only ceremonial but also moral works are barred from the formal account of
justification, not all moral works are barred. The works which follow faith
and flow from faith are not excluded, for they contend fiercely that we are
justified by them. Which works, then, do they wish to exclude? Those done
before faith was received, says Alphonsus; or those which precede faith, say
Becanus, Bellarmine, and others. But what finally are those works? They
express it in various ways, and frequently as follows: those which are done
by the powers of free choice alone. Sometimes they say: those done by the
powers of nature. Sometimes: those which are properly works of the law, which
are works done either from knowledge of the law alone or from its obligation
and compulsion—that is, as Salmeron interprets it, in order to obey Moses the
lawgiver and from a law which threatens temporal punishments or entices by
carnal promises. Bellarmine thinks that he confirms this common opinion of
all the Roman Catholics, as I judge, and this interpretation of the
apostle’s statement from the testimonies of Augustine and Jerome, arguments
drawn from Romans 4, and the purpose of the Epistles written both to the
Romans and to the Galatians. He holds that their sole purpose is to prove
that no one is justified by works of the law without faith in Christ and the
gratuitous mercy of God. According to them, the sum of the whole Pauline
disputation comes to this: to prove—not, as we hold, that no one is justified
by works but by faith alone, but—that no one is justified by works without
faith, but by works with faith, or by works flowing from faith as a prior
disposition. They contend that this, and this alone, is what Paul principally
intends in his whole

[1679 printed p. 87 | IA leaf 95]

disputation concerning justification, and they defend it most fiercely as
entirely true. We must treat it later.

3. Meanwhile, it must be observed that Grotius wished to be a reconciler
between us and the Roman Catholics. He did this not so much by bending their
opinion toward ours as by diminishing and indeed frequently refuting ours
and bringing us nearer to them. This is more than sufficiently evident to
anyone from his *Annotations on the Consultation of Cassander*, in which he
Romanizes more than Cassander himself, and his *Annotations on the New
Testament*, especially the apostolic epistles. Grotius, I say, must be
observed to have crossed into the Roman Catholic camp in this matter, as in
very many others, and to have held either a third opinion on the matter we
are now treating, or rather almost the same opinion as theirs. For he
explains the works of the law which the apostle excludes from justification
as only those external works which the Jews performed by force of the law,
which contained only temporal promises. On Romans 10:4 he writes: “This is the
hinge of this entire disputation: to distinguish internal righteousness from
external and to show that there is no way to the internal except through
Christ.” To state briefly Grotius’s entire opinion concerning the meaning and
nature of justification and the whole Pauline disputation about it, it is as
follows:

1. For him, as I have said, works of the law are ceremonial or moral works
which are external and merit praise among men. The Jews performed them by
their own power according to the law of Moses, which required a civil life
and promised only external and temporal things. He maintains this in the
preface to the Epistle to the Romans, in his annotations on Romans 3:20, 27;
10:4; Galatians 2:16, and frequently elsewhere. Thus, for him, the
righteousness of the law is that innocence which had force in judgments
according to the law, could be obtained by human aid, and was according to
the law in the manner in which the Pharisees urged it. See him on Philippians
3:9.

2. For him, the faith which is said in the Pauline epistles to be imputed for
justification is an opinion which thinks highly of God’s power, justice, and
goodness and of his constancy in performing anything he has promised, as he
writes in the preface just mentioned. It is called “faith in Christ” insofar
as Christ and his doctrine are known. So he writes on Philippians 3:9–10.

3. For him, to justify is nothing other than to purge, cleanse, and free from
vices. He writes this everywhere, especially in the preface already named and
in his annotations on Acts 13:39, so that “to justify” and “to cleanse the
heart,” Acts 15:9, are the same.

4. Consequently, according to him, the whole matter comes to this: when the
apostle frequently says and argues at length that no one is justified by
works of the law, he intends nothing else than that no one attains true and
internal holiness and purity, and thus holiness pleasing to God, from
external works pertaining to civil life, which the Pharisees urged and the
law of Moses required—a law which by external promises required only that
external righteousness. Behold the heart of Zenodotus, behold the liver of
Crates! To say nothing here of:

1. His agreement with the Roman Catholics in making justification the same
as sanctification, whereas we have already demonstrated that in Paul it is
taken in a forensic sense for the gracious pardon of guilt and acceptance
unto life.

2. His determination that the law which the apostle called “spiritual” in
Romans 7:14 and said in verse 7 condemns internal concupiscence, as the Savior
himself had previously done,

[1679 printed p. 88 | IA leaf 96]

Matthew 5:22, 28, requires with the Pharisees merely a civil, external, and
indeed Pharisaical righteousness.

3. His wish, again with the Roman Catholics and Socinians, that the law
contain only external and temporal promises, when nevertheless according to
the opinion of his own interpreters the ruler in Luke 18:18 expected eternal
life from observing it. I omit all these matters, I say.

4. What concerns the present business is this: if this Grotian interpretation
were valid, the apostle Paul, throughout this most forceful and thoroughly
divine disputation over which he labors so greatly—and which, besides matters
he repeatedly inculcates in his other epistles, he urges more frequently and
vehemently in the two most argumentative epistles of all, those to the Romans
and Galatians—would be treating only a trifling matter. He would devote
himself entirely to proving what no one except a madman ever denied: namely,
that either Pharisaical righteousness or merely external righteousness before
men does not rise to true, internal righteousness, does not cleanse the
heart, and is therefore little pleasing to God. What could be more ridiculous,
more unworthy of so great an apostle, and consequently more ungrateful to God
himself? Does this truly taste of Grotius’s acumen, before which all our
interpreters of Paul’s Epistle to the Romans are moles and creatures of
darkness? For he writes of them: “The new writers, having promised great
light, introduced more darkness.” For my part, it would be better and
certainly safer to hide in their darkness than to bask in so malignant a
light.

Thus far, in establishing the state of this controversy, we have expounded
only the opinions of the adversaries. They all hold that in the apostle’s
judgment not all works but only certain works are excluded from
justification: either ceremonial works alone, as some have held; or only
those moral works which are done without faith by the power of free choice,
as others hold; or external works pertaining to civil life, as Grotius holds.
