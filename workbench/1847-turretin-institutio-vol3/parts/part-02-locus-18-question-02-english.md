QUESTION TWO

On the Word “Church,” Its Various Meanings, and Its Definition

[Marginalia: The etymology of *ecclesia*.]

I. It is so well known as to require no proof that the Greek word *ecclesia*
is derived from *ekkalein*, “to call out,” just as *concio* is derived from
*ciendo*, that is, “by calling together.” In Hebrew it is called *qahal*, from
which the Greek *to kalein* and the Latin verb *calare*—which means the same as
*kalein*—appear to be derived. The Romans likewise had *calata comitia*,
assemblies summoned by the voice of a herald, as Gellius notes in book 15,
chapter 27, and Theophilus in his work on Justinian, book 2, title 10.

The word signifies both separation, by the force of the prefix *ek*, and
collection and congregation, by the emphasis of the verb *kaleō*. It is thus a
society of people called out from some place or condition and gathered into an
assembly. The Holy Spirit transferred this word, like a great many others that
were employed in secular usage, to sacred use in order to signify the people
of God *par excellence*: namely, an assembly of people called out from the
corrupt mass of the human race by the proclamation of the gospel to constitute
the society of the faithful, or the mystical body of Christ, into which no one
is received unless called.

[Marginalia: An explanation of *synagogue*.]

II. Corresponding to the word *ecclesia* are the words *synagōgē* and
*episynagōgē*, which occur often in the Septuagint version and several times in
the New Testament. At times *synagogue* is used for any assembly, whether
sacred or civil (Exodus 12:3, 19; Numbers 16:2), and even in a bad sense for a
profane and impious assembly (Psalm 26:5; Acts 19:32). At other times it is
used for a place of meeting (Luke 7:5), where people used to gather to hear the
law, offer prayers, and perform public exercises of piety other than those
that had to be performed in the temple.

Hence synagogues in which Christ taught are mentioned so often in the New
Testament. Their origin may be traced either to Moses, as some think from Acts
15:21, or—as others prefer, including Sigonio in *The Hebrew Republic*, book 2,
chapter 8—to the Babylonian captivity. During that captivity, since they had
no temple in which to worship God, they chose a similar place in which they
might both pray and teach. This practice remained after the captivity, so that
synagogues were erected not only in other cities but also in Jerusalem, where
the Jews relate that 480 synagogues existed. Thus it came about that the name
*synagogue* was afterward transferred to designate the Jewish church in
contradistinction to the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 8 | IA leaf 42 | PDF p. 42]

Christian church, while the name *church* was applied in a distinctive way to
the Christian church.

III. We must not suppose that this happened because, as Augustine observes on
Psalm 81, “A synagogue may even be an assembly of beasts, but a church is only
an assembly of human beings,” as if the Jewish church had been a gathering of
beasts. The Christian church, too, is described as a flock and a sheepfold
(John 10; Luke 12:32; John 10:16). Nor did it happen because the synagogue
regarded only perishable and earthly goods, while the New Testament church
pursues spiritual and heavenly goods, since the Old Testament was not without
the promise of heavenly goods as well.

Rather, it happened in order to distinguish Jews and Christians from one
another even in name. The Jews called their assemblies *synagōgai* to
distinguish themselves from the Gentiles, who called their own assemblies
*ekklēsiai*. Christians in turn had to distinguish their assemblies from
Jewish ones and use words customary and familiar among the Gentiles, all the
more because their assemblies consisted largely of Gentiles. Moreover, the
word *ecclesia* was especially well suited to express both the nature of this
society and the manner of its calling.

IV. Although this distinction is observed for the most part in the New
Testament, there are nevertheless two passages in which the apostles did not
take account of it. In Hebrews 10:25, the apostle forbids us to forsake “our
assembling,” that is, the assembly or congregation of the faithful. And in
James 2:2 the apostle speaks of someone who has entered “the synagogue” of
Christians, whether this is understood of the place of meeting or of the
assembly itself. The apostle seems to have used this word by preference either
to follow the prophetic style rather than the apostolic, as he does more than
once in his epistle, or because he addresses Jews chiefly, among whom the word
*synagōgē* was still more familiar and customary than *ecclesia*.

[Marginalia: The various meanings of *ecclesia*.]

V. Furthermore, the word *ecclesia* has many meanings. It is not, however,
used for the place of meeting, as the papists and even some of our own writers
would have it. Although *synagogue* is used among the Jews for the place of an
assembly, as is evident from Luke 7:5, and *proseucha* likewise takes its name
from the prayers offered there (Acts 16:13), the same cannot be maintained
about *ecclesia*. No passage can be brought forward from the Old or New
Testament from which this may be inferred.

The passage Bellarmine seeks from the final verse of Judith 6 does not pertain
to this point, since the name *ecclesia* does not occur in the Greek text. It
says only that “they called upon the God of Israel for help throughout that
entire night.” The Vulgate translator alone says that “they prayed within the
church throughout the entire night.” Nor does the apostle’s passage in 1
Corinthians 11:18–22 carry any more force: “When you come together in the
church,” and so forth. It can very well be understood of the assembly of the
faithful itself, meeting together for exercises of piety, and no reason
requires it to be explained of the place of meeting itself.

The fact that they are said to “come together in the church” does not prove
it, because this refers to the assembly itself, not to the place. In the same
way, senators are said to assemble in a senate and pastors in a synod, not
with respect to a place but to a gathering. Nor is it proved by the added
phrase “come together in one,” because this denotes the unity and conjunction
of persons, not the identity of a place—that is, “together, with one accord.”
Likewise, when the Hebrew word *yachad* concerns a gathering, it denotes not
the relation of persons to a place but their relation to one another, as in
Psalm 2:2, which the Septuagint renders “in one.” Finally, it is not proved by
the apparent opposition of the church to private houses: “Do you not have
houses in which to eat, or do you despise the church of God?” Contempt for the
church refers not to the place but to the assembly.

VI. Nor is *ecclesia* used in the papal sense for the pope, or for the head of
the church who bears the public person of the church, so that what is declared
to him is said to be

[1847 vol. III printed p. 9 | IA leaf 43 | PDF p. 43]

declared to the church. Bellarmine absurdly maintains in *On Councils*, book 2,
chapter 19, that the pontiff, in order to satisfy what is commanded to Peter,
“Tell the church,” must tell it to himself: “The pope must tell the church,
that is, himself.” Who does not see the absurdity of designating by the name
*church*—a concept that necessarily includes number and multitude—one man who,
according to our adversaries’ own tenets, may possibly not even belong to the
church? For he may not have been baptized, or he may have incurred
excommunication by the fact itself on account of simony or secret heresy.

[Marginalia: The threefold meaning of *ecclesia*.]

VII. Although the meanings of *ecclesia* are various and numerous, they may
all be referred to these three principal ones. The term denotes at one time
the invisible church of the elect and faithful; at another, the visible church
of those outwardly called and professing the faith; and at another, the
representative church of pastors.

The church can be considered with respect to inward communion with Christ, or
effectual calling, in relation to the elect, in whom the word and sacraments
are made effectual by the Spirit and work salvation. It can also be considered
with respect to outward profession, or the dispensation of the word and
sacraments, in relation to those who are outwardly called. Or it can be
considered with respect to sacred rule and government through rulers appointed
by Christ.

[Marginalia: For the invisible church.]

Hence, (1) it is used for Christ’s mystical body, constantly and intimately
united to him as its Head according to eternal election and effectual calling.
In this sense Christ says, “Upon this rock I will build my church” (Matthew
16:18), and Paul says, “Christ is the Head of the church and the Savior of his
body” (Ephesians 5:23), and “Christ loved the church and gave himself for her”
(v. 25). Christ is also said to have been “given” by God as “Head of the
church, which is his fullness” (Ephesians 1:22), and so throughout Scripture.

This is the catholic church that we confess in the creed. It may be considered
universally and as a whole, with respect to the entire multitude of the
faithful from which it is constituted, whatever their time and place. Or it
may be considered particularly and part by part: now of that portion which
reigns gloriously with Christ in heaven, now of that which labors and
sojourns in the world, and insofar as it is distributed into various
particular churches, which are designated by the same name as the whole.

[Marginalia: For the visible church.]

VIII. (2) It is used for the visible assembly of those outwardly called, who
profess the faith of Christ and share in the sacred rites. In this sense the
churches are said to have increased daily through the proclamation of the
gospel (Acts 16:5); Paul and Barnabas appoint elders in the churches (Acts
14:23); and the churches of the Romans, Ephesians, and others are mentioned
throughout Scripture, as are the seven churches of Asia to which Christ writes
(Revelation 1–2). Nowhere in either the Acts of the Apostles or their epistles
are any societies called churches except those Christian societies that are
in some way bound together by those common exercises.

Since the outward call is directed not only to the elect but also to the
reprobate, it follows that the church considered in this way contains two
classes of people: some who are Christians only by outward profession, and
others who are such by inward communion—that is, who not only profess that
they believe but truly believe. A particular question below will show what
distinction must be made between them.

[Marginalia: For the representative church.]

IX. (3) It is used for the assembly of the church’s rulers and pastors, to
whom the public dispensation of the word and sacraments and administration of
discipline have been entrusted. Christ refers to this in Matthew 18:7 when he
wills that a brother who refuses to be reconciled be brought before the
church: “Tell the church; but if he neglects to hear the church, let him be to
you as a Gentile and a tax collector.” Here *church* denotes nothing other
than the presbytery, to which the power and administration of the keys belong.
This is commonly called the representative church, as distinct from the
collective church, which is constituted from the people or faithful who ought
to be subject to the government of the pastors.

Although Christian presbyteries had not yet been constituted, Christ
nevertheless had regard not only for the present but also for

[1847 vol. III printed p. 10 | IA leaf 44 | PDF p. 44]

future time, and he provided for all posterity. By the name *church* he could
designate those Jewish councils by whose authority someone was put out of the
synagogue. In imitation of them, Christians had their own ecclesiastical
senates, which excluded the obstinate and rebellious from communion.

[Marginalia: The definition of the church.]

X. Hence it is evident that no definition of the church can be assigned that
fits its various meanings in such a way as to express their nature fully. The
papists labor in vain to give a definition that can be applied to every
passage of Scripture in which the name *church* occurs. For it must be
conceived differently according to its threefold relation.

With respect to the first meaning, which is the most proper and principal, it
is correctly defined as “the assembly of elect people whom God, through the
word and Spirit, calls out of the state of sin into the state of grace unto
eternal glory.” With respect to the second, which is less proper, it is “a
society of people called by the proclamation of the gospel to the profession
of one faith, the communion of the same sacred rites, and the observance of
the same order.” With respect to the third, finally, it is “the assembly of
the church’s rulers and pastors who are furnished with a legitimate calling
and power to preach the word in it, administer the sacraments, and exercise
sacred discipline, so that all things may be done decently and in good order
for the glory of God and the public edification of all.”

XI. From this it is easy to gather what must be thought of the definition
given by Bellarmine in book 3, chapter 2, where he maintains that the church,
properly so called, is “an assembly of people bound together by the profession
of the same Christian faith and the communion of the same sacraments, under
the government of legitimate pastors, and especially of the one vicar of
Christ on earth, the Roman pontiff.” This definition errs in several respects.

1. What is defined has various meanings, but the definition does not
distinguish it according to the different meanings it has from Scripture,
even though these distinctions are especially necessary for explaining its
nature. This is particularly so when the assembly of those who are called is
distinguished from the assembly of those who are called and elect by Christ
(Matthew 20; 22).

2. The definition is not essential, since it does not include but excludes
the form and proper attributes of what it defines—namely, the church—through
which the church is constituted and distinguished from other human
assemblies. It makes no mention of faith, love, and the inward virtues in
which, according to Scripture, the church’s formal character consists. It
mentions only outward profession and communion in sacred rites, which are
common to the faithful and hypocrites and can also be found in the reprobate.

3. The definition is false because it supposes that subjection to pastors,
and especially to the Roman pontiff as Christ’s vicar, belongs to the formal
concept of the church. Yet Christ not only taught nothing about this
subjection but expressly forbade it, taking away all dominion from his
disciples and claiming for himself alone the prerogative of Head and Monarch,
as will be proved in its proper place when the Head of the church is treated.
Add that if any subjection is owed to ministers, it is limited, not absolute,
and is owed to none but those whom Christ has ordained and who succeed to the
ministry of the church by a legitimate calling. This cannot be said of the
pope.

4. The definition is both broader and narrower than what it defines. It is
broader insofar as it includes among the church even reprobates and hypocrites,
who, although they are in the church, are nevertheless not of the church and
bear the name *church* only equivocally and by an improper use. It is narrower
because it excludes catechumens, the excommunicated, and the unbaptized, who
can be members of the true church even though they are not in the visible
church.

XII. The papists’ fundamental error throughout this controversy is their wish
to measure the church by the model of civil society, so that its essence
consists only in outward and perceptible things, the mere profession of faith
is sufficient to constitute a member of the church, and inward faith and piety
are not necessarily required for it. Yet the church’s nature is far different.
We must learn it not from the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 11 | IA leaf 45 | PDF p. 45]

corrupt light of reason or from our own or others’ customs, but from the
utterly pure mirror of the divine word. That word ordinarily locates the
church not in outward things, which are seen by human beings and can often
deceive, but in inward things, which are believed and known by God alone, as
will be proved below.

XIII. From the three meanings of *church* that we have discussed arises a
threefold consideration of the church, which we will pursue distinctly. The
first concerns its inward state, or the church of the elect. The second
concerns its outward state, or the church of the called. The third, finally,
concerns its government, or the calling of pastors and ecclesiastical power.
