# CHAPTER VII (continued)

3. Let us now add a third mark. God himself engraved this law, which he
promulgated in the manner just described, on tablets formed by himself. Exodus
32:15–16: *And Moses turned and descended from that mountain with the two
tablets of the testimony in his hand, tablets written on both their faces,
written on this side and that; and those tablets were God's work, and the
writing itself was God's writing, engraved on the tablets.* See also

[Printed p. 93 | IA PDF 101 | IA leaf 101 | IA decausadei02brow]

the final verse of Exodus 31 and Deuteronomy 9:10.

Then, after Moses was moved with anger and threw from his hands the tablets
first fashioned, breaking them beneath the mountain (Exodus 32:19), the Lord
said to Moses in Exodus 34:1, *Hew for yourself two stone tablets like the
former ones, that I may write on those tablets the same words that were on the
former tablets which you broke.* And when *Moses was with Jehovah forty days
and forty nights, Jehovah wrote on the tablets the words of this covenant,
those ten words* (verse 28). So too Deuteronomy 5:22: *Jehovah spoke these
words to your whole congregation—and went no further, but wrote them on two
stone tablets, which he gave to me.*

And Deuteronomy 10:1–4: *At that time Jehovah said to me, Hew for yourself two
stone tablets like the former ones, and ascend the mountain to me—that I may
write on those tablets the same words that were on the former tablets which
you broke—I hewed two stone tablets like the former ones and ascended the
mountain with those two tablets in my hand, and he wrote on those tablets,
according to the former writing, the same ten words which Jehovah had spoken
to you on the mountain from the midst of the fire on the day of the assembly;
and Jehovah gave them to me.*

This twofold writing by God of these ten words was certainly a special
privilege of this law, for we read of no other laws that he wrote in this
manner. His writing this law on stone tablets plainly indicated its perpetuity.
For legislators who wish their laws to be stable and immutable customarily
inscribe them on bronze or marble tablets. See Pliny, book 39, chapter 9.
Rhodoginus likewise testifies concerning Plato, in book 25, chapter 2, that he
wished laws to be inscribed on cypress tablets instead because he thought that
they would last longer. It should further be observed that God did not wish to
entrust the second inscription of this law to Moses, to whom he had left the
second fashioning of the tablets. Rather, God himself willed to engrave it with
his own finger on the tablets prepared by Moses. Let us hear the opponents'
objections.

*Objection 1.* That second writing of the law was not God's immediate work but
Moses' work, as is evident from Exodus 34:27–28: *Then Jehovah said to Moses,
Write these words for yourself, because according to the terms of these words
I make a covenant with you and with Israel—and he wrote the words of the
covenant on the tablets.*

*Answer.* The testimonies of Scripture cited above prove more than
sufficiently that the second writing too was God's work alone. As for the words
of verse 27 just cited, that command can be understood as a command to write
the ceremonial laws, about which he had spoken in the preceding verses. More
probably, it concerns writing the whole law, both moral and ceremonial, in the
book of the law. For a copy had to be written to instruct the priests and the
people better. The tablets, as we will hear, were stored in the ark and thus
were not to be seen or read by the people. In Deuteronomy 17:18 the king was to
copy for himself in a book a copy of the law from the one that was in the
presence of the Levitical priests. The Levitical priests therefore possessed
one copy of the law, and it is likely that this was the one Moses was here
commanded to write.

Then, the words at the end of verse 28 must be understood of God, for he wrote
the words of the covenant on the tablets, and the preceding words are to be
read parenthetically. Finally, even if this objection were true, the first
writing, about which there is no doubt, would constitute a singular privilege
of these precepts.

*Objection 2.* Moses himself wrote a great many moral laws. This prerogative of
the ten

[Printed p. 94 | IA PDF 102 | IA leaf 102 | IA decausadei02brow]

words therefore does not show that they alone are moral and that all the other
things Moses committed to writing are ceremonial.

*Answer.* 1. We do not say that Moses committed all and only ceremonial laws
to writing and that God wrote the moral ones. We affirm that the laws which
God himself wrote were moral and distinct from all ceremonial laws, or that,
among the great multitude of laws he gave to the Israelites, God engraved none
but moral laws with his own finger.

2. Those moral laws which Moses wrote were so many explanations and
applications of the moral law engraved by God, and they are therefore referred
back to it as their source.

3. The argument is not taken merely from writing—although all writing is
designed to keep the thing written long in memory and preserve it from
oblivion (John 19:27; Hosea 1:12; Jeremiah 22:30)—but from this particular
kind of writing: on stone tablets prepared for durability. See Job 19:23–24.
It is also taken from such a writing of laws promulgated in a special manner.

*Objection 3.* A temporary precept can be written by God's finger no less than
a moral and perpetual one. Therefore this writing by God proves nothing.

*Answer.* No one denies that God can engrave any temporary precept with his
finger. But since it is not established that he ever wrote such a precept, it
is certain that what he did was not done without the best reason. It is also
certain that no precept except the precepts of the Decalogue was written by
God's finger. His willing to engrave these and no others immediately with his
own hand denotes something special. What special thing could it denote other
than that they were truly moral and perpetually binding, especially since he
not only wrote them with his finger but engraved them twice on stone tablets?

*Objection 4.* God's writing is nothing other than the formation or creation
of characters by divine power. Whatever God has effected by his divine power
was not perpetual merely for that reason.

*Answer.* We grant that everything immediately created by God or effected by
divine power is not for that reason eternal or perpetual. We also grant that
God had no need of a pen or any engraver's instrument to form these characters.
Nevertheless, since every writing of laws denotes the legislator's intention
that the obligation of those laws should endure, and since the legislator
himself did not wish to entrust this work to scribes or secretaries but
engraved them with his own hand upon tablets which could last for many ages,
it is certain that he willed all his subjects to be moved by greater reverence
toward these than toward the others and to regard them as immutable and
perpetually binding.

Consequently, since in writing the other laws God employed the work of
secretaries inspired by his Spirit, but in engraving these laws willed to
employ no one's work and saw fit himself to inscribe them alone with his own
finger on stone tablets, and that a second time, he indicated beyond all doubt
that these laws are moral—at least no less moral and perpetual than certain
others which were committed to writing by him mediately, although not engraved
on tablets. What else, I ask, could he have indicated by this deed?

*Objection 5.* The inscription on stone tablets denoted the hardness of that

[Printed p. 95 | IA PDF 103 | IA leaf 103 | IA decausadei02brow]

people's heart (Ezekiel 11 and 26; 2 Corinthians 3:14). Joshua also had the law
of Moses, in which there were very many ceremonial laws, engraved on stone
(Joshua 8:32).

*Answer.* We grant that the writing on stone tablets could have signified
something of that kind, although the cited passages prove no such thing. They
rather indicate that this law written on stone tablets was a certain
representation of that law which God had promised to write by his Spirit on
the hearts of the faithful. Yet could it not also have denoted the perpetual
obligation of these laws, since God himself, the supreme legislator, engraved
them with his own finger upon those tablets? Joshua's action therefore has no
bearing on the matter. Our argument is drawn from the fact that God himself
inscribed these and no other laws upon those tablets with his own finger.

*Objection 6.* If God's immediate writing proves these commands to be moral,
then the things he did not write in this way will not be moral, because proper
signs and attributes yield conclusions both affirmatively and negatively. But
this cannot be said. Therefore, and so forth.

*Answer.* The inference does not follow, because God can engrave some moral
things with his own finger and give others to secretaries to be written. We do
not say that God writes every moral thing with his own finger, but that all the
things he writes with his own finger are moral. Nor do we say that God's
immediate writing is a proper or natural sign of moral law. Thus the proof of
the inference proves nothing, since it is merely a begging of the question. We
do not say that God's immediate writing is, by the nature of the thing, a
proper sign of moral law. We say that by this sign the Lord indicated the
perpetuity of the Decalogue: namely, by engraving it with his own finger on
enduring tablets while making no other law a participant in this dignity.

*Objection 7.* If whatever God wrote with his own finger on the stone tablets
was moral, it will follow that the preface itself—*I am your God, who brought
you out*, and so forth—is moral. It will also follow that the seventh day of
the week itself is moral, because this too is engraved on those tablets.

*Answer.* We do not say that everything the Lord engraved with his own finger
on those tablets is moral and perpetual. We say that all the laws so engraved
were moral. We know that a moral precept can be enforced by a temporary and
nonmoral argument, just as a ceremonial precept can be enforced by a moral
argument or motive without changing the nature of the command itself.

2. We deny that the seventh or last day of the week was engraved on those
tablets by God's finger. Only one day from the number seven was engraved, as
we will prove later.
