CHAPTER VIII.

On the Utility of Ecclesiastical Discipline.

The second question concerns its utility. It must not be passed over, since
it is a common reproach in England that the importunity of those who contend
for the reform of discipline cannot be excused: they supposedly stir up
controversies over trifles and disturb the Churches over a matter of no
weight or importance. But that discipline has great force in either
direction will be shown, first, by comparing it with the other practical—

[1638 control | printed p. 22 | BSB canvas 34 | bsb10507786_00034]

—disciplines. Plato said of politics in general that it is “the discipline
supervising all the others,” Aristotle that it is “the architectonic
discipline of all,”
and Plutarch, finally, that “man possesses no discipline more perfect than
politics.” This can be said far more of ecclesiastical polity, since it
commands even civil polity itself, about which they spoke. Ethics and
household government care only for private affairs; the latter cares for
public affairs. Civil politics indeed administers public affairs, and
therefore excels ethical and domestic discipline, according to the saying,
“Let the welfare of the commonwealth be the supreme law.” Yet the
ecclesiastical polity of which we are treating presides over civil polity
itself. Thus Nazianzen affirms that ecclesiastics govern even the emperor,
and do so with a nobler kind of rule, since the emperor himself is also a
sheep of the Lord’s flock. And Chrysostom says: “If a King, a Consul, or one
adorned with a diadem dares something unworthy, restrain and curb him; you
have greater power than he.” Gelasius likewise says to Anastasius: “Although
you preside over the human race by the dignity of earthly affairs, you
devoutly submit your neck to the prelates of divine affairs.”

[Marginal references: Plato, *Politics*, book 1, chapter 2; Plutarch, *On
Cato the Elder*; Nazianzen, *Oration to the Citizens Struck with Fear*;
Chrysostom, *On Matthew*, homily 83.]

Its importance shines no less clearly if ecclesiastical polity is considered
in itself. Isocrates said of polity in general, “Polity is the soul of a
city,” possessing as much force in a commonwealth as the soul does in a body.
Scultingius applies this so thoroughly to ecclesiastical polity that he calls
it the Church’s soul, sinew, and joint. I would not say that polity is the
Church’s soul except with respect to the word; but as regards the government
of morals itself, I do not hesitate to call it the sinew and joint, since
Lous himself says the same thing. When Thomas Cartwright had said that the
Church can no more stand without discipline than a city without a wall or a
garden without a fence, Lous said: “Indeed, I think one ought to say that
right discipline is more necessary to the Church than a wall to a city or a
fence to a garden.” He later compares it to a joint and sinew as well. Thomas
Bilson joins him. He freely admits that the English controversies about
discipline concern no light trifles but the weightiest business of religion,
in which the welfare and harmony of the whole Church are at stake. This is
not only because of order—though discipline preserves order in the Church no
less than military art does in a battle line. Nazianzen says of it, “Order is
the mother and security of things”—unless perhaps you mean that order which
Paul names in Colossians 2:5, in which all purity and integrity of life are
preserved.

[Marginal references: Isocrates, *Panegyricus*; Scultingius, *Hierarchical
Examination*, book 9, page 1; Lous, *Ecclesiastical Complaint*; Bilson,
chapter 13, page 315, *On Preserving Moderation in Discipline*.]

From this one can readily perceive how great the sin of the English Bishops
is today. By adulterating the discipline instituted by Christ himself, they
have not only thrown down the wall of God’s vineyard—which is itself a
monstrous thing, Psalm 80:12–13—but also cut the Church’s joints and sinews.
They have mismanaged that business in which the Church’s welfare and harmony
are at stake; finally, they have removed the restraint of order itself and
even of moral uprightness. And how far and wide, alas, does this evil spread?
The saying of Lambert Daneau must be applied here:

[Marginal reference: Daneau, *Political Precepts of Morals*.]

[1638 control | printed p. 23 | BSB canvas 35 | bsb10507786_00035]

“Nowhere do men sin more grievously or err more dangerously than in political
matters, because public political errors spread through whole nations,
provinces, and kingdoms.” Since the good and pious now mourn that this has
been done in dearest England by the Bishops, who can accuse those longing for
a remedy of frivolity without being frivolous himself? Richard Bancroft
hangs them from this hooked nose: he says that our Genevan invention—in the
points where we differ from them—contains absolutely nothing of any good
importance or substance. Lous grows still hotter, indeed rages: “We must
detest the discipline introduced by the Puritans as the impure offspring of
error and malice, the stain of all order, the destruction of grace, the
plague of charity, and the ruin and devastation of everything sacred in the
Church.” What you say is dreadful, but since it has not been proved by
dreadful experience, surely it was spoken from dreadful hatred rather than
from any other dreadful cause.

[Marginal references: Bancroft, chapter 4, page 169; Lous, *Ecclesiastical
Complaint*, page 164.]

2. But now, if the Reformed Churches are placed before our eyes, God
protests everywhere, as he once did in Micah 6:3: “What has this institution
of my discipline done to you? My people, bear witness against it.” “As
though,” Richard Bancroft says, “Henry Bullinger in a certain letter dated
March 10, 1574; Rodolph Gualther in letters to the Bishops of London and Ely;
and a certain Gantesius and other eyewitnesses worthy of trust had not borne
sufficient witness to the miserable condition of the Reformed Churches and
the confusion introduced by presbyterial discipline!” Why do you thrust
either Gualther or Gantesius upon me, when perhaps Hadrian Saravia—himself an
enemy—is on my side? As for Bullinger, his testimony, if it is true, proves
only that there are moral corruptions in the Reformed Churches. No one’s
testimony was needed to prove that; who doubts it? “Yet it is evident from
this,” you say, “that where your discipline is received, it does not yield
the fruits which you promise with such a gaping mouth that it would produce
if received, both in the *Humble Motion* and in your *Defense of
Ecclesiastical Discipline*.” What then? In the same way, the Gospel itself
which we profess does not produce wherever it is received those splendid
fruits which, when human viciousness is set aside, we nevertheless tell the
Papists spring naturally from it.

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, chapter 35, pages 452–456.]

We think it sufficient against the Papists that the Gospel always produces
some such people. Discipline likewise always produces some; why will this
not be a sufficiently suitable answer against its opponents? We are surely
equal to this point. Our writers ordinarily answer the Papists that the
bitter fruits with which they burden the Gospel we profess are the fruits of
persons, not of the faith. The same answer can readily be given to Bancroft
when he hurls against discipline the accusation they hurl against doctrine:
the stains and corruptions of the Reformed Churches must be attributed to
persons, not to discipline itself. Here it would have been right to remember
the prophet Obed’s words: “Are not you yourselves such people, and are there
not sins with you before the Lord your God?” 2 Chronicles 28:10.

[1638 control | printed p. 24 | BSB canvas 36 | bsb10507786_00036]

“The deformity of the Reformed Churches,” Bridges says, “introduced into
them under the pretext of reformation, wrings tears from me. Our Church is
much better.” You are a specter, good old man—indeed, that Laodicean
antitype, who boasts of his Church’s condition when it is most miserable,
Revelation 3:17. For the Church hidden under the Papacy is the antitype of
Pergamum; the Church revived from Wycliffe’s time, of Thyatira; the Lutheran
Church, of Sardis; the Swiss and Reformed Church, of Philadelphia; but
England is the antitype of Laodicea, as may be seen in Brightman. Laodicea
must therefore be examined as a mirror in which we may discern our blemishes.
In this way and no other will we understand how deformed our Church’s face
is, and how beautiful the face of the Reformed Churches is in the judgment of
the Holy Spirit; for blemishes of the face are discerned nowhere except in
the mirror of the divine word, James 1:23.

[Marginal reference: Bridges, book 1, pages 84, 86, and 87.]

What then? In Philadelphia as the type, and in the Reformed Churches as its
antitypes, we find nothing blamed; but in Laodicea, and therefore in England,
everything is. Why is nothing blamed in the Reformed Churches when it is
quite evident that there are corruptions in them? The reason was just given:
these belong to persons, not to doctrine or discipline. Why is everything
blamed in England? Not because of doctrine, for in Laodicea, as in England,
Christ displayed himself as the faithful and true witness, Revelation 3:14.
English discipline therefore bears the whole burden of evil. The Holy Spirit
speaks, as Brightman rightly says, of the Church’s common form and outward
appearance. It is what it is not so much by its own doing as by the
administration of its Angels, which is so constituted that one who considers
the matter with impartial eyes rightly judges it empty and devoid of every
virtue.

Let Bridges therefore cease to bewail with streaming tears the evils of the
Reformed Churches, as if introduced under the pretext of reformation, while
passing over England’s inward diseases with dry eyes, as if there were none
or as if the medicine of discipline had made them lighter. Christ exposes
them as most grievous and ascribes them to the corruption of discipline. Let
Bancroft likewise cease to impute the blemishes of the Reformed Churches to
discipline when the Holy Spirit himself does not impute them to those
Churches, since they arise only from private persons. That famous John Bodin
refutes both accusations, Bridges’s and Bancroft’s. He is a consummate
political writer and, since he writes without passion, seems to Bartholomew
Keckermann to possess something sound and admirable. Thus he says of the
Genevan discipline: “If anything anywhere among the nations is praiseworthy
and makes a commonwealth flourish, if not in riches and extent of empire,
certainly in virtue and piety, it is that censure of the pontiffs, than which
nothing greater or more divine could have been devised for restraining human
desires and those vices which human laws and judgments can by no means
correct. Yet this restraint is directed according to Christ’s rule: first
privately and amicably, then a little more sharply; then, if you do not obey,
there follows a grave and effective prohibition from sacred rites, and after
the prohibition the magistrate’s punishment. Thus those things which are
nowhere—

[Marginal reference: Bodin, *Method of History*.]

[1638 control | printed p. 25 | BSB canvas 37 | bsb10507786_00037]

—punished by laws are curbed there without violence or tumult. Therefore no
prostitution, drunkenness, dancing, beggars, or idle people are found in that
city.” Thus far Bodin. It is evident from his words that Reformed discipline
is the enemy, not the friend, and the sickle, not the patron, of corruptions.
Even if this were not true, that chaos of corruptions in England—more
numerous and more harmful—rightly stops Bancroft’s mouth, since counterfeit
Hierarchical discipline bears their blame and guilt.

3. Hadrian Saravia’s scruple remains—or rather his augury. Putting off the
character of a theologian, he plays the soothsayer instead of offering the
argument he should have made. “This new form of government,” he says, “has
only very recently been introduced after the rejection of Bishops. What it
will bring forth cannot yet be known. No reasoning can teach what evil lies
hidden in it or how weak it is against heretics, the factious, and schismatics.
There is need of experience, the teacher of fools, whom the experience of
past times does not move. But I fear that it will teach too late, when after
the evil has been received a remedy will be sought in vain. I do not wish
what I have said to be understood as though I wanted the form of government
seen in the Reformed Churches removed unless a better one were substituted.
I only want everyone to know that it is useful for its place and time. A
successor to Calvin, together with Beza, rules the Genevan Church in a small
commonwealth; as long as it remains in a peaceful state, all is indeed well,”
and so forth.

[Marginal reference: Saravia, *Against Beza*, chapter 24, pages 208–210.]

Let Lous hear this. You wrongly cursed Reformed discipline as a plague;
Saravia says no reasoning can yet prove this. Let Bancroft hear. You wrongly
denounced Genevan discipline as the importer of corruptions; Saravia affirms
that all is still well at Geneva. Finally, let Bridges hear. You bewailed the
discipline of the Reformed Churches without cause; Saravia acknowledges it
to be useful for the place and time of those Churches. As for the evil fate
which Saravia divines for the future, we answer with Tertullian: “When you
cannot condemn on the basis of things manifest, it is unjust to condemn
beforehand on the basis of things hidden.” What if this premature Saravian
condemnation is itself condemned by the Holy Spirit’s open prophecy? You say
that all is still well at Geneva, and here you are right. For Christ praises
all at Geneva as being well in the type of Geneva’s Church, namely the Church
of Philadelphia, Revelation 3:8. Yet the same Christ who says that all is
still exceedingly well there also foretells that all will remain exceedingly
well to the end. This Church’s crown will never wither, verse 11. “That pillar
is in the house of its God,” verse 12, and “it bears the name of the new
Jerusalem inscribed upon its forehead,” so that it may be all the more certain
that the key of David it bears, verse 7—that is, not doctrine only, but also
discipline, which Scripture ordinarily represents by the name of a key—will
continue until the Church of the Jews itself—

[Marginal reference: Tertullian, *Apology*.]

[1638 control | printed p. 26 | BSB canvas 38 | bsb10507786_00038]

—and will be joined together with it. It must be observed that this promise
belongs specifically to the Reformed Churches. From this one may readily
infer that not so much their doctrine as their discipline is fortified by
it. Laodicea agrees in doctrine with Philadelphia, and England, as in a type,
agrees with Geneva, so that discipline alone is the reason England does not
share in this promise. What did I say? Because of corrupt discipline, England
is not only excluded from the promise but also liable to the opposite,
dreadful threat unless she repents: “I will vomit you out of my mouth”—that
is, I will make you desolate forever, for one must not suppose that Christ
will ever return to his vomit. See then, Hadrian Saravia, how frivolous your
mind was, since you falsely foretold for Genevan discipline what Christ
himself predicts will befall your English discipline. Let this suffice,
then, for the second question about the excellence of ecclesiastical polity,
namely its utility.

CHAPTER IX.

On the Necessity of Ecclesiastical Discipline. The First Thesis: It Is Not an
Indifferent Matter.

The third question remains: the necessity of ecclesiastical polity. The
following theses must be considered concerning it. First, we express the
necessity of ecclesiastical polity negatively, so that it is necessary in the
sense in which “the necessary” is opposed to “the indifferent”; that is, it
is not an indifferent matter. Yet this appears in some places to be the
opinion of our opponents. John Whitgift says: “The controversy does not turn
on whether many things mentioned by the reformers were conveniently used in
the time of the Apostles, whether they can now be conveniently received in
certain places, and indeed whether they are conveniently received even in
some Reformed Churches. None of this is denied; nor, as we are falsely
accused, do we arrogate to ourselves the right either to disapprove or to
condemn other Churches for the order they have received as best suited to
their condition.” Again, when treating the election of Pastors, which is a
fundamental matter in ecclesiastical polity, he says: “We do not condemn
other Churches which follow a different order in their elections. I say that
no certain form of election is prescribed in Scripture. Hence every Church
may do what it judges most suitable for itself.” George Downham says: “I do
not hold the episcopal office to be of divine right in such a way that no
other kind of government is lawful; nor do I make ordination so dependent
upon a Bishop that there would be no ministers in the Reformed Churches.”
John Bridges leaves it open whether we judge discipline necessary or
indifferent. He says: “If we hold it necessary, either the superiority of
the—

[Marginal references: Whitgift, *Preface to the Reader*; the same, treatise 3,
chapter 5, division 3, page 169; Downham, *Defense of the Sermon*, book 3,
chapter 1, page 22.]

[1638 control | printed p. 27 | BSB canvas 39 | bsb10507786_00039]

—Bishops must be acknowledged, or the early Fathers who exercised it from
the very
time of the Apostles must be condemned. But if one does not wish to hold it
necessary, but an indifferent matter that can be varied and changed, it is
not right to condemn those Churches, or even to think or speak ill of them,
which use a different form of government; nor should they in turn condemn
us.”

[Marginal reference: Bridges, *Defense of Ecclesiastical Government*, book 3,
pages 319–320.]

He rightly acknowledges that discipline is an indifferent matter if it is
not necessary, and that other Churches are not to be condemned for varying
it. The words of Panormitanus tend in the same direction: “Continence among
secular clerics does not belong to the substance of the order or to divine
right, because otherwise the Greeks would sin and custom would not excuse
them, since custom has no force against divine law.” Stapleton and Cornelius
Scultingius exult because, despite the Puritans and the Reformed Churches,
the English Bishops preserved Catholic discipline for forty years. The
Genevans, Heidelbergers, and other Puritans rage against that polity in vain,
they say.

[Marginal references: Panormitanus, title *On Married Clerics*, chapter *Once
Upon a Time*; Stapleton, *Triplication against Whitaker*, article 19;
Scultingius, *Hierarchical Examination*, book 2, page 45.]

How great, then, is this crime of the English Bishops? They devastated the
Lord’s vineyard, provoked dreadful tragedies, deprived Churches of pastors,
and filled prisons with God’s servants. They mixed the English sea, which
John had foretold in Revelation 15:2, with fire; abroad they filled the bosom
of the Philistines with joy, covered the Reformed Churches of Jesus Christ
with shame, and troubled them with uproar—all while conceding that the very
discipline for which they rage so savagely is a merely indifferent matter,
judging that Churches which follow a different discipline must not be
condemned, and finally acknowledging that the contrary government for which
they torment the wretched Puritans is lawful.

“But similar concessions about your own discipline are made by you,”
Bancroft says. Theodore Beza, when rebuked by John Whitgift for his
meddlesomeness in the matter of discipline, soothed that reverend Prelate’s
mind in a letter dated March 8, 1591: “We do not compel Churches of the
opposite opinion to adopt our order, nor do we think otherwise of them than
their piety and dignity and our mutual brotherhood require. Far be this
arrogance from us! Who gave us dominion over any Church? Far be it from us to
think—provided the essentials are preserved—that nothing should be conceded
to antiquity, custom, or the circumstances of places, times, and persons,”
and so forth. Again, in his book against Hadrian Saravia, after speaking of
the tyranny of papal Bishops, he adds this exception on page 126: “Yet we do
not on that account accuse all who are called Archbishops or Bishops today of
this tyranny. What arrogance that would be! Indeed, if many who are so called
today imitate the example of those most holy Bishops and labor according to
the exact rule of the divine word to restore the so miserably deformed house
of God, why should we not acknowledge, observe, and—

[Marginal references: Bancroft, chapter 8, pages 134–135; Beza, page 126.]

[1638 control | printed p. 28 | BSB canvas 40 | bsb10507786_00040]

—treat them with all reverence as the true pastors of the Christian Church?
Much less would we prescribe our peculiar example for any Church anywhere to
follow—as certain people most falsely and shamelessly charge us—like those
most ignorant men who think nothing right except what they themselves do.”
To the same effect he had said shortly before on page 111: “But if the
restored Church of England now continues supported by the authority of its
Bishops and Archbishops, as has happened in our memory, so that it has had
from this order not only distinguished martyrs of God but also most excellent
pastors and teachers, let it by all means enjoy this singular kindness of
God. Would that it might be perpetual!” Bancroft cites these words elsewhere,
where he admits that Beza spoke them only conditionally: “Beza must be given
leave to insert his IF: ‘But if,’ he says, ‘the Church of England,’” and so
forth.

These are the Bezian concessions over which Bancroft, Bilson, Saravia,
Downham, and the other opponents triumph as victors, boasting that in them
Beza has surrendered the whole cause. But what if we conceded that these
were equal to those we cited just now? What would they gain except that Beza
is equal to their own writers? They say that in these concessions Beza
conceded the cause itself to them; then let them see that Whitgift, Downham,
and Bridges, whom we just mentioned, conceded their cause to us. Indeed, if
Beza had spoken by way of approval, as your men spoke their own statements,
and contradicted himself, as Bancroft charges, and surrendered the whole
cause, as Bilson complains, then we would say likewise that your men either
contradicted themselves or their allies and surrendered the whole cause to
us.

But how great the distance between them! Beza nowhere denies that England
must be condemned for the actual violation of discipline. Nowhere does he
concede that the English Hierarchy is lawful. Finally, nowhere does he permit
anyone to call the matter of discipline indifferent. He says that Churches
must not be compelled to adopt the Genevan order precisely because it is
Genevan. He affirms that he does not prescribe his peculiar example to other
Churches for them to follow, precisely insofar as it is his own and peculiar
to him. He does not condemn those who act otherwise than the Genevans in the
manner of those most ignorant men who so press their own practice precisely
as their own that they judge nothing else right. Why should he? Geneva has
no dominion over other Churches, and God’s servants ought not arrogantly to
wish to rule those over whom God has granted them no dominion. Beza says that
something must be conceded to antiquity, that brotherly peace must be
cultivated with those who think otherwise, that pious Bishops must be
reverenced as faithful pastors, and that they must be obeyed. Why should he
not? Christian moderation moves us to be indulgent toward those who, though
swept away by the error of antiquity, are nevertheless orthodox on other
fundamentals; and charity counsels that the bond of peace must not be broken
on account of some blemishes so long as the unity of faith otherwise—

[1638 control | printed p. 29 | BSB canvas 41 | bsb10507786_00041]

—remains intact. Finally, piety commands us to reverence and obey as pastors
those
who truly prove faithful in building God’s house or occupy the place of
pastors, although, blinded by error, they also unlawfully possess an unlawful
Archbishopric or Bishopric. Beza says finally: “For my part, let England enjoy
God’s singular kindness, although supported by Archiepiscopal and Episcopal
authority.” Why not? He does not say, “Let it enjoy this discipline,” but,
“Let it enjoy this kindness of God.” If his words refer to discipline, he
does not say, “Let it be approved by me,” but, “Let it be tolerated by me,”
since the authority to depose or the power to correct does not reside with
him. In doing this he is so far from approving that he even condemns it
indirectly. If he did not indirectly forbid it, by preferring Genevan
discipline he at least dissuaded men from English discipline. Tertullian says:
“What is permitted has its voluntary cause in the one who permits it. Some
things are not to be sought merely because they are not forbidden; indeed,
they are forbidden in a way when other things are preferred to them. For
preferring the higher thing is dissuasion from the lower.” Thus far the
first thesis about the necessity of polity.

[Marginal reference: Tertullian, *Against the Valentinians*, chapter 3.]

CHAPTER X.

The Second Thesis: Discipline Is Commanded in the Word.

The second thesis follows. Since ecclesiastical polity is not an indifferent
matter, it follows that it must be assigned the necessity of command and law,
which Tertullian describes as follows: “What is prescribed is commanded; what
is commanded must necessarily be done; if it is not done, it carries a
penalty. Where counsel is given, the decision belongs to the one to whom it
is offered; where there is a precept, there is a servant’s necessity.” That
polity possesses this kind of necessity will be demonstrated in its proper
place. Here I wish only, as if in passing, to raise the question of Hebrews
6:2, where six principles of the Christian religion are listed: faith,
repentance, baptism, the laying on of hands, the resurrection of the dead,
and eternal judgment.
What does this laying on of hands mean? Some of our writers say it signifies
election and think that the whole of ecclesiastical polity is indicated
synecdochically by it. If this is true, then some doctrine about polity is
delivered in Scripture—for that appears to be stated here in this laying on
of hands—and it is numbered among the necessary heads of the Christian
religion. The same will follow from the interpretation of the Hierarchists,
who refer it to the confirmation of adults by Bishops now in use. Is not this
confirmation a matter of discipline? Certainly, if it is necessary, the
other parts of discipline will also be necessary. Nothing will accrue to
Episcopal authority from this interpretation, since Bilson himself admits
that—

[1638 control | printed p. 30 | BSB canvas 42 | bsb10507786_00042]

—this confirmation belongs to the Bishop by merely human law. Indeed, that
human law is unjust, since
no other confirmation is established here than what obtains everywhere in
the Reformed Churches. There adults are examined by the presbytery, approved
with the people’s consent, and received in a special manner by the whole
Church as members of its communion. They are thus, as it were, confirmed
before being admitted to the Lord’s Supper. But from the Hierarchists’
interpretation two further points accrue to me and my cause.

First, George Downham’s argument against presbyterial Elders is weak if no
other passage of Scripture denotes such an Elder by the word *Presbyter*
except 1 Timothy 5:17. What then? Is the confirmation of adults, which you
nevertheless call necessary, taught in any passage of Scripture other than
this? Second, the foundation not only of George Downham but also of the other
Hierarchists is sandy: they claim that the Fathers must be trusted in matters
of discipline because for almost three hundred years they changed nothing
and corrupted nothing in the discipline they had received from the Apostles.
What then? Did the Fathers not so change in the Church’s very beginnings the
confirmation which you say was delivered here by the Apostle that this
laying on of hands was given to infants at their baptism, as is evident from
the testimonies collected in the *Censure of the Eastern Churches*? But to
return to the point from which the argument departed: from this Pauline
passage, in whatever sense it is understood, one must conclude that matters
of discipline are necessary by the necessity of command and law.

CHAPTER XI.

The Opponents’ Arguments from Absurdity against the Second Thesis; First,
That Discipline Would Then Be a Matter of Faith and Part of the Gospel.

“But many absurdities follow from your doctrine,” the Hierarchists argue.
What are they, I ask? First, Whitgift says, it would follow that discipline
is a matter of faith and part of the Gospel, for what is necessary binds to
faith. Who doubts this, unless someone calls into doubt whether the
theologians’ disputes against the Papacy concern the faith? Is not the Papacy
a matter of discipline? Certainly the universal primacy which John of
Constantinople sought was only a universal episcopate, not the Papacy that
later emerged; for the latter had not yet raised its head. Yet Gregory the
Great does not hesitate to affirm concerning it: “To consent to that wicked
title is nothing other—

[Marginal reference: Gregory the Great, book 4, letter 39.]

[1638 control | printed p. 31 | BSB canvas 43 | bsb10507786_00043]

—than to lose the faith.” Again: “You will have peace with us if you reject
the pride of a profane title, according to what the teacher of the Gentiles
says: ‘O Timothy, guard the deposit, avoiding profane novelties of words.’”
Again: “Not I alone but the whole Church is disturbed, because pious laws,
venerable Synods, and the very commandments of our Lord Jesus Christ are
disturbed by the invention of a certain proud and pompous expression.” Again:
“In this cause I defend the statutes of the Gospels and canons by the virtue
of humility and certitude.” Again: “You ought not to say that this is the same
cause, because if we bear this indifference, we corrupt the faith of the
universal Church.” Again: “Supported by pastoral constancy and Apostolic
authority, exclude the robber and invading wolf, and do not yield to him as
he rages for the division of the Church.”

[Marginal references: Gregory the Great, book 6, letter to Cyriacus; book 4,
letters 32 and 34; book 6, letter 24; book 7, letter 69.]

“Why so much from Gregory?” you ask—as if every one of these statements did
not touch our cause very closely! John Whitgift rejects Cartwright’s
affirmation that discipline concerns the faith as something new and unheard
of, because no certain form of ecclesiastical government is prescribed in
the word. On the contrary, Whitgift: Gregory affirms that the form of
government which utterly overthrows your Archbishopric and the rest of the
English Hierarchy is contained in Timothy’s deposit, found in Christ’s
commands, delivered in the statutes of the Gospels, and supported by
Apostolic authority. The Constantinopolitan and English episcopates differ
not in kind but only in degree. Each usurps authority over equal colleagues;
the only distinction is that the one is Universal and the other Provincial
or Diocesan. But the quantity of jurisdiction introduces no specific
difference, Saravia says. A man is equally a father whether he has one child
or many; a man is equally a king whether he commands a city or a nation. By
the contrary reasoning, therefore, a man is equally a tyrant whether he seeks
unjust dominion over a city or over a region.

[Marginal reference: Whitgift, treatise 1, chapter 2, division 2, page 79.]

What if Gregory’s many arguments cut the throat of the English Bishop as much
as the Constantinopolitan? He produces virtually no deposit of Timothy, no
command of Christ, no statute of the Gospel, and no Apostolic authority which
does not make equally against the English and Constantinopolitan episcopates.
In calling it a statute of the Gospels, he contradicts Sutcliffe, who argues
at length that discipline—at least ours—cannot be called part of the Gospel
because it does not share the Gospel’s properties. Childish! It is part of
the Gospel if delivered in the Gospel. Our discipline is no more an exercise
of death than yours: ours indeed excommunicates, but does yours not
excommunicate also? And as it excommunicates the impenitent, so it absolves
the penitent. At least in this respect, as it is a sanction of the Gospel
properly so called, it also brings that joyful message of the Gospel which
you object to us.

[Marginal reference: Sutcliffe, *Treatise on Ecclesiastical Discipline*,
chapter 13.]

I omit the rest of his ridiculous claims as unworthy of refutation—

[1638 control | printed p. 32 | BSB canvas 44 | bsb10507786_00044]

—indeed, unworthy even of mention. Although Bancroft’s and Downham’s claims
of this kind are mockeries rather than arguments, they should not be passed
over
entirely. Instead of offering the arguments they should have made, these men
play and scoff, though George Downham is much more restrained of the two. “We
have heard,” he says, “that your Presbyterial Discipline is essential, if not
also an article of your faith.” For this he cites Bancroft, who does not
hesitate to assert flatly that “in effect our discipline has become a certain
article of our Creed.”

[Marginal references: Downham, book 1, chapter 6, page 128; Bancroft, chapter
34, page 441.]

It must be observed that Downham himself was ashamed of this irony. Do you
not see how he corrects it? First he inserts IF; then, instead of “an article
of our Creed,” he writes “an article of our faith,” which is somewhat milder.
Yet Downham himself uses the expression “article of faith” with more than
enough hostility, although he thinks those words of William Travers—upon
which all this mockery rests—“imply nothing other than that discipline is an
article of faith in the sense that the other things delivered in the word
must also be called articles of faith.” Thus the French Confession: “We
believe that the Church must be governed by the polity and discipline which
our Lord Jesus Christ instituted, namely that there should be Pastors,
Elders, and Deacons in it.” Thus the Confession of the Belgian Churches: “We
believe that this Church ought to be governed by that spiritual polity which
God taught us in his word, so that there should be Pastors, Elders, and
Deacons in it, who constitute the Church’s presbytery.”

[Marginal references: Downham, page 442; French Confession, article 25;
Belgic Confession, article 30.]

Gualter Travers says about these confessions: “It is not unworthy of notice
that these Churches judged it necessary to insert in their confession, by
which they declare their faith, an article about the discipline of the
Church; and that in describing it they stated not what they opine but what
they believe, using the same verb they used in the articles of faith and
doctrine.” What could Momus himself criticize here? Yet Richard Bancroft
criticizes these illustrious Churches of Christ and their faith. Especially
if they are understood in Travers’s sense, he calls it a collier’s faith:
they believe what Beza believes, though neither they nor Beza himself know
what Beza believes in this matter. Fill up the measure of your companions,
who blaspheme God’s tabernacle and the saints who dwell in it, Revelation
13:6. Thus much for the first absurdity.

[Marginal references: Travers, *On Discipline*, page 21; Bancroft, page 442.]

CHAPTER XII.

The Second Absurdity: Those Who Do Not Receive It Would Then Be Condemned.

Matthew Sutcliffe states the second as follows: “If your discipline is
necessary and delivered in the word, it would bind men to obedience. It will
therefore follow that all who neither receive nor use it are condemned
without repentance.” Bancroft speaks similarly, comparing Beza—

[Marginal reference: Sutcliffe, *Treatise on Discipline*, chapter 2, page 5.]

[1638 control | printed p. 33 | BSB canvas 45 | bsb10507786_00045]

—to Pope Boniface, as though Beza condemned to hell everyone who does not
submit to his discipline, just as we know Boniface decreed: “It is necessary
for salvation that every creature be subject to the Roman Pope.” Yet nothing
of the sort follows from our doctrine, despite Sutcliffe’s most unjust
accusation, much less Bancroft’s. Bancroft omitted the qualification about
repentance which Sutcliffe thought should be added, though Sutcliffe
understood particular repentance. It would not burden our position to say
that all are condemned who do not repent even with general repentance for
their sin in opposing discipline among the other sins unknown to them,
because such people cannot seriously repent even of the sins known and
manifest to them.

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, chapter 3, page 59.]

Let Sutcliffe therefore consider that Jacob did not repent in particular of
his sin of polygamy, yet was saved through general repentance, by which he
implored divine clemency and mercy for all the sins unknown to him. In the
same way all the elect today are saved by their general repentance, although
they do not repent in particular of their Hierarchical sin. David’s adultery,
since it was known and manifest, required particular repentance, Psalm 51:1;
but those sins which he said were unknown to him required only general
repentance, Psalm 19:12. Thus much for the second absurdity.

CHAPTER XIII.

The Third Argument: A Church Which Rejects It Would Not Be a True Church of
Christ.

The third is again objected by Matthew Sutcliffe, who states it as follows:
“If our discipline is part of the Gospel, then a Church which rejects it is
not a true Church of Christ. Thomas Cartwright’s defense against Harrison is
weak and childish. It was an intolerable error to make discipline part of the
Gospel; once this was posited, however, it was even more absurd and senseless
to defend our Church, which rejects it. For this cannot be denied: if the
discipline for which they contend is part of the Gospel, then the English
Church, which rejects it, is not a true Church; and Thomas Cartwright
struggles most ridiculously with himself and his opponent.”

[Marginal reference: Sutcliffe, *Treatise on Discipline*, chapter 15, page
165.]

Weak, childish, intolerable, absurd, senseless, most ridiculous—and what
else? Could envy itself have devised anything more? But see, reader, how true
Seneca’s old saying is here: “Hatred speaks not what harms, but what lies
beneath.” This is true not only in the sense that their malice lies beneath
these words, but also because their guilt seems to lie beneath them in
Sutcliffe’s heart. What then? Is Sutcliffe not himself weak, childish,
intolerable, absurd, senseless, and ridiculous because—

[1638 control | printed p. 34 | BSB canvas 46 | bsb10507786_00046]

—among so many barbed insults he did not add even one argument? “But perhaps he
adds one elsewhere,” you say. Not even then, unless you call it weak,
childish, intolerable, absurd, senseless, and ridiculous. He argues: “If your
discipline is necessary, it follows that those which do not receive that
ordinance of Christ are not true Churches, for Christ’s sheep hear his voice,”
John 10. Do I not truly hear in this the voice of a Pelagian, Donatist,
Anabaptist, Brownist, and Papist? Am I mistaken? What? Is there no hearing of
Christ’s voice unless it is perfect, no sheep of Christ unless it hears
perfectly? No—not unless that foundation is true which you absurdly object
to us as an absurdity and prove even more absurdly: namely, that England will
not be a Church if in even one ordinance she does not hear Christ, namely the
ordinance he established concerning discipline; that is, no society is a
Church unless it hears Christ perfectly in all things.

[Marginal reference: Sutcliffe, ibid., chapter 1, page 5.]

What is this but conspiring with the ancient Pelagians and modern
Anabaptists, who required such perfect holiness in Churches that they denied
that any societies in which even the slightest inward or outward imperfection
was found were Churches? What is this but joining the ancient Donatists in
expunging from the roll of Churches every society in which any outward
impurity at least is manifest? What is this but acting entirely as a
Brownist? Do they not bring forward the same text and argue in the same way:
Christ’s sheep hear his voice, John 10; England does not hear Christ’s voice
in discipline; therefore it is not a true Church of Christ. Finally, what is
this but reviving that Papist madness which John Gerson refutes with so many
arguments: that nothing erroneous can exist in the Church either in doctrine
or discipline because the Church built upon the rock cannot err?

[Marginal references: Augustine, *Against the Collation*; Jerome, *Against
the Pelagians*; Canus, book 4, chapter 3; Gerson, *Declaration of the Defects
of Ecclesiastical Men*.]

Against Matthew Sutcliffe and all his fellows this must be maintained: just
as a private believer can be a true member of the Church although he does
not hear Christ perfectly, obey him fully, or perform his duty in all the
Gospel’s commands, so any public society can be a true Church although it
does not perfectly fulfill all the Gospel’s ordinances. Christ writes to the
Church of Sardis: “Your works are not complete before God,” Revelation 3:2.
Yet he acknowledges it as a Church, calls it a Church, and writes to it as a
Church. Let Sutcliffe hear; let the Brownist hear; let every Anabaptist hear:
Sardis is a true Church although it does not hear Christ’s voice fully,
although its obedience is not complete, and although it was not fully
reformed from paganism as it ought to have been. What if Thomas Brightman
proves that this was said of the Lutheran Church as the antitype of the
Church of Sardis? It too will therefore be a Church, although it does not hear
Christ’s voice as well as England does and is not cleansed as fully from
Papism.

Second, just as the error in English discipline does not remove the—

[1638 control | printed p. 35 | BSB canvas 47 | bsb10507786_00047]

—nature of the Church because it is only partial, not total, so also because it is not
fundamental. It is of such a kind that, although it sins gravely by violating
divine command and law, it does not simply separate the Church from Christ
its head or at once undermine or overthrow the Church’s foundation. Richard
Field, the most learned of our opponents, teaches that only fundamental
errors destroy the Church. Let Matthew Sutcliffe run through the errors Field
lists as fundamental: nowhere will he find among them an error in discipline
such as the English error. Nor is this surprising. Although Stapleton himself
vigorously contends that the doctrine of faith is not a suitable mark by
which the Church is outwardly recognized, he nevertheless grants that true
faith is the Church’s life. This life still flourishes in the English Church,
which rejects discipline. Therefore it must not be judged dead, deprived of
life, or to have breathed out its soul.

[Marginal references: Field, *On the Church*, book 3, chapter 4; Stapleton,
*Relecture of the Controversies*, controversy 1, question 4, article 5.]

Consider the examples. The Ephesian Church is rebuked for neglected
discipline, Revelation 2:4, yet it is called a Church. Discipline lies so
prostrate in the Corinthian Church, 1 Corinthians 5:1, that Hadrian Saravia
thinks no administrators of discipline had yet been appointed there; yet it
is expressly named a Church, 1 Corinthians 1:1. Why say more? Laodicea itself
is certainly called a Church, although it had corrupted discipline to such
an extent that it was miserable, poor, naked, blind, and lukewarm, and hung
midway between Christianity and paganism, just as England does between the
Reformed Churches and Papism.

Third, just as the English error in discipline does not remove the nature of
the Church because it is neither total nor fundamental, so also because it
is not persistent and obstinate. Bellarmine proves that the truth of doctrine
is not an inseparable mark of the Church, because the Church of the Galatians
was a Church although it had erred fundamentally concerning the necessity of
the Mosaic law. What can Field answer to this argument except that a Church
which errs even in the foundation remains a Church so long as it is not
persistent, and that the Church of the Galatians was not yet obstinate? The
Jewish Church similarly erred in the foundation, yet the Apostles did not
separate from it as a non-Church until it displayed its obstinacy, Acts
13:45–47. Since this is so, what covering will conceal Sutcliffe’s and the
Brownists’ error? They strip the English Church of the covering of a Church
when it errs only in the violation of discipline, far more lightly than the
Galatian or Jewish Churches, though those Churches remained for a time even
while erring in the foundation itself.

[Marginal reference: Bellarmine, *Controversy 4*, book 4, chapter 3.]

Yet Lous comes to the aid of this wavering Matthew Sutcliffe and the
Separatists’ argument: “England, when warned of sin, does not listen; it
despises the requests of those who counsel it piously and constantly opposes
the discipline introduced by the reformers. Thus if discipline is part of
the Gospel, England, which not only fails to use but plainly despises it,
must not be regarded as Christ’s faithful bride.” Richard Field responds:
“There are—

[Marginal references: Lous, *Ecclesiastical Complaint*, pages 58–59; Field,
*On the Church*, book 3, chapter 3.]

[1638 control | printed p. 36 | BSB canvas 48 | bsb10507786_00048]

—certain degrees of obstinacy which must be distinguished. Refusing to hear
a warning is a certain kind of obstinacy, but not one which produces heresy
or destroys the Church. That
arises only when someone is carried along by the torrent of violent error so
as to be prepared to overthrow the foundation itself rather than recant.”
He appeals to Nestorius. At the beginning Nestorius did not err concerning
the unity of Christ’s person but only hesitated over the expression by which
Mary was called *Theotokos*. Later, when he heard it demonstrated, he
preferred to remove the unity itself rather than confess his error or
rashness.

[Marginal reference: Socrates, book 7, chapter 32.]

If this is so, the English Church is not yet obstinate. It has not proceeded
so far that it is judged ready to remove some fundamental article rather
than depart from its former discipline. The evidence the Brownists bring
forward for making such a judgment about England is nothing. England, they
say, despises warnings. What then? Does it despise them so as to be prepared
to undermine the foundation of the faith rather than abandon its error? And
yet this word “despise” is rather harsh. But grant that it despises: I deny
that this contempt belongs to the outward court, so that England must on its
account be cut off from the body of the Church. What then? Not even a private
member is cut off from the Church for contempt of this kind if, after being
privately warned twice, he does not repent. He is finally—and not before—to
be regarded as obstinate in the outward court and cast out of the Church
when he publicly and authoritatively hears the judgment of his persistence
from the Church itself possessing authority, yet still does not repent,
Matthew 18:15–18. Why should this proportion not hold for whole Churches? A
private member does not cut himself off from the Church by private
persistence; will it cut off whole Churches?

I press the example of the Carthaginian Church, which labored under the
heresy—as Augustine says—of denying the baptism of heretics. It was warned
by the Roman Church and did not repent. Yet it was not yet obstinate, Field
says, or cut off from the body of the Church, until it was publicly and
authoritatively condemned by Churches gathered in a Synod.

[Marginal references: Augustine, *On Baptism*, book 6, chapter 18; Field,
*On the Church*, book 3, chapter 40.]

4. Fourth, just as the English error in discipline does not destroy the
nature of the Church because it is not total, fundamental, or obstinate, so
also because it is not general. In the Corinthian Church the resurrection of
the dead itself is attacked, 1 Corinthians 15:12. In the Church of Pergamum
the doctrine of the Nicolaitans is found, Revelation 2:15. In the Church of
Thyatira Jezebel is tolerated more than is right, Revelation 2:20. Yet
because a certain part of the Church, not the whole Church itself, was guilty
of these corruptions, every one of these societies is identified by Christ
himself by the name of a Church. Why, then, should England not be a Church?
Not everyone in it consents to the English violation of discipline. What was
said of the Church of Sardis can be said of this Church: “You have a few
names who have not defiled their garments; they will walk with me clothed in
white,” Revelation 3:4.

[1638 control | printed p. 37 | BSB canvas 49 | bsb10507786_00049]

Our opponents answer that England itself must be measured by the party among
the English which opposes discipline, because it is both greater and more
powerful and holds
control of affairs, and not by the party which stands for discipline, because
it is very small and possesses neither numbers nor authority. What then? If
the Church of Sardis is estimated by its larger party, it will not be a
Church at all; for of these it is said, “You have a name that you live, but
you are dead,” Revelation 3:1. I ask: if Sardis is dead, how is it a Church,
which consists only of the living? So far as I can discover, this knot cannot
be untied except by answering as above: Sardis must be measured not by the
larger party which is dead but by that smaller party which is alive, for
whose sake Sardis is reckoned a Church. The chaff is the larger party
prevailing on the threshing floor, yet it is called not a heap of chaff but
a heap of wheat, as Augustine says, because the wheat, though smaller in
quantity, is the more excellent part of the heap in dignity.

How often do the Prophets acknowledge the Israelite society as the Church
for the sake of the few remnants whom God had reserved for himself in it?
Field says that the Church existed among the Papists almost until the Council
of Trent for the sake of the orthodox people who lived there, though they
were so few in number that the Holy Spirit compares them to the inner court,
Revelation 11:1–2, which the opposing Pontifical party in that Church, like
an outer court, very greatly exceeded. Was not the outer court part of the
tabernacle and temple? Since the Holy Spirit compares the ancient Papists to
the outer court, they also appear to have belonged to the Church in some
respect: namely, as Field says, as a certain most powerful faction within the
Church.

[Marginal reference: Field, *On the Church*, book 3, chapters 6 and 8.]

Let us pass to other societies. The wife is the inferior part of household
government; the husband is far more powerful and excellent. Yet if the wife
is faithful, she sanctifies her husband, children, and the entire domestic
society, as Paul says, 1 Corinthians 7:14.

Finally, the Pelagians, who acknowledged no Church unless it was free from
every sin, objected Song of Songs 4:7 and Ephesians 5:27, where the Church is
said to be without spot or wrinkle. What answer did the orthodox ever have
to this except that this is attributed to the Church not because all or the
numerical majority are such, but because the best and principal part of the
Church is such—in this life by imputation, and in the future by
sanctification? Thus much for the third absurdity.

[Marginal reference: Field, *On the Church*, book 1, chapter 16.]
