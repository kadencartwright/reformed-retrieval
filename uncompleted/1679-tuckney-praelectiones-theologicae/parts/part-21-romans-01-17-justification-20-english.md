Christ, for at that time there were no such works. Indeed, he hated and most

We must remember that our theologians do concede that verse 7 speaks about
Paul's works before faith and the other Jewish privileges listed in the
preceding verses, which he then valued highly. But they add—and prove
invincibly—that in verses 8 and 9 Paul goes further. He includes everything
else, even the righteousness of works he had attained after conversion. He
no longer hated that righteousness as he once had; indeed, as regards
sanctification, he pursued it with the greatest love and zeal. Yet in the
matter of justification he counted it loss and dung.

3. We must now consider briefly whether this is blasphemous, as Bellarmine's
second charge alleges. We strenuously deny and detest the charge. We in turn
accuse Bellarmine of blasphemy, since he has dared to cast it not so much at
us, who truly interpret Paul this way, as at Paul himself, who both speaks
and thinks this way. If the apostle blasphemes by speaking so, then the
prophet Isaiah blasphemed before him when he called all our righteousnesses
an unclean thing, like a menstrual cloth (Isaiah 64:6). Whether “all our
righteousnesses” in the prophet or “dung” in the apostle is more offensive
may be left for our adversaries themselves to judge.

I acknowledge that the prophetic text has been variously interpreted. The
Romanists say that the prophet speaks not in the person of the godly but of
the wicked, which Bellarmine attempts to prove with many arguments in *On
Justification* 4.20. Yet two of our foremost writers, Luther and Calvin,
express nearly the same view in their commentaries on the passage. Luther
says, “We have sometimes used this text against human righteousness. The
teaching is indeed good, but it is not the teaching of this passage.” He
thinks that the godly complain before God that in the Babylonian captivity
they were punished for the wicked's crimes as though they themselves were
unclean and their righteousnesses a menstrual cloth. Calvin says, “Some are
accustomed to cite this place to prove that our works possess so little
merit that they are putrid and foul before God. But this seems foreign to
the prophet's meaning, since he does not speak about the whole human race.
He describes the complaint of those who, sent into exile, felt the Lord's

[1679 printed p. 141 | IA leaf 149]

anger against them and therefore confessed that they and their
righteousnesses were like a polluted cloth. He does not speak about
individuals but about the whole body, which, when cast beneath other men and
afflicted with utter disaster, he compares to a filthy cloth.”

Bellarmine and other Romanists offer many things to weaken or corrupt this
passage and its meaning. Since our writers have thoroughly defeated them,
and since a fuller exposition would be out of place when we cite the text
only in passing, I shall leave them aside. I am less persuaded by Luther
here. He does not exactly make the godly murmur, but does make them complain
that they are engulfed in the common disaster because of the sins of the
wicked, whom he supposes the prophet compares to the filthy cloth. Yet when
Daniel discusses and laments the same times and evils, he confesses his own
sins and guilt as well as those of the rest (Daniel 9:20). Isaiah likewise
speaks here both in his own name and in the name of the rest of the godly.

Nor is Calvin's objection valid: that the prophet does not speak of the
whole human race but only of those then sent into exile. Bellarmine and the
other Romanists give the same answer. Confessions of the godly made at one
particular time in similar circumstances both can and should be applied to
the godly of every age, as our writers and the Romanists themselves
regularly do. This confession especially must be so applied, unless perhaps
we think ourselves better than our fathers. They said no less truly than
humbly that “all our righteousnesses are like a menstruating woman's
cloth,” to speak with Bernard in his fifth sermon on Isaiah's words. Bernard
there and frequently elsewhere, as also Augustine and Jerome, interpret the
words of the righteousnesses of even the best people. So do Luther in his
*Assertion*, article 31, and Calvin in *Institutes* 3.12.4. Even among the
Romanists, Pighius is sounder on this article, while Ferus is considerably
sounder on others, and both use this text as an argument against the
perfection of good works. These passing remarks on the prophetic text are
enough. They show that Paul no more blasphemes when he calls his
righteousness from works “dung” than Isaiah does when he makes the
righteousnesses of even the most righteous people a menstrual cloth.

Let us return to the Pauline passage now under discussion. To show that
neither Paul nor we blaspheme when we interpret it of the righteousness of
works produced by faith, we make three points.

1. We do not count these good works dung insofar as they are performed in us
by the Holy Spirit. In that respect they bear their author's character and
are pure and undefiled. But insofar as they are performed by us and proceed
from us, they are stained by our pollution. “Who can bring a clean thing out
of an unclean?” (Job 14:4). In this respect the prophet and the apostle count
them dung, and all godly people conscious of their own impurity count them
refuse.

2. Paul does not call them this absolutely but comparatively: in comparison
with Christ and his righteousness applied to us through faith, the
righteousness of the unblemished and spotless Lamb (1 Peter 1:19).
Chrysostom, Theophylact, Theodoret, and others follow this comparative
meaning, which Paul's words themselves convey.

3. Most importantly for Paul's purpose, we must note—in Calvin's words—that
he considers not so much the thing as its quality. Paul counts as loss and
dung not inherent righteousness itself, or its works considered in
themselves, but the confidence placed in them for obtaining justification.
Estius says that Paul speaks not of the law itself but of the righteousness
of the law. Likewise we say he does not speak of inherent righteousness and
works produced by faith as such, but of seeking justification from them and
placing our confidence in them. He rejects the desire to obtain
justification by performing them or eternal salvation by their merits, and
to be found in them on the last day and have judgment passed upon us
according to them and their righteousness.

[1679 printed p. 142 | IA leaf 150]

In this respect, I say, they are loss and dung to Paul and to every true
believer. They stink because of the pollution mixed into them and defile the
person. They are “loss” because they do that person the greatest harm when
they draw him away from Christ, in whom alone we must place all our
confidence for salvation and justification. Thus, to use Cornelius a
Lapide's words, they are obstacles to Christianity and consequently to
grace and salvation.

We read that Jacob told Reuben that although he had been the beginning and
the excellence of dignity and strength, “you shall not excel because you
went up to your father's bed” (Genesis 49:3–4). In the same way anything
retains its dignity and excellence as long as it observes the place, order,
office, and end assigned to it. As soon as it strays from these—whether by
reaching for higher things and falling to lower ones, or by turning in any
other wrong direction—it falls and is thrown down from its throne of
dignity. We must judge inherent righteousness and its works in the same way.
If we apply them to higher ends than those God assigned them in the covenant
of grace—if we use them to obtain justification or merit eternal life—then
they have ascended their Father's bed and shall not excel. They become
losses, refuse, and damage. Paul calls them such in this sense and, with
Bellarmine's leave, does not blaspheme. Otherwise, when they preserve their
place, order, and office, they are fruits of the Holy Spirit and justifying
faith; they render grateful obedience to God and Christ; they are the royal
road to eternal life. They are glorious and illustrious, the loveliest
pearls and gems. Beside them all worldly things, the celebrated achievements
of every pagan hero, and the very best works of all unregenerate people are
themselves dung.

Once these points are established, all Bellarmine's remaining material
vanishes in smoke. He heaps up not so much arguments as bedding and, as
though he had refuted our teaching, celebrates his triumph before the
victory. His arguments were these:

“Are the fruits of the Holy Spirit, then, dung?” Far from it, good sir,
insofar as they are such fruits. But insofar as we perform these good works,
they contain something mixed in from our pollution. In that respect they
cannot justify us before God, an end for which they were never appointed. If
we apply them to that purpose they become idols. Even if those idols were
made from the purest gold without any dross mixed in, the moment they begin
to be worshiped they become filthy, and Scripture rightly calls them “dung
gods.”

Bellarmine proceeds: because Paul had properly completed his course, he said
that a crown of righteousness was laid up for him which the righteous Judge
would render. The Jesuit therefore declaims, “Ought so great an apostle to
boast in dung? What crown of righteousness is owed to dung, and who is this
righteous Judge who deigns to crown dung?” This is boastful and crafty, as
is Bellarmine's habit. He seeks to catch the unlearned and unwary reader by
eloquence and elegance of expression, and by that incessant repetition of
“dung” to make the reader sick of our teaching, which he strives by these
devices to deform and render hateful. But these tricks have no power over
the thoughtful and moderately perceptive. The answer and cleansing of that
filth are readily available to them.

1. Bellarmine speaks of the apostle's boasting, but none appears here. Paul
had said that boasting is utterly excluded in this matter (Romans 3:27).

[1679 printed p. 143 | IA leaf 151]

When “boasting” means exultation of spirit, as it often does, we have already
shown Paul boasting in hope. We readily grant that he boasts here in that
sense, but never in the Romanist sense of the merits of his good works.

2. Paul counts good works dung only insofar as we seek justification from
them. God does not crown them in that respect; indeed, he counts them dung
even more than Paul ever did. Therefore, he does not crown dung.

3. Yet the righteous Judge crowns those same good works—which Paul would
reject like dung if they were used for justification—insofar as they are
fruits of the Spirit and faith, the road to the kingdom, and testimonies of
our gratitude. He does so from his supreme indulgence, forgiving and
covering their defects and stains, and he rewards them only by the justice
of his truthfulness, insofar as he performs what he promised, not by an
exchange based on debt and merit. The Romanist Estius himself testifies to
this in his comment on the passage: “It is a kind of justice to perform what
you have promised. In this respect God has in a certain way made himself our
debtor, so that even if we cannot appeal to him on account of our merits, we
can appeal on account of his promises.” He derived this from Augustine, who
says, “Paul asks for the crown promised by mercy but now to be paid by
justice. It is just for one who owes to render; and the one who promised by
grace owes, though not from debt,” and so forth.

Bellarmine's third addition is, “What thanks do we owe God, who created us
in Christ Jesus for good works, if those works are nothing but dung?”

We answer that we owe God the very greatest thanks for our good works. We do
not speak like that boastful Pharisee, whom the Romanists now follow: “God,
I thank you that I performed these and those works so that I would be
justified by them,” for we read that he failed of justification (Luke
18:11, 14). Rather, as Paul says in this passage, we thank God that we who
are nothing in ourselves and could accomplish no such thing from ourselves
were created by God in Christ Jesus for these good works.

There is still more reason for thanks. Even if our good works were now
perfect in themselves, since they are presently owed they could not pay the
ancient debt contracted for us by Adam's sin. Indeed, they are—alas!—far too
polluted by our stains, completely inadequate and unfit either to constitute
or procure our justification, and in this respect dung. Despite all this,
God was pleased not only freely to pardon their faults and justify us
gratuitously by Christ's imputed righteousness, but also by the same grace
to reward and crown with eternal life those very works, sinful as they are
and consequently most worthy of eternal death. These are amply sufficient
and just reasons for us humbly to thank God for them forever. They do not
justify us or glorify us by their merit, as Bellarmine wishes, for they can
effect neither. If we put confidence in them, however, they are dung, loss,
and filth.

Bellarmine therefore does not validly infer that they “should not be
performed and embraced but shaken off and rejected.” As though

[1679 printed p. 144 | IA leaf 152]

the good works themselves must be rejected merely because every confidence
that we can be justified by them must be rejected! Or as though merit were
the only incentive to do good. On that reasoning a criminal who has been
pardoned and hopes to receive further rewards ought not to serve his prince
with all his power unless by that service he could procure his pardon or
merit those later rewards. Or a feeble Mephibosheth should neither wish nor
be obliged to serve David as well as he could for the immense and undeserved
benefits bestowed upon him, because in his lameness he stumbles while
serving and cannot perform anything worthy of so great a majesty.

That is how the excessively inflated Roman pride reasons: if good works are
stained by even the slightest trace of sin, they should be shaken off and
rejected, because people cannot be justified by them or merit eternal glory
through them by condignity. The humble and grateful disposition of true
believers reasons very differently. It judges, as truly as humbly, that it
can provide nothing to obtain justification or merit eternal life either by
congruity or condignity. As regards the attainment of justification and the
merit of eternal life, it rests wholly upon Christ's imputed righteousness
and merits. It both judges and labors to lament the pollution of even its
best works, to wash it away with the tears of true repentance, and to shake
it off and reject it by the daily mortification of the flesh.

Yet good works themselves, considered in themselves and insofar as the Holy
Spirit performs them, are above all to be sought. They are the road to the
kingdom and testimonies of our gratitude. Contrary to Bellarmine, they are
not to be shaken off and rejected but performed, highly esteemed, embraced,
and publicly displayed. This accords with Paul's words in Titus 3. After he
had deliberately asserted gratuitous justification, not from works, in
verse 7, he immediately adds in verse 8, “that those who have believed God”
(that is, God who justifies freely) “may take care to go before others in
doing good,” as Beza renders it, or “to maintain good works,” as the Vulgate
has it. He repeats the command in verse 14, expressing the even more diligent
duty and zeal of the true believer in this matter.

From these things it is sufficiently clear, I think, that Bellarmine sings
his triumph before victory. He undeservedly tramples and treads Chemnitz
underfoot with contempt when he concludes: “Let Chemnitz now go and complain
that we call him an enemy of good works.”

Rather, let Bellarmine finally go and complain that we call him an enemy of
grace and of gratuitous justification through faith in Christ, and a most
bitter slanderer of us who earnestly uphold good works in their proper place
and order.
