QUESTION XVI.

THE FIFTH COMMANDMENT.

Can children withdraw themselves from the authority of their parents and
contract marriage without their consent? We deny this against the Roman
Catholics.

I. This question arises, among others, concerning the fifth commandment on
honoring parents, between us and the Roman Catholics. They undermine this
commandment in more than one way when they contend that children can not only
contract marriage against their parents’ will but also profess the monastic
life. Two matters, therefore, must be discussed here. First, can children in
any way withdraw themselves from their parents’ authority against their will?
Second, is it lawful for them to contract marriage without their parents’
consent?

[Marginal heading: How parents are to be honored.]

II. In order to settle the first question more easily, we must see what is
understood by the *honor* owed by children to their parents, for many things
are contained in it.

1. *Love*, with which children ought to regard them. The lack of this,
ἀστοργία, *lack of natural affection*, is numbered among the most detestable
vices (Rom. 1:31). For if one’s neighbor is to be loved, who is nearer to us
than father and mother, whose flesh we are? Further, if benefits conferred upon
us ought to win our love for the one who confers them, from whom have we
received more or greater benefits than from our parents, from whom, as
instruments of divine power, we received whatever we are and possess?

2. *Reverence*, which is owed to them, both because of their ὑπεροχήν,
*superiority*, since they are superiors while children are inferiors and
inferiors owe honor to superiors, and because of their ἐξουσίαν, *authority*,
and power over their children by reason of generation and upbringing. Just as
this power is very great, so it demands particular honor. Contempt (Lev. 20:9;
Deut. 27:16; Prov. 20:20), ridicule, and mockery (Prov. 15:5; 30:17) are
opposed to it as abominable sins.

3. *Compliance and obedience* (Eph. 6:1; Col. 3:20; 1 Tim. 3:4; Titus 1:6).
Yet this obedience ought to be *in the Lord*, that is, so that obedience to God
is not violated and God’s own authority remains secure. For if parents should
command anything contrary to God, then it is certain that obedience is not
owed them by their children, “because it is better to obey God than men” (Acts
4:19).

4. *Gratitude*, not only verbal but also real, through all the services
necessary to sustain life that depend upon the children. This is called
ἀντιπελαργία, *reciprocal care of parents*, by the Greeks, because storks are
accustomed ἀντιτρέφειν, *to nourish in return*, their parents worn out by old
age, as Aristotle notes in *History of Animals*, book 9, chapter 13. The
apostle commends this (1 Tim. 5:4, 16), as does Christ (Matt. 15:4–5), so that
children should not even be ashamed, when servants are lacking, to perform for
their parents every kind of servile duty and service.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

III. The question is whether children can shake off that subjection and
compliance owed to their parents and withdraw themselves from paternal
authority. The Roman Catholics affirm this when they contend that

[Printed p. 93 | Internet Archive PDF 121 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

children can take a monastic vow against their parents’ will. Thomas Aquinas,
II-II, question 88, article 9, says: “After the years of puberty, children can
bind themselves by a religious vow without their parents’ will.” Bellarmine
confirms this in *On Monks*, book 2, chapter 36. We deny it.

[Marginal heading: Children cannot bind themselves by a vow against their
parents’ will or without their knowledge.]

IV. First, Christ expressly condemns this in the Pharisees, who contended that
under the pretext of piety and a vow children could justly refuse duties owed
to parents. They said in Matthew 15:4–5: “Whoever shall say to his father or
mother, ‘Whatever gift from me would benefit you,’ even if he should by no
means honor his father or mother.” Although this obscure passage has admitted
various interpretations, everyone nevertheless agrees that they thought
duties owed to parents could be refused under the pretext of religion and the
worship of God.

This may be understood of some gift that a son had offered to God on his
father’s behalf and on whose pretext he refused to assist his needy parent, as
if to say: “Whatever you could have been helped by from me is a gift”—that is,
“I consecrated to God everything by which you otherwise could have been
relieved by me or from my goods in this need. Therefore, I can no longer
devote it to your use, and this releases me from the right that you have over
me and from every honor and duty owed to you.” Beza and Scaliger interpret it
in this way.

Or, as Masius preferred in chapter 6 of *Judges*, along with not a few other
learned men, and as seems more satisfactory to us, a formula of a vow and oath
is described here. By it a rebellious son devotes to God whatever, proceeding
from him, could benefit his father, in this sense: “I swear that I will confer
no benefit upon you, any more than it is lawful to use things sacred to God.
Among such things I place by an express vow whatever assistance I could give
you.” *Gift*—or, as Mark 7:11 has it, *Corban*—supply *let it be*, that is, “let
it be a thing devoted and sacred to God, which it is no longer lawful to
touch, *whatever from me would benefit you*,” that is, whatever could or might
benefit you. For τὸ ὠφελεῖσθαι, *to be benefited*, is here a δυνητικόν,
*potential*, verb, which includes possibility. “And he shall *no longer honor
his father or his mother* afterward with any assistance”—supply, *he shall be
innocent*—or, “let him *no longer honor them*.” These are the words of the
Pharisees forbidding a son to give his parent any help, so that they themselves
might divide the son’s goods with him. This seems to be implied in Mark 7:11:
“and you no longer permit him to do anything for his father.”

That this is the genuine sense of the passage is evident from the rites of the
Jews, among whom such formulas of vows and oaths were frequent. They often
occur in the Talmud, in the tractate on oaths and vows: קרבן שאני נהנה לך,
“Let everything by which I could be useful to you be *Corban*”; or קרבן כל מה
דתהנא מני, “the thing is sacred,” which therefore could not be touched,
“whatever proceeding from me could benefit you.” The Pharisees regarded such
vows as valid on account of sacred offerings, which they valued more highly
than the command to honor parents. They are therefore said to make God’s
command void through their tradition by withdrawing children from paternal
authority. On this account Christ severely rebukes them and all who follow
them.

V. Second, the honor prescribed for parents in the law cannot be consistent
with this opinion. It withdraws children from the reverence owed to their
parents in a matter of the greatest importance, namely, in choosing such a way
of life, which calls them away from the compliance owed to their parents.
Indeed, it exempts them from duties that by nature they are indispensably
bound to perform. Nor does it help to say that the obedience owed to God, the
Father of spirits, must be preferred to that owed to carnal parents. Although
we freely grant this, it does not follow that they are free and at liberty to
devote themselves to the monastic life. Obedience owed to God does not consist
in that; rather, the monastic life conflicts with it, since God forbids us to
become slaves of men (1 Cor. 7:23).

[Printed p. 94 | Internet Archive PDF 122 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

VI. Third, children are not under their own authority. Therefore, they cannot
make decisions about themselves against their parents’ will. Thus Numbers
30:4 wills that a vow made by a maiden in her father’s house while she was
still in her youth be rendered void if her father contradicted it. Nor is the
objection valid that this treats only girls who have not reached the years of
puberty. The law does not so limit it but speaks generally about maidens who
are in their father’s house, and it is established in law that such liberty is
not given to girls.

VII. Fourth, by abolishing the obedience of children, this opinion overturns
nature itself and destroys every society among men. For who would strive to
support and educate children if, after this has been done with every expense
and concern, they belong to others and not to their parents, and by a certain
law—or rather by a Saracenic tyranny—are torn away from them? Parents could
then not enjoy the comfort and service that they expected from their children,
especially in old age, when these duties are more necessary to them.

VIII. Fifth, canon 16 of the Council of Gangra opposes this impiety. It says:
“Whoever, being children, depart from their parents under the pretext of divine
worship and do not pay them due reverence, let them be anathema.” Bellarmine
tries in vain to restrict this to those who abandon needy parents placed in
extreme necessity and make vows concerning some religious order of life
against their will. The canon speaks absolutely and without any limitation,
and it is not lawful to restrict the obedience owed to parents to extreme
necessity.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

IX. The example of Abraham, called by God from his house and kindred (Gen.
12:1), cannot support either the impiety of children withdrawing themselves
from paternal authority or the kidnapping practiced by the Jesuits, who tear
them from their parents’ embrace with impunity. For 1. Abraham was already
married and no longer under his father’s guardianship. 2. He had a special
divine command, which does not obtain here. 3. He withdrew from idolaters, but
these children withdraw from Christian parents. 4. He did not vow celibacy or
obedience to any man that would diminish paternal authority. Indeed, he did
not leave his house against his father’s will, but with his consent and in his
company.

X. When the Levites are said in Deuteronomy 33:9 to “have said to their father
and their mother, ‘I do not know you,’ and to their brothers, ‘I do not
recognize them,’” and so forth, it does show that those who disregard kinship
and family ties in order to obey God when he expressly commands something are
worthy of praise. This can be referred either to the law in Leviticus 21:10–11,
where the priest is forbidden to attend the funeral of his father and mother,
and so forth, as though he did not know them; to the deed of the Levites in
Exodus 32, where they had to kill every guilty person without distinction of
persons; or to judgments that had to be exercised without respect of persons.
But this does not apply to those who, contrary to God’s command about honoring
parents, abandon them in order to enslave themselves to other men.

XI. If the bride in Psalm 45:10 is commanded to forget her people and her
father’s house, this does not mean that she should refuse the obedience owed
to her parents. Rather, she is to understand that by the law of marriage,
sanctioned by God from the beginning, parents must be left in order that she
may cleave to her husband. Thus the duty of believers is mystically signified:
they are bound to renounce the world, the flesh, and the old Adam in order to
be united to Christ by an indissoluble bond.

XII. When Christ in Luke 14:26 commands us to hate our parents for his sake,
he does not will that the duties of piety owed to them, which are founded in
nature and are indispensable, be refused. Rather, this must be understood
comparatively: love of parents must yield to divine love, so that when
parents’ commands conflict with obedience owed to God, we place them after
God’s commands.

XIII. Although in Luke 9:59–60 Christ does not permit the man whom he

[Printed p. 95 | Internet Archive PDF 123 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

was commanding to follow him to bury his father, in order to teach that the
divine call must be preferred to the burial of one’s father, that is, to
duties owed to parents, it does not follow that a son not called by Christ can
without necessity and against his parent’s will make a vow by which he
withdraws himself from parental authority forever. It is one thing to follow
Christ when he calls; another to embrace the monastic way of life without a
call and through ἐθελοθρησκείαν, *self-chosen worship*. It is one thing to
leave dead parents, especially if they are also spiritually dead, for the dead
to bury; another to cast off parents who are spiritually and temporally alive
and devote oneself to fictitious parents who are often dead in sin.

[Marginal heading: The second question, concerning the necessity of parental
consent in the marriage of children.]

XIV. The second question concerns *parental consent* in marriage: whether it
is lawful for children to contract marriage against their parents’ will. This
question is necessary not only to restrain youthful license, which indulges
itself more than is right in this matter by mutually giving the pledge of
marriage and sometimes even fulfilling it without consulting parents or
against their will, but also on account of the Roman Catholics, whose opinion
favors this ἀταξίᾳ, *disorder*, too much. See session 24 of the Council of
Trent, chapter 1, on the reform of marriage: “It must not be doubted that
clandestine marriages made by free consent are valid and true marriages as
long as the church has not rendered them void. Therefore, those who deny that
they are true are rightly condemned, and the holy synod condemns with anathema
those who affirm that marriages of children made without parental consent are
void and that parents can make them valid or void,” and so forth. Bellarmine
holds that parental consent is required for *propriety*, but not from
*necessity*, in *On Marriage*, chapter 19.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

XV. The orthodox, on the contrary, hold that parental consent in the marriages
of children is required not only for propriety but also from necessity, so
that without it marriages are not lawful and can also be dissolved. For the
state of the question, observe the following.

1. The question here is not whether paternal authority extends so far that
parents can compel unwilling children to marry and provide spouses for them
without their consent. Both sides deny this. 2. The question does not concern
parents who are insane or deranged, or captives in a far-distant country who
cannot give consent, but parents who are of sound mind and free and can
exercise their lawful authority. 3. The question does not concern
*emancipated children* and *those of full age*, who have already passed out of
paternal authority and whom no one doubts to be under their own authority. It
principally concerns *minors*, who are still entirely placed under paternal
authority. 4. The question is not whether every parental dissent can prevent
a marriage or render it void. We acknowledge that when surly and greedy
parents through sheer obstinacy refuse consent to their children’s honorable
marriages and cannot give sufficient reasons for their dissent after their
children’s humble petition, the children are not absolutely bound to their
will. They have the right to flee to the higher authority, to whom belongs the
judgment of whether the dissent is just or unjust. After considering the
matter, that authority can determine what the children should do, provide for
them, and in this respect supply the defect of parental consent. The principal
question, rather, is whether children may contract marriage against their
parents’ will or without their knowledge, without seeking or awaiting their
consent. We deny this.

[Marginal heading: The necessity of parental consent is proved, 1. from the
law, Ephesians 6:1.]

XVI. The reasons are as follows. 1. From the fifth commandment on honoring
parents. This honor consists principally in the obedience owed to them, and
since according to Paul’s prescription in Ephesians 6:1 it must be rendered to
them *in everything in the Lord*, marriage cannot be excepted. So much depends
upon marriage in determining the condition and fortune of one’s whole life,
and as a most difficult matter it exceeds

[Printed p. 96 | Internet Archive PDF 124 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the age, understanding, and judgment of children. How could those who show
themselves disobedient to their parents in a matter of the greatest moment be
said to obey them in everything?

XVII. 2. From particular declarations pertaining to this matter, in which
parents are given commands about giving their children in marriage. “You
shall not take wives for your sons from their daughters” (Exod. 34:16); “You
shall not give your daughter to his son, nor shall you take his daughter for
your son” (Deut. 7:3); “Give wives to your sons, and give your daughters to
husbands” (Jer. 29:6); “He who gives his virgin in marriage does well, and he
who does not give her does better” (1 Cor. 7:38). How could these things be
said unless children were subject to parental authority, especially in
contracting marriage?

[Marginal heading: 3. Exodus 22:16–17.]

XVIII. 3. There is an express law in Exodus 22:16–17 concerning a defiled and
seduced daughter. She is not given in marriage unless her father consents: “If
anyone seduces a virgin not yet betrothed and lies with her, he shall endow
her and have her as his wife.” This is repeated in Deuteronomy 22:29. Yet so
that this is not understood absolutely but with the condition of the father’s
consent, it is immediately added: “If the father of the virgin refuses to give
her, he shall pay money according to the measure of the dowry.”

Bellarmine’s objection in *On Marriage*, chapter 20, is not valid: that
this concerns not a marriage already contracted but a future one because of
the preceding defilement. The force of the argument always remains. If it is
left in the father’s power whether he wills to give his defiled daughter in
marriage to the seducer who seeks it, parental consent is certainly
presupposed as necessary in the daughter’s marriage. Indeed, the argument
binds even more strongly. If this is valid with respect to a defiled daughter,
whose disgrace seems to need covering by marriage, how much more with respect
to one who is whole and undefiled?

The same is proved by parity from Numbers 30:4, as seen above. A father can
render his daughter’s vow void; therefore, he can also render her marriage
void. If a vow, which is a promise made to God, is subject to a father’s will,
how much more is a promise made to men?

[Marginal heading: 4. From the practice of the Old and New Testaments.]

XIX. 4. From the continual practice and examples of the Old and New
Testaments. Abraham seeks Rebekah for his son (Gen. 24); Hagar seeks a wife
for Ishmael (Gen. 21); Jacob seeks wives from Laban (Gen. 29). See similar
examples in Caleb promising his daughter (Judg. 1:12); in Samson seeking a
wife from his parents (Judg. 14:3); in the Israelites binding themselves with
respect to the Benjaminites neither to give their sons nor to take daughters
from them (Judg. 21); and in Tamar replying to Amnon according to law and
custom by referring the arrangement of her marriage to her father: *Ask the
king for me* (2 Sam. 13:13).

[Marginal heading: 5. Because children are not under their own authority.]

XX. 5. Those who are not under their own authority cannot make decisions
about themselves. But children who are under paternal authority are not under
their own authority. 1. They are placed among the goods of their parents (Job
1:2, 10, 12), since they are from their parents and owe themselves to them. 2.
Parents were permitted to sell their children in need for servitude (Exod.
21:7). 3. A daughter’s vow is rendered void by her parent (Num. 30), because
she is under his authority. For if one may not make decisions about paternal
goods without parental consent, and a son is given no power to spend money or
sell an estate, how would he be permitted to dispose of himself? What is
wicked in the case of landed goods is far more damnable in the case of
persons, namely, that the property of another should be alienated. We punish
thieves who seize money and household goods against their owners’ will, but
the theft is graver when it concerns a far dearer thing.

[Marginal heading: 6. From absurd consequences.]

XXI. 6. The adversaries’ opinion is burdened with the gravest absurdities. Not
only is the divine law about honoring parents violated, the authority of
fathers undermined, and public decency flowing from the law of nature trampled
underfoot, but also an heir, against a father’s will,

[Printed p. 97 | Internet Archive PDF 125 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

is born into the family; domestic peace is disturbed when, contrary to the
father’s will, a
daughter-in-law who should be in the place of a daughter is introduced; and
the welfare of children who, because of their age, cannot yet provide for
themselves is brought into the most present danger. Lacking counsel and
ignorant and inexperienced in affairs, they are easily captured by
enticements and entangled in miserable marriages. Indeed, a window is opened
to lusts when many virgins are easily enticed into illicit intercourse by the
hope of marriage.

[Marginal heading: 7. From civil laws.]

XXII. 7. The civil laws written with the highest agreement both by the ancient
jurists and by the emperors are too well known to be ignored, too clear to be
obscured, and too firmly sanctioned to be lawfully abolished. “Natural and
civil reason advises this,” says the emperor in book 1 of the *Institutes*,
*On Marriage*, “that the parents’ authorization must precede.” He does not
grant this even to a widow under the age of twenty-five, although emancipated,
nor to a soldier, although many privileges were granted to that class of men:
“A soldier who is a son under paternal authority does not contract marriage
without his father’s will.” Paul, in *On the Rite of Marriage*, denies that it
“can stand unless everyone consents, both those who join and those under whose
authority they are.” Ulpian says: “If the father is mad and the grandfather
sane, the grandfather’s opinion must be sought.”

The reasons added in the civil law must also be observed. 1. *Paternal
authority* is pressed, on account of which children are not under their own
authority. 2. *Succession of inheritance*, lest an heir arise for a father
against his will. 3. *Parental affection*, which is presumed to form the best
plan for a son, for whom it prepares everything according to his wish and from
whose offspring it hopes for a lasting remembrance. 4. *The weak judgment of
children*, and so forth.

XXIII. Many canonists among the Roman Catholics judge no differently. See the
testimonies of *Evaristus, Leo I, Pelagius, Urban, Nicholas*, and others in
*Espensaeus*, *On Clandestine Marriages*. He gathered many things supporting
this opinion from the fathers and from the pagans themselves, comic writers
as well as others. *Zanchi* and *Gerhard* should also be consulted in the
locus on marriage, page 138, and so forth. They collect various relevant
passages from Gratian, Cassander, Erasmus, Vives, Gropper, and Beatus Rhenanus.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XXIV. If Esau took a wife without consulting his parents, this is so far from
being able to confirm the validity of such marriages that it more strongly
proves their invalidity, for it was most bitter to his parents and had a ruinous
outcome. If Isaac did not render it void, this must be attributed either to
paternal indulgence, which tolerates in children many things that it cannot
change, or to Esau’s obstinacy, by which he could not be drawn away from that
unjust and unlawful union. But an argument from fact to right is invalid. We
live here not by examples but by laws. The same must be said concerning the
apocryphal example of Tobit that is alleged.

XXV. What God has joined, man cannot separate. But God cannot be regarded as
the author of a union that was made only by violating his laws. Nor, if an
ecclesiastical blessing intervened, can it make the marriage valid. It was
either extorted from the church through fraud or evil arts, or granted to the
church’s detriment by its corrupt ministers. When that blessing is rendered
void, the church’s authority is not diminished but asserted.

XXVI. The statement that a man must leave his parents and cleave to his wife
is valid only for true and lawful marriages. Their bond is so close that if
either parents or wife must happen to be left, he must cleave to his wife
instead. But in an unlawful union, who could think that the Lord, who is
himself the defender and vindicator of the right of paternal authority, has
absolved openly rebellious and defiant children from it?

XXVII. Although marriages without parental consent are legally null as
clandestine, even if the church’s blessing and intercourse have followed, in

[Printed p. 98 | Internet Archive PDF 126 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

practice they are not always annulled. This occurs from paternal indulgence,
so that greater evils may be avoided, not from justice. It is one thing to ask
about law; another about ἐπιεικείᾳ, *equity*, or the remission of law in some
case. In this latter sense must be understood whatever certain orthodox
theologians and jurists say concerning clandestine marriages if they have
been consummated by intercourse.

Otherwise, if paternal right were diminished by illicit intercourse so that
it could not render such marriages void, an opportunity would be offered for
innumerable acts of illicit intercourse and lust, and a door would be opened
for any scoundrel to seduce virgins born to respectable families and trample
paternal authority underfoot. Nor can it stand in the way that a virgin who
has lost her virginity is compelled to go without a husband and so suffers an
injury. She must impute this to her own foolishness, which will make others
more cautious lest they allow themselves to be corrupted. She ought not to
complain of an injury inflicted upon her by her parent but of the misery that
she brought upon herself.

XXVIII. The case of servants and children is not entirely the same here,
because the obligation between master and servant is merely civil, but that
between parents and children is natural. Servants belong to their masters only
by possession, but children belong to their parents by generation. Thus, the
necessity of a master’s consent in the marriages of servants is not equal to
that of paternal consent in the marriage of children. Further, if servants
are slaves, as formerly, they can marry without their master’s will neither by
divine nor by civil laws. Hence in Exodus 21:4 and Leviticus 25:44 the master
is given authority to give a wife to his servant.

XXIX. Although the consent of the contracting parties and the intention of
the minister are present in such marriages, lawful consent is nevertheless
lacking. This consent is considered not only with respect to the contracting
parties but also with respect to those under whose authority they are. Nor, if
a son is free, is he immediately ἀνυπεξούσιος, *independent of authority*, and
absolutely under his own authority. Otherwise, how could a father have sold
his son (Exod. 21), and how could the father of a daughter and the husband of
a wife have rendered a vow void (Num. 30)? If a son cannot even alienate his
parents’ goods, how much less himself?
