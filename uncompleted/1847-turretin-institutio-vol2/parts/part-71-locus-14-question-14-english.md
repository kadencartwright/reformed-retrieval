QUESTION XIV.

THE OBJECT OF SATISFACTION

*Did Christ die universally for each and every human being, or only for the
elect? The former denied; the latter affirmed.*

I. The question concerning the *object of Christ’s death*, or the
*universality of satisfaction and redemption*, was raised by various people
long ago and is still raised today. We must therefore discuss it as well, lest
anything appear lacking in this treatment of Christ’s satisfaction.

[Marginal heading: The Pelagian position.]

II. It is agreed that among the ancients the Pelagians and Semi-Pelagians
asserted universal redemption. Thus Prosper says in his letter to Augustine
concerning the remnants of the Pelagian heresy: “This is their definition and
profession: that the Lord died for the whole human race and absolutely no one
was excluded from redemption by his blood,” etc. Among the propositions in
which the Gauls boasted that they had found condemnable things in Augustine
was also “that the Savior was not crucified for the redemption of the whole
world.” Faustus, *On Free Will* 1.4, says that those who say the Savior did not
die for everyone depart far from the path of piety. Hincmar, in his letter to
Pope Nicholas recorded by Flodoard 3.14, places among Gottschalk’s errors his
teaching that Christ did not pour out his precious blood to God the Father for
the redemption and salvation of all people, but only for those who are saved,
that is, for all the elect. To the same point belong the anathemas of the
pseudo-Council of Arles in the letter of Faustus, champion of the
Semi-Pelagians, to Lucidus—a letter which Sirmond does not deny was
Semi-Pelagian. But just as Augustine had opposed them in his own day, so
Prosper and Fulgentius, his disciples, and other heralds of Christ’s grace
followed in his steps and defended the truth vigorously and courageously. It
was later confirmed by Remigius, bishop of Lyons, in *On the Three Letters*,
and by the third Council of Valence held in 855, canon 4.

[Marginal heading: The Jesuit position.]

III. The same controversy was later revived among Roman Catholics. Some
defended universal redemption with the Semi-Pelagians; others defended its
particularity with Augustine and his genuine disciples. This was especially
evident between Jesuits and Jansenists: the former, the offspring of the
Pelagians, keenly maintain the universality of Christ’s death, while the latter
vigorously contend for its particularity. They follow their master Jansenius,
who treated this argument extensively and soundly in his *Augustinus* 3.20,
the *Apology for Jansenius* published in 1644, articles 17, 19, and 20, and the
*Catechism on Grace* 7, *On Predestination*, question 65.

[Marginal heading: The Lutheran position.]

IV. The controversy then passed from Roman Catholics to Protestants, not only
because the Lutherans retain and defend universal satisfaction with the
Jesuits—as do Eckard, *Compendium of Controversies* 15, *On Predestination*,
question 6; Brochmann, *On the Grace of God* 2, questions 17–19; and
others—but especially because of the Arminians, distinguished by the name
Remonstrants,

[Printed p. 401 | Internet Archive PDF 429 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

who sought to call back Popery through a rear entrance and sacrifice to the
idol of free will. They drew most of their errors from Molina, Lessius, Suárez,
and other Jesuits, including this one about the universality of Christ’s
death. It is therefore placed second among the errors rejected and marked with
a black theta by the Synod of Dort, chapter 2, *On Christ’s Death*, in the
rejection of errors.

[Marginal heading: The Arminian position.]

V. The article reads as follows in the writings of the Remonstrants at the
Synod of Dort, p. 280: “The price of redemption which Christ offered to his
Father is not merely in and of itself sufficient to redeem the whole human
race, but was also paid for each and every person by the decree, will, and
grace of God the Father. Thus no one was strictly excluded from sharing in the
fruits of Christ’s death by an antecedent decree of God. 2. By the merit of his
death Christ reconciled God the Father to the whole human race to this extent:
because of Christ’s merit the Father could and would, without prejudice to his
justice and truth, enter into and establish a new covenant of grace with
sinners and people liable to condemnation.” They therefore hold: 1. By God’s
counsel Christ died for each and every person in such a way that his death was
not only sufficient in itself because of its intrinsic worth, but was also
sufficiently offered for each and every one. God willed that what was in itself
sufficient for all should be paid in the place, or for the benefit, of each and
every one—that is, of the whole human race—so that through its intervention
God resolved thereafter to deal graciously with humankind. Christ’s death is
thus not a promise of the new covenant but its cause and foundation. 2. By the
intention of both the Father and himself, Christ obtained for every single
person, the perishing as well as those who would be saved, restoration to a
state of grace and salvation. Thus no one is liable to or must be condemned
because of original sin, but everyone is free from the guilt of that sin. 3.
By the Father’s counsel Christ underwent death for everyone without any fixed
and definite plan to save anyone in particular. Consequently the procurement
made by Christ’s death could have retained its necessity and usefulness
complete and perfect in every respect even if the redemption procured had
never actually been applied to a single individual. 4. By his satisfaction
Christ certainly merited neither salvation itself nor the faith by which his
satisfaction would be effectually applied to salvation for anyone. Instead he
only acquired for the Father full freedom and power to deal anew with people,
to enter either a covenant of grace or a covenant of works, and to prescribe
whatever conditions he pleased. Fulfillment of these conditions would depend
upon human free will, and therefore either no one or everyone could fulfill
them. 5. Procurement extends more widely than application: salvation was
procured for everyone, though it is applied to only a few. All these points may
be gathered both from the Hague Conference and from the Remonstrants’ writings
at the Synod of Dort, in the declaration of their position concerning the
second article.

[Marginal heading: The Universalist position.]

VI. Some of our own theologians who defend universal grace approach this
position, if not completely, yet to a large extent. Since they posit a
universal φιλανθρωπίαν and love of God toward the human race, they think that
from this love the Father sent Christ into the world as a universal remedy to
procure the salvation of each and every person on condition of faith. Christ
also died with the same intention for all under the same condition, although
the fruit and efficacy of his death reaches only the few to whom God decided
by a special decree to give faith. They accordingly maintain that the decree
of Christ’s death preceded the decree of election. In sending Christ, God
regarded these no more than those, but equally appointed Christ as Savior for
everyone.

[Printed p. 402 | Internet Archive PDF 430 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Indeed, strictly speaking, he intended in Christ not so much salvation as the
possibility of salvation: namely, the removal through satisfaction of the
obstacle which justice placed in the way of their salvation. By this means the
door of salvation was opened to them, so that God, having been appeased, could
enter a new covenant with them without prejudice to justice and consider
giving them salvation. To this end, through the universal call, he takes care
that Christ, given and slain in this way for all, is offered to each and every
one. But because he foresaw that no one would believe on account of the heart’s
innate wickedness, they say that by another special decree God decided to give
some people faith, by which they would believe in Christ and become certain
possessors of salvation, while the rest remained in unbelief and were most
justly condemned because of it. In this they rightly differ from the
Arminians. All these points are not obscurely gathered from their writings.
Thus Cameron says on Hebrews 2:9, “On condition of faith, Christ’s death
pertains equally to absolutely everyone.” Testard says in *Irenicum*, thesis
78: “The end of Christ’s being given as a propitiatory in his blood was the
establishment of the new covenant of grace with the entire human race and,
since justice no longer resisted, the possible calling and salvation of all
people.” And thesis 79: “In this sense no one will deny that Christ died for
each and every person, so that God’s word may maintain its faithfulness.”
Amyraut says in his *Dissertation on Universal Grace*: “Christ’s redemption
must be considered in two ways: first as absolute, insofar as some actually
embrace it; then as furnished with a condition, insofar as it is offered under
the provision that whoever embraces it may possess it. In the former way it is
particular; in the latter, universal. Likewise, the character of this
appointment is twofold: particular insofar as it has joined to it the decree
to give faith, and universal insofar as that decree is separated from it.” He
had taught this more explicitly in *Treatise on Predestination* 7: “Since
people’s misery is equal and universal, and since God’s desire to deliver them
from it through so great a Redeemer proceeds from the mercy which he had
toward us as his creatures equally fallen into so great a ruin, the grace of
redemption which he offered and procured for them had to be equal and
universal, provided they were equally disposed to receive it,” etc.

VII. Although they all agree that Christ died for each and every person, they
do not all express their meaning in the same way. Some have openly said, as
noted above, that Christ died conditionally for each and every one, and
absolutely only for the elect. Others, seeing this position burdened with grave
absurdities, have refrained from that manner of speaking. They preferred to
say that Christ did not die for people under the provision that they believe,
but absolutely, whether they believe or not, so that access to salvation might
be free for everyone who believes and the way to the new covenant might be
equally opened to all, the obstacle of justice which prevented God from
covenanting with people having been removed through Christ’s death. Yet they
all alike reach this conclusion: Christ satisfied for and procured
reconciliation, remission of sins, and salvation for each and every one. If
many are deprived of these, the cause must not be sought in any insufficiency
of Christ’s death or deficiency in his will and intention, but solely in their
own unbelief, by which they perversely reject the grace of Christ offered to
them.

VIII. The common Reformed position, however, is that by the Father’s sheer
εὐδοκίᾳ Christ was appointed and given as Redeemer and Head, not for everyone,
but for a definite number of people who constitute his mystical body by God’s
election. For these alone Christ, perfectly conscious of his calling, willed
and resolved to die in execution of the decree of election and the Father’s
counsel,

[Printed p. 403 | Internet Archive PDF 431 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and to add to the infinite price of his death the most effectual and particular
intention of his will to substitute himself for them and acquire faith and
salvation for them.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

IX. The state of the question is therefore readily drawn out. 1. The question
does not concern the *price and sufficiency of Christ’s death*: is Christ’s
death in itself sufficient for the salvation of all? Both sides agree that,
since it is of infinite worth, it was altogether sufficient for the redemption
of each and every person if God had seen fit to extend it to the whole world.
Here belongs the distinction employed by the Fathers and retained by various
theologians: Christ died *sufficiently for all, but effectually only for the
elect*. Understood of the worth of Christ’s death, this is entirely true,
though the expression is less accurate if referred to Christ’s will and
counsel. The question properly concerns the *Father’s counsel* in handing over
his Son and the *intention of Christ as he died*. Did the Father appoint his
Son for each and every one, and did the Son hand himself over to death with
the plan and intention of substituting himself in the place of each and every
one, satisfying for them and acquiring salvation for them? Or did he will to
hand himself over only for the elect, whom the Father gave him to redeem and
whose Head he was to be? The hinge of the question must therefore be brought
back to this: we are not dealing with the nature and power of Christ’s death
in itself, but with the counsel of the Father who appointed him and the
intention of Christ who underwent it. Hence it is sufficiently clear that this
does not concern only a will of εὐαρεστίας, as the learned men repeatedly
assert, but a will of εὐδοκίας, under which no one can deny that Christ’s
mission and death fall.

X. 2. The question does not concern the *fruit and efficacy of Christ’s
death*: do each and every person actually share in it? Puccius and Huber once
maintained this, but the learned men acknowledge that its efficacy extends
only to believers. Rather, the question concerns the *appointment of God* who
sent his Son into the world and the *intention of Christ as he died*. Did this
regard each and every person, so that Christ substituted himself in their
place, satisfied for them, and procured remission of sins and salvation? Or
did it regard the elect alone? They affirm the former; we affirm the latter.

XI. 3. The question is not whether many benefits come even to the reprobate by
occasion of Christ’s death. It is owing to his death that the gospel is
preached to every creature, that the Gentiles’ gross idolatry has been
abolished from many parts of the world, that the profane impiety of people is
restrained to no small degree by the bridle of the divine Word, and that some
often obtain many excellent though not saving gifts of the Holy Spirit. All
these certainly flow from Christ’s death, since there would have been no place
for them in the church if Christ had not died. Rather, did the counsel of God
and Christ’s will make Christ’s suretyship and satisfaction in the place of
each and every one, as they maintain, or only in the place of the elect, as we
hold?

[Marginal heading: Christ is proved not to have died for everyone.]

XII. The arguments are: 1. Christ’s mission and death are repeatedly restricted
to certain people—Christ’s people, his sheep, his friends, the church and his
body—and are never extended to each and every person.

[Marginal heading: From the restriction of Christ’s death to certain people.]

Thus he was to be called Jesus because *he would save his people from their
sins* (Matt. 1:21). Elsewhere he is called the Savior *of his body* (Eph.
5:23), the *good Shepherd who was to lay down his life for his sheep* (John
10:15) and for his *friends* (John 15:13), and the one who was to die *to
gather the children of God scattered throughout the world* (John 11:52) and
*to acquire the church with his own blood* (Acts 20:28; Eph. 5:25–26). But if
Christ died for each and every person, why would Scripture so often restrict
his death to a few? How could it simply say

[Printed p. 404 | Internet Archive PDF 432 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

that he is the Savior of his people and his body if he is also the Savior of
the rest? How could it say he laid down his life for the sheep, the children of
God, and the church if by God’s counsel and Christ’s own intention he also died
for others? Would not this have been a more splendid proof of his love and a
foundation of greater consolation?

XIII. General objections are made here to no purpose. 1. “Scripture restricts
Christ’s death to a few in some places but extends it to all elsewhere.” As
will be taught below, that universality is not absolute but limited: it regards
every kind of individual, not every individual of every kind. 2. “Those texts
do not concern procurement alone, which can be universal, but procurement and
application together, which are particular.” Yet the terms the Holy Spirit
uses in the cited passages—*to be Savior, to lay down one’s life for someone,
to give oneself for someone*, and to περιποιεῖν some people—properly designate
satisfaction, procurement, and the acquisition of salvation. Even if they drew
application after them, they would be no less effective, since procurement and
application are inseparable and have the same extent, as will shortly be
proved. 3. “Christ can be said to have died absolutely for some and
conditionally for all at the same time.” This gratuitously assumes what had to
be proved: a double intention in the dying Christ, one conditional and
extending to all, the other absolute and referring to a few. Scripture nowhere
mentions such a thing. It does speak of application as conditional, but never
of redemption. Nor does the nature of the case permit it. On their hypothesis,
in the decree concerning Christ’s death according to which he died—and with
the very intention by which it was decreed, since execution must correspond to
appointment and the disposition and judgment of the dying Christ cannot differ
from those of the Father who appointed him—there was as yet no consideration
of the elect, who were only separated from others by the subsequent decree.
Moreover, who could believe that in the one most simple act by which God
decreed all things, though we conceive them distinctly, there were two
intentions so different, if not contrary, that he willed in one way to die for
everyone but in another way only for some? Indeed, since he cannot will to die
absolutely for the elect without, conversely, not willing to die for the
reprobate distinguished from them, we cannot conceive how in one act he both
willed and did not will to die for the same people. 4. “Even if the passages
spoke of the elect, they should not be understood of them alone to the
exclusion of others, just as Paul, when saying that Christ handed himself over
for him, does not exclude others from sharing in the same love” (Gal. 2:20).
But all the cited passages, if not explicitly, at least implicitly include an
exclusion in their description of the people in question—a description that
cannot apply to others. Thus although the blessing is promised to Abraham’s
seed without adding the particle *alone*, the limitation is sufficiently
inferred from the nature of the case. Likewise all these passages aim to
magnify Christ’s extraordinary love toward his sheep, for whom he lays down
his life, and toward the church and people for whom he handed himself over. But
if they have nothing distinctive here above the rest, why is the immense love
of Christ laying down his life, pouring out his blood, and handing himself over
applied specifically to them? Paul’s example does not help, because he does not
claim this as a privilege peculiar to himself, but as a good common to the
other elect and believers. He presents himself as their example, so that those
in the same state may proclaim the same thing about themselves.

XIV. The particular objections have no more solidity. 1. On Matthew 1:21:
“Although Christ is called the Savior of his people in a special sense because
salvation is actually and really conferred upon them, this does not prevent his
also being Savior of others because salvation was obtained for them, though it
was not applied on account of their unbelief. In the same way Paul says that
God is Savior of all, but especially of believers” (1 Tim. 4:10). This
gratuitously assumes that Christ is Savior of those for whom salvation is
indeed acquired but not applied. The very verb *to save* denotes the actual
communication of salvation. Christ is Jesus not merely because he wills or is
able to save and because it is not through him that people fail to be saved,
but because he really and actually saves his people—not only acquiring
salvation by his merit but also applying it by his efficacy. By imposing the
name, the angel openly identifies this as God’s intention in sending Christ
and as the end of his mission.

[Printed p. 405 | Internet Archive PDF 433 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Nor does 1 Timothy 4:10 prove the contrary. There σωτὴρ, which in the latter
clause is properly and strictly applied to believers, as the word μάλιστα
indicates, is used in the former clause in the broader sense of *preserver*,
because in him we are, live, and move (Acts 17:28). Thus in Psalm 36:6, to
which Paul alludes, God is said to σώζειν, “preserve,” people and animals.
Hence Chrysostom, Oecumenius, Primasius, and Ambrose say he is the Savior of
all *in the present*, with regard to this present life, but only of believers
*in the future*, with regard to eternal life. Thomas likewise says on this
text: “He is Savior of the present and future life, because he saves everyone
with bodily salvation, and therefore it says, ‘of all people’; likewise he
saves the good with spiritual salvation, and therefore it says, ‘especially of
believers.’”

XV. 2. On John 10:15 it should not be answered: “The sheep for whom he laid
down his life are not the elect alone.” The passage clearly teaches that it can
pertain to none but the elect, since it concerns sheep who hear Christ’s voice
and follow him, whom he knows and fervently loves, and whom he must bring so
that they become one flock under one Shepherd (vv. 15–16). He will give them
eternal life, and no one can snatch them from Christ’s hands. This cannot be
said of any but the elect, who are called sheep antecedently by reason of their
eternal appointment and consequently by reason of their calling in time.
Neither should it be answered: “He is said to have laid down his life for the
sheep because only the sheep receive the fruits acquired by his death, while
the Lord’s death is of no use to the rest because of their unbelief. Thus *to
die for someone* can mean simply dying with the intention to benefit someone,
which applies to all, or it can mean dying actually to benefit him, which is
said only of the sheep.” The expression *to lay down one’s life for someone*
can no more be referred to receiving the fruits of Christ’s death than can the
statement that he gave himself as an ἀντίλυτρον for all, which they extend to
each and every person. There is no reason why the former should refer to both
intention and effect while the latter refers only to the intention and desire
to help. Nor can any difference between the two be conceived. Whoever wills
to die for someone in order to benefit him thereby wills actually to benefit
him, and when he has the power he actually does so. Otherwise an account must
be given of why Christ obtains what he intends in the sheep but falls short of
his goal in the rest. Nor should it be answered: “He could not have died for
the sheep *as such*, because then they would have been Christ’s sheep before he
died for them and acquired them as his own. He therefore died for them only as
sinners, something they share with others, and so he laid down his life for
the others as well.” Although they were not yet *sheep in actuality*, they
were such *by appointment*, by which they were given to Christ to be redeemed
and acquired as the good Shepherd who would redeem them by his own blood. Thus
they were given to Christ in God’s counsel before they were in Christ’s hands
(John 17:24). Indeed, Christ’s own mission is founded on that gift: “This is
the will,” he says, “of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all he
has given me, but raise it up on the last day” (John 6:39). Otherwise, unless
some company of people to be saved had been before God as he resolved to send

[Printed p. 406 | Internet Archive PDF 434 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Christ, Christ’s death would have had an uncertain outcome, and this entire
mystery of redemption could have been rendered void by human obstinacy.

XVI. 3. On Ephesians 5:25 and Titus 2:14 it cannot be answered: “Christ is
indeed said to have handed himself over for the people and the church, but the
word *alone* is not present, so it cannot be concluded that he did not hand
himself over for any others.” The exclusion is sufficiently and clearly
implied by both the words themselves and the nature of the case. The handing
over in view arises from Christ’s love for the church as his bride, and such a
love necessarily includes exclusion. Thus when Paul says in the preceding
verse, “Husbands, love your wives,” the exclusion is sufficiently understood
even though it is not expressed. No one would tolerate an adulterer wrangling
in this way to conceal his crime: the command indeed said, *Husbands, love your
wives*, but it did not say them alone. 2. The handing over in view has as its
end the sanctification of the church and its being brought to salvation—that
is, application together with procurement. It is clear that this belongs to
none but the elect and the church alone. Since he handed himself over for no
one except to this end, he cannot be said to have handed himself over for
anyone who does not attain that end.

XVII. 4. On Matthew 20:28; 26:28; and Hebrews 9:28 it is objected to no
purpose: “Here *many* is contrasted not with *all* but with *one* and *few*, as
in Romans 5:19 and Daniel 12:1; indeed, it is often put for *all*.” The many in
question are described by marks that cannot be common to each and every
person: he gave himself as λύτρον ἀντὶ πολλῶν, *in the place of many*, by an
actual substitution; he poured out his blood for many for the remission of
sins; and he was offered εἰς τὸ πολλῶν ἀνενεγκεῖν ἁμαρτίας, for the actual
abolition of sin. Though “many” may sometimes be contrasted with one and few,
and even used for all, this need not immediately be the case everywhere.
Indeed, it is often so extensive as to include many while denying all. Hence
Jerome says on Matthew 20: “He did not say that he gave his life for all, but
for many, that is, for those who will be willing to believe,” who are none
other than the elect, in whom God works both willing and accomplishing. The
interlinear gloss says, “for many—not for all, but for the predestined.”
