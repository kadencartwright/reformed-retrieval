6. Ruiz: *It is one thing for GOD to concur effectively toward the material
action of sin, and another for him properly and of himself to perform the same
action. For the latter requires that GOD, by his free will, make us perform the
material action of sin by determining us, or at least by impelling us toward-*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, *On the Will of God*, disputation 32, section 7.]

[Chapter XXVI, Part B; printed page 393; IA PDF page 452; BSB canvas 445]

*it. The former, however, of itself inclines rather toward good, and is not
inconsistent with mere permission but is instead included in it. GOD is of
himself the efficient cause of every action. Yet it happens accidentally and
contrary to GOD’s intention,* says Suárez—or, as Fasolus puts it, *that the
human will determines the divine concurrence toward the material action of
sin. And GOD,* says Arriaga, *is physically more particularly inclined in
first act toward a good work than toward evil. And determining and
non-determining concurrence,* says Ruiz, *differ metaphysically in species;
for although the material terminus of each is the same, its formal terminus is
not. Theologically, however, the termini differ in species: an act is related
to determining concurrence as something to be determined by GOD so that it
may exist in the nature of things, on account of the effect that GOD has from
himself toward the whole positive reality of the act. But it is related to
non-determining concurrence precisely on account of the formal character of
freedom, GOD wishing his concurrence to be determined by no such action. Thus
a ring inserted on one foot, and several rings inserted on both feet and
fastened to a wall, are physically of the same species; but with respect to
the freedom to go where you please, they differ in species.*

[Marginal reference: Suárez, *Opuscules*, book 2, *On Concurrence*, chapter 2,
no. 4.]

[Marginal reference: Fasolus, on I–II, question 14, article 13, doubt 17, point
5, no. 384.]

[Marginal reference: Arriaga, at the same place, no. 48.]

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, at the same place.]

I reply: 1. In their causal character, *GOD’s effectively concurring toward a
material act of sinning* and *GOD’s properly and of himself performing that
same action* are not different things. For each occurs with freedom preserved:
predetermination puts nothing in the will which necessitates it more strongly
than GOD’s non-determining action puts there. 2. If indifferent concurrence of
itself inclines more toward good, and if GOD, as Arriaga says, *is physically
more particularly inclined in first act toward a good act than toward evil*,
then: (1) this concurrence is not indifferent. Perhaps the will to concur
inclines toward good rather than evil; but that is irrelevant, since GOD’s
will puts nothing real into the concurrence by which it is borne toward good
rather than evil. (2) If God supplies a weightier concurrence to a good action
than to an evil one, then he does not concur toward a good and an evil act by
one and the same concurrence numerically, because lighter and weightier
concurrences differ numerically. 3. Ruiz says that *indifferent concurrence is
not inconsistent with permission, but rather that such concurrence is
included in it.* From this it is sufficiently clear that, according to these
men, GOD is equally a permitter in acts of believing in and loving God and in
acts of blaspheming the Holy Spirit, and that by equal physical efficiency GOD
promotes both. 4. If permission is taken as the absence of compulsion and of
violence against-*

[Chapter XXVI, Part B; printed page 394; IA PDF page 453; BSB canvas 446]

*freedom, we appeal to the Scriptures which establish our predetermination:
does not predetermination include such permission just as much as indifferent
concurrence does? And those men teach such permission. Therefore determining
and non-determining concurrence do not differ in the cardinal distinction,
assigned by the Jesuit, between binding and not binding the will. Nor is there
reason for our opponents to wage such great disputes over the innocent
prepositions *pre-* and *con-*, since they attribute to God a truly causal
co-determination in the material acts of sin, and we a predetermination. Alas,
the innocent and slender preposition *pre-* has deserved so much evil that it
has caused such trouble for the Jesuit and Arminian schools! 5. He says that
*GOD is not properly and of himself the efficient cause why the material act
of sin exists rather than the honest act.* If he speaks of a moral cause in
itself, he wanders beyond the course. If he speaks of a physical cause, then
GOD’s concurrence will not be the physical cause in itself of good acts
either, because God concurs toward good and evil acts by the same concurrence
numerically. And if God is the accidental physical cause of the honest act,
let them give that act a cause in itself—namely, free choice. 6. The phrase
“rather than” implies that GOD is the cause why the material act of sin exists,
namely καλα τι, and that GOD in some manner wills sin to occur, although he
wills *rather* that it not occur—if the unfortunate will did not otherwise
force the matter to happen. 7. The Devil is not the physical cause of sin in
itself, but only its moral cause. On this reasoning it would be equally
permissible to free both the Devil and God from the causality of sin, for the
Devil is not the cause in itself why sin exists with a physical existence.
This is splendid advocacy for GOD’s holiness! 8. *GOD,* he says, *is properly
and of himself the efficient cause, with freedom, of every action.* Therefore,
I say, he causes the material action of sin—and therefore, by our opponents’
logic, also the malice inseparably adhering to the act. 9. The Jesuit also
absurdly maintains that determination is the formal ground of a cause’s
efficient causality in itself. On that account the grace of God would not in
itself be the cause of supernatural acts, since for the Jesuits neither grace
nor God himself is the determiner. Determination is only a condition of
causing, not the formal ground of causing. 10. It is false that determining
and non-determining concurrence differ theologically in species as regards
binding—that is, as the Jesuits explain it, doing violence to—the will. This
begs the question. 11. According to the Jesuit, indifferent concurrence
concurs as the cause-*

[Chapter XXVI, Part B; printed page 395; IA PDF page 454; BSB canvas 447]

*in itself of the material act in its whole generic, specific, and numerical
character. Therefore such concurrence is the cause why the material act of
malice exists rather than an honest act—yet the Jesuit denies this. The
inference is proved because efficiently giving numerical being is giving
determinate being, since nothing is more determinate than numerical being.
Thus determining and non-determining concurrence differ only in the order of
concurrence or causation, through the bare *pre-* and *con-*, as far as the
coercive necessitation of the will is concerned. Empty, therefore, is the
pretext of the Jesuits who assert that GOD’s concurrence is not the cause of
the material act of sin because it does not determinately effect that act—as
though a robber were not a murderer because he is not prepared to kill this
or that determinate traveler, but travelers in general. Smising, however,
acquits us: *Heretics*—by whom he means the orthodox—*are not blameworthy on
this point because they sometimes say that GOD wills the act of sin but not
its malice. For all theologians say this. They are blameworthy because they
say that GOD by his will necessitates our will toward the act.* Yet we do not
assert any necessitation hostile to freedom, O Jesuit. Therefore even by a
great Jesuit’s judgment we are not guilty in this case.

[Marginal reference: Smising, vol. 1, treatise 3, disputation 3, *On the Will
of GOD*, question 3, no. 75.]

Ruiz: *GOD is bound by the intrinsic and quidditative law of his own goodness
and justice, which is the dictate of wisdom concerning the honorable good,
joined with the necessary love of himself, neither to will an act of malice
nor to predetermine the act of hatred of himself.*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, book 1, *On the Will of GOD*, disputation 26,
section 6.]

I reply: We see no such quidditative law except that by which God loves
himself, loves the good, and detests injustice. Predetermining the creature to
all positive acts is in no way opposed to GOD’s holy nature. Here GOD’s free
will is a rule to him. Thus teach the great Schoolmen—Scotus, Thomas, and
Gabriel. Mæratius rightly says: *GOD acts justly according to his own free will,
as we do according to law; and if he acted otherwise, what he did would be
equally just.* So Curiel: *Observe that acts which are sins can be considered
in three ways without relation to law: (1) by imagining a possible cause under
which there were no law; (2) by considering the same acts according to their
nature and intellectually abstracting from their relation to law; (3) by
considering those acts insofar as God knows them by the knowledge of simple
intelligence as such possible acts, prior in order to an act of will or an
intellectual precept which prohibited them.* Curiel hence proves that *all
sins have the character of sins because they are-*

[Marginal reference: Scotus, book 1, distinction 38, sole question.]

[Marginal reference: Thomas Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* I, question 21,
article 1.]

[Marginal reference: Gabriel, book 1, distinction 42, question *sine vinc.*]

[Marginal reference: Mæratius, vol. 1, *On the Goodness and Evil of Human
Acts*, disputation 11, section 11, no. 6.]

[Marginal reference: Curiel, I–II, question 71, article 6, doubt 2, § 1.]

[Chapter XXVI, Part B; printed page 396; IA PDF page 455; BSB canvas 448]

*prohibited by some law, at least natural law.* Thus something is technically
bad because it departs from the rules of an art; and an act is morally culpable
because it is a παράβασις, a transgression, of law. Thus Philip Faber of Faenza:
*Malice is separable from every act. For hatred of GOD, which is an act evil
in itself, is not morally evil if elicited by one without reason, or with
invincible ignorance, or from natural inadvertence, even though he has the
act. Therefore malice is separable from an act, and GOD can will the material
but not the formal element of sin.* Two things necessarily follow. 1. God’s
willing and predetermining the will toward a material act is not morally evil,
and consequently is not contrary to GOD’s holy nature. 2. It follows that
God’s free will is for him the primary rule in external actions. Mæratius is
right here: *If, of two people moving a stone, one is commanded by GOD to move
it and the other moves it though forbidden, the one sins and the other obeys
GOD; obligation alone makes the difference.* Accordingly, in predetermining
the material act God does not sin or become implicated in wickedness; but if a
human being predetermined another to the act, he would be a cause of sin
unless exempted by law.

[Marginal reference: Faber of Faenza, professor at Padua, on book 1 of the
*Sentences*, distinction 39, disputation 54, sole question, no. 58.]

Ruiz: *The Priscillianists and Manichaeans would not have answered this
argument—“GOD is good by nature; therefore he is not the author of evil”—by
saying that it does not follow, because GOD and the stars are not subject to
law.*

[Marginal note: Predetermination of the will is not Manichaeism.]

I reply: Although the stars are not subject to law, there is nevertheless
another reason why God would be made the author of sin. Those heretics imagined
that, by the Creator’s ordination, the stars impressed upon human souls a
physical quality which fatally determined the will to all its acts. Thus,
freedom being overthrown, God would be made the cause of sin in the same way
that he is the author of rain and snow. Priscillian said that souls were
created from GOD’s substance, as the Magdeburg Centuriators note, and wholly
denied freedom of choice, as Augustine, Sulpitius Severus, and Origen teach. He
also said that nothing happens in either body or soul except by a stellar
constitution. If God implanted in a human being a soul created out of GOD’s
own substance and devoid of freedom, God would unquestionably be the author of
sin, notwithstanding this answer. See Diego Mas, the Council of Braga under
Honorius, and Leo in his epistle. The Manichaeans taught that sins flowed from
natural necessity because human beings had been created from an evil substance
by an evil god.-*

[Marginal reference: Magdeburg *Centuries*, century 4, chapter 11.]

[Marginal reference: Augustine, *Heresies* 70, book 8.]

[Marginal reference: Sulpitius Severus, *Sacred History*, book 2.]

[Marginal reference: Origen, *On Heresies*, chapter 70.]

[Marginal reference: Diego Mas, on Aristotle’s *Physics*, book 2, last chapter,
question 4.]

[Marginal reference: Council of Braga.]

[Marginal reference: Leo, epistle 3.]

*See Augustine, Eusebius, and the First Council of Braga. 2. Such a response
would not suit such heretics.

Ruiz: *You ask him,* says Augustine, *what fate is*—under whose impulse they
blaspheme God—*and he says, “The evil stars.” You ask him who made and ordered
the stars. He has no answer to give you except GOD. It remains, therefore, that
whether by a long opening or the nearest one, he accuses GOD.* You do not
satisfy the argument, Ruiz says, *by saying that the influences of heaven
necessitate only material actions, not malice, and that they are not efficient
but deficient causes, and indeed free from laws.* Elsewhere he says: *It was
not inferred from the error of the Manichaeans and Priscillianists that GOD was
the moral cause of sin, but only its physical cause, since according to them
GOD acted from necessity.*

[Marginal reference: Augustine, *Against the Manichaeans*, book 1; and *City
of God*, book 5, chapter 5.]

[Marginal reference: *On Heresies*, chapter 46.]

[Marginal reference: Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book 6.]

[Marginal reference: First Council of Braga, canons 9 and 10.]

[Marginal reference: Augustine, on Psalm 81.]

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, *On the Will of GOD*, disputation 38, section 2.]

[Marginal reference: And *On the Will of GOD*, disputation 26, section 4, no.

I reply: 1. Such a response would indeed not satisfy, because the stars would
act upon the will without any counsel or reason, by merely natural influence,
and could not temper their influences and motions in accordance with the
character and nature of freedom as God does by his predetermination. The stars
would therefore overthrow freedom in this way, and sins would thus be from the
stars as second causes and from God as first cause. 2. *If the stars
necessitated material acts but not malice*, while freedom remained unimpaired
and entire, one could answer very well that fate or the stars were not causes
of sin, even though they necessitated the material act of malice. The stars’
exemption from law in this case would absolve them of guilt. 3. Remove freedom,
as happens if the stars and fate rule the free will, and sin will not find even
a moral cause in the will. Its cause must therefore be in GOD, in the stars, or
in both. 4. It is false that the defenders of fate did not make God the moral
cause of sin, for sinners in Homer say, as Ammonius reports: *I am not the
cause, but Jupiter and fate.* The Council of Braga says: *If anyone believes
that human souls and bodies are bound by a fatal sign, as the pagans and
Priscillian said, let him be anathema.* See Alfonso de Castro. *The
Priscillianists,* says Gregory, *say that every person is born under the
constellations of the stars.* Fate is opposed to GOD’s will because fate does
not move contingent causes in accordance with their natures as God moves them.
Jerome therefore says: *The day of death has by no means been fixed for us by
fate, but by GOD’s will.* In short, this same objection was brought against
Bradwardine in his age by defenders of Pelagian grace, just as it was in the
age of-*

[Marginal reference: Ammonius, on book 1 of *On Interpretation*, last chapter.]

[Marginal reference: First Council of Braga, canon 9.]

[Marginal reference: Alfonso de Castro, *Against Heresies*, book 7, “Fate.”]

[Marginal reference: Gregory, homily 10, *On Epiphany*.]

[Marginal reference: Jerome, book 11, on Isaiah 38.]

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, book 1, chapter 28.]

[Marginal reference: Augustine, *City of God*, book 5, chapter 9.]

[Chapter XXVI, Part B; printed page 398 (misprinted “396”); IA PDF page 457;
BSB canvas 450]

*Augustine also. Thus Bradwardine, drawing from Augustine, says: *Fate is a
certain sidereal force imposing necessity according to the hour of birth.* The
hour of birth is imagined to have a connection of natural influence with the
moral disposition of the will. This is impious.

8. Ruiz: *If GOD,* he says, *wills concerning sinners’ actions only a
concurrence which, as far as depends upon itself, inclines toward good and
draws back from evil, while permitting it to be diverted to evil by the will’s
free action if the will so chooses, it does not follow that GOD is the moral
cause of such viciousness. For GOD’s direct and primary intention, as far as
depended on him, willed that such viciousness never exist in the nature of
things; but, as though compelled by the office of the universal cause, he
permits his concurrence. But if GOD, determined by himself and not by the
human being’s free will, wills and physically predetermines the material act of
hatred of GOD and the whole real opposition of that act to GOD’s Law, he will
be the primary cause in itself and will directly determine that the material
sinful action exist in the nature of things.*

I reply: 1. The Jesuit thus proves that God is not the moral cause of sin,
which is not the question; we reject that as blasphemy. Yet they dare not
remove every physical causality in the material act of sin from God, and the
present question is whether such causality implicates GOD in participation in
guilt. 2. If someone neither determines nor counsels another to commit murder,
he is not on that account the cause of murder. But if, solicited by entreaties
and constrained by the coercive force of his office, he is determined by the
author of the murder to supply strength, causal assistance, and collateral
concurrence, then in this case he truly is a joint cause of the murder—unless
the office by whose force he is compelled is above the law and absolves him
from guilt. Thus the Jesuits splendidly defend GOD’s holiness: they teach that
God is a joint cause of sin and of all crimes, and that he does this in a
servile manner, not by primary intention, but compelled by the office of the
universal cause and determined by the influence of the second cause. But this
office of the first cause, by whose force God is slavishly compelled to supply
causality to wickedness, either releases GOD from a binding law—in which case
it comes over to our opinion—or is an office contrary to the law of the holy
divine nature. In that case God acted unjustly by binding himself through his
eternal decree to supply this free concurrence, which is utterly absurd. But
if attributing concurrent causality to an act-*

[Marginal note: The Jesuits all the more blasphemously involve God in the guilt
of sin because they imagine the Holy One of Israel compelled by the force of
an office and by the function of the universal cause.]

[Chapter XXVI, Part B; printed page 399 (misprinted “397”); IA PDF page 458;
BSB canvas 451]

*involves GOD in participation in wickedness, it makes no difference whether
God supplies that causality by predetermining and precausing, moved only by his
free will, or by co-determining and jointly causing, determined by the created
will. There is no force in these innocent prepositions *pre-* and *con-* to
stir up such tragic disputes. The person who, moved only by the determination
of another’s will, contributes assistance to a killing is no less guilty of
murder than one who contributes it determined only by his own free will. There
is a degree in the guilt, but in each case there is guilt and a true joint
causation of murder. The Jesuits and Arminians implicate GOD in wickedness
twice—indeed, three times. 1. They teach that God supplies a collateral joint
causality by which an action contrary to GOD’s law exists. 2. They hold that
God is moved and determined, contrary to his own holy intention, by the impious
and most wicked will of the sinner to supply such assistance to wickedness. 3.
They hold it to be GOD’s function and office as universal cause—although every
impious function must necessarily be contrary to his holy nature—to contribute
assistance to wickedness. But I turn back against Ruiz what he alleged above.
The Manichaeans and Priscillianists said that an evil GOD, fate, and the stars
are causes of sin. I do not think their argument would have been answered by
saying that *an evil GOD, fate, and the stars are causes of sin, but not of
themselves or by their own primary intention; rather, as though by the force of
their office and function, they are determined by the impious will of human
beings to cause sin.* How much more rightly and soundly should we say that
human beings transgress the law because the law obliges them to obedience,
while God is free from wickedness and most holy and innocent because, superior
to every law he has given creatures, he predetermines wills by the dominion of
providence toward the acts, not toward the acts’ malice. Bradwardine says:
*That being is not worthily regarded as omnipotent who in doing anything
necessarily needs the assistance of a prior agent which necessarily and
naturally precedes him in acting. Nor can anyone imagine that this is by no
means contained under the Omnipotent, for it*—namely the precausation of the
second cause by which it determines GOD, the first cause, to act—*is a truly
possible being. Indeed, in that case the human being would be more powerful
than GOD. For although GOD willed that the human being freely will, and did
and would do whatever he wills and could for this end to the utmost, the human
being could freely resist him and refuse to will.* 3. Although *GOD, determined
by himself, wills and physically predetermines the material act of hatred, it
does not follow that GOD causes-*

[Marginal note: 1. Primarily. 2. And, contrary to his own holy intention and

supplying collateral joint causality toward sin—from which blasphemy we recoil

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, book 2, chapter 9.]

*the whole real opposition of that act to GOD’s law,* because the malice is not
from GOD’s determination, but wholly from the free action of the creature
subject to law. 4. If God, contrary to his intention, supplies a concurrence
which of itself inclines toward good and is diverted to evil by the free will,
then God will be the accidental cause of all the material acts of sin. This is
absurd. For: (1) the first cause is not the accidental cause of a positive
effect, since then some other God prior to that God would be the effect’s cause
in itself, for every accidental effect is reduced to some cause in itself; (2)
many positive effects—namely all the material acts of sin—would in this way be
outside God’s intention and therefore fortuitous, which is utterly absurd.

Ruiz: *Whoever is the primary cause which first determines that the material
act of hatred of God and its whole real opposition to the divine law should
exist in the nature of things was by that very fact the first cause determining
that malice, necessarily and inseparably annexed to the act, should exist in
the nature of things. The reason is that the created will is the moral cause of
malice only insofar as it is the cause of the material act to which malice is
necessarily annexed. And,* says Arriaga, *there is no action in itself toward
a privation, nor does it occur otherwise than by producing the physical entity
which the privation follows. The material is nothing but the entity of the
free action insofar as it is exercised about such an object in these
circumstances. Therefore one who loves the material loves the formal.* Suárez
says that *the effect and all its accidents in themselves depend on the same
cause.* And malice, Raynaud says, *is loved only insofar as its foundation is
loved.* Therefore, if God determines toward the act and loves the act, by that
very fact he will determine toward the malice annexed to it and will love it.

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, book 1, *On the Will of God*, disputation 27,
section 2.]

[Marginal reference: Arriaga, philosophical course, disputation 10, section 1,
subject 4, § 36.]

[Marginal reference: Suárez, book 2, *On Concurrence*, chapter 2, § 12.]

[Marginal reference: Theophylact.]

[Marginal reference: Raynaudus, *Natural Theology*, disputation 4, question 3,

[Marginal note: God is the physical cause of a moral act. But it does not

I reply: 1. The assertion that *whoever is the cause of the material act is the
cause of malice* is wholly denied. The proof is false. For it is false that
*the created will is the moral cause of malice only insofar as it is the cause
of the material act to which malice is necessarily annexed.* The created will
as a physical agent is the physical cause of the act. If we suppose it not to
be subject to law, it would never be the moral cause, but only the physical
cause. It is, however, the moral cause of malice not simply because it produces
the act, but because it produces the act while it is bound by the obligation
of law to superadd to the act an exact conformity with GOD’s law. Thus the
reason why malice is causally imputed to the human will is not simply that it
produces the act as a physical agent,-*

[Chapter XXVI, Part B; printed page 401; IA PDF page 459; BSB canvas 453]

*but that it is a human being subject to law, produces a forbidden act, and is
a moral agent. For the account of physical and moral causality is not the same.
2. It is false that malice is an accident belonging to the act in itself. And
Arriaga’s assertion—that *a human being formally causes a privation because he
produces the physical entity*—is utterly false regarding moral privation. Adam
could have produced the numerically same act of eating the fruit of the tree
of knowledge which he did produce, and produced it lawfully, on the hypothesis
that God had commanded him by his law to eat that fruit—which God could have
done if it had so pleased him. Indeed, the assertion is not true even of
physical privation. For nature’s producing a human being is not its producing
a lame and blind human being: nature as nature produces the human being, while
injured and imperfect nature produces him lame and blind. 4. It is also false
that *there is no action in itself toward a moral privation*, as is the claim
that *malice is loved only insofar as its foundation is loved*, and that *one
who loves the material loves the formal.* All these assertions are false. The
first is false because, though the sinner does not intend sin as dishonorable
evil, he nevertheless intends it under another aspect—as a pleasant and useful
good. That suffices for the person to be said to intend malice itself and not
the bare material act; hence the sinner chooses the deformity of sin rather
than to please God. See Thomas and Alvarez’s argument. 2. A material act can be
loved with a physical complacency but not a moral one, when the malice is not
loved except by a moral complacency. And God loves the material act with the
physical complacency proper to the first cause, while, with respect to the
moral complacency proper to the Lawgiver, he morally hates the malice annexed
to it. 5. This argument presses our opponents. *If the created will is the
cause of malice because it is the cause of the act to which malice is
inseparably annexed,* then for the same reason God too will be the cause of
malice, because he is the first cause of the material act of sin. For God
effects the act by true efficiency and causal concurrence, though that
concurrence is determined by the deviant will. Therefore he effects the malice
too; and while he produces the entity, he produces the privation—namely the
malice. And because the first cause cannot fail to love the material element
of sin—for it is a positive being and GOD’s work, and God cannot fail to love
everything he effects—therefore, according to our opponents, he will love the
malice. But the conclusion is absurd. 6. These teachings are unknown to the
sounder Schoolmen. Aquinas-*

[Marginal reference: Aquinas, *On Evil*, question 3, article 2, reply 1.]

[Marginal reference: Alvarez, *Responses*, last book, chapter 11, no. 5.]

[Marginal note: The sounder Schoolmen teach that God is the cause of the
material act, but not of the malice annexed to it.]

*says: *In an action joined to deformity, GOD does what belongs to the action
but does not do what belongs to the deformity. For although several things in
an effect are inseparably joined, it is not necessary that whatever is its
cause with respect to one be also its cause with respect to the other.* The
acute Cajetan says: *It follows, third, that concurring toward such an act
according to what it has from the agent as agent is not the same as concurring
according to what it has from the agent as deficient, just as being an agent

10. Fasolus urges as follows: *When two causes ought to concur toward some
effect, the effect’s absence is caused only by the cause which does not supply
what it can supply and what is necessary for causing that effect, and not by
the other cause which, as far as depends on it, supplies everything it has in
itself and can supply, and necessarily can supply, so that the effect may be
caused. Thus the absence of the fire’s heating the three young men in the
Babylonian furnace was caused by GOD, not by the fire; for the fire was not the
cause of the absence of heating, but GOD, who did not supply the necessary
concurrence which he could have supplied. But when someone sins, predetermined
by the divine decree toward the act of sin, he sins—or does not cause rectitude
in the act—solely and only because GOD freely refused to make the contrary
decree to give predetermining concurrence toward the good act. Therefore, if
GOD predetermines toward the act of sin, the person sins-*

3, no. 380.]

[Chapter XXVI, Part B; printed page 403; IA PDF page 461; BSB canvas 455]

*because of a defect of his own proximate power and the prerequired complement,
namely the lack of concurrence toward the contrary good act. Hence on this
account the creature never sins, but GOD alone does. For sinning is not simply
performing an act—if we perform the same act while asleep or insane, we do not
sin—but performing evil when we can and are bound to perform a right act. Yet
it implies a contradiction to perform a good act if we are predetermined
toward an evil act. Therefore the failure to perform the right and good act is
from God alone, who denies predetermining concurrence toward the good, which he
could supply, and not from the created will, which from the defect and absence
of divine predetermining concurrence can no more perform the good than the
Babylonian fire could burn when GOD denied his concurrence necessarily
required for the act of burning.* I reply: 1. If we suppose those two causes
to be physical causes, not moral causes, of the effect—as God and the fire are
with respect to heating—then I grant what the Jesuit says. But if one cause is
physical and above law while the other is moral, free, and subject to law,
then certainly the absence of the moral effect—that is, the non-performance of
the right act—is not imputed to the physical cause, namely God who does not
concur, because God concurs under no debt of obligation and his concurrence is
not subject to law. Rather, it is imputed to the moral cause, namely the
created will subject to law, although the good act is impossible without
predetermining concurrence toward it. For the created will determines itself
toward the evil act with as much freedom and as great an inward elective
inclination as if GOD’s predetermination toward the good act were immediately
ready and at hand. Hence the evil act is justly imputed to the will which lacks
GOD’s predetermination toward the good act as to its moral cause, since it
freely and with full choice lacks that predetermination. 2. It is therefore
false that *the will contributes everything it can toward the good act, and
that the good act is omitted solely because of the absence of GOD’s
predetermination toward it.* The good act is omitted not only because GOD’s
predetermination toward it is absent, but also because the will itself,
implicitly and as to the effect, wills to lack that predetermination and with
inward choice elicits the evil act. 3. A good act is called impossible from
the absence of predetermination toward it only in a highly improper sense. For
it is impossible in the same way that, when a free act exists, it is impossible
for it not to exist. As I taught before, there are no more degrees-*

free non-act, or sinful omission.]

[Chapter XXVI, Part B; printed page 404; IA PDF page 462; BSB canvas 456]

*of necessity in GOD’s predetermination than in the predetermined free act on
the hypothesis that the free act now exists. For GOD’s predetermining decree
and GOD’s temporal predetermination neither thrust nor impel the will toward
any other direction of freedom and elective power than the very direction
toward which the will itself most freely thrusts and impels itself. Nor does
GOD’s predetermination bend the free will more strongly toward A and not B than
the will itself freely bends itself toward the same A and not B. Here divine
fate does not drag; it guides one who wills and is free. Suppose a stone moving
from the sphere of the moon toward the center, the nature of a heavy body
thrusting it downward with force and speed of degree 6; and suppose another
external natural agent thrusting the same stone with the same force and speed
of degree 6. No one, I think, will say that the external agent thrusts the
stone toward the center with greater necessitation than the stone itself is
thrust by its internal nature toward the center. God predetermines the will
with necessitation of degree 6, and the will itself predetermines itself toward
the same act with necessitation also of degree 6. Thus the impossibilitation
with respect to another act—say, the good one—when the will performs the evil
act, will be similar to the necessitation. Yet the necessitation is improper
and agrees very well, even in equal numerical degrees—namely 6—with the
necessitation arising from the free will itself. Consequently, the Jesuit’s
statement—*it implies a contradiction to perform a good act if we are
predetermined toward an evil act*—is equivalent to this: *it implies a
contradiction not to perform evil, on the hypothesis that we perform an evil
act.* But this impossibilitation is plainly improper and hypothetical. Its
reason and cause are that because God is the first cause, infinite both in
power and wisdom, he alone is able and he alone knows how to excite and move
the will in a manner congruent with the nature and character of created
freedom, according to the same degrees of necessity—neither more nor fewer—by
which the free will itself moves and determines on the hypothesis that it is
now moving itself. 3. Nothing is more profane than this argument of the Jesuit.
For he holds that from eternity GOD decreed for the created will concurrence
toward the good act as much as toward the evil: toward either Peter’s
confession of the Savior or his denial. But the futurition of the confession,
when he denied the Savior, is plainly a non-being both in itself and in GOD’s
eternal decree. Did God, then, decree all possible things and determine from
eternity that non-beings as well as beings should come to be? Ah, foul
blasphemy!

11. Ruiz: *The office of the first cause does not require predetermination,
because GOD abundantly satisfies his office when he governs and moves human
beings who never elicit the material act of hatred of GOD. Furthermore, if
this predetermination is posited, it follows that no human beings have greater
frailty than others except that which GOD by his absolute freedom wills to
confer when and where he wills; that frailty does no harm if GOD predetermines
toward good acts; that the powers and strength of grace do no good if GOD does
not predetermine; that it is no punishment for someone to be deserted; and
that it is no benefit for another to be assisted by grace.*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, vol. *On the Will of God*, disputation 29, section
1.]

I reply: The Jesuits teach that the glory of justice and mercy shines in sins.
Therefore, although some government of GOD would shine without sin, the
glorious providence which now obtains would have been obscure unless this
unimpaired dominion over the acts of good and evil angels and of human beings
had belonged to God. 2. It is strange that the Jesuits dislike God’s disposing
of the frailty of his creature according to his absolute freedom. Woe to the
work that quarrels with the potter! 3. As far as depends upon the weakest will
equipped with predetermination of the natural order—which is its powerless
frailty—it cannot elicit supernatural acts unless the habit of grace and
special assistance toward the acts are added. Therefore one human being is
frailer than another. 4. Those with a smaller habit of grace are frailer than
others. Who will deny that the cedars of GOD—Noah, Lot, David, and Peter,
otherwise men most richly equipped with a habit of grace—grew weary and fell
under slight temptations when God withdrew predetermining and sustaining
grace, while weaker people—the thief on the cross, and women too—did not even
stumble under heavier enticements and tortures? This was so that human beings
might learn to boast neither in themselves nor even in created grace, but in
the Lord alone through Jesus Christ. May it not be said, *You stand by faith;
do not be proud, but fear?* And Paul says, *Therefore I will most gladly rather
boast in my weaknesses.* 5. Frailties do harm, because God ordinarily yields
to them so that saints fall. They are also sins and render people prone to
sinning, even though they do not break out into evil acts while GOD sustains
his people. 6. Whoever is predetermined by God toward good acts is not deserted
to that extent. 7. Let those theologians be confounded who hold that it lies
within free choice to actualize culpable frailty whenever and wherever human
beings will—that is, to stand or fall—as though GOD’s grace were superfluous.
For this is what all anti-predeterminists commonly teach.

12. Ruiz: *It follows that the office of the first cause is to rush upon a weak
human being to determine him toward a fall and to complete the temptation of
the Devil and the flesh. GOD ought,* says Suárez, *to determine the frail will
prone to evil toward good rather than evil. Add that whenever GOD does not
determine toward an evil act a person who is prone to it, he acts miraculously
by denying to the will placed in such a situation the general concurrence due
to it by virtue of the office of the first cause. But if predetermination is
not due to such a prone will by virtue of the office of the first cause, while
GOD according to his own choice effects this volition rather than that, then
the supremely good GOD will choose to destroy rather than preserve the
creature, though there is no fault in the creature. 2. GOD, in determining the
creature toward evil, would hate it. 3. In evil acts GOD would distinguish the
sinner from the non-sinner, and it would not be of one who wills or runs, but
of GOD who pursues in hatred. 4. Our destruction would be from GOD.*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, at the same place.]

I reply: 1. The office of the first cause is the Creator’s free decree by which
he determines to predetermine toward an act while the human being himself
determines himself toward the act’s malice. Such a predetermination is neither
a temptation nor the completion of temptation, unless someone says that a
physical act is formally the Devil’s temptation; but that would call GOD’s
work the Devil’s work. 2. By this argument, God’s concurring by an indifferent
causal concurrence toward the act of sin, as our opponents teach, would
complete the Devil’s temptation. But everything is safe, they say: *GOD
completes the Devil’s temptation by indifferent concurrence, compelled by the
force of the office of the universal cause.* Bradwardine refuted this long ago
in the Pelagians: *I refuse to have him for my GOD whom I, a sinner, drive
necessarily to will or even permit evil, whom I, a wretched sinner,
necessitate in turn just as my malice pleases.* 3. When Suárez makes the
creature’s frailty and GOD’s goodness causes why God would refuse to
predetermine acts of sin, he plainly reasons along with those whom Tertullian
once branded dogs—namely the Marcionites. *If God,* they say, *is good, knows
the future beforehand, and is able to avert evil, why did he allow the human
being, and indeed his image, to fall away from obedience to the law into death,
ensnared by the Devil? For if he were good, so as not to will such a thing to
happen; foreknowing, so as not to be ignorant that it would happen; and
powerful, so as to be able to repel it, then in no way would that have happened

Devil and reprobate human beings-*

I reply: 1. No action of a creature is called possible or impossible in
relation to the concurrence of the first cause, as though the creature had the
first cause’s concurrence and action in its own power, the free will
necessitated God to concur, and GOD’s absolute freedom in showing mercy and
hardening were wholly removed. Rather, an action of a second cause is called
possible or impossible in relation to the capacity and power implanted in that
second cause, or otherwise inhering in it. Nor does it follow that it is
impossible for a person to kill himself or his children simply from God’s free
denial of predetermination toward the act of killing. For whenever God denies
his predetermination, he denies it to a human being freely willing to lack it;
and whenever he supplies his predetermination, he supplies it to a human being
freely willing to have it. Indeed, God according to his own choice so adapts
his predetermination to the character and natural disposition of freedom to
act or not to act that it could not fail most sweetly to conspire toward that
act, numerically one and the same, which the-*

*free will embraces with full freedom and elective judgment. Therefore, when
the Jesuit says that it is impossible for the will to determine itself toward
an act other than the one toward which it is predetermined by God, he means an
impossibility arising from the internal nature of freedom itself. Yet this
impossibility, so to speak, is sweet, free, and elective. It is the
impossibility by which the will cannot determine itself toward an act other
than the one toward which it in fact freely determines itself. For the sake of
explaining the matter, let us suppose that in the free will there are three
numerically distinct springs from which flow three streams, or three acts: not
willing, willing, and suspending the act. Let us suppose that, according to its
internal freedom, the will can draw whichever stream it chooses from the first,
second, or third spring, but cannot draw from all three springs at once so as
copulatively to will, not will, and suspend the act. By the nature of the
matter it will necessarily elicit just one disjunctively. Now God, by his
predetermination, draws τὸ nolle, “the not-willing,” from the first spring by
exciting and moving it; and in his free decree he foreknows that the free will
will move that spring, and not the second or third, by its own free inclination.
Who would say that God here makes created freedom unable to choose to excite
and move the second or third spring in any other way than freedom itself makes
itself unable to choose to excite the second and third? But you will say: in
this way the free will cannot excite the second and third springs because God,
prior in nature, moved only the first by his predetermination; he therefore
takes away the will’s power to excite either the second or third and overthrows
freedom. I reply that the consequence would be good if God, by his infinite
wisdom, did not have all the inclinations of freedom toward exciting these
three springs thoroughly known. But since in his decree he foreknows that the
actual agitations of the second and third springs are wholly null and will be
non-beings forever, and that the will will move only the first spring, God
takes away the power of drawing streams from the second and third springs no
otherwise than the free will itself takes that power away. Yet the will takes
it away freely and therefore very improperly. Thus God in no proper sense
takes it away. But the will is properly rendered unable when it cannot excite
the spring toward which it would in fact be inclined in second act from its
own inward inclination unless it had been hindered by another and stronger
agent, and when it is bent by a stronger force toward a spring to which-*

*it would never actually have inclined if left to itself. But surely it is not
hindered or bent by GOD’s predetermination in this way. And although, because
of our intellect’s weakness—which we ought to confess to GOD’s glory is
certainly very great in so profound a mystery—we candidly acknowledge that we
are wholly unable to explain the manner in which divine predetermination and
the free determination of the created will conspire toward one and the same
act, it must nevertheless not be tolerated that the created will be made queen
and absolute mistress of divine concurrences and of all free acts. That
doctrine enervates our prayers, utterly overturns confidence in prayer, wholly
destroys our consolation and patience under so many wicked acts committed
against us by created wills, roots out religious fear and hope in GOD,
wretchedly ruins and empties GOD’s omnipotent dominion over the free acts of
angels and human beings, commits everything to the dominion of fortune,
confines GOD’s freedom in the fetters of fatal necessity, makes special
Providence the attendant of the created will, and reduces all necessity of
grace to nothing and sacrilegiously erases it. 2. Unless God determines a
human being toward the act of hanging himself with a noose, let the Jesuit see
how he would avoid having the saints give thanks to God that by GOD’s grace
they are indeflectibly freed from the tragic and mournful end of Judas the
traitor. 3. By no logic does it follow from positing GOD’s predetermination
that a human being has no inclination toward committing crimes whose material
acts are not predetermined by God. But from denying this predetermination it
follows excellently that thanks for avoiding every crime toward which, alas,
we are all too prone must be given not to GOD but to bare free choice,
determining itself alone.
