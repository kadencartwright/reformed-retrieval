Other passages may be added to those already given. For example, there is
2 Corinthians 5:1: “For we know that if the tent of this earthly house of
ours is dissolved, we will have a building from God, a dwelling not made
with hands, eternal in the heavens.” There is also Philippians 1:23: “I
desire to depart and to be with Christ.” In these passages we find that as
soon as the godly depart from here and the tent of the body is dissolved,
which happens at death, they are immediately with Christ and find a
building prepared for them in heaven. But whoever is with Christ is in
heaven, and whoever is in heaven is free from every stain of sin, for
“nothing that defiles enters it,” Revelation 21:27. Here we know in part and
prophesy in part, but when that which is perfect has come, that which is in
part will be done away. If no imperfections in what is good then adhere to
those blessed souls, least of all will the remnants of sin adhere to them.

Moreover, let the following serve as a formal argument. Holy Scripture
presents us with two things concerning this subject. First, everyone, even
the best, is defiled with sin while living here, for “there is no one who
does not sin,” 1 Kings 8:46. Indeed, “there is no righteous person on earth
who does good and does not sin,” Ecclesiastes 7:20. “If we say that we have
no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us,” 1 John 1:8;
indeed, we make Christ a liar, and his word is not in us, verse 10. Many
similar passages occur elsewhere. While we inhabit

[1679 printed p. 279 | IA leaf 287]

these houses of clay, some dust that defiles will be present in them. In
fact, when we cleanse them by repentance, some dust of this kind will be
stirred up. This, then, is the first point: as long as we live here, at
least some remnants of sin will remain.

The second point, established from the passages previously adduced, is that
we are in heaven, are with Christ, and are perfect as soon as we have put
off this body of death. From these two points taken together a third arises:
through the soul's separation from the body at death, sin is at the same
time totally and finally separated from godly souls. Methodius beautifully
illustrated this by his previously cited likeness of a tree growing within
a building.

To this may also be added a further consideration of the body, or of this
mass of flesh. I am far from saying that the whole disease of the mind
arises through the body's corrupting contagion. On the contrary, I freely
acknowledge that the soul itself is, with respect to the habit of every sin,
the first receptive subject and the subject most properly so called. This
is especially true of certain sins commonly called spiritual, which have a
smaller admixture of brutish dregs. Thus a separated soul can remain
capable of sin, which cannot be said of a dead body outside its state of
union with the soul. Nevertheless, as the saying goes, the body weighs down
the soul so that it is less able to fly to heaven; indeed, it entices the
soul toward brutish and carnal things so that it inclines and sinks
downward. Especially in the operations of sin, it rubs a great deal of
defilement onto the soul. Hence in Holy Scripture the whole corruption of
sin is frequently called “flesh,” because it is brought into action through
the service of body and flesh. Therefore, at death the soul is relieved of
so great a burden and freed from so many enticements. This by itself does
not suffice for the total and perfect cleansing from sin of a soul now
separated, for the souls of the wicked remain wicked in that separated
state. Yet for the blessed souls of the saints, this completed separation
from bodies that are both corruptible and corrupting contributes greatly to
their complete purification, until on the last day they are refashioned
into spiritual bodies possessing neither a weight that presses them down
nor any carnal enticement.
