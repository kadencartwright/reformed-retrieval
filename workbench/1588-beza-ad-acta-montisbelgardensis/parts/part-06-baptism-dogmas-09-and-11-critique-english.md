## Dogma IX

*The effect of the covenant depends neither upon baptism nor upon any adjuncts.*

### Reply to Dogma IX

*A reply to this was given above, at Theses 1 and 2.*

## Dogma XI

*In a case of extreme necessity it is unlawful for women to baptize infants.*

Dr. Jakob Andreae
Dr. Lucas Osiander

### Reply to Dogma XI

Baptism is a part of the public ministry, which by God’s express Word is forbidden to women, and indeed even to private persons. Nor can someone who, through no fault of his own, departs this life unbaptized be regarded as having despised baptism; nor can any necessity arise for transgressing the laws of the established public ministry.

1. *Women are not permitted to speak in the CHURCH, that is, to preach in an assembly of men, 1 Corinthians 14. But where no man is present, they may both preach and, in a case of necessity, baptize, because in Christ there is neither male nor female, but we are all ONE in Christ Jesus, Galatians 3.*

2. *No law exists in the Word of the Lord which, in a case of necessity, restrains women either from teaching or from baptizing.*

### Reply to Both of Dr. Andreae’s Marginal Observations on the Antithesis to Dogma XI

Here a twofold question confronts us: one concerning the necessity of baptism, the other concerning *baptism by women*, as Andreae entitles it in Greek on page 499. The latter depends upon the former, so that one who knows the former knows both. Let us therefore deal first with the former. First of all, however, we must see from the colloquy itself what our adversary’s position is, and how admirably consistent he is with himself in this matter too.

On page 458 he says: *Before infants are baptized, we cannot affirm that they believe, because faith is given to them in baptism. Therefore in receiving baptism infants are simultaneously adopted as children of God, and adoption is sealed in them.*

So he says. If these things are true, he should have said openly, rather than doubtfully and in the manner of the Academics, that infants do not believe before baptism. But if faith, adoption, and the sealing of adoption—without which no one can obtain eternal salvation—begin at baptism, it follows that baptism is so simply and absolutely necessary that whoever dies unbaptized is excluded from salvation. Otherwise it must be held that someone can be saved in Christ without faith—which they nevertheless insist must necessarily, and without exception, belong personally to each individual—and without adoption.

On page 492: *Although the children of Christians are born in the church, they are not on that account counted in the covenant unless they have also done, or unless there are also done in them, the things the covenant requires. Carnal birth from godly parents contributes nothing toward attaining the kingdom of heaven, as it is written: “Do not presume to say among yourselves, ‘We have Abraham as our father,’” and, “Whatever is born of flesh is flesh.” Therefore unless children born of godly and Christian parents are born anew of water and the Spirit—which happens in baptism—they cannot see God’s kingdom. Likewise in the Old Testament, children born of godly parents were not contained in the covenant unless the flesh of their foreskin was cut off on the eighth day. For Moses says, “The male whose foreskin has not been circumcised: that soul shall be destroyed from his people, because he has made my covenant void,” Genesis 17.* Therefore, he says on page 493, *not by a probable argument but by a most certain and indubitable consequence, in the New Testament infants born even of godly and Christian parents are not counted in the covenant unless they have also been baptized, according to Christ’s saying, “Unless one is born again,” etc.* Again, these are his words, from—

[Part II | PDF page 129 | logical source scan 128 | printed page 127]

—which it appears still more openly that Andreae establishes here the most absolute and simple necessity of baptism, following the erring Augustine—if I may say it with peace toward so great a man—right off the road. I will show later how poorly he supports that inference. For now it will suffice to set against him Dr. Herbrand, a doctor of the Tübingen church and his colleague, assuredly more learned in very many matters. On page 636 of his *Compendium of Theology* he rightly writes these very words: *As for Christ’s saying, “Unless one is born again,” etc.—a passage Augustine adduces in confirmation of his opinion—the Savior speaks of the ordinary mode and the order he himself instituted, which he does not wish to be neglected or despised wherever it can be had; he speaks by no means of a case of necessity, which has no law. As for Augustine’s authority, although he rightly ought to be held in high esteem by us, it must not be esteemed more highly than he himself wished it to be, nor so highly that for his sake we subscribe to an error.* Thus far we have heard Andreae wholly Augustinian; now let us hear him become a little milder.

When on page 495 I had denied that, according to God’s Word, infants uncircumcised in Moses’ time and dying in the wilderness must be reckoned damned, he replied: *As concerns uncircumcised infants, judgment must be made not from human reason but from the Lord’s Word. The Lord’s Word reads thus: “The male whose foreskin,” etc., Genesis 17. They therefore sinned grievously because, against so express a command of God, they did not see to the circumcision of their infants on the eighth day. Yet,* he says on page 496, *because of frequent and unexpected journeys, they can be excused to some extent from negligence and contempt of the sacrament. And it is not only probable but without doubt also a fact that Moses and Aaron reminded the Israelites of their duty in this matter too, but that people in their characteristic manner did not obey them.* Again, on page 497: *When an infant died before the eighth day appointed for circumcision, parents and children were excused before God. What he willed to decree concerning those infants was left to God’s will, concerning which we judge from the Word. Since the infant himself had not made the Lord’s covenant void, we judge that uncircumcision was not imputed to him.* So he says. From this his position is gathered: those infants who died before the eighth day were not included under the law enacted in Genesis 17 against those who die uncircumcised. Others in the wilderness were uncircumcised—and this through the fault of those who did not obey Moses and Aaron—but could nevertheless be excused by God’s peculiar grace, because they remained uncircumcised not through contempt of circumcision but because, when camp was frequently moved hastily and unexpectedly, the children would have seemed to be brought into danger of life. God, pitying them for this reason, preserved them to be circumcised later under Joshua. But if any of them meanwhile died uncircumcised after the eighth day, they cannot, according to God’s Word, be judged otherwise than to have perished outside the covenant; yet they seem to have been very—

[Part II | PDF page 130 | logical source scan 129 | printed page 128]

—few, since they grew to such a great and astonishing number in the wilderness. I will weigh Andreae’s position in its proper place. Meanwhile I once more set against him Herbrand, who on page 635 of his *Compendium* writes much more correctly in these very words: *We also know this: ceremonies and sacraments admit of dispensation, as they were not circumcised for forty years in the wilderness. For if this had displeased God, or if he had judged his covenant thus made void by neglected circumcision, he would not have tolerated it. He who immediately punished sins committed by the people in the wilderness would not have neglected this, had it been a sin.* Andreae, who spoke so confidently of Moses and Aaron reminding the people of their duty and the parents of the children being utterly disobedient, may therefore consider how he will come to terms with his colleague—or rather with truth itself. Let us now hear what he thinks about the baptized, consistently with what he said about the uncircumcised.

On page 498: *Since God has prescribed no certain time for baptism, and Christ most gravely rebuked his disciples when they forbade children to be brought to him, all parents must devote the greatest care to seeing that, with no long delay intervening, newborn infants are received into the covenant through baptism, according to Christ’s saying, “Unless one is born again of water,” etc. This benefit is no less necessary for infants than for adults, since they are conceived and born in sins and are by nature children of wrath; and unless they are received into grace for Christ’s sake, no hope of obtaining eternal life is left to them in carnal birth.* What he says is indeed true, but he runs outside the course, since it was not even asked whether baptism ought to be urged, much less was it disputed between us. Immediately returning to himself, he continues: *Therefore we judge baptism so necessary for infants that we hold unavoidable necessity alone to excuse; but we decree that negligence and contempt merit no pardon, for we bind salvation as the words read: “He who believes and is baptized will be saved”; likewise, “Unless one has been born again of water,” etc.*

I choose to examine these things separately. Although that blemish of Augustine, noticed even by the Scholastics, now needs no refutation in the Christian church, nevertheless Andreae tries to support it with some arguments of his own. Moreover, we have fallen upon times in which no heresy is so well known and condemned that some men will not most greedily swallow it when Satan has doctored and offered it. It is therefore worthwhile to refute these things too, once for all. The question is whether baptism is simply and absolutely necessary for salvation. I deny this with all the orthodox; Andreae affirms it—not indeed using those words, but beginning faith and adoption in infants, and consequently infants’ right to salvation, from baptism. If this applies to infants, it must much more be established for adults, who can and should be excused less readily.

Thus, if one connects pages 458 and 492, he argues from two—

[Part II | PDF page 131 | logical source scan 130 | printed page 129]

—presuppositions. One is that Christ testifies that the kingdom of heaven belongs to infants; the other, that it must consequently be held that they believe, since without faith no one can please God. With these established he relies upon this dilemma: infants either received that faith before baptism or receive it in baptism itself. But according to God’s Word infants cannot be said to believe before baptism; indeed carnal birth contributes nothing toward attaining the kingdom of heaven, because what is born of flesh is flesh. Therefore they are endowed with faith and adoption in baptism itself. But if this is true, then, he says in these very words on page 492, *not only by a probable argument but also by a most certain and indubitable consequence*—observe this man’s confidence, Christian reader—*it is gathered that infants born even of godly and Christian parents are not counted in the covenant unless they have also been baptized.* Therefore they also perish, since there is no life outside the gospel covenant.

For my part I embrace the first hypothesis: namely, that the kingdom of heaven belongs to infants born in the church, all of whom I also presume to be elect unless the contrary appears. But I distinguish the latter hypothesis concerning infants’ faith. I acknowledge that faith must indeed intervene so that infants contained in the covenant please God, but I deny that they can or must be endowed with a faith inhering personally in themselves. Rather, I affirm that the right to eternal life, acquired for them from the formula of the covenant by being born from godly parents, is afterward to be sealed in them by baptism if it can be done—unless the gift of actual faith from hearing, and baptism’s effect, be deferred to that very time appointed by God for believing. I demonstrated this above both from the Apostle’s express words and from Augustine himself, with all the Fathers and indeed the Scholastics agreeing. Once this hypothesis is overthrown, the dilemma built upon it also collapses. Therefore with the Apostle I conclude precisely the contrary: children are born holy from even one believing parent; if the firstfruits are holy, the lump is holy too; and if the root is holy, the branches are holy, even though some—either known to God alone or to be disclosed only in their own time—are broken off.

On page 492 our adversary, seeking to confirm infants’ inclusion or enrollment in the covenant and that salvation begins from baptism, with the time preceding baptism excluded—the other limb of the preceding dilemma—uses a twofold argument to conclude that all who die before baptism necessarily perish. The first is *that carnal birth, even from godly parents, contributes nothing toward attaining the kingdom of heaven, according to “Do not say, ‘We have Abraham as our father,’” “What is born of flesh is flesh,” and “Unless one is born again of water and the Spirit.”* The second is *that under the law of circumcision, which baptism succeeded, anyone who was not circumcised was cut off from God’s people.* He speaks here not so much against me as against God’s covenant, in which the offspring is included with the believing parent. Moreover, this enthymeme contains that greatest fault which is called *not the thing in question*. For it is—

[Part II | PDF page 132 | logical source scan 131 | printed page 130]

—indeed true what he says concerning carnal generation, nor was there any need to bring forward sacred testimonies to confirm what no one denies. But it by no means follows that the children of holy people are not born holy and consequently included in the covenant. Far from birth from the godly contributing nothing toward attainment of the kingdom of heaven, these words stand firm before the ever-truthful God, even if lying men intervene in the line of descent: “I will be God to you and to your seed”; the Apostle’s words, “The election remains for the fathers’ sake”; those I have cited so often, “If the root is holy, then so are the branches,” and, “If the firstfruits are holy, so also is the lump”; and that saying so often repeated, “For the sake of David my servant.”

Yet this is not to be understood as though, just as sin and death passed by carnal generation from one man to all, so faith and life are propagated from godly parents into children as subjects in which they inhere. God’s grace is not in human power, nor does a holy man beget a man insofar as he is holy but insofar as he is a man, and therefore one unclean person begets another. Rather, it pleased the merciful God to covenant with his elect—that is, with the true church—in such a way that believing fathers by their faith acquire for their children, even to a thousand generations, the right, so to speak, of Christian citizenship. Meanwhile it pleases God himself to choose these or those among the posterity of the godly, as is clear from the histories of Abraham, Isaac, David, and so many other saints, and finally from daily experience. For the elect are the seed of promise. It is not for men to beget children for God; it belongs to God alone to give children of promise to godly parents as it pleases him. Meanwhile, until the contrary appears, it is ours—leaving God’s secret judgments with him—to think and judge generally of infants born in the church, as we do of adults professing the faith, as members of Christ’s church by birth.

And I ask: if birth from godly parents contributes nothing toward attaining the kingdom of heaven, let Andreae tell us why he admits infants born in the church to baptism, but not the children of Turks or Jews, who have now fallen away from the covenant for a time. Is it not because the former are reckoned to belong to the covenant because they were born in the church, so that the covenant’s sign and seal ought not to be denied them, while the latter, because they were born outside the church and are therefore unclean, cannot be admitted into God’s temple? If Andreae answers that infants born in the church are admitted to baptism not as already holy and enrolled in God’s covenant but in order to be sanctified and enrolled in it, the question returns. I ask again: why admit these rather than Turks or Jews to obtain that sanctification, if birth from godly parents contributes nothing toward attaining the kingdom of heaven? The vanity of that objection is also clear because the terms “sign” and “seal,” by which the Apostle declared the chief use of circumcision—which baptism succeeded—plainly show that the first entrance into God’s covenant is birth in the church from godly parents, not baptism. For a seal, or sealing sign, necessarily presupposes as antecedent that which is sealed. Herbrand therefore judged this matter more correctly when he wrote on page 633:—

[Part II | PDF page 133 | logical source scan 132 | printed page 131]

*The children of Christians are not by nature better or holier than others begotten outside the church. But our affirmation that they are saved comes from God’s singular privilege, when God professes that he is not only our God but also the God of our seed. “The promise,” Peter says in Acts 3:39, speaking indeed of baptism, “is made to you AND TO YOUR CHILDREN”; and God says to Abraham in Genesis 17, “I will be your God and the God of your seed after you.” But we are now Abraham’s seed, Galatians 3:29. Therefore this promise belongs to us and to our infants even while still placed in the womb.* So he says. If these things are true, as they certainly are, how will Andreae’s repeated assertion be true—that birth from godly parents contributes nothing toward attaining the kingdom of heaven?

Perhaps he will object that Herbrand does not thereby exclude baptism. As if, when we say that the children of saints are born holy and included in the covenant—because, as Augustine excellently says, *The body of death in the first parents begot infants as sinners, but the Spirit of life in later believing parents regenerated believers from believers*—we therefore exclude the seal of this covenant, that is, baptism. But he would reply that Herbrand attributes that privilege not to infants indiscriminately but only to those overtaken by a case of necessity and therefore unbaptized, while he himself holds on page 498 that unavoidable necessity, arising neither from negligence nor contempt, excuses deprivation of baptism. Yet this objection will again help him nothing. It is indeed true that Herbrand finally treats those unbaptized infants whom unavoidable necessity has overtaken, a matter we will discuss in its place. But it does not follow that this privilege begins from baptism or depends upon necessity. The privilege itself is one thing; its efficacy is another—an efficacy of which baptism’s despisers could not deprive themselves unless they were born possessing the privilege.

Furthermore, Andreae expressly and more than once affirms that infants are not endowed with faith and adoption before baptism but only in baptism itself, and with actual faith inhering in them, without which neither an infant nor any adult can please God. Who does not see that this double dogma is wholly incoherent: with the first established, the second is destroyed; with the second established, the first necessarily perishes? For if infants have no faith before baptism and yet some unbaptized infants are saved, then they are saved without faith and it is false that no one without faith pleases God. But if infants are endowed with faith before baptism in a certain peculiar manner—which, lest anything be lacking from so many absurdities, Andreae himself denies on page 492 to be extraordinary—he convicts himself of falsehood when he says on page 458 that *infants are endowed with faith not before baptism but in baptism*. Falsehoods must indeed thus destroy one another.

[Part II | PDF page 134 | logical source scan 133 | printed page 132]

The foundation of his other enthymeme, drawn from the law of circumcision, which adds the threat of cutting off from God’s people everyone whose foreskin has not been circumcised, is false because Moses’ whole context is misunderstood. The law did not apply at all to infants who ought to have been circumcised at the appointed time but were not through their parents’ fault, unless on reaching maturity they neglected circumcision. This must clearly be gathered not only because infants would otherwise be condemned, but also from the law’s appendix: “because he has made my covenant void.” Why should an infant who does not yet possess the use of reason pay the penalties for contempt or even negligence in this matter? Accordingly, Exodus 4 says that God was angry not with Moses’ uncircumcised son but with Moses. Therefore, not only was failure to be circumcised nonfatal to those dying before the eighth day appointed for circumcision; it was also nonfatal to those who died uncircumcised on the eighth day through their family’s fault, unless they had later reached maturity and despised circumcision. Finally, Moses’ words, “That soul shall be cut off from his people,” also show that from their very birth infants were enrolled in the covenant and among their people; only afterward did those who neglected the sealing of that covenant fall from it.

Not only the Chaldean paraphrase but Rabbi Solomon also teaches that this law is to be expounded in this way: *If a boy was not circumcised by his parents, this was imputed to his parents until the boy grew up. For meanwhile, while he was a boy, the violation of this precept was not yet imputed to him. But when he reached age, it was imputed to him and not to his father—unless the father too was at fault.* And Aben Ezra says: *If he is a son of the commandment and does not circumcise the flesh of his foreskin, he is cut off from God’s people, because he received this command in the person of his father. But if the father did not circumcise him, let him circumcise himself when he becomes legally independent; otherwise let him be circumcised by the hands of heaven.* Heretics, however, suppose that if an uncircumcised boy died, he would have no share in the age to come. Ambrose too, in his book *On Abraham*, although he turns aside to allegory, expressly writes that this threat appears to belong to one who understands rather than to an infant. Andreae wrongly restricts it on page 498 to infants dying before the eighth day. Here I am compelled to complain of Andreae’s secretaries as falsifiers: on one and the same page, 497, they attributed two contrary positions to me. Herbrand, whose words I cited a little earlier, correctly explains the sense in which Christ’s words, “Unless one is born again of water and the Spirit,” must be received.

I proceed to our adversary’s position, expressed on page 495, concerning the uncircumcised who died in the wilderness. He makes two classes of them: one of those who died before the eighth day, to whom he thinks uncircumcision was not imputed because they cannot be regarded as having violated the law; the other of those who lived beyond the eighth day while remaining un—

[Part II | PDF page 135 | logical source scan 134 | printed page 133]

—circumcised. Of the latter, some, although sinning gravely, were nevertheless excused by God on account of the disorderly and uncertain movements of the camp and were afterward circumcised under Joshua; but others, whom God did not preserve to be circumcised later, appear—if judgment must be made from God’s Word—to have perished altogether. Andreae did not dare say this in these very words, but it follows necessarily from his sufficiently clear explanation of his position. For my part, although I wholly agree with him concerning uncircumcised infants who died before the eighth day, whether in the wilderness or thereafter, I nevertheless marvel how he can affirm that today without refuting himself. For he similarly affirms on page 477 concerning circumcision what he said of baptism: namely, that faith is given and adoption conferred upon infants in the rite itself. But if this is true, how will any uncircumcised be saved unless it be held either that some are saved without faith or that faith and adoption are falsely said to have been given in circumcision itself?

As for those who lived beyond the eighth day and were finally circumcised under Joshua, the words I cited from Herbrand more than sufficiently refute this man’s confidence in interpreting omission of circumcision so harshly. There remain those who died uncircumcised in the wilderness after the eighth day. Whether they were very few or some larger number—for who can discover it?—let Andreae consider why he consigns them to destruction if, as is altogether true, the present danger to life excused all of them from the necessity of circumcision.

On page 498 he rightly teaches that the unbaptized are now less readily excused than the uncircumcised formerly, because no certain interval is defined for administering baptism. But he then concludes that unavoidable necessity alone can excuse the unbaptized, while negligence and contempt deserve no pardon. There are matters in that position which I rightly miss. First, what must be reckoned unavoidable necessity should at least be defined generally, so that consciences may be satisfied; I will speak of that shortly. Second, everyone sees that to make negligence without any qualification equal to contempt, and that in the greatest matter of all—eternal life or death—is a severity wholly inconsistent with God’s clemency. Besides, what is the purpose of this word “deserve”? Whatever room for any excuse anyone has before God, whether in this matter or others, proceeds solely from the indulgence of a most merciful Father.

Moreover, if danger to life arising from those uncertain movements of the camp excused Israelite infants in the wilderness from the necessity of circumcision—something neither God nor Moses nor Aaron would otherwise ever have tolerated as an omission continuing forty entire years among so many thousands of infants—why will the same principle not obtain when evident danger threatens Christian children? How many things may occur, either in the child himself or from another source, on account of which the matter cannot be required of him? What if—

[Part II | PDF page 136 | logical source scan 135 | printed page 134]

—some adult is dying? In its proper place I will refute Andreae’s belief that this necessity can be met by granting to anyone, even women, the power to baptize. And who could persuade him that in ancient Israel the eighth day precisely appointed for infants’ circumcision was observed so rigidly that, whatever the infants’ state of health, they were circumcised that very day? David and his companions ate the sacred loaves; and Christ testifies that the Sabbath—whose observance was otherwise so exact that the Lord commanded even the slightest contempt of it to be avenged by stoning—was made for man, not man for the Sabbath. It therefore appears that no ceremonial law existed which charity did not moderate by a certain equity.

Nor did it happen for any other reason in the Christian church, especially in colder regions, that sprinkling replaced the ancient washing of the whole body: namely, because of the weakness of infants’ bodies, since by then very few adults were baptized. Cyprian discusses this in Book 6 of his *Letters*, even though immersion was a sacramental rite, as the Apostle teaches in Romans 6 and 5; and, as appears from the ancient councils of Laodicea and Neocaesarea, some superstitious persons only reluctantly agreed to sprinkling or pouring. Let contempt of the sacraments indeed be fatal, because one who despises the divinely appended seal counts the covenant of life itself as nothing. By what rule of law, however, would it have been fatal to an infant unbaptized through no fault of his own—unless perhaps, after growing older, he neglected baptism, as we said shortly before concerning the uncircumcised?

These principles must nevertheless be received with an exception for a danger to life in which either God’s command must be tenaciously observed or God’s glory betrayed. Then one must undergo a thousand deaths rather than violate the Lord’s Word. Witness those Maccabees worthy of eternally blessed praise, who together with their mother preferred to be tortured by every kind of most inhuman cruelty rather than taste pork. It should also be known that negligence of baptism is fatal either to parents or to infants themselves in such a way that room nevertheless remains for repentance. If their repentance is true, those who formerly neglected baptism will certainly seek it as opportunity allows. But what if some obstacle then occurs so that they cannot obtain what they desire? They must surely lament that sin all the more. Yet who will cut off the possibility of pardon from those pleading forgiveness for their fault? Faith and adoption therefore do not begin from baptism itself.

Nor did Ambrose speak rashly concerning Valentinian, who—following the evil custom of that age—came late to baptism and died on the journey. Ambrose did not hesitate nevertheless to declare that he was called to blessed life. Were women or private persons, from whom he could have been baptized, lacking in his retinue, if the shameful error of the *women-baptizers* had then prevailed in the church? Thus far concerning the matters which he proposed to us in that colloquy: some are discovered to be false from God’s Word and sure rea—

—sons, while others were certainly proposed there ambiguously and uncertainly.
