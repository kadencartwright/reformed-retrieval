[Marginalia: Transition. Lecture VI.]

Let us now proceed to the particular exegesis of the following verse. In it
we have both a clearer exposition and an amplification of the benefit
comprehended in the immediately preceding verse, both of which consist
chiefly in these three things. First, in place of the word *election*, which
he used above, he here employs the term *proorismos*, or predestination.
Second, in place of the immediate end of our election expressed there, he
here substitutes a further and more august end when he says that we were
predestined for adoption as sons through Jesus Christ. Finally, he explains
the antecedent or impelling cause of our election, first more briefly and
obscurely by the phrase “in himself,” and then more clearly and distinctly
when he adds by way of further explanation these words, “according to the
good pleasure of his will.” These, then, are the matters we must consider in
this verse.

As to the first of these three, I remember touching above upon what the word
*proorismos*, predestination, properly designates: namely, to appoint
beforehand, to determine beforehand, and to order and dispose something to an
end. Thus it is not so much the end itself as the means to the end that are
properly said to be predestined, that is, directed and ordered. So too the
words *proetoimazein* and *procheirizesthai*, sometimes used in Holy Scripture,
are synonyms of *proorizein*, that is, they are used in the same sense. In all
of them the prefix *pro-* designates, so to speak, the priority of the divine
counsel, that is, its very eternity, insofar as the divine decree preceded
from eternity not merely the result or outcome of the things predestined but
also the actual causes themselves, or the causes brought into act.

[Marginalia: *Proorismos*. The distinction between predestination and
election.]

It must, however, be observed in passing that, although the Apostle here takes
election and predestination as the same insofar as each is restricted to
those chosen and to be saved, some difference can be noticed when the two are
weighed more precisely. Election seems to designate God’s purpose concerning
us with a certain reference to its opposite member, namely, to the reprobate
or those left behind who were not chosen. For election properly signifies a
selection of some people out of the crowd, number, and lot of the rest. In
that selection made by God, so to speak, the others before the ages—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 57 | Caput I p. 15]

—were left and passed by in their miserable state and were not taken up with
the elect to life, salvation, and glory. Predestination, however, denotes
that same decree with reference to the means that serve its execution, and so
determines beforehand that whole progression of causes by which God actually
conducts the elect to the appointed end. For, as we noted above, to
predestine is to ordain that those very people who have been predestined are
to be conducted to the proposed end through certain suitable means, and to
establish and designate all those means for that same end. Hence in the
Scriptures we frequently see this word *predestine* joined with the means
themselves. In the present place, for example, we are said to be predestined
to adoption, which, with respect to the ultimate end, properly has the
character of a means. Elsewhere we are predestined to good works, as below in
chapter 2, verse 10; elsewhere to be conformed to the image of Christ, as in
Romans 8:29; and elsewhere to know God’s will, as in Acts 22:14. I do not deny,
however, that it is also joined with the end itself, as in Acts 13:48, where
all who were ordained to eternal life are said to have believed. And in the
following verse 6 God is said to have predestined us to the praise of his
glorious grace. For this is to be repeated from the common construction. In
these passages Scripture sometimes uses a word for predestination, sometimes
for preparation beforehand, sometimes for ordination, and sometimes for
appointment beforehand.

Let us now see, in the second place, to what or to what end the Apostle says
God predestined us: *eis huiothesian dia Iēsou Christou*, that he might adopt
us as sons through Jesus Christ. To understand this end, a few things must be
said about the term adoption, or *huiothesia*. It is certain that every son of
God is such either by nature or by grace. By nature the Son is the *Logos*
alone, consubstantial with the Father—I mean that eternal Son who is of one
substance with the Father, the second Person of the glorious Trinity. At the
time determined from eternity he assumed human nature into the unity of his
own person. Christ, insofar as he is man, is customarily called the Son of God,
not indeed by nature but by the grace of that wonderful conception from the
Holy Spirit and of the hypostatic union with the *Logos*, and consequently in
a manner proper to himself and by a grace peculiar to himself. This is true
and orthodox. Yet we prefer to speak of Christ’s human nature *according to
assumption*, that is, insofar as it was assumed by the Deity, so that the
unity of the Person may be apparent in this account, rather than to posit here
a distinct mode of sonship by the grace of conception and personal union. We
must not appear to make two sons, that is, two persons, in Christ the
God-man. Although there are two natures, there is nevertheless only one Son
of God, just as Christ the God-man is one person, but with this distinction:
that same Christ, the one Son of God, is and is called the Son of God in one
respect insofar as he is God, or by reason of the divine nature—namely, God’s
natural or only-begotten Son, *ho monogenēs*, alone begotten from the Father’s
substance. In another respect, insofar as he is man or by reason of the human
nature, he is the Son by the peculiar grace proper to himself of conception
from the Holy Spirit and hypostatic union with the *Logos*.

[Marginalia: Adoption. Note in passing the distinction.]

To return, then, to the subject: someone is again called a son of God by grace
in two ways—no mention being made here of Christ’s human nature for the reason
just given. He is a son either by the grace of creation, as are the blessed
angels and man before the fall, or by the grace of adoption, as are all the
elect and regenerate after the fall. These are born children of wrath, or at
least became such by their own choice and fault, as did our first parents,
and thus were utterly estranged from God and his grace through and because of
sin. By the mercy of the most gracious God alone and his benevolent will, in
Christ and through Christ—the only-begotten and firstborn Son, the uniquely
beloved Son—they are adopted by God as sons. In this way they obtain the
right and name of sons and come to be counted as Christ’s brothers and
fellow heirs.

[Marginalia: Resumption.]

This adoption is, in turn, not so much twofold as it is considered in two ways
in Scripture: first, as begun here through faith and hope; then, as perfected
and consummated in the future life. In the former sense, if we regard God, it
can refer to the very acceptance by which he accepts us as sons even in this
life, according to John 1:11: “As many as received him”—namely, Christ
manifested in the flesh—“to them he gave *tēn exousian*,” that is, the
excellent right and dignity, “to become sons of God.” If we regard the
inheritance itself, it can refer to the very right by which they may claim
that inheritance even now as their own by the right of adoption. In the
latter sense, if you regard God, adoption refers to that glorious
manifestation in which, in the final and absolute glory of the elect, God will
manifest that he adopted them as sons for himself through Christ, something
not yet evident to all in this life. This accords with 1 John 3:2: “Beloved,
we are sons of God, but what we shall be has not yet been manifested. We know,
however, that when he comes, we shall be like him,” and so forth. But if you
regard our inheritance, it will signify its full possession or enjoyment,
into which we shall then at last be admitted and, as sons, enter upon that
paternal inheritance. The Apostle takes the word adoption in this latter
sense in Romans 8:23 when he says, “awaiting adoption,” that is, “the
redemption of our body.”

If, then, you take adoption in this latter sense, for its perfect attainment
or its completion in the future life, it will designate an end of our
election further than the preceding one, namely eternal life. It is therefore
the ultimate end not absolutely—for that is expressed in the following
verse—but with respect to us. If we take it in the former sense, for that
right by which God most good and great counts and accepts us as sons as soon
as we have been grafted into Christ through faith and justified, adoption
will signify a means of salvation, one which by nature precedes what was
expressed in the preceding verse. For adoption in this sense coheres
immediately with effectual calling and justification, which precede
sanctification by nature. Indeed, some interpret adoption in this passage as
effectual calling. But if we are to approach one side or the other, we
certainly prefer to interpret the name adoption here of the eternal
inheritance itself. First, we see the name adoption explained in this way more
than once by our Apostle—not only in the passage from Romans just cited, for
example, but also in Galatians 4:5, where he says that Christ redeemed us “so
that we might receive adoption.” He appears to interpret this in Hebrews 9:15
when he says that we were redeemed by the death of the Testator so that those
who were called might receive the promise of the eternal inheritance, meaning
by the name *eternal inheritance* here the same thing he had designated there
by the word *adoption*.

Second, in this way the very noteworthy gradation in these three verses is
preserved. When the Apostle illustrates our election, he ascends in order
through those three ends: from the immediate end, which is our
sanctification; through the intermediate one, namely perfect adoption, or
glory and eternal salvation; to the supreme end, namely the praise of God’s
glorious grace. However the matter may stand—that is, in whatever sense you
take the word adoption here—the end here will always be more august than the
preceding one, for it necessarily includes the former within itself. It is
something greater, worthier, and more divine to be adopted as God’s sons than
to be holy and blameless. The latter only designates God’s image in us, while
the former brings with it a nearer and closer bond with God and thus a greater
dignity. Not everyone who is like another is immediately his son or heir; but
if we are sons, not only must we bear God’s image, but we are also joined more
closely to him so that we may live with him forever. “If sons, then heirs,”
says the Apostle in the same passage of Romans 8.

[Marginalia: The distinction of adoption. John 1:11. 1 John 3:2. Romans 8:23.
Application of what has been said. Piscator. Galatians 4:5. Hebrews 9:15.]

Let us see further through whom he says we are to be adopted as sons by the
Father: “Through Jesus Christ,” he says. This has already been discussed
several times above. Here we observe only that, just as above, when the
Apostle mentions every spiritual blessing and eternal election, so also here,
when he mentions predestination, he always leads us as if by the hand to
Christ. We cannot even think, much less hope, that election to salvation,
predestination to grace, or any spiritual blessing can come or extend to us
apart from Christ.

[Marginalia: Through Jesus Christ.]

Here, however, whenever the Apostle places Christ beneath everything as the
foundation of our entire salvation, laid and founded by God himself—that is,
laid down or established in his eternal counsel so that through Christ alone
he might conduct all the elect to their end—we must always hold what I
remember observing above on verse 3. Christ is not regarded simply and
separately, as consubstantial and coeternal with God the Father, as I might
put it, and thus coeternal and equal in all things. For in that case we would
have to say that we were chosen not so much *in* him as *by* him, together
with the Father and the Holy Spirit, since the three Persons of the most Holy
Trinity neither can nor should be divided in the work or counsel of our
election. Rather, he is regarded as the Mediator manifested in the flesh and
thus as the head of the predestined, as Augustine treats this matter expressly
in book 1 of *The Predestination of the Saints*, and as, God graciously
helping, we shall see more fully below. In him and through him alone it pleased
the Father freely to adopt and reconcile to himself all those destined for
salvation.

[Marginalia: An observation on the manner in which you consider Christ.]

Here again we must observe that order of causes and means which serve our
salvation and which we sampled in the preceding verse. Just as our alienation
from God through the fall and sin preceded our reconciliation to God through
Christ in that series of causes and means, so creation in integrity preceded
that alienation. To grasp this order more distinctly, let us note once and
for all—so far as certain stages and a sequence can be conceived by us
separately and according to our mode of understanding in that eternal decree
and counsel of our God, which from eternity he foresaw in one single act, as
it were, and in the most simple stroke of his mind—let us note, I say, that
our God maintained, as it were, the following most wise and holy order in
decreeing. First of all, he established the end itself: namely, to manifest
the glory of his infinite grace and mercy in saving some human beings, just as
craftsmen of every kind are also accustomed first of all to set before
themselves the end of their work. Then, to attain this end, he decreed to
create man upright and holy, yet in such a way that he could fall from that
state into sin by his own choice and at the instigation—

[Marginalia: Inference. Note the sequence of the divine counsel. 1. 2.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 58 | Caput I p. 16]

—of the devil, and so render himself and his descendants liable to divine
judgment. Third, he decreed to produce that work by far the noblest and most
glorious of all, and thus far more magnificent than creation itself: namely,
the redemption of those whom he was going to take up to life and choose in
Christ. In this work he first of all ordained that the eternal Word should
become flesh, and thus designated and appointed the Word so incarnate as the
head and mediator of the people to be saved. Then in him he selected for
himself, out of the whole mass of fallen humanity which he had eternally
present before his eyes, that definite number of people in whose salvation he
had decreed to manifest the riches of his glorious grace. By this very act of
choosing he inscribed their names, as it were, in the book of life and entered
them in that register of his decree, never to be erased from it. Finally, he
predestined those same people not only to attain the end itself in Christ,
that is, eternal salvation, but also to the antecedent and intervening means
by which they would be conducted to that salvation, the end, as though along
a certain road marked out and determined by God himself from eternity.
Specifically, he decreed that those thus chosen in Christ would through the
same Christ be redeemed from eternal death, effectually called, given faith,
reconciled to himself, adopted as sons through Christ, justified, sanctified,
armed by the power of his Spirit against the force of all spiritual enemies,
sealed, and crowned with the gift of final perseverance, so that they would
be conducted to the very harbor of salvation and the goal of eternal life and
admitted into the possession of the heavenly inheritance itself.

[Marginalia: 3.]

From all this it becomes evident that, if we regard the execution of that
eternal decree concerning our salvation, its foundation is placed in Christ
the Mediator himself. For when God most good and great devised the manner and
means of saving us—if I may speak of his majesty in this human and
anthropopathic way with Holy Scripture—he determined that his Word should
become incarnate and that he would graft all his elect into him as their head,
adopt them to himself in him, join them to him, and render them righteous,
holy, and at last glorious. But if we regard the natural order of the causes
themselves and investigate the causes of that decree concerning our election,
far be it from us to say that the heavenly Father sought in Christ the
Mediator the cause of his decree to choose and save us. On the contrary,
because God had determined to save some and to reveal his mercy in their
salvation, he therefore first of all predestined Christ himself as Mediator
and decreed that he should be made man, in whom and through whom all the
remaining elect would be saved.

[Marginalia: Consequence.]

Otherwise it would follow that God first determined to execute his counsel
about saving human beings before he had formed any counsel about saving them;
that he thought about humanity’s deliverance from the fall before he thought
about the fall itself; and that he decreed to permit man to fall into sin
before he had decreed anything about the future condition of human beings for
all eternity. These consequences follow if, by an inverted and perverse order
of causes, we say that God decreed to save chosen human beings because he had
appointed Christ as Mediator and Savior. Since these claims are utterly
absurd, insane, and blasphemous, let us speak instead according to the right
order of causes: because God decreed from eternity to save man, he therefore
also appointed Christ from eternity to be his Savior; chose in Christ from
eternity the man who was to be saved; and predestined that same man from
eternity to be adopted to himself through Christ—that is, to be redeemed,
reconciled, justified, sanctified, and glorified. This the Apostle plainly
asserts here.

[Marginalia: Confirmation.]

We must nowhere seek outside God himself the higher cause why God decreed to
save man in Christ, why he chose him in Christ, and why he determined to adopt
him through Christ. Instead, let us see that cause with our Apostle in the
following phrase—the third matter to be explained in this verse—where he
expresses it in this way: *eis auton, kata tēn eudokian*, and so forth. It must
be held that interpreters explain the words *eis auton* in two principal ways.
First, “unto himself,” that is, what he says below, “unto his own glory,” so
that the phrase denotes the supreme end of our election. It is used in this
sense at the end of Romans 11: “From him, and through him, and unto him are
all things.” But because that end is supplied in the immediately following
verse, I prefer with the most learned Beza to take *eis auton* as *en heautō*,
“in himself.” By this form of speech the Apostle excludes every other cause
outside God himself, as if he wished to signify to us that in his decree
concerning our election and predestination God regarded no cause outside
himself by which he was impelled or moved—none present or future, in heaven
or on earth—but found the causes of his most wise counsel in himself alone.
Here we should remember that the other two Persons of the glorious Trinity
are by no means to be separated or excluded from the Father, whether we
regard the decree itself or its execution, while nevertheless preserving for
each his manner and order of working derived from the order of the Persons
themselves.

[Marginalia: Transition to the third part. In himself. Thus Piscator.]

If no prior cause of this counsel can be found or ought to be sought outside
God himself, someone might ask what cause it then has in God himself. This is
what the Apostle explains when he adds *kata tēn eudokian*, and so forth. In
God himself it has no other cause than the *eudokia* of his will, that is, his
benevolent affection or free benevolence. Some indeed think that both words,
*thelēmatos* and *eudokias*, signify the same thing, namely God’s free love,
and that the words are combined emphatically according to a Hebrew idiom. Yet
when we weigh the distinction that can be observed between *will* and
*eudokia*, it seems preferable that will—as something more general, which
regards all people, even the reprobate, and indeed all creatures concerning
whom God performs his will, even when he abandons them or casts them into
eternal punishment—is restricted by the word *eudokia* as a particular
difference to that species of will which is joined with favor and
benevolence. Such is the will of God on which alone the decree of election
and thus the entire work of human salvation and redemption depends, and
according to which God is said here to have chosen us.

The word *will* denotes the freedom of the divine decree and God’s supreme and
unqualified right, power, and judgment to dispose, determine, and decree
concerning all creatures as it has seemed good to him. The word *eudokia*,
however, denotes free benevolence and mercy toward those whom, through no
merit of their own above the rest, he deigned to choose because it so pleased
him, seemed good in his eyes, and was pleasing to him by a benevolent and
gracious will toward them. There therefore seems to be no absurdity whether
we say that we were chosen by the *eudokia* of God’s will or by his
loving-kindness or mercy, but with this distinction: mercy—

[Marginalia: According to his good pleasure. Note in passing.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 59 | Caput I p. 17]

—and loving-kindness regard the chosen people themselves and their condition
when considered in itself, insofar as they were miserable and liable to
eternal death. Because God himself is good, gracious, and merciful, he willed
to deliver the miserable and take them up to life. The *eudokia* of his will,
however, seems to regard the elect in comparison with the rest who were not
chosen. Why did God deign to choose these miserable people and not those who
were equally miserable? Why did he choose these rather than those? No reason
can be given except this *eudokia* of his will, that is, his most free and
holy will, joined with a benevolent affection or free benevolence toward them.
No cause for it can or ought to be sought anywhere outside God himself;
indeed, none whatever can be rendered or assigned, since none has been
revealed in Holy Scripture.

[Marginalia: Piscator, namely.]

Some say that the word *eudokia* can concern the reprobate no less than the
elect, and thus by its proper signification includes no meaning of benevolence
or benevolent affection. Although I would not contend vigorously with them on
this point, the testimony they cite from Matthew 11 to prove it does not seem
necessarily to establish it: “Yes, Father, because *houtōs egeneto eudokia
emprosthen sou*,” “because it so pleased you”—namely, to hide these things
from the wise and reveal them to little children. For in that passage
*eudokia* could be said principally in respect to the elect, to whom it seemed
good to the heavenly Father to reveal the mysteries of salvation, and not in
respect to the reprobate, from whom he hid the same things. But because one
word had to be added to both preceding members, it was rightly the word that
properly regarded the preferable and worthier member, namely the elect, and
regarded the reprobate only improperly and by an extended usage.

[Marginalia: Matthew 11:26.]

But leaving aside this exactness of language, let us now pass to the following
verse. In it the Apostle partly presents that supreme end of our election and
predestination to adoption as sons through Jesus Christ; and partly repeats
by way of explanation that same grace of adoption which he mentioned in the
preceding verse, illustrating it in other words as occasion offers: “To the
praise,” he says, “of the glory of his grace.” Here we recognize a Hebraism
known to everyone and very commonly used by the Apostles and Evangelists, in
place of “his glorious grace.” We need not, however, recognize the transposed
construction that some posit here, as though the Apostle said “the glory of
grace” in place of “the grace of glory.” On the contrary, the noun *grace*
seems to require the latter position because it has the character of the
underlying subject, that is, the substantive, as the grammarians speak, to
which *glory* is added for amplification and intensification. The Apostle thus
indicates not just any grace but one that is glorious, ample, magnificent,
and beyond every praise, and which therefore can never be praised and
celebrated by us sufficiently according to its worth and magnitude.

[Marginalia: Transition. To the praise of the glory of his grace.]

For if divine grace had extended itself toward us only so far as to deliver
us freely from the eternal death to which sin had sentenced us, that grace
would certainly have been very illustrious: it would have exempted us from so
great a death, disgrace, prison, and eternal torments to be suffered in hell,
so that either we might altogether cease to exist or might lead some sort of
tolerable life here upon this earth. But that grace has overflowed so greatly
upon us through Christ and in Christ that he not only held us as reconciled,
dear, and accepted by himself, but even freely adopted us to himself as sons
and heirs—heirs, indeed, of that heavenly glory which is his own palace and
kingdom. Moreover, we were redeemed in so wonderful a manner: by the death of
his one and uniquely beloved Son and by transferring onto him for our sake
the punishments owed by us. That grace now certainly becomes for us glorious,
divine, and stupendous, to be adored in a kind of wondering silence by us who
are struck by its infinite magnitude. As the royal prophet sings in Psalm
65:2, “Praise awaits you in silence, O God, in Zion.”

The Apostle further seems here to allude to what is written near the end of
Deuteronomy 26: “that he may make you exalted”—the prophet says, addressing
the Israelite people—“above all nations which he made, in praise, name, and
glory.” And in Isaiah 43:7 and 21: “Bring my sons from afar and my daughters
from the ends of the earth, everyone who is called by my name, whom I created
for my glory, whom I formed, yes, whom I made.” And in the same chapter,
verse 21: “My people whom I formed will declare my praise.” The Apostle
therefore teaches that God set this end before himself in predestining us to
life and salvation in Christ: that his beneficence toward us should be
acknowledged by us for what it is in itself—that is, as worthy of every
praise, proclamation, celebration, love, obedience, and admiration—and as
such should perpetually be praised, exalted, and proclaimed. Nothing else can
be expended or bestowed by us upon his glorious majesty to testify our
gratitude to him with every zeal, nor does his supreme goodness and
self-sufficiency require anything else from us. Nothing can be added to him
by us or in any way diminished through us.

[Marginalia: A notable account of the reason. Psalm 65:2. Deuteronomy 26, last
verse. Isaiah 43:7 and 21.]

But let us see how the Apostle describes in the following words this glorious
grace, most worthy of every praise. “With which,” he says, “he *echaritōsen*
us”—that is, he freely made us dear and acceptable to himself in that beloved
one, who alone by his own merit and his own grace is dear and acceptable to
the Father, as one worthy in himself of all the Father’s love and favor. In
him the Father *eudokēsen*, fully rested content, and in him the Father was
fully satisfied. But we are accepted by the Father only in him and through
him, insofar as the Father beholds us in him as given to him, belonging to
him, indeed, as living members grafted into and most closely united to him.
Otherwise, in ourselves and apart from him, we would undoubtedly be hateful
and abominable to God—not insofar as we are human beings or creatures, for
the benevolent creator God cannot hate the works of his hands as such, but
insofar as we are rebels and apostates from him, polluted and corrupted by
the most foul stain of sin, which his supreme holiness and the purity inward
and essential to him cannot but supremely abhor.

[Marginalia: With which he *echaritōsen* us.]

Now if someone asks what grace the Apostle means here when he says that, in
predestining us, God set before himself as the end of his counsel and work
concerning our salvation through Christ “the praise of his glorious grace,”
it will not be enough to answer, “The grace by which he freely made us
acceptable,” and so forth. It will still be asked further what that grace is
by which he made us acceptable to himself in Christ Jesus. For example, God
could be said to cheer or gladden us in two ways. First, he does so by
furnishing us a cause and matter of gladness, such as health, the remission of
sins, or any other temporal or spiritual benefit. Then he does so by
producing gladness itself in our hearts, affecting us with gladness, and thus
pouring, as it were, that oil of joy upon our souls. The scholastics would say
that by the latter we are gladdened formally, but by the former causally. In
the same way the Grace by which the heavenly Father is said to *charitōsai*
us can be taken in two ways. First—

[Marginalia: Exegetical continuation. Comparison. Application.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 60 | Caput I p. 18 (through Lecture VI close)]

—it can mean that grace by which he destined us from eternity for love and
acceptance. This is nothing other than that very *eudokia* of his will which
was discussed in the preceding verse, that is, his free and wholly voluntary
benevolence, acknowledging no cause above itself outside God himself. Second,
it can mean that grace by which, according to that *eudokia* of his, he
actually holds us dear and accepts us in Christ. This appears to be the free
grace of adoption itself, by which he beholds us as given by himself to
Christ, redeemed and reconciled through Christ, and delivered and relieved by
Christ from that hateful burden of sins, and actually embraces us as sons
with paternal love, regarding us as Christ’s brothers and members grafted
into him.

There are some who take this grace in the former sense, as though the Apostle
were saying, “To the praise of that very grace by which he was moved and
impelled to predestine us to salvation in Christ.” We, however, prefer to
interpret it in the latter manner, moved especially by this reason: this
latter grace, through which the Father actually adopts us and makes us dear
to himself in Christ, is the completion and execution of the former grace and
necessarily includes and presupposes it within itself. Because from his own
bare will he pursued us from eternity with free benevolence and mercy, he
therefore also decreed from eternity to reconcile us to himself in Christ and
to make us dear. As he decreed from eternity, so he also actually did: he
imparted that grace of adoption to us and made us dear to himself. He did this
so that his grace toward us, so great and so glorious, might be praised
forever. To that same end he also predestined us from eternity to adoption and
chose us from eternity in Christ: namely, so that that eternal grace, now
actually disclosed and put into execution in time, might be praised and
celebrated for all ages.

[Marginalia: Thus Rollock. Confirmation.]
