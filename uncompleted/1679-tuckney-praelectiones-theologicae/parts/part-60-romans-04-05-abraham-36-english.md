2. Concerning the incapacity to understand, will, and therefore exercise
an act of faith which Castellio attributes to the dying, “when the senses
both of body and mind are so weakened that they have no strength even for a
brief prayer,” I say two things.

1. This is often not the case. In many people, not only the power to
understand but also the power to speak remains vigorous until their final
breath. Moreover, Cicero proves from Posidonius and Homer in book 1 of *On
Divination* that the mind becomes far more divine as death approaches and
that the dying even prophesy; others have transmitted and written more on
this subject. Certainly, just as in the sick we sometimes see a certain
revival of the body immediately before death, so too we see one of the mind
and faith. Thus we read that Jacob, already confined to his bed, “strengthened
himself so that he sat upon the bed and blessed Ephraim and Manasseh,”
Genesis 48:2, and that “when he had finished instructing his sons, he drew
his feet into the bed and immediately expired,” Genesis 49, final verse.
Likewise, we owe the swan song recorded in Psalm 72 to David when he already
lay upon his deathbed, as Muesius thinks, and it is believed that he expired
with those words called his last words in 2 Samuel 23. Nor do we lack
entire books containing the sayings of the dying. In these people and
others like them the mind is not so dulled in death itself that, contrary
to Castellio's affirmation, Intellect and Will have no power in them.

2. But what shall we say of those who very frequently are extinguished by
lethargic, delirious, or other acute diseases, and sometimes even by sudden
death? When bodily organs are damaged and the senses weakened, the soul—as
all who stand by testify—feels and understands nothing and is not even
capable of doing so, as most commonly happens.

Shall we say with Castellio that faith can have absolutely no power at that
moment? Quite the contrary. Since those present cannot know what God does
or does not do in people breathing their last, it is rash to assert this.
As Cicero rightly says, “The sleeper's body lies like the body of a dead
person, but the mind is vigorous and alive.” With their senses bound in
sleep, the prophets had divine dreams; placed in ecstasy, the Apostle heard
“unspeakable words,” 2 Corinthians 12:4. But these things, you will say,
were extraordinary. I answer: why can it not be believed that God very
frequently works in the dying outside the custom and order of those in good
health, and in that sense ordinarily? “God sails well,” said Aeschylus;
“you have seen the Lord's end,” says the Apostle James. Certainly, when
everything else fails in the final struggle, Christ does not fail in act; rather, he then
especially completes everything. He “will guide us even unto death,” Psalm
48, final verse; he loved his own “unto the end,” John 13:1; and he is able
to save “completely,” Hebrews 7:25—that is, forever and to the end, as
Theophylact explains it.

And, if it is permissible to compare small things with great, just as
orators place each of their strongest points first while nevertheless
reserving equally excellent things for the end, so God, most wise and at
the same time most indulgent, customarily adorns the beginning and ending
of our faith—the natal and death days of believers—with the foremost
displays of his grace. “You have kept the good wine until now,” John 2:10.

I do not now dispute whether Jeremiah the prophet, John the Baptist, and
others sanctified from birth or infancy then possessed the use of reason so
that they could exercise acts of intellect, will, and therefore faith.
Castellio will not say that they did, I think. Yet he will not deny that
through God's application to them they were then truly sanctified and that
the seeds of all the grace in which they afterward so abounded were
implanted in them. When Castellio has once explained to us the manner of
this implantation, though they could neither understand nor will nor
exercise an act of faith, then, aided by him, we in turn will unfold for him
the reason and manner by which God can perfect the same grace in those
breathing their last—who are perhaps more capable then than in infancy of
understanding, willing, and producing an act of faith. But even if it were
granted that these dying people, like infants, cannot exercise acts of
reason and faith and so do nothing themselves, our theologians rightly
affirm that in the very first infusion of grace a person is passive with
respect to God's operation. What absurdity would there be if matters stood
the same way in its final consummation? We then credit God alone—who here
especially is Alpha and Omega, beginning and end—with both the firstfruits
and the harvest of our grace and salvation, while we ourselves do nothing.
Everything is begun and completed “not by might nor by power, but by the
Spirit of the Lord of hosts,” who is the author and finisher of our faith.
Thus “the same hand that alone laid the foundation will at last also bring
forth the capstone,” while people acclaim it, “Grace, grace to it!”

Perhaps grace is less pleasing to Castellio, who prefers to be so fond an
adorner—and indeed so strenuous a defender—of free will.

3. Third, then, let us see whether the weapons he aimed at us can be turned
back upon himself. He contends that grace is perfected and sin abolished
before death in such a way that neither can be accomplished in death
itself. Here I ask two questions.

First, if someone has been wicked throughout the whole course of his past
life, is no hope whatever of his salvation left at the point of death? Has
the final verdict of rejection already been cried out against him forever?
This is certainly a hard saying, consonant neither with Holy Scripture nor
with Christian charity. If Augustine is a harsh father to infants,
Castellio is surely just as harsh a father to the dying. Such a person
should not be summoned to admonish or console someone lying in this
condition and breathing his last. He must be numbered among those
“miserable comforters” of whom Job speaks, because he brings the sufferer
nothing but the burden of eternal condemnation. But if there is no longer
room for counsel and admonition because the person suffers from lethargy or
another disease that removes every power of hearing and sensation, prayers
still remain. Castellio will not refuse at least to pour out prayers for so
wretched a person, for “he who restrains prayer empties out the fear of
God.” Yet on his position even these prayers must be denied, since there is
now no remaining hope or even possi-

[1679 printed p. 284 | IA leaf 292]

bility not only of this life, but of eternal salvation. Paul was not so
disposed in a similar peril, Acts 27:20.

But perhaps Castellio cares less for people whose salvation is so
desperate.

Second, then, I ask about others whose hope is not so feeble and dead, but
is alive even in death itself, as the Apostle speaks in 1 Peter 1:3. I mean
those who are truly but not yet perfectly sanctified. Though they have
advanced some distance with Paul, they humbly confess that they “have not
yet been perfected”; they complain of sin still dwelling in them and long
above all to be delivered from this body of death. With Augustine and
Theonas in Cassian's twenty-third Conference, and notwithstanding
Castellio, let us regard them as truly holy, yet not perfect and not yet
wholly cleansed from every remnant of sin. Now suppose one of them suddenly
and unexpectedly falls into a lethargy from which he does not awaken before
death, or dies by some other sudden death. What should be thought or hoped
about one who dies this way? Perhaps we are excessively credulous, but with
Paul we hope for everything good and auspicious, since in their name the
Apostle says, “I thank God through Jesus Christ,” and affirms that no
condemnation remains for them. We believe that in justification the guilt
of the sins for which they repented was removed according to the promise of
1 John 1:7. We also believe that the power and stains of those sins, greatly
diminished through their continual practice of mortification, are at last
entirely removed and washed away by death, even if sudden. But what must be
determined concerning them according to Castellio's position? If he says
they perish in an evil state, let him beware lest he condemn the generation
of God's children. But if, as he argues against us, this separation from sin
can occur neither by faith nor by death, let him see what answer he can give
himself.
