Third, the Disputant must admit that consultation properly so called occurs in
God, and that he can adopt a new counsel concerning infinitely many other
worlds to be created, redeemed, glorified, and destroyed which did not inhere
in God from eternity, and that God today is doubtful and destitute of counsel.
Perhaps in this way he could serve as a reserve soldier for the Qur’an, the
impure Vorstius, and Socinus.

But you will say: *Was God in potency to create the world before he actually
created it? Is he today in potency to will more things outside himself than he
actually wills?* I answer: I think this must be conceded to the Disputant, but
by no means to me. God never was in a potency of creating, knowing, or willing
which could be actualized, determined, or in any manner perfected. An objective
potency capable of actualization can be in the thing to be created or willed;
but every potency in God toward either intrinsic or extrinsic operations is
actualized and determined from eternity. It does not follow, therefore: God is
in potency to create and will; therefore he is in an actualizable and
perfectible potency.

The Disputant continues: *Moreover, the approving will, taken strictly—that is,
for the will which is only approving—is commonly distinguished from the
effective and self-executing will alone, namely, when God is said to will
something which he nevertheless does not undertake to effect or execute; in
this sense we do not resent the distinction.* I answer: the Disputant therefore
confesses that he has been delivered from the verbal dispute from which he
suffered and that he sought a knot in a bulrush. Yet he was by no means
ignorant of our meaning. Why, then, together with the slanderer Jacob Arminius
and the Jesuits, do you tacitly ascribe to our theologians a conflict between
GOD’s wills?

Second, he still does not return to our position. For in this distinction,
which he says he does not resent, the Disputant does not take the phrase
“approving will” as it is commonly used. GOD’s approving will is commonly
directed not to the existence or nonexistence of things or events, but to the
legality of things—that is, their conformity to GOD’s will revealed in the
Word—or to their illegality or dissonance, even if those things or events never
exist in the nature of things. But until the Disputant explains himself more
clearly, his evident meaning is that by an approving will God wills, desires,
longs, and wishes that the final obedience of all the reprobate should exist in
the nature of things; yet God does not produce it by efficacious grace, and
thus does not will that obedience by an effective will. On this account God is
defeated in this inefficacious, approving will and frustrated in his desire by
which his will is directed toward the obedience and salvation of every single
person.

[Marginalia: The approving will is wholly rejected by the Disputant in the
sense in which our theologians use it.]

[Additions, Part II; printed page 618; IA PDF page 692; BSB canvas 668]

These things suit Arminius’s palate, but ours not at all. The Disputant
therefore plays games and deeply resents the distinction commonly received by
our theologians. For he says: *It can scarcely be understood how a person can
resist God—or even how he can resist a human being, I suppose. Then “resist” is
taken for opposing, striving against, or withstanding, or for hindering or
acting contrary to. Certainly, a person can oppose and often does oppose the
will of GOD’s good pleasure no less than the will of sign, and wills something
other than God wills in either manner. Likewise, he can act contrary to what
God commands—that is, act against the will of command—but he cannot hinder or
act contrary to the will of sign.*

[Marginalia: The Disputant maintains that people can break GOD’s will, both of
good pleasure and of sign.]

I answer: certainly none of our theologians ever taught that a human being can
resist and hinder God in his immanent acts, or place an obstacle before him
which prevents him from willing, decreeing, or approving good. But since it
seemed right to the Disputant that a human being can act contrary to what God
wills and approves to be done by him, it is plain that a person can will and do
the contrary of what God approves—that is, according to the Disputant’s
interpretation, what God wills and intends him actually to do. This is for God
to be defeated, mistaken, and disappointed in his own will and intention—that
is, in the thing willed and intended. I do not see why the Disputant did not
cite Arminius, whom he defends against us; beyond doubt he therefore does not
act candidly.

Second, to say that a person can act contrary to what God willed by the will of
good pleasure and by the effective or permissive will, and so frustrate God in
the thing decreed from eternity, is ἀθεόλογον—godless theology. *For who resists
GOD’s will? I will perform all my will,* says Jehovah. Yet the Disputant does
not mean that a person can hinder GOD’s eternal and immanent acts, or place an
obstacle before God which prevents him from decreeing or establishing from
eternity or forming a counsel within himself from eternity; that is the dream
of fevered people. Therefore, people can break GOD’s decrees, and when Paul
says, *Who resists the will of GOD’s good pleasure?* the meaning is that no one
can resist it or act contrary to it. The Disputant replies: *Certainly, a
person often opposes GOD’s will of good pleasure.* Not even the impure Socinus
would stomach that. I acknowledge that a person can strive against and try to
hinder a thing which God has decreed should occur, as Joseph’s brothers tried
to resist Joseph’s exaltation, which God decreed would occur. But by his
irresistible yet permissive decree God decreed that very resistance and
striving. Thus it is impossible to withstand the thing decreed. The Disputant
also vigorously

[Additions, Part II; printed page 619; IA PDF page 693; BSB canvas 669]

maintains with Arminius that GOD’s will is directed toward the permission of
sin, but in no way or sense toward the permitted sin, because the permitting
will is purely negative with respect to sin.

But against this, the permitting will with respect to sin is either positive,
negative, or a kind of mean. Arminius favors the phrase “a relaxed will.”
Certainly, GOD’s permitting will is not positive, effective, or approving;
this needs no proof. It is not purely negative. A stone stands purely
negatively toward sin’s entrance; in this way all acts of GOD’s will concerning
sin would be denied of God—namely, God purely does not will sin, purely does not
intend sin to occur, and purely does not care whether sin occurs or not. But in
that case providence would be exiled, Epicureanism introduced, and fortune would
reign in the fall and ruin of so many most noble creatures. GOD’s permitting
will concerning sin must therefore be a mean between GOD’s positive and purely
negative will.

Yet this will is not a mean by negation of both extremes, because the negative
will of God and all its acts are truly denied of GOD’s permitting will
concerning sin. Hence these propositions are true: *The permitting will by no
means does not will sin to occur.* Or: *By the permitting will God by no means
does not intend, or by no means does not care, whether sin occurs or not.* And
because an affirmation follows from two negations, this is true: *In some
manner God wills sin to occur.* But the adversaries would not admit the truth
of these propositions, for they profess to acknowledge these as true: *The
permitting will does not will sin to occur*; likewise, *God does not care
whether sin occurs or not.* It therefore remains that the permitting will
concerning sin is a mean by participation in both extremes. It is not purely
negative, lest GOD’s providence appear asleep. Nor is it purely affirmative or
positive, because God does not effect or approve sin but hates it.

[Marginalia: GOD’s permitting will does not stand purely negatively toward
sin.]

I remember that in the heat of disputation this phrase escaped me: *God is the
primary cause of Joseph’s criminal sending into Egypt.* But I wish I had spoken
more cautiously. Nevertheless, the preceding and following context of my words
sufficiently vindicates me. For I speak of the sending which is criminal, not
insofar as it is criminal. I deny that the most holy God should be called the
cause, author, effecter,
