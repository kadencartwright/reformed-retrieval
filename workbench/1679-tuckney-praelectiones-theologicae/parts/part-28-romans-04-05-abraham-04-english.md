ROMANS 4:1

This first clause contains the sum of the argument drawn from Abraham's
example. Its simple meaning, stripped of figures of speech, is: Abraham was
not justified by works of law, as will be established below. Paul presents
this simple and genuine meaning in three forms: first, by the logical
inference conveyed in “therefore”; second, as an interrogation, “What,
then?”; and third, through the rhetorical figure of communication, “What,
then, shall we say?”

First, I acknowledge that the Greek particle “therefore,” when joined to
other particles, is often redundant and is sometimes explained as “also,”
“certainly,” or “indeed.” Nor do I deny that it could be explained that way
here. Yet there is no reason not to take it in its more usual inferential
sense. The sole question is the antecedent from which, or the premises from
which, the inference is drawn. How this chapter connects with what precedes
is worth investigating, since, as Toledo observes, clarifying this matter is
“very necessary for a proper understanding of what is said.”

Some—Pseudo-Jerome, Anselm, Aquinas, Estius, Sasbout, and others—treat the
particle as transitional rather than inferential. On their view Paul does
not draw a consequence but moves from one subject to another, resuming the
question previously proposed in Romans 3:1: “What advantage, then, has the
Jew? Or what benefit has circumcision?” He repeats it here because he had
said that God justifies circumcision by faith and uncircumcision through
faith without distinction.

There are two questions there. The former concerns the Jews' advantage,
which Paul discussed in the preceding chapter and showed to be none in
respect of justification. The latter, concerning circumcision's benefit,
has been deferred until now and is here repeated in other words, especially
on occasion of Paul's teaching that God justifies circumcision and
uncircumcision without distinction. It followed that being circumcised or
uncircumcised made no difference, giving rise to the question, “What benefit,
then, has circumcision?”

Abraham first received the command of circumcision and is proclaimed
righteous and God's friend in Scripture. Paul therefore resumes the
question in Abraham's person, since it might seem necessary to admit that he
was justified through circumcision. A Jew could ask, “What benefit did
circumcision bring him, if not righteousness?” Paul answers that although
the sign of circumcision was not useless to Abraham, he was justified by
faith and not by circumcision. This is what the chapter develops at length.
So Estius.

[1679 printed p. 178 | IA leaf 186]

Cardinal Toledo rejects this account for two reasons. First, both questions
were resolved in the preceding chapters, where circumcision and
uncircumcision were extensively discussed and compared. Second, Paul speaks
generally about works in this chapter. His doctrine would be deficient
unless he showed generally that justification is not by works. It would not
follow that righteousness is not by works but by faith if he merely argued
that it is not by circumcision.

The particle “therefore” thus expresses a proper inference, not a bare
transition. Or, in Piscator's words, “Paul passes to Abraham's example as a
corollary. From his already established doctrine of justification he infers
that Abraham—whom the Jews opposed to Paul's doctrine as though he had been
justified by works—obtained nothing according to the flesh.” It is therefore
both a conclusion from what precedes and a proposition for what follows.
Bucer rightly observes that once Paul had abundantly proved in the preceding
chapter that no one can be justified by works, it followed with the utmost
clarity that Abraham could not be either. The inference thus follows from a
proposition already proved and established.

At the same time, the inference anticipates the Jews' opinion about Abraham:
although others were not justified by works, certain especially holy men
such as Abraham, David, and others like them must be excepted from the
universal rule and justified by the abundant works they possessed. Paul
answers the contrary and shows that not even these holiest of men were
justified by works. He proves in the first five verses that Abraham was
justified by faith alone; in the following four, David locates blessedness,
and therefore justification, in the forgiveness of sins. So much for the
inference.

Second, Paul presents it as an interrogation: “What, then, shall we say that
Abraham found according to the flesh?” Luther takes the question as a Jewish
objection: “What shall we say that Abraham found?”—as though they asked, “Did
he merit absolutely nothing by his works?” Paul answers: absolutely nothing
from his works, not even from circumcision itself. He had been justified
before he was circumcised, though circumcision was a work God commanded and
a precious work of obedience. If the work of circumcision contributed
nothing to Abraham's righteousness, then it is more than certain that no
other work contributes to righteousness. Just as Abraham's circumcision was
an outward sign, a seal of righteousness, so good works are outward signs,
not things that justify a person.

Luther's conjecture does not fit, however. First, if the question were the
Jews' objection, it would be phrased differently: not, “What, then, shall we
say that Abraham found?” but, “What, then, shall we say Abraham did not
find?” Second, an interrogative objection would receive an answer. Suppose
the Jews asked, “Did Abraham not find righteousness by works?” Paul would
have to answer, “Not at all.” No such answer appears. Instead, the next words
continue immediately, “For if Abraham was justified by works, he has
something about which to boast,” which plainly proves what Paul had asserted
in the immediately preceding words.

[1679 printed p. 179 | IA leaf 187]

On Luther's conjecture, however, Paul's question contains no such assertion,
since Luther makes it the Jews' question; Paul would therefore prove
something he had not previously asserted. Manifestly, then, and by the
common judgment of the interpreters, it is Paul's own question. He is not
questioned by the Jews but questions them: “What did Abraham obtain from
works?” As will shortly appear, this is a stronger negation. We may therefore
take it as certain that this is Paul's question. Two matters remain to be
asked about it: whether its punctuation makes one simple question or two,
and what Paul intends by it.

As to the first, punctuation—which perhaps did not exist in Paul's
autograph—varies or can vary among manuscripts and interpreters.

First, punctuation may follow “therefore”: “What, then?” as in Romans 3:3,
“For what?” and Acts 21:22, “What, then, is it?” Here it would read: “What,
then? What must be said? Shall we say that Abraham found something according
to the flesh?” This gives two questions.

Second, it may follow “shall we say”: “What, then, shall we say?” Grotius and
Ludovicus de Dieu preferred this punctuation, which often occurs in this
epistle (Romans 3:5; 6:1; 8:31; 9:14). It would read: “What, then, shall we
say? That Abraham found something according to the flesh?” This also gives
two questions.

Third, the Arabic interpreter places it after “Abraham”: “What shall we say
about Abraham the patriarch? Shall we say he attained this by works of the
flesh?” This translation renders Paul's sense very well, though its
punctuation does not accord with the Greek text.

Fourth, the Syriac interpreter supplies another punctuation after “found.”
In De Dieu's rendering it reads: “What, then, shall we say about Abraham the
patriarch that he found? Was it in the flesh?” This likewise conveys the
true sense and produces two questions.

Fifth, manuscripts and interpreters commonly make only one question, place
its mark at the final word, and read the whole without a break: “What, then,
shall we say that Abraham our father found according to the flesh?” This
expresses Paul's thought clearly enough.

The second inquiry is what Paul intends by using a question here.

First, he more confidently denies that Abraham found righteousness according
to the flesh or from works. Negative questions by their nature and use
affirm more strongly, as in Romans 3:29: “Is he the God of the Jews only? Is
he not also of the Gentiles?” Paul immediately adds the affirmation implied
in the negative question: “Yes, of the Gentiles also.” Likewise, affirmative
questions deny more vehemently and confidently, as though the speaker
appointed his opponent to judge the matter's truth. Thus Romans 3:31 asks,
“Do we then nullify the law through faith?” and denies it with abhorrence:
“May it never be!”

[1679 printed p. 180 | IA leaf 188]

So here Paul's question, “What, then, shall we say that Abraham our father
found according to the flesh?” denies the point more openly than if he had
said, “Absolutely nothing.” Anselm reads it so. Bucer therefore rightly says
Paul speaks for clarity and confidence. Chrysostom likewise says that Paul
asks questions to which no answer follows because in truth no answer could
be given except a negative one. The sense is that Abraham found absolutely
nothing toward justification according to the flesh or from works. This is
the conclusion of what precedes, indicated by “therefore”; it is also the
proposition for what follows and the sum of this new argument. The elliptical
negative question contains its own answer, which Paul undertakes to prove in
the following verses: “For if Abraham was justified,” and so forth.

Second, others think Paul also intends something else. His purpose is not
only confidence but avoidance of Jewish offense through the rhetorical
figure of communication noted above. To gain goodwill, he addresses them and
includes himself with them: “What shall we say?” not “What will you say?” He
calls Abraham “our father,” common to them and himself, so that he might not
seem a degenerate son rejecting his father, but might attract them as
brothers to a harmonious acknowledgment of this truth. But let us proceed.

“What, then, shall we say that Abraham our father found, obtained, or
attained?” The Hebrew word can mean both “find” and “attain something in any
way,” since finding is one way of attaining a thing. So the very common
phrase “to find favor” in the Old Testament's Hebrew is rendered in Greek in
Luke 1:30 as “you have found favor.”

Beza notes that “finding” properly describes something discovered by chance,
the Greeks calling an unexpected gain a “find,” whereas the present verb, in
his judgment, describes what someone obtains by meriting it through his own
labors. He therefore disliked “found” and rendered it “obtained.” Ambrose
likewise explains “to find” here as “to merit.” Budaeus says the Greek verb
can mean to merit or to obtain something in return for what one has given,
done, or endured, which fits this passage very well. Yet Budaeus also
testifies that it can describe every kind of attainment, even things given
freely, and supplies examples from Demosthenes. Thus it makes no difference
whether with Erasmus and the Vulgate we retain “found,” with Beza use
“obtained,” or with others render it “attained” or “acquired,” although a way
of finding through merit fits the passage admirably. These points are
straightforward.

The final phrase of the clause, “according to the flesh,” contains greater
difficulty. Two questions may be asked: where the phrase should be placed or
how it should be construed, and—depending on the former—what it properly
means here.

[1679 printed p. 181 | IA leaf 189]

As to the first, interpreters offer two constructions. It can be joined to
“our father,” giving the sense, “What shall we say that Abraham, our father
according to the flesh, found?” This denotes their carnal descent from
Abraham. Or it can be joined to “found,” as commonly read: “What shall we say
that Abraham our father found according to the flesh?”

The Vulgate adopts the former construction, followed by Origen, Chrysostom,
Theophylact, Erasmus, and, among our writers, Bucer. Bucer says, “Both the
sense and expression are so Pauline that I would not readily dare declare
one more genuine than the other, except that the former seems clearer and
brings a fuller consideration of the example.” Against the objection that
this reading introduces a hyperbaton, he answers, “Hyperbaton is not so
foreign to Paul. The sense is: The Jews boast about our father Abraham, from
whom we trace our descent according to the flesh, and I do not disdain that
glory. Let us therefore see what this father of ours according to the flesh
obtained. Thus Paul beats down the arrogance of the Jews, who were always
talking of Abraham as their father, yet were his children only according to
the flesh, whereas believers have obtained him as father by a far more
excellent kinship.”

Peter Martyr similarly says that Paul “rebukes the Jews for boasting only in
nobility of flesh and not in piety, while restricting their sonship to a
merely carnal relation, as also in Romans 9:3.”

Although Calvin does not reject the other reading, he finds it probable that
“according to the flesh” is joined adjectivally to “father.” He writes:
“Besides the fact that domestic examples usually affect us more, the dignity
of their lineage, in which the Jews boasted, is expressly marked again. Some
think this was added contemptuously, in the sense in which Abraham's children
who are not spiritual or altogether legitimate are elsewhere called carnal.
I instead think that the Jews' special status was expressed because being
Abraham's children by nature and fleshly descent was more excellent than
being so by adoption alone, provided faith was joined to it. Paul therefore
grants the Jews a closer bond, but only to urge them not to depart from their
father's example.” So Calvin.

Pareus readily embraces this transposition against the Roman sophists, “lest
they quibble that justification is removed only from those works of Abraham
which he performed according to the flesh—that is, before conversion and
without faith.”

Other passages, especially in Paul's epistles, favor this construction:
Romans 1:3, Christ “from David's seed according to the flesh”; Romans 9:3,
“my kindred according to the flesh”; verse 5, Christ “according to the
flesh”; and 1 Corinthians 10:18, “Consider Israel according to the flesh.” In
all these passages “according to the flesh” denotes carnal or natural,
external generation or origin. Why should it not do so here?

Other interpreters follow and defend the second construction. The Syriac
renders it, “What did he find or obtain in the flesh?” Or De Dieu's fuller
version reads, “What shall we say that Abraham the patriarch found? Was it
in the flesh?” The Arabic likewise asks, “Shall we say that he attained this
through works of the flesh?”

[1679 printed p. 182 | IA leaf 190]

Ambrosiaster, Aquinas, Cajetan, and among our writers Beza, Piscator, and
others join “according to the flesh” not to “our father” but to “found.” Four
considerations favor this reading.

First, all Greek manuscripts join the words not with “our father” but with
“found.”

Second, the alternative produces an ambiguity, hyperbaton, or transposition.
Although Paul does not always avoid these in his writings, as Bucer says,
they are defects of speech in themselves and should not be invoked unless
the sense and texture of the passage demand them. Neither requires such a
transposition here.

Third, the sense of the passage and Paul's thought favor the contrary. Paul
does not ask simply what Abraham found. Both Paul and the Jews knew that, as
Abraham is praised as righteous, made the father of all believers, and
appointed heir of the world by God, he had found righteousness even before
God. Paul asks about the way and mode of attaining it, as though he said,
“Come now, by what means? By what merit of his own? By faith or works?”
Certainly by faith, not works. Thus “according to the flesh” asks about the
way Abraham found righteousness: whether by works and carnal circumcision,
along with his other works dependent upon it. The question includes a denial
of this, which Paul proves in the next verse.

Fourth, throughout the rest of the chapter Paul answers the question posed
in this first clause by proving that Abraham was justified neither by
circumcision nor by other works. Some words in the question must therefore
indicate what is being asked. Only “according to the flesh” does so. The
phrase should consequently be referred not to “our father” but to “found”:
not “our father according to the flesh” but “our father found according to
the flesh.” Thus the question and its ensuing answer and proof agree.

The arguments advanced for the former construction are not compelling.

First, Bucer's assertion that it is clearer is denied. It is not clearer in
word order, because it introduces a hyperbaton from which the latter
construction is free, nor in sense. “What shall we say Abraham found
according to the flesh?” is sufficiently clear and fits perfectly the proof
that follows throughout the chapter.

Second, although the former construction is frequent in Paul, as the cited
examples show, the latter is not unusual. In Galatians, Hebrews, and
elsewhere, Paul repeatedly uses “in the flesh” and “carnal things” for
circumcision in the flesh and all other external works of law, as Bucer
himself admits.

Third, it is said that the former construction gives fuller consideration to
Abraham's example. It does not.

[1679 printed p. 183 | IA leaf 191]

If it contains no more than carnal or natural descent from Abraham, the
preceding words “our father” already express all of that, so there was no
need to mark it again, as Calvin claims. Although domestic examples usually
affect us more, calling him “our father” already made this a sufficiently
domestic example.

Fourth, it is said that joining “according to the flesh” to “our father”
more effectively beats down the Jews' boasting in Abraham. It restricts
their privilege to mere carnal descent, implying that although they are
Abraham's children, they are only carnal and according to the flesh, while
believing Gentiles possess that sonship by a better title, according to the
Spirit.

I answer that this could indeed restrain Jewish boasting, and Paul actually
does restrain it in Romans 9 and in verses 9 and following of this very
chapter. But in the chapter's first part he pursues this epistle's principal
doctrine, proving that Abraham was justified by faith and not works of law.
To indicate this he joined “according to the flesh” with “found” and asks
what Abraham obtained according to the flesh. He did not join it with “the
father,” as he does later when restraining their arrogance over fleshly
descent from Abraham. Had that been his intention here, he would have said
“your father” rather than “our father,” as Beza rightly notes.

Even the Jews themselves, though they were more than sufficiently proud of
being descended from Abraham and flattered themselves that they would always
remain God's unique people however they lived, were not so senseless as to
believe that no Jew, however wicked, could fall from that privilege and
God's grace.

Fifth and finally, Pareus suggests that the former reading should be embraced
lest joining “according to the flesh” with “found” give Roman Catholics
occasion to object that Paul discusses only works done according to the
flesh without faith before conversion, excluding only these from
justification.

I answer that we should not leave the straight road to evade an ambush of
robbers which anyone can invent without just cause. Nor should we depart
from a passage's true sense to anticipate Roman objections, which will never
be lacking even in the plainest matters. Their present claim belongs among
such objections. We have already sufficiently demonstrated that Paul here
excludes from justification all works of Abraham and everyone else,
including works performed from faith after conversion.

Let the order of the words therefore stand as found in the text: “What,
then, shall we say that Abraham our father found according to the flesh?”
This was the first inquiry.

The second concerned their meaning: what does it mean that Abraham found
something “according to the flesh”? The phrase's sense varies according to
Scripture's varied use of “flesh.” Properly and primarily it means the
external and natural part of the body. From this it can mean natural
generation; the corruption of nature, which has its seat in the flesh or
uses it as an instrument; the outward person; or things done by or in any
way associated with the outward person.

[1679 printed p. 184 | IA leaf 192]

Thus “according to the flesh” often refers to carnal or natural generation,
as in Romans 1:3; 9:3, 5, and elsewhere. Sometimes it refers to the
corruption of nature, as when people are said to exist and walk according to
the flesh in Romans 8:1, 4, 5 and 2 Corinthians 10:2–3. Sometimes it refers
to the outward person, as in 2 Corinthians 5:16: we now know no one, not even
Christ himself, according to the flesh. At other times it refers to outward
privileges, works, and similar things, the sense in which “flesh” appears in
Galatians 3:3.

Ambrose and others consequently understand “according to the flesh” as
“according to circumcision performed in the flesh.” Paul proves in this
chapter that Abraham neither sought nor found anything toward justification
through it, because he was justified before he was circumcised (verses
10–11). In this sense not only circumcision but sacrifices and other duties
are called carnal in Hebrews 9:10 and 7:16. I do not deny that this meaning
of flesh is included here, because circumcision formed part of the question
in Romans 3:1, which is answered in this chapter's verses 9–11 and following.
It must only be granted that Paul does not speak solely of circumcision and
the ceremonial law as though removing justification from ceremonies alone.
Some Roman writers contend for that restriction; their later and more
sensible writers include works of the moral law, and even works proceeding
from faith. They deny the latter point, but we maintain it and have already
sufficiently established it.

“According to the flesh” therefore means according to works, as Paul plainly
shows in verse 2, where he proves that Abraham did not find justification
according to the flesh because he was not justified by works. “According to
the flesh” and “from outward works and merits” amount to the same thing here.
They are called flesh because they fall under carnal eyes, are approved by
carnal or natural judgment, and are opposed to Christ's spiritual
righteousness. Just as verse 4 opposes “according to grace” to “according to
debt,” so “according to the flesh” and “according to debt” are equivalent.

The sense of the question and of the whole clause is consequently this: Come
then, in light of what has already been said, what shall we say that Abraham,
that most holy man whom we acknowledge as our father and as it were the head
of the covenant, obtained from circumcision and every external thing—indeed,
from his works, virtues, and holiness? Absolutely nothing toward
justification. The question implies this in the present clause and the
following verses prove it.

Only a few corollaries remain from this clause and Paul's example. They are
especially useful to pastors and teachers in the church both for instructing
the people and convincing opponents.

First, they should use examples in teaching and argument. Examples are
especially useful in theological instruction and disputation, just as in
learning other sciences and arts. The path through examples is short and
direct: they place a matter before the eyes and summon not reason alone but
the very senses as witnesses. The apostles, Christ, and Paul here lead us in
this method of teaching and argument. Having already established gracious
justification by argument, Paul completes his treatment here through
Abraham's example.

[1679 printed p. 185 | IA leaf 193]

If we follow Paul as our guide, this should be done with examples drawn above
all, where possible, from sacred Scripture. Paul does this here. Scripture
supplies the richest store of weighty examples of every kind beyond all
other writings, since all God's ways in administering affairs are mercy—in
freely bestowing good things—and truth—in fulfilling promises and the other
things recorded in his word. Moreover, by divine appointment and blessing
Scriptural examples possess greater efficacy, as Paul testifies: “Whatever
things were written beforehand were written for our instruction, that
through perseverance and the encouragement of the Scriptures we might have
hope” (Romans 15:4).

Care should meanwhile be taken that examples are, so far as possible,
distinguished, clear, and especially suited to the matter at hand. All these
qualities shine brightly in the example of Abraham presented here.

Second, Paul uses a gentle communication in “What shall we say? Abraham our
father,” making the Jews against whom he argues better disposed toward him.
This can teach pastors and church doctors that, although they must not court
popular applause through ambition and vainglory, they should prudently seek
their hearers' goodwill. People can thus be more readily attracted and moved
both to acknowledge the truth and to practice every good work (2 Timothy
2:25–26).

Religion and faith, as Lactantius says, are naturally persuaded, not
commanded. Human understanding likewise desires to be led gently, not
dragged by a twisted neck. Indeed, understanding and will are so closely
related that understanding usually follows the will. Excessive passions in
the will blind us, whereas moderate ones make us more attentive and
teachable. Excessive contention causes truth to be lost and unheard amid
the din and shouting of quarrels; alienated affections widen divisions not
only between judgments but also between minds. “Human anger does not produce
God's righteousness” (James 1:20), and “where jealousy and contention exist,
there are disorder and every evil work” (James 3:16).

I therefore reject the syncretism of certain people and Grotius's apology
for peace, which is almost wholly devoted to courting Rome. I reject those
who make a wasteland while crying peace and desire a truce so that they may
plot against truth more safely. Nevertheless, union among those who differ,
especially in this most turbulent age, should be voluntarily embraced with
both arms by every sensible person, provided it costs no truth. To pursue it
more successfully and weld together minds as well as judgments, quarrels and
shouting should be banished. Sober and godly people should conduct
conferences and joint deliberations rather than controversies, like the
apostles' meeting in Acts
15, joining their efforts to investigate truth rather than sow disputes.
They should remember Paul's communication, no less Christian than
rhetorical: “What shall we say?” He gently summons even opponents to a
common council to uncover truth. By saying “our father,” since they are
brothers through him, Paul—like father Abraham long ago—bars the way to
mutual contentions (Genesis 13:8).
