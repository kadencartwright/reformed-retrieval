# Refutation of Arguments Proposed in Favor of the Worship of Images

This is a fresh English translation of the complete French text at CO X/1
193–197. The document is Calvin's response to a series of arguments or
objections and addresses an unnamed “Madam.” Biblical and patristic language
is translated from the printed French and Latin phrases. No wording from Beaty
and Farley's modern translation supplied the English.

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 193]

Madam, since it pleases you to give me a hearing, I shall briefly set out the
arguments advanced by our opponents. It was said that the decision of this
difficulty depended on the interpretation of the second commandment. That is
true, for it is also the sole and unique foundation of our dispute. We must
therefore consider whether our interpretation conforms to the truth [or
whether it must be understood that we can approve images].

We were admonished not to take negative commandments according to the letter,
because Saint Paul says: the letter kills, and the Spirit gives life. I answer
that this is alleged very much out of place. In the apostle's passage “the
letter” signifies the whole law, and the practice of this commandment among
the Jews shows that it must be taken simply according to the letter and as it
is written. It is not without reason that God made so brief a summary of his
commandments, so that the people might learn them. Nor is it credible that God
wished to envelop the truth, which is also sufficiently clear in his
commandments.

Against us this commandment was alleged: You shall not kill [to show that the
commandments must not be understood without exception, seeing that a
magistrate is permitted to kill, and even private persons such as Phinehas]. I
answer that when an exception must be given to a commandment, it belongs to
the lawgiver who made it; it is not lawful for human beings to do so.

Against this, one could allege the abuse of the

### [CO X/1, p. 194]

Manichaeans, who said that animals must not be eaten. To apply this argument,
however, one would have to produce an express passage commending the making
and worshiping of images, for there are enough other passages approving the
executions carried out by the prince and the administration of justice to
preserve the people's peace. As for the Sabbath, I suppose it was meant that
this commandment is ceremonial like the fourth. But I hold it to be purely
moral.

Concerning the cherubim, I shall answer from Tertullian's treatise *On
Idolatry*, where he speaks to certain Christian craftsmen who had no
difficulty making images for pagans: *seorsum figurae quae disp.* That is: the
things ordained for some secret dispensation must be set apart. If someone
wishes to say that this serves against us, what follows must be taken too:
*Ne facias adversus legem simulacrum aliquod nisi deus iusserit*—Do not make
any likeness contrary to the law unless God has commanded it.

It was said that the commandment may be understood by considering what comes
before or after it. First it is said: You shall not make foreign gods for
yourself. At the end: You shall not worship them.

One must press the question whether the commandment is violated by the service
and honor rendered to images when one attributes to them what is rendered to
the one God.

Now sacrifice, the consecration of temples and altars, and incense are owed to
God. Therefore, if you attribute them to images of the saints, you make them
foreign gods. Having considered everything, I find nothing the Gentiles
attributed to idols that is not attributed to images. It was said that the
ancients thought images were alive. I suppose some crude people said this, but
most did not. I shall cite Saint Augustine on Psalm 113, which contains the
same excuses people advance today, and shows by

### [CO X/1, p. 195]

that passage that there were no images in the church and that they were
content to have vessels. Lactantius says in *On False Religion*: Why do you
not instead address yourself to the one who is in heaven? Arnobius says the
same. Sacrifice is owed to God. Augustine, *Against Faustus*, book 20.
Incensing is one part, and it was something unheard of during Tertullian's
life, for he says: *Thura non emimus*—We do not buy incense. He says the same
about the crowns now given, in *On the Soldier's Crown*.

As for prostration, I said that there is a reverence owed to God which I call
religious, and another owed to human beings which is civil. Indeed, when Saint
Augustine speaks of *dulia*, he says there are two kinds of service: one owed
to God and the other, civil, owed to princes. Why, then, should a civil bow be
made to images? There is no order or reason in it.

I say that there are things sung in church which are repugnant to God, as in
this statement about the Virgin's image: *Non omnibus es omnia*—You are not all
things to all. There is another sequence in which it says: *Iure matris
impera*—Command by a mother's right. And I ask whether that is not properly a
kind of impiety and idolatry.

The pagans attributed knowledge of things to come to idols. The same is done
with Saint Anthony of Padua, Our Lady of Good News, and others.

It was said concerning the law of nature that it does not forbid likenesses.
But Saint Paul says that these people changed the nature of the incorruptible
God into a corruptible thing. As for Bethel, no likeness was erected there,
but only a testimony to having seen the vision of God there.

Concerning worship, it was said that internal, not external, worship had to be
understood. I say further that Origen, on Exodus 20, homily 8, *utrumque
resecat*—cuts off both. The same author on the first chapter of Romans, book
one. From this I conclude that by the proper sense of the word, “to worship”
must be taken in its own meaning, and that all honor, both internal and
external, is forbidden to images.

It was said that no one is so crude that he could worship images, and that one
does not say: Image, I pray to you. The answer is that the image has been given
the name of the thing signified, by which it is named and called; yet people
do not cease to address the image. And I believe that the people, infatuated
with certain images, attach the saint to the image.

As for the cross, one will find the idolatry attached to it through the
consecration customarily performed in church upon the wood of the said cross,
which is called our hope. I acknowledge, moreover, that the sign of the cross
is very ancient in the church, and I distinguish between images and this sign,

### [CO X/1, p. 196]

indeed the wooden cross. I believe its first use was good, but it deteriorated
over the course of time. This sign was not practiced until Constantine, who
falls between the third and fourth hundred years; even then no worship was
performed. Helena found it and sent it to Constantine, who placed it in the
forum. Concerning the nails it is said that she cast one of them into the sea,
made another into a bit for Constantine's horse, and that another was put into
Constantine's diadem; she was praised for this. In this matter we can do no
other than what God's Word shows us concerning the bronze serpent, which
Hezekiah shattered when he saw the people idolizing it.

Concerning Theodoret's *On Sacrifices*, I answer that the sacrifices ordained
by God began before those of the Gentiles. But the devil, as an ape and
imitator of God's works, wished to counterfeit God's legitimate sacrifices.

From Acts 10 and Revelation 21 I had proved that Saint Peter and the angel had
not approved the honor. Some said that Cornelius still adhered to paganism,
but on the contrary he is called religious, and Saint Peter went to him to
show him the true Messiah. Cornelius is not reproved by Saint Peter merely on
account of humility, but because he needed to be reproved and had exceeded
proper measure. God's servants have never received in Scripture the honors
belonging to princes. Saint Augustine says the same in *Against Faustus*, book
20, chapter 21. The angel likewise says: *Conservus tuus sum, adora deum*—I am
your fellow servant; worship God. I therefore conclude that if the prototype
does not bear the honor, still less should it be rendered to images and to the
type.

As for martyrs buried beneath altars, Saint Augustine says in the place
already cited: *Colimus martyres cultu dilectionis et societatis*—We honor the
martyrs with the observance of love and fellowship. In *On True Religion*,
chapter 16, where he speaks to Christians rather than Gentiles, he says that
the saints are to be honored for imitation, not worshiped for religion. And
of the angels he says: *Honoramus eos caritate non servitute*—We honor them
with love, not service—which is the *dulia* that people have wished to
separate from *latria*. Saint Augustine says in *On the Morals of the Catholic
Church* that in his time there were worshipers of images and paintings whom
he greatly blamed.

As for Saint Basil, who says various things about the monastic life, I say
that I cannot think Basil contradicted himself. Saint Paul and Saint James are
alleged concerning faith and works. That will be answered in due time and
place.

There is at present the same ignorant idolatry that there was anciently. The
Council of Nicaea cannot be accepted without an error of faith. The said
council was assembled without the churches of Gaul and Germany. At the
Council of Frankfurt the pope's ambassadors

### [CO X/1, p. 197]

were present. We maintain that images are to be removed. Charlemagne forbade
their worship but did not remove the images, which caused us to fall back into
idolatry. We do not condemn councils, for we approve the first four.

As for our confession, which is said to have been made by the ministers alone,
each person gave his opinion and the whole was settled by the testimony of
Holy Scripture. It will also be found that those said to be separated from
the Roman church, who are in the scattered churches, have the same doctrine.

As for the council to which the matter of images is deferred, I can hope for
no council from which fruit may be obtained unless it is held in a place where
the ecclesiastics do not have coercive power; unless security is given to us,
notwithstanding the ordinance of the Council of Constance; unless it is not
an appendage of the Council of Trent; and unless, finally, proceedings are
conducted by the authority of Scripture and without reliance on prescription.
We do not despise the church, nor have we separated ourselves from it. If
someone asks where it has been since the introduction of images, I also ask
where it was in Elijah's time. I think that ours has been in the hand of bad
husbandmen.

## Translator's notes

1. CO's base witness is an old copy in Gotha MS 404, fol. 48 and following.
   Its subscription says “To be sent to M. de Bèze,” and its heading says “6
   February.” Neither supplies a year; CO's “(1562?)” is an editorial
   conjecture, not a date in the body.
2. The words in square brackets in the first paragraph were erased in the
   manuscript, according to CO. The bracketed Phinehas clause is a marginal
   addition. Both remain legible historical layers and are retained in square
   brackets rather than silently promoted into or discarded from the main
   state.
3. The body abbreviates Tertullian's first phrase as *seorsum figurae quae
   disp.* CO expands it in an editorial footnote. The transcription preserves
   the body's abbreviated form; the translation conveys the sense of the
   expansion but does not insert the rest into the French witness.
4. CO prints *prestation* on p. 195 and tentatively suggests *prosternation* in
   a footnote. “Prostration” follows the proposed sense because the paragraph
   immediately contrasts religious and civil reverence and then asks about a
   bodily inclination; the printed form remains unchanged in `french.txt`.
5. After “Council of Nicaea,” CO's note records *second* as an addition in
   another hand. The English does not silently incorporate that later hand,
   although the historical reference is evidently to the image controversy
   associated with the Second Council of Nicaea.
6. The source contains compressed citations and several unattached clauses.
   The translation preserves their sequence rather than constructing a new
   critical apparatus or silently correcting the argument's historical
   claims. The body gives no place, year, signature, or formal closing, and the
   identity of “Madam” is not supplied.
7. The unusual *par aucteur de lescripture* is translated contextually as
   “by the authority of Scripture.” The printed *aucteur* is retained in the
   French rather than silently expanded to *authorité*.
