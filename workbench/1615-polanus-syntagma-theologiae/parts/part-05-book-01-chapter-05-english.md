CHAPTER FIVE

On the Highest Good.

To the philosophers, the highest good and blessedness are one and the same,
but theology distinguishes these two. According to theology, therefore, the
HIGHEST GOOD of rational creatures—and consequently of man—is God alone, as
the first principle and final end of every good.

[1615 printed locator I]

The *highest good* is spoken of comparatively in relation to things called
good equivocally. The highest good, however, is the same as the one unique
good which is good univocally; God alone is that good.

This is confirmed both by testimonies of Holy Scripture and by other
arguments drawn from Holy Scripture.

[1615 printed locator K]

These are the testimonies. In Genesis 15:1 God himself says of himself to
Abraham: “Do not fear, Abram; I am your shield and your very great reward.”
Psalm 16:5: “Jehovah is the portion of my inheritance and my cup.” Psalm
33:12: “Blessed is the nation whose God is Jehovah.” Psalm 40:5: “Blessed is
the man who makes Jehovah his trust.” In Psalm 73:25 the prophet speaks to
God: “Whom have I in heaven besides you? Besides you I delight in no one on
earth.” Psalm 142:6: “I cry to you, Jehovah, saying: You are my refuge, my
portion in the land of the living.” And Psalm 144:15 says: “Blessed is the
people whose God is Jehovah.”

The other arguments drawn from Holy Scripture are these.

I. He who is our salvation, glory, strength, shield, and indeed everything
necessary for our blessedness, is our highest good.

God alone is our salvation, glory, strength, shield, and indeed everything
necessary for our blessedness.

Therefore God alone is our highest good.

Many passages of Scripture prove the minor premise, among which are these.
Psalm 27:1: “Jehovah is my light and my salvation; whom should I fear? Jehovah
is the strength of my life; before whom should I tremble?” Psalm 28:7:
“Jehovah is my strength and my shield.” Psalm 62:3, 7: “He alone is my rock
and my salvation.” Psalm 3:4: “You, Jehovah, are a shield around me, my glory,
and the one who lifts up my head.” Psalm 18:2–3: “From my innermost parts I
will love you, Jehovah, my strength and my fortress, my mighty God, my rock to
which I flee, my shield and the horn of my salvation, my high stronghold.”
Colossians 3:11: “Christ is all and in all.” First Corinthians 15:28: “God will
be all in all”—that is, he alone, without any mediator or medium, will forever
be the immediate cause and author of all our good and all our felicity, and
the immediate object in which our felicity will consist.

II. Whatever makes us truly blessed is our highest good. For the highest good
is the true cause of our blessedness and makes us free from every evil and
every misery.

But God alone makes us truly blessed. No one makes a man blessed except the
one who made him. For he who generously gives so many great goods of his
creation to good and evil alike—that they may exist, may be human, may thrive
in their senses, be strong in power, and abound in wealth—gives himself to the
good so that they may be blessed, since it is also his gift that they are
good. By his own goodness he restores goodness in us who had become evil
through sin, and he takes away the sin under which we were burdened. Thus
Psalm 32:1–2 says: “Blessed is he whose rebellion is lifted away, whose sin is
covered. Blessed is the man to whom Jehovah does not impute iniquity and in
whose spirit there is no deceit.” Psalm 36:10: “With you is the fountain of
life, and in your light we shall see light.” See Psalm 94:12–14 and 1
Corinthians 11:32: “When we are judged, we are instructed by the Lord, lest we
be condemned with the world.”

III. Whatever makes the soul tranquil and at peace and frees it from every
vicious affection is our highest good. For that man is happy whose tranquil,
peaceful soul neither grieves nor fears, neither anxiously desires nor hopes
in uncertainties, but rests in holy and godly joy and in hope, which it holds
like a safe and firm anchor of the soul.

God alone does this, as David professes in Psalm 23:3–4: “He restores my soul;
he leads me in the paths of righteousness for his name’s sake. Even if I
walked through the valley of death’s shadow, I would fear no evil, because you
are with me.” See Psalm 27:1–3. Certainly God alone can make a man’s soul
tranquil and peaceful both in life and in death.

IV. That in which alone our perfect and solid consolation against every evil
is found is our highest good.

But in God alone is our perfect and solid consolation against every evil,
James 5:11. Evil is the privation of a good required for the perfection of any
creature. Therefore, and so forth.

V. He whose knowledge and trust truly make us blessed and good is certainly
our highest good.

But the knowledge of and trust in God alone truly make us blessed and good,
as these testimonies of Holy Scripture make plain. Proverbs 3:13: “Blessed is
the man who obtains wisdom and the man who advances understanding.” Solomon
speaks of saving wisdom and understanding, of which 2 Timothy 3:14 says: “From
childhood you have known the sacred writings, which can make you wise for
salvation.” John 17:3: “This is eternal life: that they know you, the only true
God, and Jesus Christ, whom you sent.” Isaiah 53:11: “By knowledge of himself
my righteous servant will justify many, whose iniquities he will bear.”
Second Peter 1:2–4: “May grace and peace be multiplied to you in the knowledge
of God and of Jesus our Lord, since his divine power has given us everything
that pertains to life and godliness through knowledge of him who called us to
glory and virtue. By this he gave us those exceedingly great and precious
promises, so that through them you might become partakers of the divine
nature, having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust.” And
verse 8: “For if these things are present and abound in you, they will render
you neither idle nor unfruitful in the knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ.”
Psalm 2:12: “Blessed are all who trust in him.” Jeremiah 17:7: “Blessed is the
man who trusts in Jehovah and whose confidence is Jehovah.” Psalm 146:5:
“Blessed is he whose help is the mighty God of Jacob, whose hope is in Jehovah
his God.”

VI. He whose fear and love prove us to be truly blessed is certainly our
highest good.

But the fear and love of God alone prove us to be truly blessed. Psalm 112:1:
“Blessed is everyone who fears Jehovah and greatly delights in his
commandments.” Psalm 1:1–2: “Blessed is the man who does not walk in the
counsel of the wicked, does not stand in the path of sinners, and does not sit
in the assembly of scoffers, but whose delight is in the law of Jehovah and
who meditates on his law day and night.” Psalm 119:1–3: “Blessed are those
whose way is blameless, who walk in the law of Jehovah. Blessed are those who
keep his testimonies and seek him with the whole heart. They also do no
iniquity.” Isaiah 56:2: “Blessed is the mortal who does righteousness and the
son of man who takes hold of it, keeping the Sabbath so as not to profane it
and keeping his hand from doing any evil.” Matthew 5:3 and several verses
following:

[1615 BSB witness | digital canvas 28 | printed p. 4]

[1615 printed locator A]

“Blessed are the poor in spirit, those who mourn, the meek, those who hunger
and thirst for righteousness, the merciful, the pure in heart, the
peacemakers, and those who suffer persecution for righteousness and for
Christ’s sake.” Proverbs 8:34: “Blessed is the man who listens to me, watching
diligently at my doors day by day.” And 28:14: “Blessed is the man who is
continually very fearful, but he who hardens his heart will fall into evil.”
Ecclesiastes 12:15: “Fear God himself and keep his commandments, for this is
the whole of man.” James 5:11: “Behold, we call those blessed who endure
adversity.” Revelation 16:15: “Behold, I come as a thief. Blessed is he who
watches and keeps his garments, lest he walk naked and his shame be seen.”

[1615 printed locator B]

Many such statements occur everywhere in the Scriptures of both Testaments.
These also belong to that class: Proverbs 8:17: “I love those who love me.”
John 13:17: “If you know these things, you will be blessed if you do them.”
John 14:23: “If anyone loves me, he will keep my word, and my Father will love
him, and we will come to him and make our dwelling with him.” The fear and
love of God alone, I say, prove us blessed—that is, show and declare it—not as
a cause but as a sure and infallible sign. Godliness is the way to
blessedness, not the cause of blessedness. Thus Bernard says that good works
are the way to the kingdom, not the cause of reigning.

[1615 printed locator C]

For the free remission of sins makes us blessed, as Paul teaches in Romans
4:7–8 and Psalm 32: “Blessed are those whose iniquities are forgiven and whose
sins are covered. Blessed is the man to whom the Lord will not impute sin.”
God forgives our sins not because we feared and loved him, but so that we may
fear and love him, as Psalm 130:3–4 says: “If you should mark iniquities, Jah,
Lord, who could stand? But with you there is forgiveness, so that you may be
feared”—that is, worshiped reverently. Luke 7:47: “Her many sins are forgiven,
for she loved much; but he to whom little is forgiven loves little.” Psalm
18:2–3: “From my innermost parts I will love you, Jehovah, my strength;
Jehovah, my rock, my fortress, and my deliverer; my mighty God, my rock to
which I flee, my shield and the horn of my salvation, my high stronghold.”

[1615 printed locator D]

VII. What is absolutely perfect, entirely sufficient, and supremely desirable
is our highest good. For these are the three conditions of the highest good,
which even the Gentile philosophers acknowledge. It must be absolutely
perfect, for if the highest good lacked anything it certainly would not be
highest. It is entirely sufficient, since he to whom it belongs lacks
nothing. It is supremely desirable, because whatever ought to be desired is
in it, from it, and for its sake; all other goods are referred to it.

[1615 printed locator E]

But God alone is absolutely perfect, entirely sufficient, and supremely
desirable. Even the Gentiles confess this.

VIII. The first idea of the good is the highest good.

The *first idea of the good* means the one ineffable principle of all things,
from the one all things and to the one all things; the universal good, not in
predication but in efficient causation—that is, the exemplar and cause of all
created goods, from whose goodness all other goods are derived; beauty
itself; the first beauty; the sea of beauty; τὸ πρῶτον φίλον, the first
beloved; αὐτόφιλον, the beloved itself; sincere and pure.

[1615 printed locator F]

But God alone is the first idea of the good. For the ideas of all things—that
is, eternal exemplar forms—are in the divine mind, as Plato also held. His
opinion was interpreted by Augustine in volume 4, book *Eighty-three
Questions*, question 46; Clement of Alexandria in book 4 of the *Stromata*,
p. 230, and book 5, pp. 236 and 249; Theodoret in the second discourse of *The
Cure of Greek Maladies*, on the principle, p. 36, and the fourth discourse,
on matter and the world, p. 64 of the 1592 Commelin edition; Tertullian in *On
the Soul*, chapter 18; Eusebius in book 15, chapter 44 of the *Preparation for
the Gospel*; and Lucius Annaeus Seneca in Letter 59: “Hear what an idea is,
that is, what it seems to Plato to be: an idea is the eternal exemplar of
things which come into being naturally.”

[1615 printed locator G]

For by *ideas* Plato did not understand some kinds of forms flying about in
the air, as is falsely attributed to him, but, as has now been said, exemplar
forms: incorporeal, invisible, eternal, and immutable; the truths of all
things that exist, always existing in the divine mind, always remaining in
the same condition. They themselves were not formed, but all things were
created by God according to them, and by participation in them whatever
exists comes to exist in whatever manner it exists. For God did not look upon
anything set outside himself in order to fashion according to it what he was
fashioning; to suppose this is sacrilege.

[1615 printed locator H]

*Idea* and εἶδος differ. You ask what the difference is? One is the exemplar;
the other is the form taken from the exemplar and imposed upon the work. The
craftsman imitates one and makes the other. A statue has a certain appearance:
this is its εἶδος. The exemplar itself has a certain appearance; looking upon
it, the artisan shaped the statue: this is the idea. Do you still desire
another distinction? The εἶδος is in the work. The idea is outside the work,
and it is not only outside the work but before the work. Lucius Annaeus Seneca,
Letter 59.

[1615 printed locator I]

The orthodox Fathers subscribe to this and testify that God is our highest
good. AUGUSTINE says in volume 1, *Retractions*, book 1, chapter 1: “Without
controversy, the original region, as it were, of the soul’s blessedness is God
himself.” And a little later: “In the third book, if you ask what seems right
to me, I say that I think the highest good of man is in the mind. I would have
spoken more truly: in God. For the mind enjoys him so as to be blessed in him
as its highest good.” See volume 7, book 2 of *On the Merits and Forgiveness
of Sins*, chapter 17.

[1615 printed locator K]

FULGENTIUS, book 1 of *On Predestination to Monimus*, p. 36 of the Basel
Henricpetri edition: “The highest good—which was not created and from which
every mutable created good comes—would rightly rule over the created good;
and by participation in it the created good would be blessed, if it served
the highest good with humble love.”

From these things it is evident that God alone is our highest good.

Therefore, to remove some false opinions about the highest good, no worldly
thing is man’s highest good so as to make him blessed. This is both because
every such thing is subject to vanity, changeable, unstable, fleeting, and
perishable, and because none of them makes the mind tranquil but instead
disturbs it, as Solomon’s book called Ecclesiastes teaches in chapters 1 and 2
and at 12:10: “Vanity of vanities; all these things are vanity.” Thus the
faithful man reasons:

Whatever is vain and does not make the soul tranquil but only disturbs it is
not our highest good.

But all worldly things are vain and do not make the soul tranquil but only
disturb it.

The following show this to be so.

I. Every man’s occupation and fruitless toil in the affairs of this world,
through which he can obtain nothing that remains durable either in itself or
in the man himself and supplies true rest and tranquility. Ecclesiastes 1:3:
“What profit does a man have from all his labor at which he labors under the
sun?”

II. The destruction of whole families and nations. The earth itself reproves
their momentary condition, since it remains continually like a theater in
which the same comedy or tragedy is always acted with only the persons
changed. Ecclesiastes 1:4: “One generation passes away and another comes,
although the earth remains forever.”

III. The changeable nature of created things, undergoing innumerable changes
every day: the sun, the air or wind, the rivers, and finally all the things in
whose knowledge or use man labors and wearies himself. Ecclesiastes 1:5–11:
“The sun rises and the sun sets, and hastens again to its place where it
rises. The air hastens toward the south and circles toward the north;
circling about in its course, the air hastens and returns according to its
circuits. All rivers hasten to the sea, yet the sea is not filled; wherever
the rivers hasten, from there they return quickly. All these things are
wearied; no one could express it. The eye is not satisfied by seeing, nor the
ear filled by hearing. What has been is what will be, and what is done is what
will be done; there is nothing new under the sun. Is there anything of which
it is said, ‘Look, this is new’? It already existed in ages before us. There
is no memory of former things, nor will there be any memory among those who
come later of the things to come.” Romans 8:20: “This created world was made
subject to vanity, not willingly, but because of him who subjected it to that
vanity.” First Corinthians 7:31: “Those who use this world should be as though
not abusing it, for the form of this world is passing away.” First John 2:17:
“The world is passing away, and its lust, but he who does God’s will remains
forever.”

IV. The changeability of times and of all things which are done and occur at
the definite times appointed by God. They continually flow, turn, and turn
again in a perpetual cycle, so that no action or event subject to time’s
instability endures forever. All things happen and occur in their turns, and
so all things readily change and alternate. Ecclesiastes proves this by an
induction of examples in the first eight verses of chapter 3, so that we may
understand that no profit from these actions and events, however earnestly
anyone strives, can come to a person and remain. Consequently all worldly
things are vain and do not make man’s soul tranquil but only disturb it.

It necessarily follows that no worldly thing is man’s highest good and none
of them makes a man blessed.

Therefore HUMAN WISDOM—consisting in the knowledge of natural things and of
things done in the world, handed down by men through the human spirit in moral
philosophy and other subjects—is not man’s highest good.

I. It is laborious and troublesome. It is compared with the evil trouble God
inflicted on men so that they should afflict themselves, justly avenging the
profane audacity of human ingenuity from the time of the first parent of the
human race. Ecclesiastes 1:13: “I applied my soul to investigate and explore
by wisdom everything done under heaven. God gave this evil occupation to the
sons of men, that they should occupy themselves in it.” Verse 17: “I also
applied my soul to know wisdom and the knowledge of every kind of madness and
folly; I knew that this too was an affliction of the spirit.”

II. It concerns things which are vain in themselves and bring neither eternal
salvation nor the kingdom of heaven. Ecclesiastes 1:14: “I saw all the deeds
done under the sun, and behold, they are all vanity and affliction of spirit.”

III. It is food for wind, or a grazing of the soul. That is, it yields no
other worthwhile result than the fruitless weariness of a soul that feeds on
wind, by which the soul is not nourished and its hunger is not satisfied.
Ecclesiastes 1:14.

IV. It cannot correct even the least crooked thing or supply its innumerable
defects, much less furnish heavenly perfection. Ecclesiastes 1:15: “What is
crooked cannot be corrected, and what is lacking cannot be numbered.”

V. It does not produce εὐθυμία, tranquility, so that not even kings find
quiet in it. Solomon, the wisest and most powerful king, teaches this by his
own example in Ecclesiastes 1:12–13: “I, Ecclesiastes, was king over Israel in
Jerusalem. I applied my soul to investigate and explore by wisdom everything
done under heaven. God gave this evil occupation,” and so forth. Verses
16–17: “I spoke with my heart, saying, Behold, I have magnified and added
wisdom above all who were before me in Jerusalem, and my soul perceived much
wisdom and knowledge. I also applied my soul to know wisdom and the knowledge
of every madness and folly; I knew that this too was affliction of spirit.”

VI. It does not produce αὐτάρκεια, self-sufficiency, but is joined with much
indignation and grief. So far is even the most sagacious soul from finding
anything in this inquiry that satisfies it that, on the contrary, the farther
it advances in searching out this wisdom, the more it is offended; and the
more it learns, the more it is tormented and grieved both by ignorance of
many things and by the utterly uncertain knowledge of what it has perceived.

[1615 BSB witness | digital canvas 29 | printed p. 5]

[1615 printed locator A]

Ecclesiastes 1:18 according to the Vulgate, or chapter 2:1 in the edition of
Tremellius and Junius: “Because in the multitude of wisdom there is much
indignation, and he who increases knowledge increases grief.” Ecclesiastes
9:1–2: “When I applied my soul to know wisdom and to perceive the business
done upon the earth—even though someone should not take sleep with his eyes
day or night—I perceived the whole work of God: man cannot attain the work
done under the sun. However laboriously man seeks it, he will not attain it;
even if the wisest man thinks he knows it, he still cannot attain it.”

VII. It has no power to avoid the events and accidents of human affairs.
Ecclesiastes 9:1 and following.

[1615 printed locator B]

VIII. It was not sought by those truly faithful and approved by God. First
Corinthians 1:26: “You see your calling, brothers, that not many of you are
wise according to the flesh, not many powerful, not many noble.” Chapter 2:1:
“When I came to you, brothers, I did not come with excellence of speech or of
wisdom, announcing to you the testimony of God.” Verses 4–5: “My speech and
proclamation did not consist in persuasive words of human wisdom, but in a
spiritual and powerful demonstration, so that your faith should not rest in
the wisdom of men but in the power of God.”

IX. It will be abolished and perish. First Corinthians 1:19–20: “It is
written: I will abolish the wisdom of the wise and remove the understanding
of the intelligent. Where is the wise man? Where is the scribe? Where is the
disputer of this age? Has God not made foolish the wisdom of this world?”

[1615 printed locator C]

X. It is useless for the kingdom of heaven and brings eternal salvation to no
one. First Corinthians 1:21: “Since in the wisdom of God the world did not
know God through this wisdom, it pleased God to save believers through the
foolishness of preaching.” Chapter 2:4: “My speech and proclamation did not
consist in persuasive words of human wisdom, but in a spiritual and powerful
demonstration.”

XI. It is sought for the sake of something else, not for its own sake. Who is
content merely to know and does not desire some fruit from his knowledge?
Arts are learned so that they may be practiced; they are practiced for the
supports of life, for pleasure, or for glory. But what is not sought for its
own sake is not the highest good.

XII. It is not with man in the grave. Ecclesiastes 9:10: “Whatever your hand
finds to do, do according to your ability, for there is no work, reasoning,
knowledge, or wisdom in the grave to which you are going.”

[1615 printed locator D]

XIII. It does not even supply earthly goods. Ecclesiastes 9:11: “Turning, I
perceived under the sun that the race does not belong to the swift, the battle
to the strong, food to the wise, riches to the prudent, or favor to the
skillful, but time and chance happen to them all.”

XIV. It is generally far removed from us, as Solomon confesses in
Ecclesiastes 7:23–24: “When I said, I will be wise, wisdom was far from me.
Whatever has existed is exceedingly far off and very deep; who can find it?”

It is objected: If wisdom is not man’s highest good, then it is no better than
folly. But this is absurd. Therefore the former is also absurd.

REPLY: The consequence of the first premise is denied. Although wisdom is not
man’s highest good, it is nevertheless superior to folly.

I. As light is better and more useful than darkness, so wisdom is more
excellent and useful than folly. Ecclesiastes 2:13: “I saw that wisdom has an
advantage over folly as light has an advantage over darkness.”

[1615 printed locator E]

II. Wisdom gives sight, while folly makes blind. Ecclesiastes 2:14: “The wise
man has his eyes in his head, but the fool walks in darkness; yet I knew that
the same event happens to them both.” The wise man has the eyes of the soul in
his head as in a citadel, by which he foresees what ought to be pursued and
what avoided. The unwise man lacks the mind’s eyes and does not know what he
ought to do.

III. The most excellent fruit of wisdom is not empty speculation but
knowledge of divine providence. Instructed by it, we may know the proper times
for acting and bear the changes of human affairs with an even mind.
Ecclesiastes 3:1 and following enumerates several such changes: “For every
thing there is its season and for every purpose a time under heaven: a time
to give birth and a time to die; a time to plant and a time to uproot what was
planted; a time to kill and a time to heal; a time to tear down and a time to
build; a time to weep and a time to laugh; a time to mourn and a time to
dance,” and so forth.

[1615 printed locator F]

IV. Wisdom preserves its possessor alive. Ecclesiastes 7:12: “Some are in the
shade of wisdom and others in the shade of money, but knowledge has this
greater advantage: wisdom preserves its possessor alive.”

V. Wisdom is better than strength. Ecclesiastes 9:16–18: “I say that wisdom is
better than strength, although the wisdom of that poor man was despised and
his words were not heard. The words of the quiet wise are to be heard rather
than the cry of one who rules among his fools. Wisdom is better than weapons
of war, but one sinner destroys much good.”

[1615 printed locator G]

VI. It gives more strength to the wise man than ten rulers in the same city.
Ecclesiastes 7:19: “Wisdom gives more strength to the wise than ten rulers in
the same city.”

VII. It is useful for preserving a commonwealth. Ecclesiastes 9:15: “In it was
found a poor wise man, and he delivered that city by his wisdom.”

VIII. It is advantageous for directing human actions. Ecclesiastes 10:10: “If
after the iron has become blunt a man does not sharpen its edge but increases
his strength, wisdom is advantageous for directing.”

Nor is GLORY—that is, human praise and the celebrity of a name spread far and
wide and commended to posterity—man’s highest good.

[1615 printed locator H]

I. It is generally obtained by deeds which human judgment considers
excellent but which before God are nothing other than splendid and glorious
sins, since they were done without faith and for one’s own glory. Such were
these: the Roman consul Brutus killed his sons when they plotted treason
against their country, concerning whom Virgil says, “Love of country
prevails, and a boundless desire for praise”; Torquatus killed his son because
in fighting the enemy without the commander’s order he violated military law;
Camillus, expelled from his country, later magnanimously freed it when it was
oppressed by the Gauls; Mucius Scaevola, having killed a servant instead of
King Porsenna, voluntarily thrust into the flame the right hand that had
missed its victim; Curtius, armed and sitting on his horse, voluntarily threw
himself into a chasm in the earth so that Rome might be freed from plague;
the Decii devoted themselves to death so that the legions might be saved and
victorious; Marcus Pulvillus, while consecrating a temple to Jupiter, was not
moved or dismayed by the announcement of his son’s death; Regulus returned to
Carthage for the most exquisite tortures; Fabricius despised the gold of King
Pyrrhus; and Alexander the Great and Julius Caesar subjugated many kingdoms
in a short time.

[1615 printed locator I]

II. It hinders true faith in God. Those who value human glory above godliness
and above the free and candid confession of Christ’s truth cannot truly
believe in Christ, as Christ says in John 5:44: “How can you believe when you
seek glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from God
alone?” Likewise many of the Jewish rulers believed in Jesus but did not
confess it because of the Pharisees, lest they be expelled from the synagogue:
“For they loved the glory of men more than the glory of God,” as John
12:42–43 says.

III. Glory too is vanity, for oblivion finally follows it, erases everything
in the course of time, and utterly destroys it. Ecclesiastes 2:16–17: “There
will be no perpetual memory of either the wise or the fool, since in coming
days everything that now exists is given to oblivion; and the wise man dies
with the fool. Therefore I hated this life, because this work done under the
sun seemed evil to me, for all these things are vanity and affliction of
spirit.” Certainly glory is inconstant, of little weight, and uncertain
because of human instability.

[1615 printed locator K]

IV. Christ, whom we ought to imitate, did not seek it. John 5:41: “I do not
receive glory from men”; and 8:50: “I do not seek my own glory.” Therefore he
also forbade the publication of the miracles he had performed. Matthew 8:3–4:
“Jesus stretched out his hand and touched the leper, saying, I will; be
cleansed. Immediately his leprosy was cleansed. Then Jesus said to him, See
that you tell no one, but go, show yourself to the priest and offer the gift
Moses commanded, as a testimony to them.” Matthew 9:30: “Jesus sternly warned
them, saying, See that no one knows.” Matthew 12:16: “He warned them not to
make him known.”

V. Men truly faithful and approved by God not only did not seek it but even
despised it, choosing instead to bear the reproach of Christ. Thus Moses in
Hebrews 11:24–26: “By faith Moses, when grown, refused to be called the son of
Pharaoh’s daughter, choosing rather to suffer evil with God’s people than to
retain the temporary enjoyment of sin, regarding Christ’s reproach as greater
riches than Egypt’s treasures, for he looked to the recompense of reward.”
Thus Paul and others in 2 Corinthians 4:5: “We do not proclaim ourselves but
Jesus Christ as Lord, and ourselves as your servants for Jesus’ sake”; and
6:8: “Through glory and dishonor, through slander and praise, as deceivers and
yet true.”

VI. Those who seek and are enslaved to such glory are generally unbelievers,
hypocrites, and displeasing to God. Second Corinthians 5:12: “We do not again
commend ourselves to you, but give you an occasion to boast about us, so that
you may have an answer for those who boast in appearance and not in heart.”

VII. It helps no one against bodily death, much less eternal death. Psalm
49:13–14: “This is their way, yet their posterity approve their words. Like
sheep they are laid in the grave; death will feed upon them until the upright
rule over them in the morning, and the grave consumes their form, taken from
its dwelling.”

VIII. It accompanies no one after death. Psalm 49:17: “When he dies he will
take nothing away; his glory will not descend after him.”

IX. The faithful should seek it only insofar as it pertains to God’s honor.
Matthew 5:16: “Let your light so shine before men that they see your good
works and glorify your Father in heaven.” Philippians 2:15–16: “That you may
be blameless and sincere, God’s blameless children in the midst of a crooked
and perverse generation, among whom you shine as lights in the world, holding
forth the word of life, so that I may boast in the day of Christ that I did
not run or labor in vain.” First Thessalonians 2:10: “You and God are
witnesses how holily, justly, and blamelessly we conducted ourselves among
you who believe.” Romans 1:8: “First, I thank my God through Jesus Christ for
you all, because your faith is proclaimed throughout the whole world.” First
Thessalonians 1:3 and following: “Continually remembering your active faith,
laborious love, and patient hope in our Lord Jesus Christ before our God and
Father, knowing, brothers beloved by God, your election. For our gospel came
to you not in word only but also in power, in the Holy Spirit, and in full
assurance, as you know what sort of men we were among you for your sake,” and
so forth. But the highest good must be desired absolutely, whereas empty glory
must not be desired, since God forbids it in Matthew 6:1 and several verses
following; Galatians 5:26: “Let us not be desirous of empty glory”;

Philippians 2:3: “Doing nothing through rivalry or empty glory”; and 1
Thessalonians 2:6: “Nor seeking glory from men.”

X. The pursuit of it excites mutual envy. Galatians 5:26: “Let us not be
desirous of empty glory, provoking and envying one another.”

XI. Desire for glory is common to man and brute beasts. Is it not found in
horses, when the victors exult and the defeated grieve? So great is their
love of praise, so great their concern for victory. Not without reason the
greatest poet says this ought to be tried: “What grief defeat brings each and
what glory the palm.”

XII. It is placed only in men’s opinion and in the estimation and judgment of
others.

Nor is PLEASURE OF THE FLESH man’s highest good.

I. It is vanity. Ecclesiastes 2:1: “I said in my heart, Come now, I will test
you with joy; therefore enjoy the good. But behold, this too is vanity.”

II. It is sheer madness. Ecclesiastes 2:2: “Of laughter I say, It is mad.”

[1615 printed locator B]

III. It brings no profit. Ecclesiastes 2:2: “And of joy I say, What does it
do?”

IV. It does not make even kings blessed, as Solomon teaches by his example in
Ecclesiastes 2:3 and following: “I explored in my heart by continuing drinking
in my flesh, while guiding my heart with wisdom. I explored by embracing
folly until I should see what good the sons of men might do under heaven
during the number of their days. I made great works; I built houses and
planted vineyards for myself. I made gardens and orchards in which I planted
every kind of fruitful tree. I made pools of water to irrigate a grove rich
in trees.”

[1615 printed locator C]

V. It is food for wind, or a grazing of the soul. Ecclesiastes 2:10: “Whatever
my eyes desired I did not withhold from them. I did not restrain my heart from
any joy, but my heart rejoiced in all my labor, and this was my portion from
all my labor,” and so forth.

VI. The soul cannot and ought not rest in it. Ecclesiastes 2:11: “I looked at
all the works my hands had made and at the labor in which I had labored, and
behold, they were all vanity and affliction of spirit, and there was no profit
under the sun.” First Corinthians 7:29–31: “Those who have wives should be as
though they had none; those who rejoice as though they did not; those who buy
as though they did not possess; and those who use this world as though they
did not abuse it, for the form of this world is passing away.”

VII. It is not peculiar to man but is common to him and the other animals.

VIII. It is brief, whereas the highest good must be everlasting.

IX. It does not permit the seed of God’s Word to take root in the heart and
produce fruit pleasing to God. Luke 8:14.

X. It weighs down the soul so that it does not rise to God. First Peter 2:11:
“Abstain from the lusts of the flesh, which wage war against the soul.”

[1615 printed locator D]

Indeed, not even PLEASURE JOINED WITH WISDOM is man’s highest good.

I. Even so, it is vanity.

II. Not even when joined together do they bring tranquility to the soul, as
Solomon teaches by his own example in Ecclesiastes 2:3: “I explored in my
heart by continuing drinking in my flesh, while guiding my heart with wisdom,”
and so forth. In his own example Solomon depicts the pursuit of worldly wise
men.

It is objected: Therefore every pleasure must be avoided.

REPLY: There is a certain honorable pleasure of soul and body which God by no
means forbids, provided a person enjoys with a cheerful and grateful heart the
things granted by God’s gift.

Nor is PLEASURE OF THE FLESH JOINED WITH HONOR man’s highest good.

I. These cannot be joined, since one devoted to fleshly pleasure necessarily
lacks honor, while one who pursues honor must abstain from such pleasure.

II. Honor is lasting esteem bestowed on someone by the favorable report of
the people. But one who pursues fleshly pleasure has a bad reputation even in
the judgment of the wise.

[1615 printed locator E]

Nor are THE HIGHEST DIGNITY AND POWER IN THE COMMONWEALTH man’s highest good.

I. It frequently becomes injury to others, arming every injustice, as it
were, with public authority. Good God, how many and how great are the evils
that rage in both commonwealth and church when wicked princes and magistrates
disturb everything at their pleasure, despising the fear and laws of God!
Ecclesiastes 3:16 and following: “Again I saw under the sun wickedness in the
place of judgment and wickedness in the place of righteousness. I said in my
heart, God will judge the righteous and the wicked, for there is a time for
every purpose and for every deed there. I said in my heart concerning the
condition of the sons of men that God was proving them, so that they might
see that they themselves are beasts,” and so forth. See also chapters 4 and
5. Those who seek honors and rule over others without fear of the divine
Majesty, as though they could obtain felicity in this way, overturn everything
by their disordered ambition. No place for justice remains, and they
themselves have no tranquility. They permit many corruptions to creep into
divine worship, provided they themselves remain eminent. Indeed, in a badly
governed commonwealth subjects are compelled by the tyranny of evil princes
to embrace and retain corruptions of religion along with political vices.

[1615 printed locator F]

II. It always has much vanity, trouble, and grief joined to it. Or do you
think safe even those who, amid the insignia of office and abundant wealth,
are surrounded by guards in the splendor of a royal court? Their fear is
greater than other men’s: they are compelled to fear as much as they are
feared. Though surrounded by troops of attendants and protecting their
enclosed flank with a crowd of bodyguards, just as they do not allow their
subjects to be secure, they themselves necessarily are not secure. Their own
power frightens those whom it makes terrible. It smiles in order to rage,
flatters in order to deceive, entices in order to kill, and exalts in order
to cast down. By a kind of interest on injury, the greater the sum of dignity
and honors has been, the greater the interest of punishment exacted—to use
Cyprian’s words from his second letter. Ecclesiastes 4:15–16: “I saw all the
living who walk under the sun join themselves to the second youth who was to
stand in the former’s place. There was no end of all the people, yet those
coming after would not rejoice in him; this too is vanity and affliction of
spirit.”

[1615 printed locator G]

III. It can be lost, so that one who possessed highest power may become a
servant. Ecclesiastes 10:7: “I saw princes walking as servants upon the
earth.” Jehoiachin is an example: after losing his kingdom he was led captive
to Babylon with his most precious spoil and a multitude of captives, 2 Kings
24. Seventy kings, with the thumbs and great toes of their hands and feet cut
off, gathered bones and crumbs under Adoni-bezek’s table like dogs, Judges
1:7.

IV. It is bounded entirely by the narrow limits of this life. Even if a man
retains dignity while he lives, in death he must leave it, as Solomon and
innumerable other kings and princes did. What death breaks off and takes away
from man is not the highest good.

[1615 printed locator H]

V. It often falls to wicked, foolish, and unworthy men. Psalm 12:8: “The
wicked walk on every side when vileness is exalted among the sons of men.”
Ecclesiastes 4:13: “Better is a poor and wise youth than an old and foolish
king who no longer knows how to be admonished.” Chapter 10:5–7: “There is an
evil I saw under the sun, an error proceeding from the ruler: folly is set in
high and great dignities, while the rich sit in a low place. I saw servants
riding upon horses and princes walking as servants upon the earth.”

VI. The faithful should not pursue it, although when legitimately offered it
may be admitted and accepted by them with a good conscience. Whatever seems
high and great to others in human affairs lies beneath the Christian’s
conscience. He who is greater than the world can no longer seek or desire
anything from it. After the faithful soul, looking to heaven, recognizes its
author, it begins to be what it believes itself to be—higher than the soil
and more exalted than all earthly power. He who has renounced the world is
greater than its honors and kingdom; therefore one who dedicates himself to
God and Christ desires heavenly, not earthly, kingdoms.

[1615 printed locator I]

VII. It is often an evil to its possessors and ruins them. Ecclesiastes 8:9:
“There is a time when man rules over man to his own hurt.” Pharaoh king of
Egypt is an example: trusting in his power and persecuting the Israelites, he
brought himself to destruction.

VIII. Christ our Lord fled from it. John 6:15: “When he knew they were about
to come and seize him to make him king, he withdrew again to the mountain by
himself.” He who gives heavenly kingdoms does not seek earthly ones.

IX. It cannot always be possessed or retained without sin. Naaman, for
example, could not retain his office without sin, 2 Kings 5:18.

X. Through it a man cannot stand firm for even a single night. Psalm 49:12:
“Nevertheless man in honor does not remain overnight; he is like the beasts
that perish.” Ecclesiastes 8:8: “No man has power over his spirit to retain
it; there is no power in the day of death and no discharge in that war.”
Belshazzar king of Babylon is an example, Daniel 5:30.

XI. Through it even the most powerful monarchs cannot obtain from their
subjects, much less from neighboring or distant peoples, whatever they want.
The example of Rehoboam son of Solomon and examples of kings in our own age
teach this.

XII. Dignity and power do not make even kings immune from the mockery of
their subjects, which fills royal souls with indignation. Job 12:21: “He pours
contempt upon princes and loosens the belt of the strong.”

[1615 printed locator K]

XIII. Dignity and power often cause their possessors not to keep promised
faith, through contempt or disregard for those to whom they promised it, as
daily experience testifies. This is utterly foreign to the nature of the
highest good.

XIV. Dignity and power drive the ambitious and greedy to afflict others
contrary to divine and human laws, to seize and forcibly retain what belongs
to others, and to become authors of many murders. Examples are Pharaoh, who
oppressed Israel; Haman, the Jews’ enemy; and Xerxes the Great.

XV. It drives man to lift his heart against God and provokes abominable
pride, as the examples of Pharaoh, Nebuchadnezzar, Antiochus Epiphanes, and
Herod teach.

XVI. It is brief and momentary. Isaiah 40:6: “A voice says, Cry; and he says,
What shall I cry? All flesh is grass, and all its loveliness is like the flower
of the field.” First Peter 1:24: “All flesh is grass and all man’s glory like
the flower of grass; the grass withers and its flower falls.”

XVII. It does not rescue from oblivion after death. Ecclesiastes 8:10: “I saw
the wicked who had ruled buried after they died. They departed from the holy
place and were forgotten in the city where they had acted so. This too is
vanity.”

Nor are RICHES man’s highest good.

I. They are generally acquired unjustly, or at least with great labor under
the divinely inflicted punishment of anxious occupation and troublesome care;
or, even if justly acquired, men nevertheless use them unjustly. Jeremiah
5:27: “As a cage is full of birds, so their houses are full of deceit;
therefore they have become great and rich.” Hence CHRIST calls riches
“unrighteous mammon” in Luke 16:9, 11. Ecclesiastes 2:26: “God gives the sinner
the occupation of gathering and heaping up.”

II. They are increased and guarded with troublesome torment. Ecclesiastes
5:12: “The rich man’s abundance does not permit him to sleep.” Those whom you
consider rich—joining estate to estate and house to house, extending boundless
fields by excluding poor neighbors, and piling up immense weights of silver
and gold and huge heaps of money—are tormented amid their riches by the
trembling care of uncertain thought: lest a robber plunder them, a murderer
attack them, or the hostile envy of someone richer trouble them with
calumnious lawsuits. Their food and sleep are not secure. The man sighs at a
banquet though he drinks from a jewel; when his exhausted body has been laid
amid feasts in the deep hollow of a softer bed, he lies awake on feathers. The
wretch does not understand that these splendid things are punishments, that
he is bound to his gold, and that riches and wealth possess him more than he
possesses them. O detestable blindness of minds and deep darkness of mad
desire! Though he could free and relieve himself of these weights, he goes on
brooding over his swelling fortune and clinging to heaps of punishment. He
gives nothing to dependents or the needy. Men call the money theirs while
guarding it anxiously, locked at home as if it belonged to someone else. So
Cyprian writes in his second letter, to Donatus.

[1615 printed locator B]

III. They are corruptible. Matthew 6:19: “Do not store treasures on earth,
where moth and corrosion corrupt and thieves break through and steal.” First
Timothy 6:17: “Charge those rich in this age not to be high-minded or set
their hope on uncertain riches, but on the living God, who richly gives us
all things to enjoy.” James 5:2–3: “Your riches have rotted and moths have
eaten your garments. Your gold and silver have corroded; their corrosion will
testify against you and eat your flesh like fire. You have stored treasure in
the last days.”

IV. They can be lost by the very man who acquired them with great trouble.
Matthew 6:19; Proverbs 23:5. Riches do not always remain with the same owner
but travel here and there. Ecclesiastes 5:14–16: “Those riches perish through
an evil venture, so that when he has fathered a son he has nothing in his
hand. As he came naked from his mother’s womb, he returns as he came and
carries away nothing from his labor in his hand. This too is a grievous evil:
just as he came, so he departs.”

[1615 printed locator C]

V. They are inferior to heavenly goods, which are incorruptible and eternal
and which CHRIST therefore commands us to acquire. Matthew 6:20: “Store up
treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor corrosion corrupts and thieves do
not break through or steal.”

VI. They make man’s mind their servant and fasten it to the ground so it
cannot rise to heaven. Matthew 6:21: “Where your treasure is, there your heart
will be also.” Matthew 13:22: “The care of this age and the deceitfulness of
riches choke the word, and it becomes unfruitful.” Certainly most rich men are
servants of money.

VII. They blind man’s mind so it cannot see true goods. Matthew 6:22: “The
lamp of the body is the eye; therefore, if your eye is sound, your whole body
will be full of light.”

VIII. They do not make man acceptable to GOD or obtain anything from GOD for
him, as the example of the rich banqueter and his brothers shows in Luke 16.

IX. They hinder the worship of GOD and draw men away from him. Matthew 6:24:
“No one can serve two masters; he will either hate the one and love the other,
or cling to one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and mammon.”

[1615 printed locator D]

X. When increased, they multiply vanity, although it ought to be enough for a
man that he himself is vanity. Ecclesiastes 6:11: “When things are multiplied,
they multiply vanity; what more does man have?”

XI. They do not preserve this temporal life; GOD does by his providence.
Matthew 6:25 and following: “Do not be anxious for your life, what you will
eat or drink, nor for your body, what you will wear. Is not life more than
food and the body than clothing? Look at the birds of heaven: they neither
sow nor reap nor gather into barns, yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are
you not much more valuable than they?” and so forth. Luke 12:15: “Watch and
beware of greed, for no one’s life consists in an abundance of possessions.”

XII. They deliver no one from temporal death, much less eternal death.
Proverbs 10:2: “Treasures of wickedness do not profit, but righteousness
delivers from death.” Proverbs 11:4: “Wealth does not profit in the day of
wrath.” Psalm 49:6–9: “Those who trust in their wealth and boast in the
abundance of their riches: no one can redeem his brother or give God his
ransom—for the redemption of their life is costly and ceases forever—so that
he should live eternally and not see corruption.” He sees wise men die and
fool and brute alike perish, leaving their wealth to others. Psalm 52:5–7:
“God will demolish you forever, seize and tear you from your tent, and uproot
you from the land of the living. The righteous will see and fear, and laugh
at him, saying, Behold the man who did not make God his strength but trusted
in his abundant riches and strengthened himself in his substance.” Luke
16:22–23: “The rich man also died and was buried; and in hell he lifted his
eyes, being in torments,” and so forth.

[1615 printed locator E]

XIII. We are forbidden to seek and heap them up. Matthew 6:19: “Do not store
treasures on earth, where moth and corrosion corrupt and thieves break
through and steal.” Matthew 10:9–10: “Do not acquire gold, silver, or copper
in your belts, a bag for the journey, two tunics, sandals, or staff, for the
worker is worthy of his food.” Proverbs 23:4: “Do not weary yourself to become
rich.”

XIV. Their possession is bounded by this narrow life, so that no one retains
them when he leaves it but, willingly or unwillingly, leaves them to others.
First Timothy 6:7: “We brought nothing into the world and certainly can carry
nothing out.” Job 1:21: “Naked I came from my mother’s womb and naked I will
return there. Jehovah gave and Jehovah took away; blessed be Jehovah’s name.”
Psalm 49:10, 17: “He sees wise men die and fool and brute alike perish,
leaving their wealth to others”; “When he dies he will take nothing away; his
glory will not descend after him.” Ecclesiastes 5:15–16: “As he came naked
from his mother’s womb, he returns as he came and carries away nothing from
his labor in his hand. This too is a grievous evil: just as he came, so he
departs. What profit has he from laboring for wind?” See Luke 16:19–21.

[1615 printed locator F]

XV. Desire for and possession of riches bring men many evils. First Timothy
6:9–10: “Those who want to become rich fall into temptation and a snare and
many foolish and harmful desires which plunge men into destruction and ruin.
For the love of money is the root of all evils; pursuing it, some wandered
from the faith and pierced themselves everywhere with many griefs.”
Ecclesiastes 5:13: “There is a grievous evil I saw under the sun: riches kept
by their owner to his hurt.”

XVI. They do not bring tranquility of soul but increase restlessness, since
the rich man’s desire cannot be satisfied. Ecclesiastes 6:7: “All man’s labor
is for his mouth, yet his desire is not satisfied.”

XVII. We are forbidden to trust in them. First Timothy 6:17: “Charge those
rich in this age not to be high-minded or set their hope on uncertain riches,
but on the living God, who richly gives us all things to enjoy.” All earthly
things are perishable and give their possessors no stable confidence, since
their possession has no permanence.

XVIII. Their possessors are not made better by them but generally worse:
luxurious, intemperate, proud, cruel and harsh toward the poor, and wanton.
Proverbs 10:16: “The income of the wicked tends to sin.” James 2:6–7: “Do not
the rich tyrannically oppress you and drag you before tribunals? Do they not
blaspheme that noble name by which you are called?” James 5:5–6: “You have
lived in luxury on earth and acted wantonly; you have fattened your hearts as
in a day of feasting. You have condemned and killed the righteous man; he
does not resist you.” The rich banqueter in Luke 16:19–21 is an example.

XIX. No one enjoys them all; by inheritance they are generally transferred
to foolish men who foolishly dissipate what was acquired with great labor and
sweat. Ecclesiastes 2:18–21: “I hated all my labor under the sun because I
must leave it to the man after me. Who knows whether he will be wise or a fool,
yet he will rule over all my labor which I performed and wisely acquired
under the sun? This too is vanity.” See verses 22–24.

XX. They often come into the hands of men connected neither as sons nor by
blood or affinity. This fiercely torments the soul, deprived of God’s Spirit,
of the man who gathered great wealth. Ecclesiastes 6:1–2: “There is an evil I
saw under the sun, and it weighs greatly upon men: a man to whom God gives
riches, possessions, and honor, so that his soul lacks nothing it desires,
yet God does not give him power to enjoy it, but a stranger enjoys it. This is
vanity and a grievous evil.” Luke 12:20: “Fool, this night they require your
soul from you; whose will the things you prepared be?”

Egypt’s cities in Ezekiel 29:19: “Thus says the Lord Jehovah: Behold, I give
Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon the land of Egypt, so that he may carry off its
multitude, take its spoil, and seize its prey; it will be wages for his army.”
So also kings who bought peace, such as Hezekiah in 2 Kings 18:14 and Jehoahaz
in 2 Kings 23:35; and confessors of heavenly truth whose possessions were
plundered, Hebrews 10:34.

XXII. Men approved by GOD did not seek them. Moses regarded Christ’s reproach
as greater riches than Egypt’s treasures, Hebrews 11:26. Others “wandered in
sheepskins and goatskins, destitute, afflicted, mistreated—of whom the world
was not worthy—wandering in deserts, mountains, caves, and holes in the
earth,” Hebrews 11:37–38. Second Corinthians 6:4: “In everything approving
ourselves as God’s ministers, in much patience, afflictions, necessities, and
distresses.”

XXIII. CHRIST our Lord did not seek them. Second Corinthians 8:9: “You know

Indeed, not even RICHES JOINED WITH WISDOM are man’s highest good.

I. Even so, they are vanity, Ecclesiastes 2:11.

II. Even so, they do not make the soul tranquil, as Solomon teaches by his
example in Ecclesiastes 2:4 and following: “I made great works; I built houses
and planted vineyards for myself. I made gardens and orchards in which I
planted every kind of fruitful tree. I made pools of water to irrigate a grove
rich in trees. I bought male and female servants, and servants were born in
my house. I also possessed herds and many flocks, more than all before me in
Jerusalem,” and so forth. “I looked at all the works my hands had made and the
labor in which I had labored, and behold, they were all vanity and affliction
of spirit, and there was no profit under the sun.”

OBJECTION: If riches are not man’s highest good, then they are evil.

REPLY: They are not evil if their possession is just and their use lawful.
Ecclesiastes 2:24 and following: “There is nothing good for man except to eat,
drink, and make his soul enjoy good from his labor. I also saw that this is
from God’s hand—for who could enjoy it more than I? To the man who is good in
his sight God gives wisdom, knowledge, and joy, but to the sinner he gives the
occupation of gathering and heaping up, to give it to him who is good in
God’s sight. This too is vanity and affliction of spirit.”

[1615 printed locator C]

Nor are VIRTUE and its action man’s highest good, although the philosophers
proclaim it as the highest good with the highest praises.

I. We must not boast in virtue.

II. Virtue cannot make anyone blessed. Lactantius, book 3 of the *Divine
Institutes*, chapter 8: “Although no one is blessed without virtue, virtue
itself is not the cause of blessedness but the way to blessedness.”

III. It is not sought for its own sake but for the blessed life. Hence many
philosophers commonly say that the reward of virtue is the blessed life.

IV. It cannot subsist without another good, for it needs many external aids
and does not rest on its own powers. But the highest good ought to need no
external aid.

V. Its power is placed in enduring evils. Lactantius, book 3 of the *Divine
Institutes*, chapter 11. But the highest good must be separated from every
evil.

VI. It is not blessed through itself. Lactantius, the cited book, chapter 12.

VII. It is not always like and the same. But the highest good is always like
and the same and alone can neither be diminished, increased, nor changed.

[1615 printed locator D]

VIII. It testifies to our calamities, as may be proved by considering the
kinds of virtue. What is the purpose of temperance except to restrain
drunkenness, feasting, lusts, and the base and foul movements of souls? These
things show that temperance has no place except in souls still subject to such
corruption. The more inward that corruption is, the more miserable it makes
us; like a domestic enemy, it rages in the inner chambers of our hearts.
These affections are vices which hinder us from doing what we will, as Paul
teaches in Romans 7:17 and following: “Now it is no longer I who perform it,
but sin dwelling in me performs it. I know that no good dwells in me—that is,
in my flesh—for to will is present with me, but I do not attain the doing of
what is good. I do not do the good I will, but I do the evil I do not will. If
I do what I do not will, it is no longer I who perform it, but sin dwelling
in me performs it,” and so forth.

What is prudence’s office except to provide that we are not deceived by error
in choosing goods and avoiding evils? Certainly if we were not enveloped in
error and darkness, we would not need this remedy. When it is applied, it
declares that men are not yet happy but entangled in great and grievous
errors. Justice too, by which each receives his own, is necessary to restrain
robbery, plunder, and violence. Yet it cannot accomplish so much that just and
upright men do not often act unjustly and frequently suffer injustice from
others. What shall we say of fortitude? It arms men so they can endure pain,
danger, torment, and death itself if necessary, and consequently testifies to
our evils.

[1615 printed locator E]

Nor is VIRTUE JOINED WITH HONOR man’s highest good.

I. No virtue can be dishonorable; if it has anything disgraceful, it ceases
to be virtue.

II. If virtue joined with honor were man’s highest good, it would follow that
one who pursues virtue ought to serve men’s opinion. Whoever does this departs
from the right and good, because it is not in our power for virtue to be
honored according to its merits. What happens if, through men’s error and
depravity, a bad reputation follows virtue, or envy presses and even oppresses
it?

Nor is THIS EARTHLY AND TEMPORAL LIFE man’s highest good.

I. It is subject to innumerable evils.

II. It is brief, whereas the highest good is everlasting.

III. The highest good must not be sought in the lowest region of the world,
the earth from which the body comes.

IV. The highest good does not belong to the body.

V. This life is vain. Whatever pertains to the body and lacks immortality
must be vain.

VI. It does not make blessed.

VII. The saints and men pleasing to God regard it as nothing in comparison
with that other life.

VIII. Men regarded among the Gentiles as wisest and bravest—Cato, Brutus, and
others—determined to break it off for themselves. If this life is the highest
good, why did they seek death’s aid to end it?

[1615 printed locator F]

Indeed, not even HUMAN IMMORTALITY is man’s highest good, although Lactantius
repeatedly held that it is.

I. Immortality is only an accident of man, whereas the highest good must be a
thing subsisting in itself.

II. God produces it in man.

III. Immortality does not make one blessed by itself. The devil is an
immortal spirit but is not therefore blessed; and the souls of reprobate men
are immortal, or of an immortal nature, but are not therefore blessed.

Nor is ABSENCE OF PAIN the highest good, as the sick and those in pain
suppose, and as Diodorus thought.

I. A man can give it. What is more ridiculous than to regard as the highest
good what a man can give?

II. Desire for it is common to man and the other animals, since every animal
flees pain.

III. He who never grieved would be most miserable, because he lacks a good.
We must therefore grieve in order to enjoy the good, and indeed grieve
severely and often, so that afterward freedom from pain may be more pleasant.

[1615 printed locator G]

Nor are the FAVOR AND GRACE of any prince, relative, or other person man’s
highest good.

I. They are men. Psalm 146:3: “Do not trust in princes, in any son of man, in
whom there is no salvation.”

II. Every man is vanity. Psalm 62:9: “The sons of lowborn men are only vanity,
and the sons of distinguished men are a lie; placed together in the scales,
they would rise above vanity.”

III. No man can rescue anyone from death. Psalm 49:7–9: “No one can redeem
his brother or give God his ransom—for the redemption of their life is costly
and ceases forever—so that he should live eternally and not see corruption.”
Psalm 146:3: “Do not trust in princes, in any son of man, in whom there is no
salvation.”

IV. Every man is mortal. Psalm 146:4: “His spirit departs; he returns to his
earth”—namely, not the spirit but the man in his body—“on that day his
thoughts perish.” Isaiah 2:22: “Cease from man, whose breath is in his
nostrils, for of what account is he?” Isaiah 40:6–8: “A voice says, Cry; and
he says, What shall I cry? All flesh is grass, and all its loveliness is like
the flower of the field. The grass withers and the flower falls when Jehovah’s
Spirit blows upon it; surely the people is grass. The grass withers and the
flower falls, but the word of our God remains forever.”

[1615 printed locator H]

V. We are forbidden to place trust in men. Psalm 146:3.

VI. Trust placed in men is accursed. Jeremiah 17:5: “Cursed is the man who
trusts in man and makes flesh his arm, while his soul departs from Jehovah.”

VII. The truly faithful did not hesitate to reject the grace and favor even
of men who could benefit them greatly in bodily affairs. When grown, Moses by
faith refused to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter, choosing rather to
suffer evil with God’s people than to retain the temporary enjoyment of sin,
Hebrews 11:24–25. Samuel, Elijah, Elisha, Isaiah, Jeremiah, John the Baptist,
and other prophets did not hesitate to rebuke kings severely for their sins.

VIII. Human grace is vain, temporary, brief, and even momentary.

IX. Men’s wills are inconstant and pliable; through others’ slanders or even
slight suspicions they commonly change grace into hatred and favor into
anger.

[1615 printed locator I]

Nor is FORTUNE ACCOMMODATING ITSELF TO MEN’S TEMPERAMENTS their highest good
and the cause of human felicity.

I. Fortune is nothing in itself but a mere fiction of Gentile poets, who
imagine it as a goddess giving goods and evils to whomever she wishes.

II. Fortune is denial of God and his providence. For one who persuades himself
that the felicity he enjoys and the goods he possesses come not from God but
from fortune despises and denies God and his providence and very easily
abandons his pure worship.

[1615 printed locator K]

[1615 BSB witness | digital canvas 33 | printed p. 9]

[1615 printed locator A]

III. Fortune was not acknowledged even by the sounder Gentile poets,
philosophers, and historians. Juvenal’s lines are well known:

> No deity is absent if prudence is present; but we
> Make Fortune a goddess and place her in heaven.

IV. If fortune is anything, it is nothing other than the sudden and
unexpected outcome of contingent events.

Therefore the error of those who hold a man happy for as long as fortune
accommodates itself to his temperament and humor is most serious.

[1615 printed locator B]

Nor is A RELIGION INVENTED BY HUMAN CHOICE—even if publicly and commonly
received and cultivated with great zeal—or even the external observance of
ceremonies commanded by God, especially without true faith, the highest good
and cause of human felicity.

I. It draws away from the true God, who is the author of every true good.
Isaiah 1:4: “Ah, sinful nation, a people heavily laden with iniquity, a most
evil seed, corrupting children! They have forsaken Jehovah; they have
contemptuously provoked the Holy One of Israel; alienating themselves, they
have turned backward.”

II. We must flee it with every effort. Isaiah 1:11–14: “What is the multitude
of your sacrifices to me? says Jehovah. I am full of burnt offerings of rams
and the fat of fed beasts, and I do not delight in the blood of bulls, lambs,
and goats. Why do you come? Is it to appear before me? Who required this from
your hand, that you should trample my courts? Do not continue to offer a gift
of vanity. Incense is an abomination to me. New moon and Sabbath, the calling
of assemblies—I cannot endure iniquity and the solemn gathering. My soul
hates your new moons and appointed feasts; they are burdensome to me, and I
am weary of bearing them.”

[1615 printed locator C]

III. It is evil. Ecclesiastes 5:1: “Guard both your feet when you go to the
house of God, and be ready to hear rather than to give the sacrifice of
fools, for they do not perceive that they do evil.”

IV. It profits its worshipers nothing. Isaiah 1:15: “Therefore, when you
spread out your hands, I hide my eyes from you; even when you multiply prayer,
I do not hear. Your hands are full of bloodshed.”

V. It is not the cause of eternal blessedness. Matthew 15:9: “In vain they
worship me, teaching doctrines which are commandments of men.”

Consequently those err who affirm that the religion of Numa and worship of
many gods caused the felicity of the Roman commonwealth. Those who proclaim
to simpler people that the religion of the Roman Church, especially as it has
existed from Gregory the Great until the present day, was and still is the
cause of the Christian commonwealth’s felicity deceive them no less.

[1615 printed locator D]

Finally, lest I enumerate more false opinions about the highest good—Varro
recalls 285 different opinions—neither is THE COMPLETE CONDITION OF ALL GOODS,
perfected by the aggregation and acquisition of the goods of the body and of
what they call fortune, our highest good.

I. It is uncertain.

II. It is confined within the limits of this life.

III. There are no goods of fortune, since fortune is a Gentile fiction, as
was said a little earlier.

Thus far it has been stated what is and what is not the highest good of
rational creatures and especially of man. Their blessedness follows.
