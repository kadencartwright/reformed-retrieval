# On Church Property

*An undated French consilium, probably written after 1543; a fresh translation from CO X/1 249–251.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 249]

Concerning the advice our brother requests about the properties which he now holds by gift of the late countess and which formerly belonged to the church of Neuchâtel, without entering into a long dispute, here is the judgment I can make in good conscience according to the grace God has given me.

First, insofar as he has been impoverished for the Word of God and the confession of the gospel, it is not contrary to reason that he

### [CO X/1, p. 250]

be supported from the goods of the church. For even if there were good order and administration, when such a case occurred it would be fitting and equitable for a portion to be applied to this use: assisting those who had been stripped of their substance or otherwise impoverished for testimony to the name of Jesus Christ. I therefore see no wrong in his using the said properties for his relief, recognizing that by this means God has come to his aid after testing him through the loss of what he had previously given him.

It is true that the form of the gift is not very well founded according to the terms of the instrument. But I attend to equity rather than to a legal formality—as reason also requires—and especially to the fact that the princess, being in the country of France, would not have dared act otherwise. Since there is no defect in the substance of the matter, I do not doubt that he may use it in good conscience before God. For, as I have said, this property is justly applied to the maintenance and support of those who otherwise would have been in need because they suffered loss for the gospel.

There would be greater difficulty concerning perpetual succession by his heirs. Just as it is lawful to meet the present need of someone who has suffered loss as he has, so to make a perpetual alienation in favor of his household is a matter of perilous consequence whose example is neither good nor to be approved. It would be quite proper for the children to be advanced by some grant, so that they may be set on their way to serve God, and even for the church to make up what the father lacks in that respect. I would therefore not condemn such a grant but would rather regard it as praiseworthy, provided that the alienation of the goods and properties were not made so as to defraud the poor forever in time to come.

Indeed, it is a great and pernicious fault today in several places where the gospel is preached that ecclesiastical goods are dissipated or put to profane use, with no regard for the end to which they were dedicated. Although little account is made of it, this is an abuse which God will shortly reform with severity if people do not correct themselves.

I know the common excuse: these goods were given to monks and priests out of superstition, to endow masses and anniversary services, to practice idolatry, to feed themselves in idleness, and so forth; therefore all of this should be abolished. Alternatively, princes may appropriate them to their domain, as formerly they did inheritances called escheats. But none of this has any standing before God. For God's Word teaches us the proper use of goods dedicated to his honor. And in the ancient church there were holy

### [CO X/1, p. 251]

ordinances enacted concerning this which were ratified by the emperors. Thus, although priests and monks usurped under false pretenses and without valid title what did not belong to them and badly administered what they had wrongfully seized, by their own doings they could not cause what had been well ordered—and even founded upon God's authority—to be annulled or destroyed. It has always remained the case that these were offerings consecrated to God which it is not lawful to profane.

This is also why we condemn the monks and priests as sacrilegious. By not serving the church they have devoured the property assigned to its true ministers. In addition, they have seized the substance of the poor and consumed it in gluttony, pomp, debauchery, and things still more vile. The duty of princes is therefore to restore such property to its natural use rather than to take the place of those whom we justly rebuke. If no one opposes them in this world, let them fear that God will put his hand to it.

As for private persons, there is very great danger in [soiling] their hands with it. Anyone who mixes it with his own goods in order to enrich himself all the more will be utterly astonished when his property diminishes twofold and threefold, for God will never permit such a means to prosper in the long run. Let us therefore value God's favor and blessing more than accursed riches which melt away without anyone knowing how and bring their possessors to ruin.

Finally, beyond the present evil and offense, we have to consider the stumbling block we place before unbelievers. We drive them back from the gospel because it seems that we sought religious change as an opportunity to fish in troubled waters, as the proverb says—that is, that we sought a means of plundering the goods of the church as though they had been exposed as spoil.

There is likewise the bad example we show those who come after us. Since God has granted us the grace to call us first to the knowledge of his Word, we will be all the more culpable if we introduce such a vice for others to follow, founding themselves on what we have done.

## Translator's notes

1. The unnamed brother, the countess's gift, and the properties once belonging to the church of Neuchâtel are part of the transmitted body. CO's editors infer that the autograph was written after the death in 1543 of Jeanne de Longueville, duchess and countess of Neuchâtel; the body itself supplies no date.
2. “Instrument” translates *instrument* in its legal sense: the document by which the gift was effected. Calvin distinguishes an imperfect legal form from what he judges to be an equitable substance.
3. *Obitz* are endowed anniversary services or masses for the dead. “Escheats” translates *heritages ... caducques*, estates falling to a lord or sovereign for want of a valid heir.
4. At p. 251 CO prints a four-dot gap after *den* and notes that the manuscript word is illegible, suggesting *soullier* (“soil” or “defile”) only tentatively. The translation supplies “[soiling]” in brackets and does not insert the conjecture into `french.txt`.
5. *Pescher en eau trouble* is the same “fish in troubled waters” proverb used in English. The text explains its particular accusation in the next sentence: profiting from ecclesiastical change by plundering church property.
