By way of an appendix, let us undertake, if you please, a friendly and calm
examination of what our most learned paraphrast and commentator recently
published on the words, “Do not think that I have come to destroy the law or
the prophets,” and so forth (Matthew 5:17). Since his remarks are connected
with the matter we have been discussing, it will be worthwhile to examine
them.

[1679 printed p. 156 | IA leaf 164]

The learned man writes as follows.

In the New Testament the word *nomos*, or “law,” has two meanings:

It may be taken for the whole civil order of the Old Covenant, considered
simply in itself as delivered by Moses.

Or it may be taken insofar as it excluded Christ and the gospel: that is,
insofar as the Pharisees, other Jews, Judaizing Gnostics, and other
heterodox people opposed it to Christ after his coming so that they would
not receive him and gospel faith.

In the former sense, he acknowledges that the law both permitted and
commanded many things which were to be reformed and corrected by the
“reformation” of Hebrews 9:10 after Christ's coming. Some, such as the
ceremonies, would then cease. Others among its civil and moral provisions,
such as those concerning divorce, were imperfect and were to be changed for
the better when Christ proposed and imposed requirements higher and more
perfect than the law seemed to demand, and more suitable for the church's
mature condition under the gospel.

He nevertheless acknowledges that this law was well suited to the immature
church under the law and was given to the people of that time in order to
eternal salvation. He says the evangelists and Acts usually employ *nomos*
in this sense: the code and body of constitutions delivered through Moses
before Christ's reformation and not opposed to him.

But the Jews, especially the Pharisees and their Gnostic followers,
believed that after Christ came and the gospel arose this Mosaic polity
still had to be retained tenaciously in every respect, that justification
was to be expected from its observance, and consequently that the gospel,
as contrary to it, should not be admitted but persecuted with fire and
sword.

From this the learned man carves out the second meaning of “law,” insofar as
it excluded Christ and the gospel. He thinks the apostolic epistles usually
employ it in this sense, and that most of many of those epistles was written
against this Jewish and Gnostic opinion. Among other passages he lists
Romans 3:20: “Therefore no flesh will be justified in his sight by works of
the law.” He explains this as observance of the form Moses prescribed insofar
as it differed from Christ's reformation, and as bare observance of the
external and ceremonial part without the inward and more substantial part:
circumcision without inward purity.

He likewise includes Romans 3:21 and 28, where we are said to be justified
“apart from law” and “apart from works of law”—that is, apart from law
insofar as the Jews made it exclude Christ. He similarly takes verse 27,
where the “law of works” is opposed to the “law of faith,” to mean the
external law and the internal law. In verse 21, where Paul says God's
righteousness is manifested “apart from law,” he understands law in his
second sense, excluding Christ, while “attested by the law” means law in the
first sense, as delivered through Moses.

[1679 printed p. 157 | IA leaf 165]

In Romans 4:13, “the promise to Abraham through law,” he similarly
understands “law” as the command of circumcision. In the same way he expounds
verses 14–16, where Paul speaks of those who are “of law” and says that “law
works wrath,” as law in the second sense. He treats other passages in Romans
6–8 and “from works of law” and similar expressions in Galatians 2–4 in the
same way. Since all these directly concern the matter we have discussed, let
us examine them with an impartial eye.

We freely concede that some things the learned man says are entirely right.

1. The law has one character as delivered through Moses and serving the
gospel like a tutor, and another insofar as Jews and Pharisees abused it
abominably by opposing it to the gospel and insisting it be retained in
every detail, thus utterly rejecting and most bitterly persecuting faith in
Christ for its sake.

2. We also acknowledge the truth of his statement that the law delivered
through Moses was given to the Jews in order to eternal life. Paul himself
says in Romans 7:10 that “the commandment was unto life.” By accident and
our fault it is the ministry of death (2 Corinthians 3:7), but in its nature
and God's primary institution—when he first gave it to Adam and later
through Moses to the Jewish people—it was unto life, since it showed the way
to life and exhorted them to walk in it.

I say something greater: faithful Jews obtained eternal life in its right
observance. When performing the ceremonies they perceived by faith the
Messiah hidden beneath them, and when terrified by the moral law's commands
and threats they fled to that Messiah to obtain life. Thus, burdened with
the law and their sins (Matthew 11:28), they came to him and found rest for
their souls.

3. It is consequently very true that the Pharisees and other Jews opposed
law to gospel when, after Christ's coming, they impiously and stubbornly
clung to the ceremonies and used both ceremonial and moral works for
justification. They “rejected God's counsel against themselves” (Luke 7:30),
and when zeal for law made them repel faith in Christ, they “judged
themselves unworthy of eternal life” (Acts 13:46).

All these points are entirely correct and worthy of so great a man. Yet,
with his leave, I must hesitate about other claims and even hold the
contrary.

1. He repeatedly locates Christ's “reformation” under the gospel, at least
in part, in Christ's giving new moral commandments and commanding things
higher and more perfect than the law demanded. This tastes of Romanism and
Socinianism, though I by no means accuse him of either, since I know he has
renounced them many times in his writings. Nor do I intend to argue against
his view here, since it is foreign to the present purpose. Perhaps another
and more suitable occasion for speaking will arise.

2. He asserts that the greater part of many epistles was written against
the Gnostics, to whom he refers almost everything in the epistles everywhere
and even some things in the gospels. I will offer these incidental comments.

[1679 printed p. 158 | IA leaf 166]

It is perhaps not entirely certain that the Gnostics arose so suddenly, much
less that they were then known by this name. Many things in the apostolic
epistles certainly fit their teachings and manner of life remarkably well.
In some places Paul even seems to allude to their name, as in Romans 2:20,
“having the form of knowledge,” and especially 1 Timothy 6:20, “knowledge
falsely so called,” and similar passages, so that the Gnostic name may have
been known at that time. Yet writers give various accounts of their origin.

Irenaeus makes Carpocrates the sect's author. Epiphanius writes that it grew
from the errors of Simon, Menander, Saturnilus, Basilides, Nicolaus,
Carpocrates, and Valentinus. Jerome says in *On Illustrious Men* that
Basilides was their author and that they arose under Emperor Hadrian around
the year 129; Danaeus follows him. But this was thirty years after the death
of John, the last apostle, and more than sixty years after the martyrdom of
Peter and Paul.

Perhaps Jerome meant the time when they became better known by the name
“Gnostics.” Earlier John called them Nicolaitans in Revelation 2:6 and 15,
and others called them Borborites. Augustine and Philastrius make them
disciples of Nicolaus, placing them sixth among the heretics, immediately
after the Nicolaitans and before the Carpocratians and Valentinians. Thus it
is quite possible that they arose and spread their poison in Nicolaus's day,
not only during the old age of John but in the time of Peter, Paul, and the
other apostles.

Simon Magus, the leader and prince of all heresies, lived and spread his
plague in the apostles' time, as Acts and church histories make sufficiently
clear. Eusebius says “the head of all wickedness and beginning of every
heresy began” with him, while Irenaeus says “all heresies arose” from him.
The Gnostics followed Simon's footsteps in both doctrine and life, and
Irenaeus testifies that the knowledge falsely so named of the Simonians had
its beginnings there. We therefore cannot deny that the apostles struck at
them in their epistles: Peter and Jude by attacking their abominable
doctrines and shameful lives, Paul by alluding to the Gnostic name they
claimed, and John by censuring the Nicolaitans by name.

Yet when the learned man drags almost everything in the apostolic letters
and writings to this subject and everywhere interprets it of the Gnostics,
he seems to indulge his favorite theory too much. He suffers the fate of
those who serve a hypothesis excessively and transfer everything they meet
to it: like the man who said every ship entering the harbor was his, or
those who look through tinted glass and think everything they see through
it has the same color.

At least this much I can affirm more confidently: this approach causes him
to expound many apostolic passages differently from other ancient and modern
orthodox interpreters. He attempts to evade and weaken many testimonies
which our most learned and godly writers commonly adduce with sound judgment
against Romanists, Socinians, and other heterodox people. Grotius, whom he
usually follows, did the same. This comes at great cost to truth and to all
Protestants,

[1679 printed p. 159 | IA leaf 167]

especially the orthodox. These incidental remarks may already have been
longer than was fitting.

3. More directly connected with our subject, his earlier statements seem
excessively doubtful or inconsistent. He explains Paul's denial that anyone
is justified by works of the law as law without the pardon and mercy Christ
brought. To explain Paul's words about the law's curse, he adds, “from which
no one but Christ delivers us.” As to the negative part, this is entirely
true and confessed by every Christian: the law and its works, insofar as the
Pharisees opposed them to the gospel, can never justify anyone. Indeed, even
Romanists dare not say that law considered in itself and apart from this
opposition to Christ's gospel can justify anyone without the pardon Christ
obtained.

Yet the affirmative question remains doubtful: Does law apart from the
Pharisaic opposition to gospel—his second meaning—when taken in the first
sense as delivered through Moses and joined with Christ, justify a person
formally or meritoriously?

We concede, first, that insofar as law makes us guilty of sin and death and
thus incidentally sends us outside ourselves to Christ to obtain
justification, it operates toward our justification according to the order
and grace of the gospel. We further concede, second, that we are justified
according to the law, insofar as Christ our surety rendered to law in doing
and suffering everything which by God's indulgence is freely imputed to us.
But Paul does not therefore say that we are justified by works of law which
we perform—the very matter he discusses—even with Christ joined and with God
forgiving our sins and defects for Christ's sake. He says precisely the
opposite. The Romanists nevertheless say this. If the learned man says it
too, I boldly declare that he says the same as the Gnostics whom he attacks
so vehemently. They did not exclude Christ from our justification, but
wanted law joined with Christ to justify us. This is my principal concern.

The remaining points, though no less harmful to the truth we have defended,
depend on the preceding claims and can be recognized more easily. I will run
through them briefly and with a lighter hand.

4. He expounds the “law of works” and “law of faith,” which Paul
distinguishes and opposes, as external and internal law. It is as though,
when Paul denies that the law of works justifies, he excludes from
justification only outward works, on which Jews alone relied as Romanists
today rely upon the work performed, while inward works proceeding from a
heart purified by faith justify us—as the Romanists assert. But we already
defeated this Grotian and thoroughly Roman interpretation by many arguments.

5. Similarly, he explains Romans 3:20, where no flesh is justified in God's
sight by works of law, as observance of the form Moses prescribed insofar as
it differed from Christ's reformation. He further defines it as bare
observance of the external and ceremonial part without the inward and more
substantial part, purity of soul. It is as though Paul excluded only
ceremonial or outward moral works from justification. What is this but
purely Grotian and Roman?

[1679 printed p. 160 | IA leaf 168]

6. By explaining justification “apart from law” and “apart from works of
law” as law insofar as the Jews made it exclude Christ, he seems to affirm
that law in the first sense—as delivered by Moses, not excluding Christ but
joined with him—justifies us. This suffers from the same disease as the
former claim and is rejected for the same reason.

7. In Romans 4:13, “the promise was not made to Abraham through law,” he
interprets law as the command of circumcision. He follows Pseudo-Jerome, whom
Bellarmine judged to be Pelagius: “Here he calls circumcision law.” But every
orthodox writer I know, and even the Romanists themselves, understands the
passage not only of the command of circumcision but adds law taken more
universally.

The preceding verses frequently mention circumcision because Paul had
raised the question whether faith was imputed to Abraham for righteousness
after he was circumcised or while still uncircumcised. Yet he continues the
general controversy treated through so many chapters: whether we are
justified by faith or works of law. Here “law” is taken generally. Some even
think that because Paul uses *nomos* without the article, it includes the law
of nature. It certainly includes the moral law, since verse 15 says this law
works wrath, which most properly fits the violated moral law. Pareus
therefore reasonably expounds it of both natural and written law.

Grotius, whom the learned man usually follows, interprets it of Mosaic law:
“The promise was not made to Abraham under the condition of observing the
law of Moses, which did not yet exist.” Both Grotius and the learned man
identify Galatians 3 as a parallel passage. It is consequently very clear
that Paul does not speak here of the command of circumcision, imposed on
Abraham a few years after the promise, but of the law promulgated 430 years
after the promise, as Paul explicitly says in Galatians 3:17.

We may add only Estius, who writes: “A Jew could say, If righteousness is not
from circumcision, at least it is from law,” openly distinguishing
circumcision from law. “Thus Paul teaches what he taught in the previous
chapter, that righteousness is not from law.” In that previous chapter “law”
did not mean merely the law of circumcision, except by synecdoche insofar as
circumcision, its chief part, signified the whole Mosaic law.

I will not pursue other examples. In Romans 7:4 and 6 Paul says we are dead
to and freed from law, and Romans 8:2 says the law has freed us. The
paraphrast takes these in his second sense: law insofar as the Jews opposed
it to gospel, not law in itself as delivered through Moses. I strongly deny
this. Through

[1679 printed p. 161 | IA leaf 169]

Christ Jesus we are freed from the covenant and curse of law, its
provocation to sin, and its own sterility and inability to produce any good
or obtain righteousness for us—not merely from law insofar as the Pharisees
opposed it to Christ. Because of the weakness of the flesh the law itself is
powerless. For us as fallen people it cannot produce these things, not only
because Jewish wickedness opposes it to faith but because law itself lacks
the power to generate spiritual life in us while dead in sins and to produce
life's operations.
