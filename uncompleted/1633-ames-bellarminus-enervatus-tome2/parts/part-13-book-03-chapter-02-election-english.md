On election.

According to papal practice, pastors are first ordained and afterward elected;
this is just as if some prince were crowned, or a magistrate inaugurated,
before he had been elected. This is the rationale of Bellarmine's method.

16. Bellarmine. *The right of electing ministers does not belong to the people
by divine right. But if at any time the people had any power in this matter,
they had it all from the connivance or concession of the Pontiffs.*

Protestants. I. To the former part of this proposition we oppose the judgment
and arguments of Cardinal Cusanus, and in him those of the whole Council of
Basel. For thus he writes in *On Catholic Concord*, book 2, chapter 18:
*Ecclesiastical rulers ought to be constituted by consent, by divine and human
right. Pope Avitius said that he who ought to preside over all must be
elected by all, distinction 66, Archbishop; and that no one should be given to
those unwilling, since according to Leo no principle allows one who was not
elected to be held among the bishops. Furthermore, in order that one body may
be constituted in concord from subjects and a president, reason and natural
law, together with divine law, require in this spiritual matrimonial union
the mutual consent which we find in the election by all and the consent of the
one elected. He is not a bishop who is not elected. Parish priests also, and
curates, are to be elected. The laws and sayings of the holy Fathers declare
that no one ought to be ordained to a presiding charge unless elected by those
over whom he ought to preside, so that he may know that his presidency has its
origin from those over whom he presides, and so may feed them in love without
pride.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n287 | printed p. 70]

*And in chapter 34: The power of superiors is rooted in the election and
consent of their subjects. A right rule presupposes the free subjection of
Christians, since they themselves are not constrained beyond the bounds of
liberty.* II. Bellarmine's impudence cannot be sufficiently condemned in the
words, *If at any time the people had any power in this matter,* by which he
makes popular election doubtful not only by right but also in fact. For the
Jesuit Azor, part 2, book 3, chapter 28, and book 6, chapter 14, admits *that
there was an ancient rite and custom of electing bishops in which they were
elected in the presence of the people, and indeed sometimes by their votes;
and that this manner of election lasted until Gregory I, and indeed continued
until the times of Charlemagne and Louis the Pious. The Apostles set over the
people ministers approved by the testimony of the people; following the
example of the Apostles, the Church enacted canons that the rulers of churches
should not be elected without the vote of the people.* III. It is a most
disgraceful evasion to refer this to the *connivance* or *concession* of the
Pontiffs, since it is plain that the Pontiffs have no singular authority except
from the connivance and concession of the churches. But all those things which
are known formerly to have been done without the Pope, and which nevertheless
have now been seized by him, the Pontificians wish to have understood as if
the Pope's seizure were of divine right and neglect of the Pope had occurred
by his connivance or concession. This is just as if some thief or robber,
pleading his cause, should allege in his defense that those goods which he
took away from their rightful owners had previously been in their possession
only by his connivance or concession. IV. Since divine right neither
constitutes nor acknowledges the Pontiffs themselves (as is demonstrated in
its proper place), it cannot be that either the election of pastors or
anything else belongs to them by that right.

Bellarmine's arguments.

17. Bellarmine. *Pastors are to be called by God, as Aaron was, Hebrews 5. But
Aaron was elected by Moses alone.*

Protestants. I. Aaron's calling is not established there as a pattern of the
manner in which all pastors are to be called, but as an example of a lawful
calling which in its essence ought to agree with all Pontiffs and also with
pastors. In the Vulgate version *and* is lacking, as Cajetan rightly observed.
But if it is read correctly, *just as Aaron also,* then Aaron is manifestly
introduced as an example or instance to illustrate the general rule, namely,
that all rulers of the Church ought to be called by God. II. If Bellarmine's
gloss were valid, it would follow from it that none of Aaron's successors was
a lawful priest, because no one was elected and called in precisely the same
manner as Aaron, who was both instituted without any hereditary right and
designated by name

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n288 | printed p. 71]

by God, and in addition, as Cajetan notes, confirmed *by the judgment of the
flowering rod and the burning of the envious.*

18. Bellarmine. *Christ willed that the Apostles should govern the Church with
the power and in the manner by which he himself had governed it while present,
John 20:21; but he elected the Apostles and disciples without any counsel or
vote of the multitude.*

Protestants. I. If all these things were granted, the Pope would gain nothing
more than any pastor. II. The likeness noted there—*as the Father sent me, so I
also send you*—does not hold in every quality; *it is not an equality,* Toletus
notes. Although, therefore, this likeness can extend to many things, it most
properly concerns the general essence of calling and the supply of the gifts
which were required for it; it cannot be dragged by any ropes to the mode of
external government.

19. Bellarmine. *It does not belong to sheep to feed and rule; but the people
are sheep, and electing pastors pertains to government and rule.*

Protestants. I. Although election pertains to the establishment of government
and rule, it is nevertheless not an act of rule or government. II. Rational
sheep can elect a pastor for themselves, just as a bride elects a bridegroom
for herself—not by jurisdiction or government, but rather by subjection, as
was explained before from Cardinal Cusanus.

20. Bellarmine. *The Apostles sent bishops created by themselves to absent and
unbelieving peoples.*

Protestants. I. The Apostles instituted no bishops or ordinary pastors in this
manner. II. Cardinal Cusanus answers in book 2: *It is not to be concluded
(from what the Apostles did among unbelievers) that churches were at any time
provided for without election, because at that time they were not yet
churches; rather, men were sent to build churches, and therefore election
could have no place, because the church which would elect was then lacking.
Hence those who were sent, bearing the figure of Christ, following Adam, built
an Eve for themselves from their own rib, that is, from the divine word, and
they espoused her; and so, once Eve was brought forth, thereafter another was
not given unless the bridegroom was lacking.*

21. Bellarmine. *Our same conclusion is proved from the decrees of the ancient
councils: that of Laodicea, that of Pope Martin, the Second Council of Nicaea,
and the Fourth Council of Constantinople.*

Protestants. I. The most ancient of these councils, namely that of Laodicea,
was held in the year 364, and it testifies nothing other than that election is
not to be permitted to disorderly *crowds.* In the others something was
perhaps attempted against the liberty of the people, but in vain, as Azor
admits, who concedes (in the place previously cited) that popular

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n289 | printed p. 72]

election continued until the times of Charlemagne and Louis the Pious. II. In
none of these councils is anything said about *divine right,* or anything
about the Roman Pontiff; they therefore pertain in no way to the proof of
Bellarmine's conclusion.

22. Bellarmine. *The presbyters of Alexandria always named as bishop one
elected from among themselves and placed in a higher rank. Jerome to Evagrius.
Athanasius created Frumentius bishop of the Indians. Rufinus, book 10, chapter
9. Epiphanius ordained Paulinianus presbyter of a monastery without consulting
the brethren. So Augustine was designated bishop of the English by Gregory I,
and Boniface bishop of the Germans by Gregory II.*

Protestants. I. The presbyters of Alexandria are not said to have elected, but
to have named the man elected. That election also was not to the pastorate,
but to a place of rank among the pastors. II. Concerning Frumentius, Rufinus
first relates that through his work a church had been gathered, congregations
held there, and churches built, with no bishop consulted; and that afterward
Athanasius was asked (by Frumentius himself, beyond doubt, and with the consent
of the Indian Church) to provide them with a suitable bishop, according to the
custom of those times. Therefore, although Athanasius ordained Frumentius
bishop of the Indians, perhaps without observing the right order with perfect
accuracy, he nevertheless did not do this either without the consent of the
Alexandrian Church or while doubting the consent of the Indians to the calling
of the man through whose work they themselves had been called. III. The deed
of Epiphanius shows that ordinations at that time were sometimes rushed
forward in an exceedingly perverse and inept manner. *He ordered Paulinianus,
who was unaware and entertained no suspicion at all, to be seized by many
deacons, and his mouth to be held lest, perhaps wishing to free himself, he
should adjure them by the name of Christ. First he ordained him a deacon, and
again, with immense difficulty, while his mouth was held, he ordained him a
presbyter.* These things, therefore, are related by Epiphanius himself in his
letter to John of Jerusalem in Jerome. From them it appears that nothing can
be established about divine right from this example of an ordination so
ridiculous and disorderly, much less from any later singular deed of the
Gregorian age. Yet Epiphanius in the same place sufficiently indicates what
order and right ought to have been observed at that time, when he says that he
*rebuked the brethren because all the brethren did not commend him with their
testimony.*

23. Bellarmine. *The people cannot judge who is suitable for the ministry. The
greater part is evil. Popular election is liable to tumults and seditions.*

Protestants. I. In a well-constituted church the people are not so stupid,
base, and sordid as among the Pontificians,

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n290 | printed p. 73]

much less so wicked. II. A people experienced and
practiced in affairs judges many things no less well than the learned; and in
*Politics* 3 Aristotle answers these very objections concerning the power of
the people, saying *that the whole people is in no respect inferior to one man
or to a few.* III. In judging, the people can, and ordinarily ought to, be
directed by the judgment of other pastors, either preceding or accompanying
the election. IV. We nowhere read that tumults and seditions arose among the
orthodox over the election of a presbyter or of a pastor instituted by Christ,
although they did sometimes occur over the election of bishops who drew from
the pomp of the world. Those arose more from the canvassing and ambition of
the bishops than from the discord of the common people. Yet God's ordinances
are not to be changed because of the troubles to which they are liable;
rather, those troubles are to be guarded against and remedied for the sake of
the ordinances which we are bound to observe. V. If this manner of election
had been so troublesome, the ancients would not have endured it for so long.

24. Bellarmine. *This manner of election by the votes of the people gradually
crept into the Church. In apostolic times pastors were elected without the
people's knowledge. Then the people began to be called upon to give testimony
about the life and character of the person to be elected. Tertullian, Apology,
chapter 39: not by a price but by testimony. Then only those whom the people
had requested began to be ordained. Leo, letters 87 and 89. Finally the custom
prevailed in certain places that the people should even usurp for themselves
the right of voting. Chrysostom, On the Priesthood, book 3. But after this had
been tolerated for some time, and the confusion and unworthiness of the matter
became apparent, election and request were gradually taken away from the
people, and only testimony concerning life and character was left. For even
now, in ordinations, the people are asked through a deacon whether they have
anything against those to be ordained.*

Protestants. I. It is asserted with the utmost impudence that *in apostolic
times pastors were elected without the people's knowledge,* since no testimony,
example, or trace of this kind of election is found in all Scripture. Indeed,
the contrary is so manifest that the Jesuit Azor himself, *part 2, book 6,
chapter 14,* admits *that it cannot be denied that the Apostles observed a rite
by which ministers were set over the Church after being approved by the
testimony of the people, Acts 1, 6, 13, and 14; and that the Church long
preserved this rite and form because it wished to follow the example of the
Apostles.* II. That testimony of which Tertullian makes mention had the force
of election, for it is presented as the principal and sole cause of the honor
of the elders, whence they are also there called *approved elders.* III.
Request differs from election in word only, not in fact. IV. Bellarmine
ridiculously contradicts himself when he teaches that the people's power in
the election of

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n291 | printed p. 74]

pastors gradually increased until the times of Leo and Chrysostom, although
previously he contended that the people had been excluded from
election by the Council of Laodicea and others before these times. V. The right
of the people in voting is slanderously attributed to *usurpation.* For the
Church, as Azor admits in the place cited, *enacted canons that the rulers of
churches should not be elected without the people's vote, or at least their
testimony, assent, request, nomination, or approval.* VI. Nor does Bellarmine
deal candidly when he does not concede that the people possessed the right of
voting except *in certain places and for a certain time, around the age of
Chrysostom.* For Cyprian testifies that in his age this custom was observed
*through nearly all the provinces, letter 68.* Azor also (in the place cited)
affirms the same and shows that it was observed *in Africa, Greece, Spain,
Gaul, and everywhere,* and *lasted until the times of Charlemagne and beyond.*
VII. Contrary to every right and lawfulness, *election and request were
gradually taken away from the people,* because upon them depended, as Azor
observes: *1. that a pastor not be set over unwilling sheep; 2. that the pastor
be loved and honored more by the sheep; 3. that a spiritual marriage between
the pastor and the church be contracted by the mutual consent of both.* VIII.
Although the Pontifical bishops mock God and his Church when, in an unknown
language, they ask for the testimony or judgment of the people concerning the
life and character of those to be ordained—of a people either absent, or not
even knowing them by face or name, or at least belonging elsewhere and caring
nothing about them—nevertheless, by that very act they testify against
themselves what right the people once had in election, and that the ordination
of pastors ought to occur in the very church over which they are set.

Our arguments.

25. Protestants. The keys of the kingdom of heaven are in some manner given to
the whole Church, Matthew 16 and 18; therefore, so is this power of electing
those who use the keys by virtue of office.

Bellarmine. 1. *Peter is said to have received the keys in the person of the
whole Church because he received them for the benefit of the whole Church,
intending thereafter to communicate them to his successors together with all
the other bishops and presbyters.*

Protestants. I. *The School of Paris, fortified by this infallible foundation,
very plainly and constantly taught, in agreement with the mind of all the
ancient Doctors of the Church, that in founding the Church Christ gave the
keys, or jurisdiction, first, more immediately, and more essentially to the
whole Church than to Peter; it belongs primarily, properly, and essentially to
the Church, but instrumentally, ministerially, and only with respect to its
execution to bishops.* Treatise *On Ecclesiastical and Political Power*, part
1. II. It is not sufficient to establish that manner of speaking that the keys
were given for the benefit of the Church, unless the Church can also at least
provide for itself that they be handled by those from

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n292 | printed p. 75]

whose ministry it can receive the greatest benefit. III. What is said—that
Peter or any other minister of the Church *transmits the keys to his
successors,* much less that he can properly *communicate them to others*—is
false. When a Pontiff dies (Bellarmine says in *On the Pontiff*, book 1,
chapter 12), *the keys remain in the hands of Christ.* But no certain and
designated subject intervenes between Christ and the succeeding minister for
their transmission except the church over which he is set. Nor can anything
be more fitting than that the ministers of Christ should receive from the hand
of his bride, as it were, the power to minister to her.

Bellarmine. 2. *The power of binding and loosing is said to be in the Church
because it is in its prelates.*

Protestants. This can indeed be conceded: what ministers possess or do with
the consent of the Church may be attributed to the whole Church. But if the
Church had no part either in the instrumental transmission of this power or
in governing its use, then no reason would allow this manner of speaking. What
Bellarmine adds here about one or more prelates being understood by the name
of the Church was discussed by us before.

26. Protestants. The Christian people have a divine command to hear true
pastors and flee false ones; therefore, they also have a command to elect good
ones and reject pernicious ones.

Bellarmine. 1. *The people indeed ought to distinguish a true prophet from a
false one, but by no rule other than carefully observing whether the one who
preaches says things contrary to those said by his predecessors, or to those
said by other ordinary pastors, and especially by the Apostolic See and the
principal Church.*

Protestants. I. The question now concerns not the rule, but the right and power
of election. II. Bellarmine's rule is altogether Lesbian: it can be adapted to
any heretics after they have continued for some time and spread themselves
abroad, especially if they have some apostolic see favoring them. By this rule
Christ himself was rejected and condemned by the Jews.

Bellarmine. 2. *Since the people are unlearned, they cannot judge the teaching
of a pastor in any other way.*

Protestants. This was the very voice of those who were once Christ's enemies,
John 7:49: *this crowd, which does not know the law, is accursed.* But Christ
sends everyone, even the unlearned, to the Scriptures so that they may form a
judgment from them, John 4:39; and he also promises knowledge of the truth to
those who seek with a devout heart, John 7:17. Nor, however, are all those who
are counted among the people so unlearned as the Pontificians would wish, or
as most of their petty sacrificing priests are accustomed to be.

Bellarmine. 3.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n293 | printed p. 76]

*If the people could judge for themselves, they would not need preachers.*

Protestants. I. First it should be observed how Bellarmine here sophistically
changes the terms so that he may appear to say something. He does not say that
on that supposition the people would not need *pastors,* about whom the
question is being asked, but *preachers,* because among the Pontificians
*priests, confessors,* and especially *bishop-satraps are* pastors (and he
always wishes those to be necessary), although they are not preachers. II.
True pastors have other duties besides the single duty of preaching, and even
those who excel most in judgment need them on account of those duties. III.
The use of preaching also is not only to form judgment, but also to move and
bend the affections of the heart. IV. It is not necessary for the election of
a pastor that everyone among the people be able to judge *by themselves*
without the assistance of those more skilled, much less that they be able to
do this perfectly; but all ought to know enough to flee a false teacher.

Bellarmine. 4. *The people absolutely ought to follow their own pastor rather
than another who teaches contrary things and is not their pastor, even if it
should perhaps happen that their pastor errs.*

Protestants. This is the one thing which certain impious mockers of all
religion among the leading Pontificians especially wish and seek: that the
people follow any masked bishop and petty sacrificing priest, even if he leads
them away from Christ. But although this is impious, the very danger which
threatens the Church from evil pastors proves that the right of election
belongs to it. For Cardinal Cusanus speaks rightly and piously: *A strong
reason is assigned why consent is necessary. It is manifest that knowledge of
the law is to be sought from the mouth of the priests, and furthermore that
the people are not excused by false teaching which is sown through the
ignorance or malice of a priest. Hence, if the consent of the people ought not
to intervene in the selection of a president, they would be unjustly punished
for the ignorance or malice of a priest when their consent never intervened
in his presidency, for nothing could then be imputed to the people
themselves.*

Bellarmine. 5. *The Lord commands that false prophets not be heard by the
people, but not that the people depose them and substitute others.*

Protestants. I. Just as power is rooted (as Cusanus says) in the free
subjection of the people, so also deprivation of power is rooted in the lawful
withdrawal of that subjection. Therefore, when the people are commanded *to
hear* this or that man—that is, not to subject themselves to his ministry—they
are at the same time commanded, so far as the matter itself is concerned, to
deprive him of that ministry.

27. Protestants. Bishops are not lords but ministers of the

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n294 | printed p. 77]

Church, 2 Corinthians 3 and 1 Peter 5. The Church is the pillar and foundation
of the truth, 1 Timothy 3.

Bellarmine. 1. *Bishops are ministers by ruling and commanding.*

Protestants. I. Those who minister to a society by ruling and commanding can,
usually do, and (by common law) ought to be elected by that society. II.
Ecclesiastical ministers cannot lawfully command the Church except insofar as
they declare the commands and mandates of Christ, and this agrees perfectly
with election by the Church. III. At least some things they do are of such a
kind that in them they execute not only Christ's will toward the whole Church,
but also the will of the Church toward particular members, so that they not
only rule but also obey the Church.

Bellarmine. 2. *The Church is the pillar of truth because it always hears
Peter.*

Protestants. I. This impudence is exceedingly ridiculous: the Ephesian Church,
planted by Paul, watered by Timothy, and, so far as can be understood, never
even greeted by Peter, always hears Peter; truth is not truth unless it is
heard from Peter. II. Through whomever the Church heard and received the
truth, it is certain that it has a full right to preserve that truth and to
continue all those functions which are necessary for its preservation.
Otherwise it would not be a *pillar and support* but, as it were, a *lodging
house* of truth. Yet it would not have this right if it could not elect and
institute ministers for itself.

28. Protestants. Not only the Apostles but the whole Church elected Matthias
and Barnabas so that God might take one from among them, Acts 1.

Bellarmine. 1. *This was done by Peter's concession, not by necessity.*

Protestants. I. No more by Peter's concession than by that of the other
Apostles. We do not read in Scripture that Peter alone ever did any such
thing, nor is anything related or indicated about his concession. Peter could
perhaps go before the others with his vote (which is the view of Chrysostom
whom Bellarmine praises here), but he could prescribe nothing to the other
Apostles. II. If the Apostles permitted this to the Church by concession, they
nevertheless sufficiently indicated by their own example what most serves the
Church's use.

Bellarmine. 2. *It was not so much an election as a request that God elect.*

Protestants. A complete election by men could have no place in the calling of
an Apostle; insofar as it could, it was observed.

Bellarmine. 3. *From one example it is not deduced that something is of divine
right.*

Protestants. This can rightly be deduced from one example joined with others,
when its rationale is common and divinely approved. Thus Cyprian, or the
African Synod, in letter 68, pronounces

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n295 | printed p. 78]

concerning this example: *it is observed according to divine teachings.*

29. Protestants. Deacons also are elected by the Church, Acts 6.

Bellarmine. 1. *It is not proved by this example that this is by divine right.*

Protestants. Divine right is that which is promulgated by God, even if it is
done by an example of this kind whose rationale is not singular but common.

Bellarmine. 2. *It was granted to the people by the concession of the
Apostles, as Luke himself openly testifies.*

Protestants. Luke testifies to this neither openly nor obscurely, unless all
those things which the Church did by the institution and direction of the
Apostles were granted to it by concession.

Bellarmine. 3. *The question concerns the election not of deacons, but of
pastors.*

Protestants. The argument, therefore, binds more strongly, as the African
Synod notes in Cyprian in the place cited: *We observe that the Apostles
observed this not only in the ordinations of bishops and priests, but in those
of deacons.* If the Apostles were unwilling to elect even deacons without the
votes of the Church, they certainly were unwilling to thrust pastors upon
churches which did not consent. For there are more and weightier reasons
requiring the Church's consent in the institution of pastors than in that of
deacons.

30. Protestants. The Apostles created presbyters by votes, Acts 14:23,
*χειροτονήσαντες.*

Bellarmine. 1. *The word χειροτονία can signify three things: 1. To elect by
votes. 2. To choose and determine, in whatever manner it may be done, Acts
1:26 and 10:41. 3. To ordain by the imposition of hands, as among
ecclesiastical writers.*

Protestants. I. The first signification alone is proper and native, as
Bellarmine himself admits; the others are figurative. But in interpreting the
Scriptures one must not depart from the native signification of a word unless
it manifestly conflicts with the sense of the context. But no such reason can
be given why in this place the word should signify something other than what
it properly conveys, namely, to elect by votes. II. That second signification
is a metaphor so strained that no example of it can be given except Acts
10:41, where it is transferred from men to God by analogy. But we ought not
and cannot determine from the sense a word has when attributed to God what it
signifies in human use. The contrary is true, because names of this kind were
first imposed upon created things and afterward applied to signify divine
things imperfectly. Even in that place, when this word is applied to God in
the calling of the Apostles, a certain analogy of attestation is suggested
between that calling and the

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n296 | printed p. 79]

ordinary one which is conferred by the votes of men, so that even from this
very transfer of the word it is sufficiently apparent what sense it has in
the ordinary calling of ministers. III. In Acts 1:26 it is not the same word,
but
*συγκατεψηφίσθη,* which also does not signify there (as Bellarmine wishes) *to
cooperate,* as an act of God, but *to be numbered together,* as an act of men,
which is its proper sense. IV. The third signification was in use neither in
sacred nor profane writings in the time of the Apostles. Such a use of the
word is found neither in the Greek version of the Old Testament, nor in the
New, nor in any Greek writers except those of a more recent age. Luke would
therefore have been like a barbarian at that time to those for whom he wrote,
either signifying nothing to them or something other than he intended, if by
*χειροτονίαν* they had understood ordination. V. Among the seventy translators,
in Acts, and in the epistles to Timothy, ordination is consistently signified
by another word, when it is called *χειροθεσία, χειροταθεσία,* or *ἐπίθεσις
χειρῶν;* and in 2 Corinthians 8:19 *χειροτονία* denotes election by votes. It
is not credible that in this single place Luke departed without cause not
only from his customary phrase, but also from the version of the seventy
translators, whose phrases he was accustomed to retain, and confused the
reader with a word having an alien sense. VI. Among the ancient
ecclesiastical writers also, this word signifies election by votes. The
witnesses of this are Theodore Balsamon and Zonaras on Apostolic Canon 1: *In
former times, when it was lawful for the peoples of cities to elect rulers of
sacred things, the people themselves assembled, and some selected this man,
while others selected that one, and so forth. Those electing extended their
hands. The name χειροτονία was taken from this, in which sense the Fathers of
the synods are also found to use it, calling a vote χειροτονίαν.* This
testimony appears in the notes with which Christopher Justellus illustrated
the *Code of Canons of the Church*, page 198. VII. If at a later time it
sometimes signifies ordination, this happens by a metonymy of the subject for
the adjunct, because ordination was an adjunct consequent upon election, or by
synecdoche, because it denoted that whole action of which ordination was a
part. By all these arguments it is proved that neither Bellarmine's second nor
his third sense can be admitted in this place.

31. Bellarmine. 2. *If we take it in the first manner, then it must be noted
that χειροτονίαν does not signify to elect by votes given by others, but by
one's own vote. Luke therefore means that Paul and Barnabas created bishops
not according to the votes of the people, but according to their own votes
alone.*

Protestants. I. No example can be given where *χειροτονία* signifies electing
in an assembly in such a way that the vote of only one or two is expected. II.
We do not deny that Paul and Barnabas cast their votes, but we deny that they
alone did so. III.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n297 | printed p. 80]

The one who presides over a *χειροτονία* is called *χειροτονῶν,* in which sense
it is attributed to chief presbyters or bishops, sometimes in Eusebius, as in
book 6, chapter 43; and in the *Life of Constantine*, book 3, page 146,
according to Stephanus's edition, it is declared what *ἐπιστῆναι τῇ

32. Protestants. Cyprian, or rather the African Synod in Cyprian, letter 68,
among many other things which it brings forward for the people's right in the
election of ministers, has this: *The people especially have the power either
to elect worthy priests or to reject the unworthy; and we see that this very
thing descends from divine authority, and so forth.*

Bellarmine. 1. *Cyprian only means to say that this descends from divine
examples. But examples do not make a thing of divine right; God's precepts
do.*

Protestants. I. Cyprian undoubtedly *meant* to say what he said in express
words: *that this descends from divine authority, and according to divine
teachings.* II. Examples which are commanded or approved by God and rest on
those principles which God commands to be observed make a thing of divine
right just as much as a direct precept does. But such examples are brought
forward and so explained by Cyprian.

Bellarmine. 2. *Cyprian attributes to the people the power of electing and
voting only in the manner of witnesses, not of a judge.*

Protestants. I. This is not *especially to have the power of electing and
rejecting.* II. If this had been so, then the people would be *free from the
contagion of guilt when communicating with a sinful priest,* which Cyprian
denies. III. Then they also would not *be bound to separate themselves from a
sinful prelate,* which he affirms. Certainly all these things would have been
written to the people in vain if it did not belong to the people to judge.

33. Protestants. The Council of Nicaea, in its letter to the Alexandrians in
Theodoret, book 1, chapter 9, attributes to the people the power of election.

Bellarmine. *Nothing else can be gathered from this except that the custom of
admitting the people to election began at that time. It is not thereby
gathered, however, that this is of divine right.*

Protestants. I. The Synod does not speak of *custom,* but in express words of
the Church's *laws and sanctions.* II. It is sufficiently manifest, both from
what was said before and also from the fact that it had such universal
approval from the Council of Nicaea and even from the emperor Constantine in
the letter which he wrote to the Antiochenes in Eusebius, *Life of
Constantine*, book 3, that this custom did not begin at that time. III. Divine
right is proved against the Pontificians in this manner: it is splendidly
illustrated from the nature of the thing.

34. Protestants. Councils, Fathers, emperors, civil and canon laws, histories,
Pontificians, and the Pontiffs themselves, and so forth, give testimony for
the election of the people.

Bellarmine. 1. *They do not testify that it is of divine right.*

Protestants. I. They presuppose and illustrate divine right. II. They testify
that the power which the Pope now arrogates to himself and his men is not of
divine right. III. Among the Pontificians, so ancient and so long-lasting a
custom has the force of divine right in those matters which favor their
tyranny and superstition. *Custom (Bellarmine says in chapter 3) is the best
interpreter of law.*

Bellarmine. 2. *In all these, one or two excepted, no mention is made of
election, but only of request or testimony.*

Protestants. This is both openly false and empty, because request is an
imperfect election, and testimony, necessarily required beforehand as a cause,
has a force plainly similar and equal to it.

Bellarmine. 3. *The people elected by the concession of the Pontiffs.*

Protestants. By the same concession by which they once partook of the cup, or
even the bread itself, in the Lord's Supper.
