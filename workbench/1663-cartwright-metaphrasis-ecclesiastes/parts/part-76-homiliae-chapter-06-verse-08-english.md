VERSE 8. For what advantage does the wise person have over the fool? What is
there for the poor person to enjoy while walking before the living?

Another argument for

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA245 | printed pp. 245–246 | begins before complete restinguendum from divided restinguen- / dum; ends on p. 246 immediately before repeated Vers. 9]

quenching the passion that inflames people to pursue money and riches is taken
from a circumstance common to those who obtain them: fools receive their
fruit equally with the wise. For just as the subject
who possesses riches is the same—namely, either a fool or a wise person—so the
fruit of riches returns to each. A fool is fed and clothed just as comfortably
and delicately as a wise person. Thus in the use of riches the wise person has
no advantage over the fool. He may indeed surpass a poor countryman in his
choice of food and bodily care, but in these things he will not surpass a
fool. Here “wisdom” and “folly” are taken in a different sense from their
usual sense in the Psalms and Proverbs. The wise person is described here as
one who knows how to conduct himself agreeably among people, while the person
who does not know this is regarded here as a fool—not the person who either
orders his life according to the observance of God's commandments or refuses
to do so.

The fact that God has made riches and their use equally available to fools
and the wise should therefore rightly temper still further our zeal for
amassing riches. We consequently learn to devote strenuous effort to those
things by whose possession and use we are distinguished from the wicked and
foolish.
Thus the Gentiles once strove to distinguish themselves from the Jews, and
the Jews from the Gentiles. We wish even by outward marks and insignia to be
as unlike as possible to those whom we hate or despise. We make a great show
of our hatred and immense contempt for the wicked; let us therefore take care
to be distinguished from them. Since we cannot attain this distinction by
riches, nor by anything whatever except piety, let us devote our strength and
vigor to acquiring piety. We should strive for this all the more because by
his immoderate desire for riches, this man who is wise in his own opinion is
more foolish than a fool of the present kind.

In the latter half-verse there appears to be the greatest difficulty. Passing
over the interpretations of others, I would suppose that there is an ellipsis
of the contrary, as in 1 Timothy 4 and often
elsewhere. Thus, just as in the first half-verse Solomon affirmed that riches
contain nothing in which a wise person surpasses a fool, so in the latter he
says that the poor wise person who knows how to conduct himself among people
lacks nothing in which he might be surpassed by a rich person, whether wise
or foolish. For a prudent person, however slender his means, who knows how to
adapt himself to people's customs, lives no less comfortably than someone who
lives amid an abundance of every kind. By his prudence and skill he obtains
enough food and bodily care, and he is free from many troubles that torment
those who abound in wealth. Nor does the disjunctive accent *Zaceph-caten*
prevent the word *iodei* from being connected with the words that follow in
the same verse, since by everyone's agreement the same construction is
supplied from verse 10 of this chapter.

Civil prudence must therefore be preferred to riches. By its aid a person
often lives more comfortably than when riches abound together with civil
prudence. See Ecclesiastes 9:13 and 7:12. There are occasions when a poor
person preserves his life by his prudence, while a rich person equipped with
prudence cannot extricate himself because the designs of others keep him
entangled in his riches as they seek to strip him bare. If civil prudence must
be preferred to riches, how much more must heavenly prudence be preferred.
