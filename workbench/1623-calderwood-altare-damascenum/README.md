# David Calderwood, *Altare Damascenum* (1623)

> **Working edition in progress:** This directory contains an AI-assisted,
> diplomatic-normalized Latin transcription and an independent close English
> translation. Neither has received human review, and neither is
> publication-ready.

## Work identity

- **Author:** David Calderwood (1575–1650), writing in the first edition under
  the anagrammatic pseudonym “Edwardus Didoclavius”
- **Title:** *Altare Damascenum, seu Politia Ecclesiae Anglicanae obtrusa
  Ecclesiae Scoticanae*
- **Edition used:** 1623, without stated place or printer
- **Structure:** Calderwood's large examination of English ecclesiastical
  polity, with Daniel Tilenus's *Paraenesis* quoted and refuted at relevant
  points, followed by separately authored or anonymous Scottish-polity
  documents

## Controlling witness and rights

The controlling witness is the 1623 first Latin edition in the Princeton
Theological Seminary Library, digitized by the Internet Archive as
[`altared00cald`](https://archive.org/details/altared00cald). The volume and its
scan are public-domain source material. Internet Archive page images, not its
OCR, control the transcription.

The cleaner Leiden reset of 1708,
[`altarasce00cald`](https://archive.org/details/altarasce00cald), is a comparison
witness only. It explicitly identifies David Calderwood and calls itself an
edition “more elegant and corrected than the former,” but the digitized copy
is textually defective near the beginning: it jumps from printed p. 3 to
printed p. 6. The p. 3 catchword `Capituli` does not join p. 6 (`Hoc caput
alterum`), so printed pp. 4–5 are absent. Nothing is silently supplied from
that reset.

The 1621 English
[*Altar of Damascus*](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A17571.0001.001?view=toc)
is a much shorter antecedent, not a translation of this expanded Latin work,
and it has not been used as an English wording source. The selected modern
translation of propositions identified in the
[research inventory](../../research/untranslated-latin-treatises.md) is both
partial and CC BY-ND; it likewise has not been used as a wording source.

## Composite map and attribution warning

The controlling volume has 1,088 access-PDF pages (1,090 IA frames). Its
principal boundaries are:

- PDF pp. 1–4: cover, binding, and ownership matter, excluded.
- PDF p. 5: 1623 title page; PDF p. 6 is its verso.
- PDF pp. 7–12: Calderwood's unnumbered address `Lectoribus benevolis`.
- PDF pp. 13–967: the main *Altare Damascenum*, printed pp. 1–955.
- PDF pp. 968–973: index to the main work.
- PDF pp. 974–976: Andrew Melville's separately attributed
  `Anti-Tami-Cami-Categoria`.
- PDF pp. 977–992: the separately attributed letter `De regimine Ecclesiae
  Scoticanae`, by “Hieronymus Philadelphus.”
- PDF pp. 993–1084: the anonymous *Vindiciae* of that letter against John
  Spottiswood.
- PDF pp. 1085–1088: blank or binding matter, excluded.

The main work is itself composite in presentation. Calderwood incorporates and
refutes Daniel Tilenus's *Paraenesis ad Scotos*, and the final chapter directly
addresses Tilenus's taunts and accusations. Quoted adversarial matter must not
be attributed to Calderwood merely because it appears within his book. The
letter on Scottish polity, Melville poem, and anonymous *Vindiciae* are also
distinct documents, not silently part of Calderwood's continuous authorial
prose. Parts 01–31 contain none of the appended documents. Parts 01–26 do not
reach the embedded Tilenus refutation; Part 27 opens Chapter V by reporting
and quoting Tilenus’s claims before Calderwood answers them. Parts 07–31 also
include long passages that Calderwood expressly quotes from Thomas Brightman,
Tilenus, Eusebius, Scaliger, Luther, German theologians, Francis Bacon,
English jurists,
patristic writers, imperial and conciliar legislation, English canons,
canonists, and other named writers; the paired files retain their quotation
boundaries and attributions.

## Editorial method

Latin long *s*, ligatures, line-end divisions, and typographic `u/v` are
normalized. Edition spelling and substantive wording are retained. Running
heads, signatures, catchwords, ornaments, and bare printed page numerals are
omitted. Marginal references and quoted documentary material are retained;
source markers record printed page, IA leaf, and access-PDF page.

The English is a fresh, full translation of the paired Latin, not a summary.
It has not been copied from either the short 1621 English antecedent or a
modern selection.

### Part 10 bounds and evidence

Part 10 begins exactly where Part 09 ends, in the lower part of shared printed

difference, no analogous inequality exists within the other ecclesiastical
functions, and Scripture uses the names and descriptions of bishop and
presbyter interchangeably. It then includes the beginning of his induction
from shared powers, through the power to preach and administer the sacraments
and the question whether presbyters exercise that power as Christ's immediate
ministers or as episcopal assistants and vicars. The pair retains the extended
attributed quotations from Jerome, Downham, Bellarmine, Junius, Tilenus,

The defender of true and Christian ecclesiastical polity describes the order

so forth is vacant, the dean and chapter notify the king that they have been
bereft of a bishop, and humbly ask the royal majesty to grant them power to

[Marginalia: *De Cler.* c. 8.]

Pamelius admits that it lasted to Gregory, indeed to the emperors Charlemagne
and Louis. Baronius reports that episcopal election, usurped by Charlemagne's
predecessors, was restored to its former liberty by a law enacted with Louis,
his partner in empire, found among their ecclesiastical and civil capitularies
at book 1, chapter 84, and inserted in Gratian, distinction 63, chapter 34.

[Marginalia: Ann. 813. nu. 7.]

That capitulary assigns election to clergy and people according to the sacred

election descends from divine authority: *Therefore a people obedient to the
Lord's commands and fearing God ought to separate from a sinful prelate and
not*

[Marginalia: Li. 1. ep. 4.]

*mingle in the sacrifices of a sacrilegious priest, since it especially has
power either to elect worthy priests or reject the
unworthy. We see that this too descends from divine authority: a priest is
chosen in the people's presence before everyone's eyes, and one worthy and
suitable is approved by public judgment and testimony.* Here the people have
power to elect the worthy and reject the unworthy. This letter is not
Cyprian's alone, but that of an African synod and many bishops. They bring
examples from the election of Matthias in Acts 1:23 and of the deacons in
Acts 6:5, where the multitude of believers receives the role of discerning
and electing suitable men. Acts 14:23 may be added, where Paul and Barnabas
are said to create presbyters by votes in every church. For χειροτονέω
properly means to stretch out, extend, or raise the hand, as though τὴν χεῖρα
τείνω, which was done in voting; hence it means not only extending the hand to
vote, but electing a magistrate by votes when construed with an accusative.
As Athenian citizens signified their vote by raising hands at the herald's
request in the forum, so believers did in the church at the presiding
officer's request. It never means laying hands upon someone being ordained or
inaugurated, whether in pagan writers or elsewhere in Scripture. In 2
Corinthians 8:19 it is properly used of one elected by votes; elsewhere it is
metaphorical for deciding or designating in any manner, as in Acts 4, for God
did not choose the apostles by any stretching out or imposition of hands. The
Septuagint expresses inaugural imposition by another term, ἐπίθεσις τῶν
χειρῶν. Luke departs as little as possible from the Septuagint's familiar
words and phrases, then current among Hellenists. We should not believe he
attached a new meaning never before used by Greek authors, and one rejected
by the very custom of voting through outstretched hands. They raised their
hands when signifying a vote, for the herald said, “Whoever disapproves of
this or that, ἀέρτω τὴν χεῖρα, let him raise his hand.” Who could aptly call
placing and therefore lowering hands upon the ordinand's head a raising of
hands? The fathers nevertheless departed from the word's genuine meaning and
called the imposition of hands in ordination χειροτονίαν. Sometimes they
retain its proper meaning, as Pseudo-Ignatius does in the letter to the
Philadelphians, and as the Council of Laodicea does according to Balsamon. Its
canon says: *It is not proper for τὰς χειροτονίας to be made in the presence
of hearers.*

[Marginalia: Can. 5.]

“By Χειροτονίας,” he says, *he here means τὰς ψήφους, that is, votes or
election. Since in elections some disgraceful things are said about those
elected, the synod does not wish hearers to be present.* So he explains it.
Zonaras follows the same interpretation and says on Apostolic Canon 1: *Now
χειροτονία is called the completion of prayers and invocation of the Holy
Spirit when*

[Marginalia: Zonaras in Can. 1. Ap.]

[1623 printed p. 330 | IA leaf 342 | PDF p. 342 | catchword `sacerdotio` omitted]

*one is initiated into priesthood and receives consecration. It is so called
because the pontiff extends his hand, blessing the man selected for sacred
things.* (But that extension is not the rite of consecration from which
ordination should be named; it only precedes it and is not an upward extension
such as was used in voting.) *But formerly αὐτὴ ἡ ψῆφος χειροτονία ὠνόμαστο,
the vote itself was called chirotonia. For when the people of cities were
permitted to elect sacred rulers, they assembled, some choosing one and some
another. For the majority's vote to prevail, the electors are said to have
extended their hands. From the hands thus extended they counted those who had
chosen one or the other, and appointed to the high priesthood the man elected
by the votes of more. Hence the name chirotonia, which the synodical fathers
are also found to use in this way, calling a vote, ψῆφον, chirotonia.* The
church's power in elections to other offices indicates that it possessed a
right in electing pastors. Paul's travelling companion, *χειροτονηθεὶς ὑπὸ τῶν
ἐκκλησιῶν, elected by the churches' votes,* was chosen to carry their gift for
the relief of the Jerusalem church, 2 Corinthians 8:19. And Acts 15:22 says,
*Then it pleased the apostles and presbyters, with the whole church, to send
to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas ἐκλεξαμένους ἄνδρας ἐξ αὐτῶν, chosen men
from themselves, Judas and Silas, with the synod's letter. In elections of
ministers in the apostles' time and by their institution, the people and the
whole multitude had a great part. This is democracy's first and perhaps chief
effect: the multitude itself elects its magistrates and governors,* says
Spalatensis.

[Marginalia: *De rep.* li. 1. c. 22. num. 10.]

He continues: *The church has after its manner something of a democratic form
in itself—not only because men may and customarily are taken even from the
lowest people for ecclesiastical offices and ministries, including the
episcopate if suitable, though this scarcely has the least savor of democracy,
and in this the people are merely passive and derive no power or authority
from it.* He therefore asserts that they actively and executively concur in
their manner in excommunication and had an active part in elections by
apostolic institution; he teaches this more fully in book 3 and proves by many
examples that it was the ancient church's custom and lasted almost to our
times.

[Marginalia: Cap. 3.]

*Indeed, after the Council of Nicaea the church perpetually maintained the
same ancient custom in elections almost to our time, so that election was
made by clergy and people, as is now to be proved from the fathers, events,
councils, and laws, and from the attestations and decrees of Roman pontiffs.*

[Marginalia: *De Rep. Eccl.* l. 3. c. 3. n. 12.]

That the people's part was not only to testify to the candidate's life and
conduct, but also to decide, elect, propose, request, and sometimes assent to
a request, we proved above from Eradius, Ambrose, Flavian, Nectarius,
Evagrius, Proclus, and Alcuin. If the people requested an unworthy or
uncanonical man, Celestine taught in his letter to the bishops

[Marginalia: Dist. 62. c. 2.]

[1623 printed p. 331 | IA leaf 343 | PDF p. 343 | split `Episco-` / `pos` joined]

of Calabria and Apulia that they should be instructed, not deprived of their
right. Spalatensis asserts that this and no other is the meaning of the
Laodicean canon: *the people must not be permitted absolutely to fulfill their
will in electing persons, when they choose even unqualified persons contrary
to the canons.*

[Marginalia: Laod. Concil. can. 13.]

*Martin of Braga understood this canon in that sense. In his collection he
places it at distinction 63 in these terms: “It is not lawful.” The people may
not elect those promoted to the priesthood, but the bishops must judge and
approve whether he is instructed in speech and faith and in the spiritual
life. By election, therefore, he also understands approval. Simple election
is left to the people by the most common right, provided approval or rejection
of the elect belongs not to the people but to the ordaining bishops.*

[Marginalia: Spalat. *de Rep.* l. 3. c. 3. num. 50.]

The church's practice before and after that canon shows its genuine meaning.
No one is so foolish as to advise the clergy to follow the multitude or be
swept away by its errors. But common sense teaches that a bishop must not be
forced upon an unwilling church, *lest an unwilling people despise or hate an
unwanted bishop and become less religious than is fitting because they were
not permitted to have whom they wished.* The Council of Paris in 559 decreed:
*Because ancient custom is neglected and the canons violated in some matters,
it has pleased us that the canonical decrees be observed according to ancient
custom. Let no bishop be ordained against the citizens' will unless the
election of people and clergy has sought him with fullest consent; and let him
not be intruded by princely command or on any condition against the will of
the metropolitan and the bishops of the province.* Princes had then begun
gradually to invade the church's rights when they saw bishops become powerful
in worldly affairs and rivals of magnates in many places. At first they wished
only to be counted among those consenting and ratifying a completed election,
and initially not even that; but finally they arrogated all power to
themselves, leaving in some places to the chapter a shadowy election—or rather
approval—of the nominee. Someone may say that the people's power was
transferred to the king with the people's consent, since all the kingdom's
citizens consent through their delegates in parliament to the law enacted
about this form of election. So Whitgift of Canterbury replies. But
Cartwright answers that this is precisely the question: whether they did so
lawfully. Has this egg of papal election lost its poisonous nature because
Parliament sat upon it? The citizens' consent is given once in parliament
when the law is enacted; particular consent is required whenever a pastor is
elected. A parliament of the estates is not an assembly of the church. The
national assembly of the church—that is, a lawful and free national council,
especially where the church is reformed—must define this form of election as
lawful before anything concerning it is enacted in parliament; that is, this
form

[1623 printed p. 332 | IA leaf 344 | PDF p. 344 | split `electi-` / `onis` joined]

of election can be enacted in parliament if it was first instituted by a
council of the church, where the church is represented as an ecclesiastical
and spiritual community, not in a parliament of the estates. I say this on
the supposition that particular churches can surrender their right and can do
so only following a decree of a general synod. But they cannot transfer that
right—especially a perpetual and almost hereditary right of election—to a
national council, much less to the prince. For, as Cartwright says, the right
of election is part of those liberties which Christ purchased by his blood;
it may no more be transferred or alienated than the inheritance of the
kingdom of heaven to which it is attached.

[Marginalia: 2 Repl. 1 part. p. 226.]

The church must take care concerning all means of salvation. It ought,
therefore, to elect a pastor who is one among a thousand, as Job says—the most
excellent, holy, learned, and vigilant it can—and not depend on others'
judgment, especially that of princes, or rather courtiers. Canonists and
imperialists fight each other, the former for the pontiff and the latter for
the emperor. On either side there is flattery; few stand for the church.
Because pontiffs despoiled churches of this liberty, emperors and princes
should not claim it for themselves but, as the church's foster-fathers and
advocates, if they delight in these titles, restore it to the churches. Let
them exact taxes and receive regalian rights or other civil jurisdictions
annexed to the temporalities of episcopates, even those of lower courts; but
let them leave the election of pastors—no small liberty of the church, on
which its welfare depends—to the churches, lest they be charged with greater
sacrilege than if they plundered the estates of a thousand episcopates.
Experience loudly declares that churches flourish more or less according as
election is more or less free, and flourish most where, under the presbytery's
instruction and guidance, leading citizens and faithful commoners together
request and elect, or possess the power Cyprian describes of receiving the
worthy and rejecting the unworthy. But when, while they sleep unwillingly,
groan, yet dare not speak, a bishop is set over them whom the papal or princely
court has sent, anyone who does not see that Epicureanism is brought into the
church from one side and popery from the other—or popery from the first and
Epicureanism from the second—is blind. Let the reader see more on episcopal
election above at pp. 6–9.

We spoke of royal assent at p. 21. We do not condemn royal assent simply, for
we know that emperors and princes sometimes gave their assent to the free
election of clergy and people. But we wholly deny that election so depends
upon the prince's assent that, if assent is denied, every election becomes
void, or only the election he strengthens by assent has force. The former
grants the prince a privative power in matters Christ granted primarily and
immediately to the church, so that the church would have no pastors if the
prince so pleased. But the prince has only a power

[1623 printed p. 333 | IA leaf 345 | PDF p. 345 | split `potesta-` / `tem` joined]

cumulative, not privative, in the liberties Christ delivered to the church.
The latter, if it does not remove every election, at least removes free
election; it makes no difference whether the prince elects beforehand or
gives assent afterward, for they come to the same thing. Pamelius said above
that if the people requested or elected someone contrary to the canons, clergy
and people were customarily summoned repeatedly until they agreed upon a
suitable man. But if someone is canonically elected by clergy and people, it
would be unjust for the prince to render the election useless at his mere
pleasure. The election of city magistrates does not so depend upon the supreme
magistrate's will. Moreover, what need is there of the prince's particular
assent in every election when the lawful form of election previously received
by the church can be enacted by authority of laws—that is, by consent of
prince and estates in parliament?

We treated the bishops' confirmation by the metropolitan at pp. 50 and 54.

Three matters must be considered in the consecration of bishops: (1)
consecration itself, (2) the consecrator, and (3) the rite of consecration. As
to the first, we showed that bishop and presbyter have one and the same
ordination, and that no essential difference has yet been introduced into the
church, not even under popery itself. We proved bishop and presbyter to be of
the same order; therefore a bishop does not need a new ordination, or, as they
call it, consecration. Bonaventure says: *A man is not so properly said to be
ordained when promoted to the episcopate as when promoted to the priesthood,
but is more properly said to be consecrated,* because, as he shortly before
said, *just as he receives no other keys, so he receives no other order.* And
below: *A priest is not anointed from necessity of order, but rather for
solemnity, that he may worthily consecrate the most sacred body of Christ.*
Again: *As conferral of the pallium does not give an archbishop a new order
beyond that of bishop, so conferral of staff, ring, mitre, or anything similar
does not give an order beyond that of priest. Such things are sacramentals
rather than sacraments. For sacramentals do not only precede a sacrament; they
can also follow it.*

[Marginalia: In lib. 4 dist. 25. qu. 3.]

The bishop therefore received the sacrament of order or ordination when he
was ordained presbyter. What more does he now receive but certain theatrical
sacramentals instituted not by Christ but by the church? In the first century
after the apostles there was no promotion to the episcopate other than by
election. Willet also gathers this from Ambrose and Jerome: Ambrose says,
*There is one episcopate and presbyterate and the same ordination*; Jerome
says, *At Alexandria the presbyters called one elected from themselves and
placed in a higher rank the bishop.*

[Marginalia: Synop. pag. 241.]

Albinus Flaccus Alcuinus gathers from the same words of Jerome that a bishop's
consecration was nothing other than election. *What episcopal consecration was
in antiquity is shown*

[Marginalia: Libro de Divinis officiis cap. 36. vel pag. 53.]

[1623 printed p. 334 | IA leaf 346 | PDF p. 346 | split `Hierony-` / `mus` joined]

*by Jerome in his letter to Evagrius, saying: At Alexandria, from Mark the Evangelist to
Heraclas and Dionysius, the presbyters always called one elected from
themselves and placed or stationed in a higher rank the bishop, just as an
army makes an emperor and deacons elect from themselves one they know to be
industrious and call him archdeacon. An archdeacon has the same consecration
as the other deacons, but is placed over them by the brethren's election.*
Jerome likewise says on the epistle to Titus: *Before schisms arose in
religion and it was said among peoples, and so forth, it was decreed
throughout the world that one elected from the presbyters should be set over
the others.* As an archdeacon with the same consecration as other deacons is
set over them by the brethren's election, so a bishop with the same
consecration as the other bishops—that is, presbyters whom Scripture calls
bishops—was set over them by the brethren's election. This Albinus Alcuinus,
Bede's disciple, flourished around 780. As to the second point, the ordainer
or consecrator: granting without conceding that a new consecration must take
place, we say that any bishop can be consecrated by any bishop and this is not
peculiar to the metropolitan. We proved this above at pp. 54–55, and there
showed from Bede that presbyters also consecrated bishops. Indeed, if we
granted the hierarchists that Timothy was an ordinary bishop, 2 Timothy 4
would prove that presbyters also laid hands upon bishops in their ordination,
for in the previous chapter we taught that *presbytery* means an assembly of
presbyters. The Rhemists admit that from this passage of Paul was derived the
canon of the fourth Council of Carthage permitting presbyters in a
presbyter's ordination to place their hands beside the bishop's hands. Their
claim that the lesser must be blessed by the greater is therefore worthless.
And how does a bishop consecrate a bishop, even an archbishop, and the bishop
of Ostia the pope? Let Bonaventure answer: *A bishop blesses a bishop. It must
be said that there is no impropriety in this, because he is blessed only
insofar as he lacks episcopal order or dignity.* The consecrating presbyters
are greater than the presbyter being consecrated bishop.

[Marginalia: In 4 sentent. Dist. 25. q. 1.]

The theologian of Auxerre says in book 4 of his *Summa*: *If there were only
three simple priests in the world, one of them would have to consecrate
another bishop and the other archbishop. Presbyters, like archbishops, receive
the keys of the kingdom of heaven in their ordination, for they too are
successors of the apostles.* Presbyters could do this in no case unless they
possessed that power of consecration by virtue of their presbyteral order; but
enough was said about this in the preceding chapter. As to the number of
consecrating bishops, three are required here. But it was not so among the
ancients. *They said that bishops' ordinations and depositions must take place
in councils. If any such thing were done elsewhere, they held it null. Divine
tradition,* says Cyprian, *and apostolic ordination must be observed*

[Marginalia: Sutclivius *de Pontif.* lib. 1. cap. 1. pa. 62.]

[1623 printed p. 335 | IA leaf 347 | PDF p. 347 | split `servandum` / `& tenendum` joined]

*and maintained, as is maintained among us and through almost all provinces:
for ordinations duly celebrated, the nearest bishops of the same province
must assemble with the people for whom the prelate is ordained. Traces of the
same custom appear in the acts of many synods,* says Sutlivius. *But when that
custom gradually began to become obsolete, the custom nevertheless remained
that individual bishops were ordained not by the Roman bishop alone but by
two or three bishops; the apostolic canons commanded this to be observed.* It
is common knowledge that those canons are spurious. The fourth canon of Nicaea
decreed that if all the bishops of a province could not assemble because of
urgent necessity or length of journey, ordination was to be performed by
three, while the absent bishops cast their votes and gave written assent.
Balsamon understands χειροτονίαν in this canon as election, not χειροθεσίαν,
consecration. In any case, whether for election or ordination, all were to be
notified to assemble; those unable to come signified their consent by letter,
and election and consecration were completed in the same action. But the broad
English dioceses prevent ordinations from taking place in a synod of the
bishops of the province, as we said above at pp. 54–55. We also treated the
rites of consecration at pp. 56 and 58. The earlier edition of the Book of
Ordinations, subscription to which is required according to the Lincoln
ministers' interpretation of Article 36, commands that in consecration and
ordination they use an alb, cope, surplice, and pastoral staff.

[Marginalia: Lincol. epitome pa. 102.]

The author of the petition to the queen reports that they use the surplice,
more rarely the cope, and never the pastoral staff; the Lincoln ministers,
however, say it is possible that some use them all.

[Marginalia: Petit. p. 66.]

In any case, that ordinal commands the use of them all. It also directs the
book of the Gospels to be placed upon the head of the man consecrated and the
pastoral staff delivered into his hand, as the same author of the petition
reports, though now they deliver the Bible into the consecrated man's hand.
Yet with his staff he drives more than with food he feeds, and with the staff
he does not repel dogs and wolves, but strikes and casts out his fellow
pastors.

[Marginalia: Petitio p. 5.]

The fourth Council of Carthage, canon 2, does indeed say that *when a bishop
is ordained, two bishops shall place and hold the book of the Gospels over
his head and neck; while one pours a blessing over him, all the other bishops
present shall touch his head with their hands.* But that council is spurious,
as we have often said. Fulke says those canons were patched together from
decrees of Roman pontiffs.

[Marginalia: *Contra Rhemist.* in 1 Tim. 3. sect. 7.]

Albinus Alcuinus says: *That two bishops hold the book of the Gospels over his
head and, while one pours a blessing over him, the other bishops present
touch his head with their hands, is found in neither ancient nor new
authority, nor in Roman tradition.*

[Marginalia: Lib. *de divinis officiis* p. 62. 63.]

Let the reader therefore see what judgment must be made about the fourth

[1623 printed p. 336 | IA leaf 348 | PDF p. 348]

Council of Carthage. It does occur in the Roman Order at p. 72, but the preceding evidence shows
that it was either composed or interpolated after Alcuin's time. Amalarius
Fortunatus, bishop of Trier, says: *The little book according to whose order
ordination is celebrated among some says that two bishops shall hold the
Gospel over his head, something neither ancient authority intimates, nor
apostolic tradition, nor canonical authority.*

[Marginalia: *De Eccles. offic.* li. 2. cap. 14.]

Durandus says the bishop is admonished by this rite that *he ought not to be
reluctant to bear the burden of evangelical preaching everywhere.*

[Marginalia: Durand. in *Rational.* lib. 2. c. 11. n. 8.]

Let the reader also see the blessing of the staff in the same Roman Order.
When it is given, the Order says: *Receive the staff of the pastoral office,
the power of binding and loosing; be persevering in correcting vices, hold
judgment without anger, soothe the minds of your hearers in fostering
virtues, and in tranquility do not abandon the censure of severity.* Yet the
Roman pontiff does not use the pastoral staff, *both because of the history,*
says Durandus, *if it is lawful to believe it, and because of the reason*—a
ridiculous one; let the reader see it, for I am weary of transcribing it.

[Marginalia: *Ration.* lib. 3. c. 15. num. 14.]

I think conferral of staff and ring crept in among the rites of consecration
when the custom was introduced of investing bishops and, as it were, inducting
them into possession of temporalities by staff and ring—a rite of investiture
which some think arose from the Lombards together with feudal law. In the
consecration of bishops there is a sacramental sign. But sacramentals and
symbolic signs in divine worship will be treated in their own place—for they
regard consecration as a sacrament, while we regard it as a religious
action—as will litanies, collects, readings, and similar things. We shall also
speak of priestly vestments in general in a more suitable place. It should be
observed in passing that Albinus Alcuinus reports, among other things, that the
elect is brought in by the archdeacon dressed in a dalmatic and the pontiff
then says: *All the clergy and the consenting people of the city with its
adjacent districts have elected this presbyter or deacon to be consecrated
their bishop.* He says *with its adjacent districts*; consequently the consent
not only of the city but also of the neighboring region, at least its suburbs,
was employed in the election.
