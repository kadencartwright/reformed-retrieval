SECTION TEN NOTION VIII

PERKINS

God’s will is known not only from the written Word or revelation, but also from the event. For what happens, happens for this reason: because God willed it to happen.

ARMINIUS 1

From an event it cannot be concluded that God willed something, but only either this, or that he was unwilling to prevent the event which he foresaw would happen.

2. Otherwise the distinction between God’s action and permission is abolished. For some things happen because God does them, but others because he allows them to be done, according to Augustine and the truth of the matter itself. Yet willing that something happen and being unwilling to prevent its happening are not the same. In the former way the event is resolved into God’s will as its first and principal cause; in the latter way it is resolved affirmatively into a secondary cause and negatively into the divine will, insofar as it did not prevent it. Such prevention also occurs either through power according to the manner of nature or through persuasion according to the manner of the free will. But permission and prevention will be treated more fully later in their proper place.

RESPONSE 1

But what Perkins says seems to me by no means alien to reason. And, unless I am mistaken, the matter concerning every good event is placed beyond controversy. Likewise, we agree that it is no less true concerning all events arising through natural and necessary causes. Indeed, it is true concerning all free acts as well, provided that they have not been sinful.

[Praefatio; printed p. 30; BSB canvas 38]

For God brings all of these about, with not even the adversaries resisting, and therefore wills them to be done. Finally, this is true concerning all sinful acts as well—I mean the acts themselves that underlie the sin. Arminius subjects all of these to divine efficiency. Hence it appears at once that all things which happen do so with God willing them to happen, since sins as sins cannot be included univocally together with other things under the term *things*, because they are the disorders of certain things—namely, of actions or acts—rather than things. Finally, that sins themselves as sins, that is, malice itself, are done with God willing can be proved both by Augustine’s express authority, which Arminius corrupts throughout—for in *Enchiridion to Laurentius*, chapter 95, it reads thus: “Nothing, therefore, happens unless the Almighty wills it to happen, either by allowing it to happen or by himself bringing it about”—and from Arminius’s own confession (what does he expressly profess? That “God willed Ahab to fill up the measure of his crimes”; for from this it evidently follows that God willed Ahab to sin), and then by a twofold argument.

[Marginalia: p. 162.]

1. God, when intending an end of such a kind that it cannot be procured without the existence of sin, must will sin to exist. This is proved from Arminius, p. 102: “For in order that God may attain the end of his ordering, it is necessary that he administer the entire affair in such a way that this end results from it.” But God intends an end of such a kind that it cannot exist without sin, such as the display of either his justice or his mercy. Although Arminius is accustomed to deny this, it can nevertheless easily be proved from Arminius himself, since Arminius himself admits what is equivalent to it. For he acknowledges on p. 162 that “the proper, immediate, and adequate cause why God permitted Ahab to perpetrate the slaughter of Naboth was that he might fill up the measure of his crimes.” Consequently, who does not see that this was done by God toward this end, that Ahab might heap up upon his own head the measure of divine wrath, or of divine judgments? But what else is this than a very great display of divine justice in Ahab’s condemnation? Likewise, on p. 164 he teaches that Ben-hadad’s release by Ahab was permitted by God insofar as it was a sin. Do you ask toward what end? Consider what follows: “For he willed Ahab to heap up wrath for himself unto the day of wrath because of his grave crimes.” Again, on p. 27 he identifies the cause why God permits sin as God’s ordering, “so that the permitted sin may in fact be a means of illustrating his glory.” But how will this illustration of his glory occur except either in the manner of mercy that spares or of justice that punishes? And we have noted on that same passage that the things he philosophizes about concerning the second cause of the permission of sin—which he determines to be the display of divine perfection—cannot admit any other sound sense.

2. The second argument is this: the one who denies efficacious grace for avoiding sin, although he can confer it and can do so without any loss to freedom of choice—and from the denial of which sin must occur—must be said to will that sin happen. But whenever sin occurs, God has already denied efficacious grace for avoiding sin, although he could have granted it with freedom of choice unimpaired. Likewise, sin must happen from the denial of this grace.

Now all of these propositions are true according to Arminius himself.

1. Namely, whenever sin happens, God did not at all graciously bestow efficacious grace for avoiding sin.

2. Efficacious grace also does not at all impede the exercise of the human will’s freedom. For although they are accustomed to object to us that efficacious grace, according to our interpretation of it, cannot be consistent with freedom of the will, they nevertheless profess that this grace, constituted according to their own doctrines, is by no means repugnant to freedom. But in this passage I frame the argument according to their own opinion on this matter.

3. Finally, from the denial of efficacious grace, sin must occur. For from permission it is necessary that what is permitted be done; otherwise it would not have been permission, as Arminius teaches on p. 153. But according to Arminius himself, the permission of sin is nothing other than the denial of efficacious grace for avoiding sin. For permission in general is the suspension of an efficacious impediment. But efficacious grace alone is an efficacious impediment to avoiding sin. Therefore the suspension, or denial, of an efficacious impediment to avoiding sin is the suspension, or denial, of efficacious grace for avoiding sin.

2. But let us consider Arminius’s arguments to the contrary. “Otherwise,” he says, “the distinction between God’s action and permission is abolished.” On the contrary, I say, it is not abolished. For positing a genus does not abolish the distinction between its species. But something’s happening with God willing it stands as a genus to these two species: [Something’s happening because God brings it about, and something’s happening because God permits it.] For God wills something to happen either by bringing it about himself or by permitting it to happen. Augustine himself, whom Arminius both here and elsewhere violently drags into support of his own opinion, testifies to this clearly. For although he recognizes that some things happen because God brings them about and others because God permits them, that same Augustine nevertheless also professes that both happen with God willing: “Nothing happens unless the Almighty wills it to happen.” For although he considered—and considered seriously—that of the things which happen, some happen because God brings them about and others because that same God permits them, he nevertheless openly professes in the same passage concerning them all: “Nothing, therefore, happens unless the Almighty wills it to happen.” We have established this still more forcefully in what follows. Nor in this way is the event resolved into God’s will as its principal cause, for it is resolved into God’s will only as a cause that does not grant an efficacious impediment. But a cause that does not grant an efficacious impediment is not a principal cause. Or if it is to be regarded as an efficacious cause, Arminius will be constrained by this very point just as much as we are. For according to Arminius, no sin is said to happen unless God has first denied an efficacious impediment, that is, unless he has first permitted it to be done. Further, for God to will sin in this manner is nothing other than for God to will that the creature do or omit something by whose action or omission it is constituted a sinner; in these points nothing occurs that is not also consistent with the tenets of the Arminians. In sum, two absurd doctrines have the appearance of following from this teaching: one, that God must therefore be implicated in the guilt of sin; the other, that the human being must be freed from guilt. But neither of these is deduced by a sound consequence. For God does not himself sin by willing that another sin by his permission. For he is not restrained by any law, and consequently he has no law which he transgresses. Indeed, it can happen that without sin a human being wills another person to do that by which he is constituted a sinner, as whenever he seeks money from a usurer to be repaid with interest. For it can happen that the one who lends money in this way sins, while the one who receives the money nevertheless does not sin. In the same way Christian princes do not sin whenever, in making a peace treaty with the Turk, they require a Muhammadan oath from him as security for the treaty, although by requiring this they will that to be done which cannot be done by the Turkish Emperor without grave sin. The second difficulty has a greater appearance of truth, but only an appearance. For if a person who sins is to be freed from the guilt of sin because God wills him to sin by his permission, then the reason will surely be either that the person’s action conforms to God’s will, or that through God’s will the creature’s will does not act freely. But neither of these can be said. For the conformity of our action that protects it from sin is conformity to a divine command or prohibition—which indeed is customarily called God’s will, but improperly—not conformity to God’s will properly so called. For God can will, with his will properly so called, the death of our father or ruler; yet if we should at any time desire this, notwithstanding that conformity, we would be bound by a most grievous sin. Nor will it result from God’s will that whatever someone does or omits in sinning is done any less freely, a point which we shall deliberately treat and demonstrate in what follows. And indeed Arminius acknowledges that we will prevail in this case provided that we establish this; but if we do not establish it, I do not refuse that we should lose our case.
