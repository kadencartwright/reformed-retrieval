## Chapter IV. On the Comparison of the Sacraments

Question 2 concerns the comparison of the sacraments of the New and Old
Testaments: whether both the former and the latter, by God's institution,
operated with efficacy of the same kind, though not of the same degree. We
affirm this. Some papists deny it with an exception for circumcision, such as
Lombard, Alexander of Hales, Bonaventure, Scotus, Durandus, Biel, Paludanus,
and Suárez himself. But Bellarmine, with Thomas and others, denies it
absolutely, without any exception.

Bellarmine 1. The prophets teach throughout that the old sacraments did not
jus—

[1632 witness | printed p. 27]

—tify. The apostles call them useless, weak, beggarly, and shadows.

Protestant reply. I. When Scripture reproves the old sacraments as useless,
it does not speak of them according to God's institution and their lawful
use, but as they were wrongly understood and employed by the Jews without
faith, repentance, and obedience.

Bellarmine 1. That usefulness from the work of the one performing it is
common to every good work. But the question is whether those sacraments were
useful in addition by divine institution.

Protestant reply. And we answer that they were useful in a distinctive way by
divine institution, but to those who used them rightly and lawfully.

Bellarmine 2. Paul distinguishes the new sacraments from the old in such a way
that he says ours save, whereas the old are beggarly, and so forth.

Protestant reply. No such distinction or opposition is directly employed
anywhere. What is said of the new over the old concerns the doctrine of the
gospel, to which the new sacraments are appendages, not the sacraments
considered in themselves. In this sense the ministers of the New Testament
are likewise everywhere extolled above the ministers of the Old Testament,
although their persons possess no efficacy above the prophets.

Bellarmine 3. In those passages the apostle says that the ceremonies were
useless for justification when he says that they were shadows and figures of
our realities. Therefore he intends that they were useless as they had been
instituted by God.

Protestant reply. These two things are most properly joined as the question
and the third argument: the sacraments did not justify by themselves, because
they were instituted as figures of Christ who justifies.

Bellarmine 4. Paul compares circumcision with uncircumcision. But
uncircumcision is not only nothing without faith; it is absolutely nothing
and avails nothing.

Protestant reply. Bellarmine therefore maintains that circumcision instituted
by God availed no more in any respect than if it had not been instituted, and
consequently that God's institution itself was nothing and availed nothing.
But the apostle intends no more than that, under the New Testament,
circumcision neither benefits nor harms a faithful person.

2. Protestant argument 2. Paul also sometimes speaks of the sacraments of the
Old Testament not absolutely but comparatively. For he did not intend to
diminish the efficacy of those sacraments but to magnify the efficacy of ours,
which give richer grace than they did.

Bellarmine. If ours differed from the old only in that ours gave greater grace
and they less, Paul would not rightly say that they are nothing and avail
nothing. Nor would he compare circumcision with uncircumcision or distinguish
the old sacraments from the new as a shadow from the body.

Protestant reply. Paul nowhere says that circumcision was ever absolutely
nothing. For, as Cajetan rightly observes

[1632 witness | printed p. 28]

on 1 Corinthians 7, “It is blasphemous to say that what belongs to the divine
covenant and what belongs to the divine command is nothing for salvation.”
Nor does Paul equate uncircumcision with circumcision under the Old Testament,
nor make the old sacraments shadows of the new sacraments, but shadows of
Christ.

3. Protestant argument 3. Paul also sometimes speaks of the sacraments of the
Old Testament with reference to the time when they had already been abrogated
through Christ.

Bellarmine. In Romans 2 Paul says, “Circumcision indeed profits if you observe
the law,” but this belonged to circumcision while it was still in force. In 1
Corinthians 7 he speaks of those who had been circumcised while the law was in
force.

Protestant reply. In the former passage, as in many others, we concede that
the law in force is in view. But in 1 Corinthians 7 it is plainly held, as
Cajetan says, “that circumcision ceased to be the matter of the covenant and
of the divine command. It is indeed blasphemous to say that what belongs to
the divine covenant and what belongs to the divine command is nothing for
salvation.”

4. Protestant argument 4. The ceremonies of the law are called shadowy in
relation to the fulfillment they would possess in Christ and with respect to
the manner in which they signified future realities.

Bellarmine 1. Paul calls the ceremonies not *shadowy* but *shadows*.

Protestant reply. A metaphorical shadow and a shadowy thing are one and the
same.

Bellarmine 2. By the same reasoning our sacraments will be shadowy, because
they represent the resurrection and future glory.

Protestant reply. They too are shadowy mirrors and riddles with respect to
future glory, 1 Corinthians 13:12, although considered absolutely they possess
the character of light.

Bellarmine 3. Those words, “it is nothing,” “it avails nothing,” and similar
expressions can only be explained in terms of efficacy. The apostle's dispute
with the Jews also concerned efficacy, not the manner of signifying.

Protestant reply. Paul therefore denies that the old ceremonies possessed the
efficacy the Jews wished them to possess, and he proves this from the fact
that they were shadows signifying the efficacy of the Christ who was to come.
From the affirmation of their shadowy signification, Paul infers the denial of
the kind of efficacy the Jews desired. Bellarmine, on the contrary, infers
from the denial of efficacy the denial of such signification, or at least
that Paul does not designate it by the title *shadow*.

5. Bellarmine. The sacraments of the old law had no absolute promise of grace
attached to them. Hebrews 8:6.

Protestant reply. The old sacraments—those, that is, which were proper and
ordinary sacraments—had as absolute a promise of grace as the new, but one
less clear, less immediately ample, and less efficacious. In this respect the
New Testament is said to have been established upon better promises.

[1632 witness | printed p. 29]

6. Bellarmine. The old law did not justify; therefore neither did its
sacraments.

Protestant reply. The law did not justify insofar as it was distinguished from
the promise. But the promise contained in the law, and the sacraments which
presented that promise, justified in the same manner as ours.

7. Bellarmine. The promises attached to the old sacraments were fulfilled
even if people did not believe.

Protestant reply. They were not fulfilled for the consolation and salvation
of those who did not believe.

8. Bellarmine. Our sacraments are said to save, regenerate, and so forth.
Nothing like this is read concerning the old sacraments.

Protestant reply. Because the old sacraments were less clear, these things are
also said less clearly of them. They are said, nevertheless, as will presently
appear.

### Our Arguments

9. Protestant argument. From its first institution circumcision had attached
to it the promise than which none is greater: “I will be your God, and the God
of your offspring after you.” Genesis 17. Christ interprets it in Matthew 22
in such a way as to teach that eternal life is contained in it, and in
Ephesians 2:12 Paul shows that living hope depends upon it.

Bellarmine 1. Abraham was most righteous before circumcision. Therefore the
promise to him did not concern the remission of sins.

Protestant reply. I. Abraham was not so righteous by inherent righteousness
that he could not become more righteous. Moreover, this is “the common
teaching of the theologians”—he means the scholastics—“that the sacraments
confer grace, either by producing it where they do not find it already
produced, or by increasing it when it is already produced.” Cajetan on 1
Corinthians 10. He was not so justified through the remission of sins that
this remission and righteousness could not be sealed more and more, still
with benefit. 2. Scotus answers in Book 4, distinction 1, question 6: “Just as
if the Blessed Virgin, in the fullness of grace, had been baptized, she would
not have received any new grace there, and yet baptism would not then have
been a false or uncertain sign, because it would signify either that grace was
then produced or that grace given earlier was present, so circumcision would
not be a false or uncertain sign even if it did not give grace, but would be a
sign of grace already present.” Durandus likewise teaches in Book 4,
distinction 4, question 8 that one baptized with the baptism of the Spirit is
still bound to be baptized with the baptism of water.

Bellarmine 2. We read that when God enjoined circumcision upon Abraham, he
promised him only earthly things according to the letter, that is, the
propagation of his posterity and the land of Palestine.

Protestant reply. We read in the apostle Paul, Galatians 3, that the blessing
promised to Abraham was plainly the same as that by which believers from the
Gentiles are now justified.

[1632 witness | printed p. 30]

Bellarmine 3. By those words, “I will be your God,” only a promise of special
protection is signified. In this manner God was the God not only of the good
but also of the wicked, provided they belonged to the people of Israel.

Protestant reply. Special protection does indeed extend in some measure to the
wicked who are within the church. But it is plain from Jeremiah 24:7, 31:33,
and 32:38 that something more is contained in that promise, for there all the
things that concern the new covenant are summarily explained by the same
phrase. For God is our God by communicating beatific goodness to us, and we
are God's people by rendering to him fitting faith and obedience.

Bellarmine 4. In Matthew 22:32 Christ signified nothing other than that God is
the God of those who exist, whether they are good or wicked, blessed or
damned.

Protestant reply. It is indeed signified that those whose God is God exist,
but at the same time it is also signified that they live. This is proved from
the conjoined cause of the blessed life. Cajetan observes well: “He does not
say that God is not *Lord*, because God is Lord of both the living and the
dead. Rather, he says that God is not the *God* of the dead, because for God
to be the God of someone, or of some people, is for him to be worshiped, held,
and esteemed as God by that person or those people.”

Bellarmine 5. In Ephesians 2 the Gentiles are said to have been without
promises because they lacked true faith.

Protestant reply. The state of the Gentiles is there opposed to the state of
the Israelites. The opposition consists at least partly in this: the Gentiles
were without the promises that had a distinctive currency among the Jews.
Those promises were none other than those contained in the covenant made with
Abraham, the sign of which was circumcision.

10. Protestant argument. In 1 Corinthians 10 the apostle expressly testifies
that the fathers had eaten the same spiritual food and drunk the same
spiritual drink, which he also affirms was Christ. From this, as from an equal
case, he concludes that one must not place excessive confidence in the
sacraments of the New Testament.

Bellarmine 1. The fathers are said to have eaten the same food, not because
our food and theirs is the same, but because the food was the same for all of
them. That food and drink, moreover, were not sacraments. They were shared
with beasts and had no promise attached to them.

Protestant reply. I. It is certain that their drink was the same as ours, if
Christ is ours, because verse 4 says, “That rock was Christ.” II. No other
reason can be given why Paul here mentions only baptism and spiritual food and
drink except that he wished to compare our sacraments and the old sacraments
with one another. III. Unless those sacraments were the same as ours with
respect to efficacy, what consequence would there be in the apostle's
argument: If their sacraments did not ren—

[1632 witness | printed p. 31]

—der them acceptable to God without faith and obedience, then neither do
ours? IV. Sacraments properly consist in their use, so that a sacrament is not
a sacrament except in its sacramental reception. Beasts therefore could share
in the things that were the matter of the sacraments, although they were true
sacraments for people, not beasts. V. Because these sacraments were
extraordinary, they did not need to have a spiritual promise distinct from
the ordinary promises. It was enough that in a distinctive manner they
represented the benefits of those promises.

11. Bellarmine. Second, manna and water are called spiritual food and drink
because of their cause: they were produced miraculously through the agency of
angels.

Protestant reply. On this reasoning there would be no more efficacy in the
apostle's argument than if he had drawn it from the ravens' food that was
prepared for Elijah through the agency of ravens. 1 Kings 17. Nor would there
be any basis for his distinction in verse 5: “But with most of them God was
not pleased.”

Bellarmine. In John 6 Christ asserts that manna was merely corporeal food.

Protestant reply. Christ does not assert this there. Rather, the comparison is
made not between manna and the Eucharist, as Bellarmine maintains, but between
manna as it was extolled by the Jews in verse 31, apart from the reality
signified, and Christ himself. He so depreciates manna that he comparatively
denies it to be heavenly in verse 32.

Bellarmine. When the apostle says, “They drank from the spiritual rock,” the
meaning is not that they possessed a spiritual rock, that is, Christ, but that
they drank water from the spiritual rock, that is, from Christ as its cause.

Protestant reply. But the water flowing from that spiritual rock was also
spiritual in its own manner. Therefore to drink from the spiritual water and
to drink from the spiritual Rock are one and the same.

12. Bellarmine 3. The apostle's purpose is not to show that excessive
confidence must not be placed in the sacraments, but rather that the
sacraments must not be abused.

Protestant reply. I. To place excessive confidence in the sacraments is to
abuse the sacraments. Bonaventure's observation in Book 4, distinction 1,
question 4 is weighty: “We must take care that, while we give too much to
corporeal signs, we do not take honor away from the grace that cures.” 2. The
apostle's purpose is sufficiently evident from verses 5 and 6: namely, to warn
Christians by the example of the Jews not to suppose that they will be
acceptable to God because of the sacraments if they gratify their desires.
“But with most of them God was not pleased. These things, moreover, were
examples for us, that we should not desire evil things,” and so forth.

13. Protestant argument. In Colossians 2:11–12 Paul attributes the same thing
to circumcision and bap—

[1632 witness | printed p. 32]

—tism when he designates the same grace by the names of circumcision and
baptism.

Bellarmine 1. Paul calls baptism circumcision because circumcision was a
figure of baptism.

Protestant reply. It is asserted without any reason that circumcision was a
figure of baptism. A sacrament is not the sign of a visible sacrament but of
invisible grace. Nor was there so great a correspondence between circumcision
and baptism as the nature of a figure would require.

Bellarmine 2. Paul does not attribute the effect of circumcision to baptism.
For he attributes to baptism the stripping away of the whole body of sins,
whereas Jewish circumcision cut away not the whole body but one small part.

Protestant reply. The effect signified by circumcision was circumcision of the
heart. Romans 2:28–29. But when the heart is stripped of sin, the whole person
is set free. Outward circumcision applied to one part of the body no more
restricts that signification than the sprinkling of the face alone does in
baptism.
