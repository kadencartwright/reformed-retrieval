### Erastus's fifteenth argument against excommunication

It must not be passed over, he says, that the Apostle did not write that if
someone recognizes himself to be unworthy, he should abstain from the Supper.
Rather, taking it as settled that he ought to approach, the Apostle repeatedly
commands him to beware of eating judgment upon himself. For he wished everyone
to partake, while at the same time desiring that everyone partake worthily.

**Reply 1.** Indeed, the Apostle not only desired this; by setting God's
terrifying judgment before them, he also took care that no one should
voluntarily bring judgment upon himself. Yet I am not at all sure that those
who compel everyone intending to attend the Lord's Supper—even persons neither
suspected nor accused—to give an account of their faith and repentance before
their pastor observe this as they ought. In this they certainly employ greater
severity than we do, while allowing everyone else to excommunicate themselves
at their own discretion.

**Reply 2.** We certainly do not deny that Christ's words, “Do this,” contain
an express command. Consequently, we do not deny that the Apostle intended
those who felt themselves unworthy not to suppose that, merely by abstaining
from the Supper, they were guiltless before God, since they ought instead to
strive to approach worthily. We are so far from allowing anyone, so far as it
can be prevented, to release himself from the command to celebrate the Lord's
Supper that, on the contrary, the presbytery declares the obstinate unworthy to
approach the Lord's Table precisely in order to awaken in them a desire to
approach it worthily after attesting their repentance. And whenever the Lord's
Supper is celebrated, if people are found not to have attended it, they are
summoned before the presbytery—excepting those already under excommunication
and those absent because of illness or some other necessary impediment. There,
those who had excluded themselves are sharply reproved

[Scan 54; printed page 32]

because, scorning their own salvation, they delayed giving evidence of their
repentance. They are earnestly admonished to embrace so great an offered grace
at the first opportunity. The rest, unless they bring a just excuse, are
reproved in proportion to their offense.

**Reply 3.** We will never concede that Paul commanded even those who felt
themselves unworthy to approach the Supper unworthily rather than abstain from
it. That would plainly be nothing other than driving people, under the pretext
of obedience, to transgress the command. A person who approaches the Supper
unworthily sins even more gravely than one who, conscious of his own
unworthiness and not yet strong enough to overcome temptation, prefers to
abstain from the Lord's Table rather than bring a graver judgment upon himself.

### Erastus's sixteenth argument against excommunication

Christ does not wish his external kingdom in this world to be confined among
Christians within narrower limits than those within which God formerly chose
to enclose the church among the Jews. God willed that all—both Jews and
circumcised foreigners—should share the same ceremonies, and he commanded
criminals to be punished with the sword. Therefore, Christ likewise wills all
the baptized, or Christians who hold a right opinion concerning religion, to
use the same external ceremonies, while the magistrate restrains the wicked.

**Reply 1.** We concede the proposition but distinguish the assumption. God
willed all Jews to share the same sacred rites in such a way that he
nevertheless excluded some unless they attested their repentance by open
confession and renunciation. We say that this is the order we too should
follow when we defend excommunication.

**Reply 2.** Moreover, the statement about punishing criminals with the sword
does not apply in every case, since not every crime was ever capital. Indeed,
people were excluded from entering the Temple for many deeds to which no civil
penalty was attached.

**Reply 3.** Finally, I answer that, if the parts of this argument were to
cohere, it should instead have been framed as follows: Christ does not wish his
kingdom under the Gospel to become narrower than it was under the Law. But it
would become narrower if excommunication had a place among Christians when it
had no place among the Jews. Therefore, and so forth.

**Reply 4.** In this argument we deny the assumption not only for the reason
just given but for two other equally evident reasons. Excommunication is so
far from diminishing the assembly of the faithful that, on the contrary, it
greatly increases it. It is practiced both to prevent the leaven from corrupting
the whole lump, as the Apostle teaches us, and so that a sheep which has
strayed—otherwise doomed to perish, because it is separated for no other
reason—may be recalled to the way and restored to the fold.

[Scan 55; printed page 33]

By contrast, who does not see that killing the obstinate would diminish the
number of sheep, at least as regards the external assembly, without any
remedy, and would in a short time reduce it to a very small and almost
nonexistent number if this opinion prevailed? Yet in these theses Dr. Erastus
contends that he would prefer such killing even for those who used leavened
bread during the season of unleavened bread.

### Erastus's seventeenth argument against excommunication

The parables of the tares, the wedding feast, and the dragnet appear to bear on
this subject. They take nothing away from the magistrate as he lawfully
performs his office, but merely warn that there will be good and evil persons
in Christ's external kingdom, or the church. They should not be cast out, lest
at some point we uproot the good along with the evil.

We recognize this argument of the catholic Christians against the Donatists,
whose successors today are the Anabaptists, but it was advanced with a very
different purpose: partly against voluntary separations, as if the presence of
wicked people meant that no church existed, and partly against the excessive
severity of some in exercising excommunication. We too condemn both abuses,
since this power was given not for destruction but for edification. Some evils
must not be disturbed rashly or treated violently, but healed gradually. Yet
abolishing the institution itself is one thing; condemning its abuse, as the
catholic Christians formerly did against the Donatists and as we likewise do
to the best of our ability, is another. Those parables speak of the universal
cleansing of the church, which will never occur before the last day. That does
not prohibit particular removals lawfully carried out either by the magistrate
or by ecclesiastical judgment.

### Confirmation of the perpetual use of excommunication in the church

These, then, are all Dr. Erastus's arguments. On their basis he has no doubt
that he has shown excommunication not merely to rest on no word whatsoever of
the Prophets or Apostles, but openly to be refuted both by God's commands and
by the examples of the Prophets and Apostles. We do not object to others
judging by what right he draws that conclusion from these reasons. We now come
to the third part of his writing, in which we intend to confirm
excommunication both by God's express Word and by the examples of the ancient
and apostolic church.

### First argument for excommunication

The very name “tree of life” shows that the tree was partly a sacrament and
token of the animal and at the same time immortal life which Adam and Eve then
possessed, and partly also a type of Christ, from whom they could hope for an
even better life. The application of the name “paradise” to the heavenly
dwelling and John's usage in Revelation show the same thing. Their expulsion
from that paradise, which deprived them of the sight and use of this tree, was
nothing other than what Augustine also calls it: excommunication, and indeed
the gravest excommunication of all. Insofar as it concerned the former
immortal life taken away from them, it was irrevocable, not only in those first
two persons but in all their descendants.

[Scan 56; printed page 34]

Although the decree “You shall surely die” embraces both deaths, God's
immeasurable goodness prevented him from executing it in both respects. He
punished their sin so that death itself did not follow immediately, but rather
mortality after the enjoyment of animal life for a time. Moreover, not only
did he refrain from inflicting the second death; to those who repented and
embraced the blessed Seed who was to be born, he gave, in place of the animal
immortality they had lost, a spiritual immortality infinitely more excellent.
Finally, in place of that type, which was never restored to them, he instituted
another sacrament that was much more explicit and better suited to the
condition into which they had fallen through sin: namely sacrifices, through
which he then began to seal our reconciliation with God in the hearts both of
those two and of their believing descendants. The rest, who lacked faith,
remained liable to perpetual excommunication and consequently to perpetual
destruction.

“But,” someone will say, “where is the presbytery? This therefore has nothing
to do with the excommunication we are discussing.” I deny, however, that
because God himself both decreed that judgment with himself in heaven and
pronounced the sentence extraordinarily on earth, it follows that this
excommunication was in substance different from that which the Sanhedrin
exercised under the Law and which the presbytery now exercises on earth in the
Christian church. We must acknowledge that the patriarchal and Levitical
sacrifices were the same in substance, although differing in very many
circumstances.

### Second argument for excommunication from the Old Testament

In Cain we have another example not merely of a greater excommunication but of
the greatest—indeed, of a dreadful anathema. When the Lord pronounces him
accursed for fratricide and adds the penalty of perpetual exile from his face,
he simultaneously bars him from external worship. Augustine therefore rightly
says that from that time two cities began: the one God's city, which is the
church, whose peculiar rites are sacred rites joined to the promises and
outside which there is no salvation; the other the city of this age, from
which everyone who does not wish to perish must depart.

[Scan 57; printed page 35]

Nor is there any reason to reject this again as our own invention. Luther
expressly writes that Cain was excommunicated, which must be understood as an
anathema. Aben Ezra, in expounding the words “from the face of the Lord,” also
rightly observes, in my judgment, that since the earth is otherwise the
Lord's, the phrase must refer specifically to the place appointed for the
worship of God, where God was also accustomed to appear to the fathers—for
example, where Adam customarily assembled with his family. Seeing himself
driven from this place with no hope of return, that wretched man rightly
concluded that he was also being expelled from God's presence.

Thus, even before the Law of Moses, excommunication was employed with God
himself as its author. That Adam afterward exercised it with his family is
evident from the distinction between the sons of God and the sons of men,
which plainly came into use under Enosh, Adam's grandson. For this is how that
passage of Moses should be translated and explained: around the birth of Enosh
(which occurred approximately 106 years after Abel's murder), designation by
the name of God began. The anathema with which Cain had been struck was
extended to his heirs who followed their father's impiety, except for any who
joined themselves to Seth's family. The separation between the two families
continued until corruption finally invaded Seth's own family—that is, the
church—which also became the cause of the flood. How, then, could
excommunication be a recent human invention when God himself exercised it
from the very beginning of the world, before the established order of his
house existed?

### Third argument for excommunication from the Old Testament

Turning now to the Law of Moses: once civil and ecclesiastical courts had been
established, it cannot be denied that not everyone, although circumcised, was
admitted indiscriminately to sacred assemblies. And what is it to be
excommunicated if not this? First, unclean people were barred from entering the
Temple, from sacrifices, from eating the Passover, and indeed from common
social intercourse, since whatever they touched also became unclean. Dr.
Erastus values this argument so little that he repeatedly calls those who
resort to it nearly insane. It is therefore worth examining his reasons one by
one. If I become a little more lengthy in doing so, and repeat certain matters
I touched on above, I hope a kind reader and friend of the truth will readily
forgive me.

### Refutation of the objections against the third argument

**Objection 1.** Scripture is speaking, Dr. Erastus says, about the uncleanness
under discussion as it affected the Jews, and that legal uncleanness has
nothing to do with our purpose.

**Reply 1.** We wish he had explained at least a little more clearly in what
sense he calls this uncleanness “legal.” We conjecture that he means an
uncleanness which was merely carnal or external and affected nothing but the
body. However that may be, we judge it highly pertinent to our purpose. Let us
suppose that it was exactly as he wishes. Yet whom will he persuade of the
claim he so often repeats: namely, that those who committed a crime or sin were
not regarded as unclean, while those who outwardly touched a corpse or some
other forbidden thing of that kind were regarded as so impure that they
defiled anyone else by their touch, much less were themselves permitted to
attend sacred rites? Indeed, who would not rather judge either that God's Law
was most unjust—which it is wicked even to think—or that, since a stain of the
soul is more shameful than one merely of the body, the former people were
regarded as that much more impure than the latter?

**Reply 2.** We will shortly demonstrate that this was indeed so. Furthermore,
he must say either that these ceremonies of legal uncleanness were empty or
that they foreshadowed something. Once contentiousness is set aside, what that
something was can be clearly established: precisely what is common to all
ceremonies, namely that carnal things are “dead analogues” and figures of
spiritual things. Bodily filth thus signified and set almost visibly before
the eyes the inward pollution contracted either from a person's very origin,
from later vices, or in both ways, so that the very thing people most wish not
to know would force itself even upon unwilling eyes.

**Objection 2.** In writing that figures customarily set forth things not
present but future or past, Dr. Erastus has plainly confined the use of legal
ceremonies within far too narrow limits. Circumcision alone can demonstrate
this, for its use also concerned a present reality: the signified pollution of
the flesh.

**Reply 2.** No one could state the matter more clearly than the Apostle does
when he says that the record of rites which stood against us was nailed to the
cross and taken out of the way through Christ. Those rites were symbols not
only of a future atonement but also of a present condemnation deserved before
God.

**Reply 3.** Indeed, even today Baptism is plainly a symbol both of the native
corruption presently in us and of our simultaneous renewal. Thus, when we
reason from this external, legal uncleanness to the inward reality,

[Scan 59; printed page 37]

as from a shadow to the substance itself, we do not force ambiguous or
invented allegories upon anyone. We assert what the thing itself proves to be
true.

**Objection 3; Reply 1.** Dr. Erastus denies that souls were contaminated by
that legal—that is, external—uncleanness. But I ask whether it is not a sin to
think, do, or say what God has forbidden, however slight the matter may be. If
this is so, then in order to know whether someone has sinned, the inquiry must
not concern the nature of the commanded thing—whether it concerns the soul or
the body, is external, or properly concerns the conscience. The ultimate
question is whether anything has been done against God's command. Thus, when
Paul speaks about political ordinances of every kind, he says that the
magistrate must be obeyed not only because of wrath but also because of
conscience. The proper consideration is not the command itself, or even the
magistrate himself, but God who gives the command; whoever disobeys him becomes
guilty of sin. The same must apply all the more to distinctions of foods, to
avoiding contact with this or that object, and finally to anything instituted
by God either for its own sake or to test human obedience. A voluntary
transgression of his command—even if he commanded us to beat the air, drive a
nail, or perform the very lightest action—renders the conscience itself guilty
of sin and therefore impure and unclean. Otherwise, by what right would God
have punished our first parents, and us in them, with the irrevocable penalty
of the first death for eating from the forbidden tree?

**Reply 2.** Dr. Erastus objects, acutely enough, that many uncleannesses of
this kind could be contracted accidentally and without any fault, as when
someone happened to touch an unclean object. Some such impurities were,
moreover, purely natural, as with a discharge or menstruation. Finally,
sometimes legal uncleanness could not be avoided without sin, much less could
it pollute the conscience. Thus the person who buried a corpse was regarded as
unclean, although it would have been wicked to leave the dead unburied. To the
first kind of legal uncleanness I answer that, although one who knowingly and
deliberately polluted himself sinned more gravely, ignorance of the law does
not excuse the sinner. Since we can never be sufficiently attentive and
circumspect in avoiding what God forbids, no room remains here for wholly
excusing the sin.

[Scan 60; printed page 38]

As for the other two kinds of uncleanness, I acknowledge that they did not
pollute the conscience. Consequently, those regarded as unclean in these ways
were merely kept apart and were not properly excommunicated. Nevertheless,
God willed in this manner to set before everyone, as if reflected in a mirror,
our native stain and the origin and nature of death. This should not seem
strange. A person guilty of accidental and involuntary homicide, although
forbidden to leave his city of refuge until the death of the high priest, was
certainly not excommunicated before God or, as Moses commonly says, cut off
from Israel. Yet throughout that time he was barred from access to the Temple
and its sacrifices. So were lepers—even Miriam, Moses' sister, was not
excepted—until they were cleansed of their leprosy. Here, however, we are
speaking of those truly and properly excommunicated.

This distinction among kinds of uncleanness certainly does not prevent the
following inference from being entirely firm: everyone rendered unclean by
legal uncleanness needed sacrifices for an offense, even if he had done
something against one of God's commands unknowingly. He was therefore guilty
of some sin, and that conclusion can in no way be denied.

**Objection 4; Reply 1.** “Yet,” Dr. Erastus says, “they were not barred from
sacrifice, but were rather compelled to offer it.” This conclusion is empty.
Properly understood, these two things are not contradictory, such that positing
one abolishes the other. They were indeed invited to sacrifices, but first of
all to those sacrifices whose necessity was imposed upon them, in which they
had to confess the sin for which they would otherwise suffer according to
God's words, “He shall bear his iniquity.” They returned to favor with God and
his church on no other terms. This was a particular confession made in a set
form of words and therefore called a “confession of words.” The Jewish
teachers, certainly reliable witnesses in this matter, also give its formula
in connection with these words of Moses: “And it shall be, when he has
trespassed in one of these”—external uncleanness had also been listed at the
beginning of the chapter—“he shall confess that he has sinned in that matter,
and shall bring an offering for his offense.” They say that the confession was
made as follows, to which David in Psalm 51, Daniel in Daniel 9:4, and the
prodigal son in Luke 15 also appear to allude. Such an offender placed his hand
upon the head of the victim to be sacrificed, specifically between its horns,

[Scan 61; printed page 39]

and then said: “I beseech you, Lord: I have sinned, acted wickedly, and
transgressed before you; I have done such and such. Behold, I repent and am
ashamed of my works; I shall never return to this matter.” To return to the
point: the Law itself certainly shows that those invited to sacred rites only
after confession and atonement were prohibited from them if they refused
these. What else do we do, and what else do we seek, when we excommunicate
someone? Do we wish him to remain alienated from the Lord? On the contrary, we
wish the person who alienated himself from God to attest his repentance and be
received again. We are so far from severity in this matter that, except for
the gravest sins and scandals, we require only private repentance before the
presbytery; and in public repentance the pastor acts before the church as the
spokesman of the repentant sinner.

**Objection 5; Reply 1.** Perhaps one objection remains: if what we have said
is valid, since everyone was already a sinner at that time, everyone should
have been excommunicated. I answer that whenever we sin, if God deals with us
according to strict right, we are thereby alienated from him and are never
received into favor without repentance and faith going before. But far be it
from us to say that a sentence of excommunication had to be—or now must
be—pronounced every time a sin occurs, or to demand a public testimony of
repentance from every person. How many things known only to ourselves do we
commit day by day and hour by hour which are forgiven through private
confession before God with prayer? How many things do we privately commit
against our neighbor that need not be made public, and whose guilt can and
should be erased among Christians by a plea for forgiveness made in faith and
by mutual reconciliation? Why were daily prayers instituted under the Law
together with morning and evening sacrifices? Why do we now begin sacred
assemblies by confessing our guilt, unless daily sins may be forgiven us
without any other judicial inquiry? Public satisfactions must therefore be
understood only of graver sins joined with scandal to the church. Some were
undertaken voluntarily even when no sentence of excommunication had preceded,
both under the Law, as we have said, and in the earliest church, as may be
understood from the history of John the Baptist and from Acts when adults were
admitted to Baptism. Others followed a preceding sentence of excommunication.

[Scan 62; printed page 40]

For since it was the duty of the priest and judges to pronounce from the Law
of the Lord concerning controversies of this kind, whether the dispute
concerned law or fact, it is plain that the judges had to pronounce some
decision. Nor could the matter have remained secret even if the Sanhedrin had
wanted it to, since confession and atonement for the sin took place in the
Temple.

**Objection 6.** We are greatly astonished that Dr. Erastus repeatedly writes
that under Moses those who lived impurely or committed crimes were not impure
or unclean, but only those who polluted themselves with legal and external
uncleanness, as by touching a funeral, corpse, or some other forbidden thing.

**Reply 1.** As we said above, it would be most unjust to imagine that God
severely punished external contact by his Law while virtually winking at
manifest crimes. In cases such as murder and adultery, for which capital
penalties were appointed, the Sanhedrin did not need to be greatly concerned
with spiritual restraint when they occurred, unless perhaps the magistrate
failed to perform his duty. Even today, if magistrates discharged their
office, we would have no need to concern ourselves with excommunicating people
of that kind. But when offenses to which either no penalties or merely civil
fines were attached occurred, Moses expressly signifies that the order we
have described had to be observed. He even specifies several such crimes: if
someone under oath departed from the truth; if someone happened to violate an
oath; or if someone retained with perjury something fraudulently taken. These
must be understood as universal examples, like almost all commands: since not
every individual case can be comprehended in laws, certain rules are
established so that the same law may govern like cases. Accordingly, the same
solemn form of judgment was observed in Ezra against those who had married
foreign wives, and the sentence of anathema was decreed against the rebellious.

In explaining this, however, we cannot approve Dr. Erastus's statement in
those theses that the entire affair had nothing to do with the sacraments. But
why? “Because,” he says, “it is established that not Ezra the priest, but the
princes and elders—that is, the magistrates—promulgated that edict.” Yet if the
reasoning holds that whatever the magistrate decrees has nothing to do with
sacred things, where will the matter end? Indeed, if this were true, by what
right could he wish all sacred and civil things to belong to the same power,
as he contends was once the case? We therefore deny that his conclusion
follows from his evidence,

[Scan 63; printed page 41]

namely that this entire affair was something civil. We instead maintain that
the magistrates performed their office: attending to the mouth of the Lord as
he spoke through Ezra, they confirmed by their authority what Ezra reported
from God's Word. Those who adjudicated the whole affair themselves show that
these matters were referred to Ezra as properly belonging to him: “Arise,”
they say, “for this affair pertains to you, and we are with you. Act boldly,
therefore.”

That argument consequently collapses altogether. The confiscation of goods
does not prove the same conclusion, but rather confirms precisely the
opposite. How could those struck with anathema—and therefore accursed and
separated from the Israelite commonwealth—have been permitted to celebrate
Passover or the other sacred rites?

What more need be said? The very thing that occasioned this judgment plainly
confirms the same point. Ezra and the priests intended no one to celebrate
Passover unless he had separated himself from the impurity of the native
peoples. Because this had not been done, Ezra made that lament. Those who
retained profane wives and celebrated Passover must therefore have acted
against the Law, as Ezra's own most grave and sorrowful confession before God
also declares. Unless they had openly acknowledged this sin, added an
expiation, and begged pardon for it, would they, I ask, have been admitted to
the sacred rites even if confiscation of their goods had ceased? In light of
what has already been said, it will be easy to judge that Dr. Erastus has no
warrant for calling Ezra's action into doubt by asking whether he acted
rightly or otherwise.

We wish the same to be said of Josiah's action. When Dr. Erastus said that
even the polluted were summoned by him to celebrate Passover, he should not
have omitted that they were summoned on the condition that they renounce in
deed and word the idols they had served. The history relates that this was
diligently done. But what if someone had refused to assent to that
renunciation of the idols and former crimes? He would plainly either have
been expelled or, if this had happened through error, would have required
expiation, as occurred under Hezekiah. The history accordingly reports that
gatekeepers were also appointed. From the history of Jehoiada it is clear that
their principal office, or certainly one of their chief duties, was to repel
from the Temple gates any who approached in a state of pollution.

### The fourth argument for excommunication from the Old Testament

There remains the notable passage in Numbers 19, which discusses the water of
separation. Moses says that the unclean must be sprinkled with it, and adds:
“The man who is polluted and has not purified himself—that soul shall be cut
off from the midst of the congregation.” A little earlier he had said:

[Scan 64; printed page 42]

“That soul shall be cut off from Israel, because he has polluted the sanctuary
of the Lord and the water of separation has not been sprinkled upon him; he
shall remain unclean.” We say that these words necessarily establish the
following. First, the unclean persons in question were separated—that is,
excluded from dealings with everyone else. But once the period of seven days
had ended, if they had not been duly purified within it by application of the
water of separation (that is, water appointed as a visible testimony for
reconciling these separated persons to the church), and if they nevertheless
dared to mingle with the rest as though duly purified, then they were erased
from the number of Israelites and regarded no differently from the
uncircumcised. These facts plainly prove both forms of excommunication
discussed above. One was voluntary, when someone who knew himself to be
unclean separated himself of his own accord from the others and attended to
the rites of expiation. By the other, undoubtedly imposed by the judgment of
the Sanhedrin to which judgments concerning clean and unclean persons
belonged, a person was prohibited from every sacred rite and thus from hearth
and altar. We will discuss this more fully shortly. This is enough concerning
the testimonies of the old covenant.

### The fifth argument for excommunication from the New Testament

From the New Testament we first produce the passage in Matthew: “If your
brother sins against you, go and reprove him between yourself and him alone.
If he hears you, you have gained your brother. But if he does not hear you,
take one or two others with you, so that the entire matter may be established
in the mouth of two or three witnesses. If he neglects to hear them, tell the
church. If he neglects to hear the church, let him be to you as a Gentile and
a tax collector. Truly I say to you, whatever you bind on earth will be bound
in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven.”

With the universal church of every age and place, we affirm that by these
words the discipline which flourished under the Law among the ancient people
by God's authority is established, by that same authority, in the Christian
church. From this discipline the judges themselves, the accused, and the
whole assembly may each understand their duty. I think that the whole of this
discipline may suitably be comprehended in the following theses.

It is God's will that not even one of the least should perish, but rather that
those who have strayed be recalled to the way by the most suitable means
possible.

Therefore, in cases of offenses that are not manifest, your very first effort
must be to admonish the offender privately, between yourself and him, so that,
having recognized his sin and repented, you may gain him and rescue him from
Satan.

[Scan 65; printed page 43]

If he shows himself obstinate, do not even then cease trying to do him good.
Instead, by bringing one or two witnesses, compel him, if possible, to take
account of himself.

If he is still rebellious, only then report the matter to the church—that is,
the presbytery divinely appointed by the church for this very purpose—so that
it may remind him of his duty.

If he is obstinate even toward the presbytery, then let him plainly be regarded
not as a brother, but as though he were uncircumcised and a tax collector.

The judgment of the church that has bound such a rebellious person with the
bonds of so grave a condemnation will be ratified in heaven, as will its
absolution of him if he acknowledges his sin.

We therefore explain this passage in this way, with all the interpreters we
have seen, both recent and ancient, agreeing without controversy. Dr. Erastus,
however, moved by the following arguments, thinks otherwise.

### Erastus's first objection

“It concerns,” he says, “lighter offenses which people are obliged to forgive
one another, and which we possess the power to retain—not those grave crimes
which some wish to punish by excommunication.”

I will again explain our interpretation of this passage at greater length.
Christ—not a civil but a spiritual lawgiver, who testified both in word and
deed that his kingdom is not of this world when he refused either to judge a
division of inheritance or to pronounce sentence upon an adulteress—is not
discussing forensic disputes in which we desire compensation for damage done
to us, or otherwise pursue our private right before the magistrates. Still
less does he discuss the right of appeal or other things of that kind. Indeed,
he is not even discussing the Christian forgiveness of private injuries,
which is an entirely different subject and which Christ later addresses
distinctly in his reply to Peter. He is discussing stumbling blocks: things
which, when they occur, disturb the state of our conscience, not because of
some private loss, but because either we ought to be troubled in spirit when
something violates God's command, or evil examples incite us to evil, as the
Apostle says elsewhere. In short, since Luke interprets the sins against
someone meant here as stumbling blocks, they properly concern the conscience,
the kingdom of heaven, and God's tribunal, not just any resentment conceived
against a neighbor. Although these matters are all too often closely joined,

[Scan 66; printed page 44]

they must nevertheless be carefully distinguished in this passage and require
different remedies: civil matters require civil remedies and spiritual
matters spiritual ones. The former are to be sought from human laws and
magistrates; the latter from God's Spirit, who admonishes and even effectually
changes us in our hearts, either immediately through conscience itself or
mediately through brothers or the public ministry.

Christ, therefore, the true and only physician of this evil, first identifies
the source of almost every stumbling block in this discourse so divinely
recorded by Matthew: people despise one another, and especially the weaker.
After immediately threatening the authors of so great an evil with judgment
at the outset, in verses 6 and 7 and following, he supplies three remedies.
The first, in verses 8 and 9, is that we should escape the occasions which
Satan seizes from within ourselves to disturb us, even at the cost of any
civil loss whatever, rather than voluntarily plunge headlong into the
stumbling block itself. Nothing ought to be dearer to us than our own
salvation and our neighbor's. The second remedy, from verses 10 to 15, is that
we should extend a hand to others, especially the weak. We will do so if we do
not despise them, since the weaker they are, the more God Most Good and Great
cares for them, and the greater the danger in which they find themselves, the
more earnestly we ought to guard against their falling altogether. A most
beautiful application of this doctrine can be seen in Paul's discussion of
the use of Christian liberty in Romans 14 and 1 Corinthians 8.

In the third place, Christ teaches what order ought to be observed, once
stumbling blocks have occurred, in recalling to the way brothers who have
placed a stumbling block before others. Here he treats a single case: a
stumbling block that is not public but, for example, committed within private
walls and not openly. For if such a thing is committed openly and as though
with everyone aware of it, one may easily infer from this passage the course
to follow, which Paul also teaches in express words and by his own example. In
offenses of this sort committed privately, the twofold circuit of admonition
must be used before the matter is reported to the presbytery, lest the fire
which charity commands us instead to extinguish be rashly spread. The matter
itself therefore shows that a public offense requires a public remedy by
authority if public reproof, of which the Apostle speaks in 1 Timothy 5:20,
does not suffice—for not everyone is like Peter in Galatians 2:14. This is so
whether the matter concerns one person, as in Titus 3:10, or several, and
whether the question concerns

[Scan 67; printed page 45]

doctrine or morals. The Apostle commands that the leaven be removed so that
the entire lump may not be infected—not only with the verb *paraiteisthai*,
“to reject” (Titus 3:10), but also *aphistasthai*, “to withdraw” (1 Timothy
6:5), and even *aphorizein*, “to separate” (Acts 19:9).

In hidden cases of offense, then, Christ commands that, caring simultaneously
for our brothers' reputation and salvation, we first employ private and
brotherly admonition. If this succeeds according to our desire, then, says
Christ—rousing people to this duty of charity by the hope of so great a
gain—“you have gained your brother”; that is, you have saved him by recalling
him to acknowledgment of his sin. But if you accomplish nothing, Christ says,
do not cease to seek your neighbor's salvation; act prudently, however. Try
the same thing again, but with witnesses present: partly so that the repeated
admonition may carry greater weight, and partly so that, if the accused must
be reported—that is, if he despises this admonition too, and the presbytery
must be approached rather than allowing an obstinate brother to perish—you
will not lack witnesses through whom, if necessary, both your charity may be
established and that obstinate person convicted.

When the church has rendered judgment, calling the sinner to repentance, and
he persists in his obstinacy, only then is the final remedy needed: cutting
off, as the Apostle says in imitation of Moses. Having thus performed your
duty, regard him no differently from one who is uncircumcised and sins
shamelessly. You should not even wish to eat an ordinary meal with such a
person, much less permit him to share the common sacred rites. Whoever is
obstinate to this degree should know that the judgment of the church which he
despises until he repents is no less ratified in heaven than on earth, since
the church acts in God's name whether it binds or looses. The Apostle teaches
in the relevant passages that this cutting off must be understood so as to
preserve charity as far as possible, and that it is applied for the sinner's
own salvation.

Christ, then—as Dr. Erastus also acknowledges—when using the formula “Tell the
church,” took account of the place in which he then lived and the time which
still prevailed, just as elsewhere, when speaking of a brother's
reconciliation with someone he has injured, he mentions the altar and an
offering. Therefore, with all the ancient interpreters I know and all the more
recent ones, I judge that the formula “to be as a Gentile and a tax collector”
is clarified by “to be put out of the synagogue,” and that “to be delivered
to Satan,” “to be cut off,” “to be barred from common fellowship,” “to be
excommunicated,” and “not to be counted among Christ's members” all signify
precisely the same thing in general.

[Scan 68; printed page 46]

Dr. Erastus, however, contends against that most widely accepted
interpretation that this entire passage contributes nothing to the case for
excommunication. He does so through the following objections, which I will
attempt to refute one by one.

### Erastus's first objection to our preceding explanation

He first differs from us about the subject under discussion. He wishes Christ
here to speak of lighter offenses which people are obliged to forgive one
another and which we possess the power to retain, whereas we understand him
to speak of those grave crimes which we wish to treat by excommunication. He
also says, “Christ does not here instruct his disciples concerning public
injuries, but concerning how private injuries are to be settled between
private persons.” And again: “Christ does not here institute spiritual
discipline or government, but teaches how private injuries are to be resolved
without offending one's neighbor.”

**Reply 1.** I first answer generally concerning injuries, which I say may be
considered in two ways. In one respect, one person is injured by another's
wrongful and unjust deed or word, whether in his person or reputation, which
the Greeks call *hybris*, or in his goods and possessions, which the Greeks
call an *adikēma*. In the other respect, the conscience of someone not
properly or directly injured in this way is offended. Thus no truly good man
can calmly bear anything unjustly said or done. The truly Christian person
goes further still and is offended whenever he sees for himself, or learns
from elsewhere, that something has been committed against God through a
violation of either table of the Law. Hence Paul's words in 2 Corinthians
11:29: “Who is offended, and I do not burn?” This produces that agitation of
spirit which, especially in the sacred writings, is called zeal. At times it
is just and joined with holy indignation, like that of Moses when he reproved
the Israelites quarreling with one another—which concerned the second table—
and when, after the first table had been violated, he broke the two tablets.
The same was true everywhere of all the prophets, and especially of Christ
himself. At other times this zeal is joined with ignorance, as it very often
was among the Jews when they took offense at the words of Christ, whom they
did not sufficiently know, and later persecuted the Gospel in the persons of
the apostles. This also happened to Paul himself while he was still a
Pharisee. Such words or deeds, considered in relation to God himself, are
properly called *hamartēmata*, sins, or *blasphēmia*, blasphemy. Considered in
relation to the people whose consciences are thus offended, the sacred
writings call them by the general name not *adikēmata*, wrongs, but
*hamartēmata*, sins, and properly and particularly *skandala*, stumbling
blocks.

[Scan 69; printed page 47]

Latin theologians translated the latter as *offendicula*, because the
offended mind, as if striking against them, recoils. Hence the verbs
*skandalizein*, to cause someone to stumble, and *skandalizesthai*, to be made
to stumble, occur so frequently in the apostolic writings.

Both these terms and the realities themselves are therefore plainly confused
by Dr. Erastus's interpretation. I prove this by the following arguments.

**Reply 2.** First, I prove it from the words of Matthew and Luke. In the
sacred writings *hamartanein*, “to sin,” is very often used for *adikein*, “to
wrong,” but the terms “stumbling block” and “causing to stumble” always refer
to the conscience and God's tribunal. Although wrongs and outrages often
occasion stumbling blocks, this is not invariably so. Even when it does
happen, I say that the two things must be distinguished as different, since
the latter arises from the former.

**Reply 3.** No one will ever persuade me that Christ spoke either in a civil
fashion, like a jurist, or in an ethical fashion, like a philosopher, about
settling mutual injuries amicably and peaceably, rather than about pacifying
the conscience and securing the salvation of someone who has sinned either by
injuring his neighbor or directly against God. The former is distinct from
Christ's spiritual kingdom; the latter is most closely joined to the ministry
of the Gospel.

**Reply 4.** This is also demonstrated most clearly by the word *kerdēsas*,
“you have gained”—that is, for Christ, by rescuing him from sin and death. To
understand it merely as recovering the other person's goodwill would be very
feeble. The word “church” proves the same point, for it is utterly absurd to
understand it as a hearing before purely civil judges. Otherwise judges would
have had to sit day and night if they were to be summoned concerning every
sin committed by one person against another that could not be amicably
settled. By contrast, the church—that is, the presbytery—must necessarily take
cognizance of stumbling blocks, whether public in themselves or private at
first but later exposed by the sinner's unyielding obstinacy, lest the
sinner's blood be required from the hands of the pastors and elders. The words
“Tell the church” do not merely declare what may or may not be done. Like the
preceding commands, “Reprove” and “Take,” they command what must necessarily
be done as a duty of Christian charity.

### Erastus's second objection

I come to Erastus's second assertion. He contends that private offenses or
injuries are discussed here—that is, those by which only a single individual
is injured, to whom the words “Reprove,” “Take,” and “Tell the church” also
belong. He confirms this opinion by Christ's words “against you,” “between
yourself and him alone,” and “let him be to you.” I, however, understand a
private stumbling block

[Scan 70; printed page 48]

to mean one committed privately—that is, not openly or publicly before anyone
who might hear. Yet it is not private to such an extent that only one person
has necessarily been offended, much less can it be understood as a hidden
civil injury to a single person.

**Reply 1.** For if that were the case, what witnesses could be brought against
a person who denied it during the repeated admonition? And by what testimony
could the unyieldingly obstinate accused finally be convicted by the church?
Indeed, if the injury were known to the victim alone, the duty of reproving or
rebuking would not even seem properly to belong to the very person against
whom the civil wrong had been committed. What if the obstinate adversary
answered instead that he himself had been injured? Or what if—as happens all
too often—he denied the whole deed, knowing that no witnesses could prove it?
Would not the victim, by proceeding further as Christ commands here, increase
the discord and even bring harm upon himself? Such a person will therefore
have discharged the duty of a Christian by privately reminding his injurer of
his duty. When he could not safely proceed further, he should instead testify
that he freely forgives the injury he received and leave the obstinate person
to God's hidden judgment.

**Reply 2.** Since this is so, I acknowledge that the phrases “against you,”
“between yourself,” and “let him be to you” designate some individual person.
But I do not think they can be understood as referring to the sole person
aware of the offense, nor do I think that the duties of reproving once and
again and finally reporting the accused to the church properly belong to the
person injured. They belong instead to some third person whose conscience was
offended when someone injured another brother or sinned directly against God,
not openly and publicly, to be sure, yet not without witnesses by whom he may
be convicted if necessary. There is a middle case between what is done
publicly and what is committed with only one person aware, and this is the
precise case Christ treats here.

### Erastus's third objection

I now come to the third thing Dr. Erastus maintains: only lighter offenses are
discussed here, which people are obliged to forgive one another and which we
possess the power to retain. He confirms this by three arguments, which we
will examine one by one.

His first argument, intended to prove that only trivial injuries are discussed
here, is this: “Grave crimes could not have been settled between private
persons in this way.”

**Reply 1.** Yet how many utterly certain arguments refute this conjecture! In
the case of even the most atrocious

[Scan 71; printed page 49]

injuries, and indeed crimes—except intentional homicide in the Israelite
commonwealth—private persons are lawfully permitted to settle between
themselves as far as private loss is concerned. Christians are even obliged
to forgive the most atrocious injuries. They are not necessarily compelled to
pursue them before the magistrate, as Joseph's example shows, nor are
Christians permitted to pursue their right from hatred or a desire for
vengeance. As we showed shortly above, Christ does not simply tell the
Christian injured by another what he may or may not do on his own account. He
tells him what Christian charity obliges him to do toward a person who has
knowingly hardened himself against God.

Dr. Erastus then adds a second reason for his position: “It is probable that
persons accused of grave crimes would not have confessed them.” But what kind
of reasoning is this? The slightest offenses too can be denied, and very often
are. Nor, even with witnesses present, can an accused person be convicted in
the church any more readily of slight offenses than of the gravest ones if
only one person was aware of the sin, which is how Dr. Erastus wishes these
private injuries to be understood.

### Erastus's fourth objection

His other argument is no stronger: “A person guilty of graver crimes would not
be allowed to go unpunished.”

**Reply 1.** Dr. Erastus presupposes two things which the context itself
refutes. The words “If he does not hear the church” plainly show that the
church was by no means silent, but rendered a judgment according to the
allegations and evidence—and indeed a judgment of condemnation. As for the
penalty prescribed to an accused and convicted person in matters of
conscience, it is understood from the laws concerning offerings for sin:
confession attested by a sacrifice before the accused was admitted to sacred
rites. Once those ceremonies were abolished, it is perfectly clear what the
presbytery established for such sinners: that they should attest their
repentance by renouncing their sin. If a person did not do so, for as long as
he remained obstinate he was regarded no differently than formerly either an
uncircumcised person or one circumcised outwardly but inwardly unworthy to be
counted among the Israelites. If the matter of which he was accused and
convicted was also such as to merit some civil penalty under the laws, the
magistrate did not neglect that punishment either, as is still observed today
in Christian churches. But Dr. Erastus apparently thought either that a
person not punished with a civil penalty was released unpunished,

[Scan 72; printed page 50]

or that the words “Let him be to you as a Gentile and a tax collector” refer
only to that individual accuser. Two very strong arguments refute this
interpretation.

**Reply 2.** How well, I ask, would public peace and concord have been
protected if this accused person were regarded as impious and wicked by one
person but as an upright and honorable man by everyone else?

**Reply 3.** Moreover, Dr. Erastus does not appear to have considered that the
church's judgment does not simply and properly punish either a private or
public offense, but rather an unyielding obstinacy against the church that
submits to no other remedies. This is the gravest sin of all and can therefore
be healed only by a salutary cautery.

**Reply 4.** If someone asks why the pronoun “to you” was added rather than
“to you all,” “to the church,” or the indefinite “as a Gentile,” I answer that
it was done because, when a private—that is, nonpublic—stumbling block was
under discussion, the case was stated in the person of a single reporter.
Nevertheless, the matter itself cries out that this must not always be
understood as referring precisely to one person. For what if the stumbling
block, although not public, had been presented not to one but to several
people, though still within private walls? Should the accused then have been
reported to the church immediately? Should not his conscience and salvation
instead have been cared for by the procedure Christ prescribes here and by
milder remedies?

Moreover, what Christ immediately adds in the plural, addressing the
disciples and understanding them as a lawful assembly gathered under the name
of the church, surely demonstrates that the obstinate accused was not released
unpunished. Indeed, it shows that his obstinacy against the whole church in
the person of the presbyters was condemned by a public—that is, ecclesial—
judgment. The words “you bind and you loose” cannot belong to any single
private person, or even to several private persons in the church, but only to
persons invested with ecclesiastical authority and assembled together. The
matter itself and the spiritual kingdom of Christ plainly show that this does
not refer to prisons or bodily punishment, but to a binding or absolution of
the spirit or conscience, lawfully performed on earth and also ratified in
heaven.

### Erastus's fifth objection

This also refutes Dr. Erastus's assertion “that the subject is private and
lighter injuries whose penalties are discretionary, and consequently that an
injury might be regarded as so slight as to deserve no penalty, or punished

[Scan 73; printed page 51]

by a light reproof after which the offender, no better for it, might persist
in wrongdoing; and that the accused might even escape through favor or
partiality.”

**Reply 1.** Besides the fact that we have already demonstrated by decisive
arguments that the subject here is not civil injuries but stumbling blocks by
which a sinner wounds his own conscience and those of others, Christ's words
do not contain even the slightest mention of such matters. Nor could any
graver penalty be inflicted than to be regarded as a Gentile and a tax
collector—that is, outside God's church both on earth and in heaven.

**Reply 2.** What more need be said? If Dr. Erastus were right that the subject
here is the brotherly reconciliation of private and indeed slight injuries,
which we are also obliged to forgive, how could these things be consistent
with one another? Does someone appear to forgive an injury if, not content to
reprove privately the brother who injured him—especially slightly—he acts as
though he were a judge, brings witnesses against the person who denies it or
refuses to submit, and finally summons the accused before the tribunal of the
church, whether a civil court as Erastus wishes or an ecclesiastical one?
Christ's words do not simply show what may be done or omitted with a good
conscience. They show how a Christian must deal with a neighbor who has caused
some stumbling block in order to care for his conscience and salvation. This
also makes clear that what Christ later answered Peter concerns private
injuries that must be forgiven and differs from the preceding treatment of
stumbling blocks. Although it too, like everything else, is governed by
charity, it must be treated in an entirely different way unless the sinner
shows himself amenable to healing at the outset. This is enough concerning
that first fourfold objection.

I now answer certain other points on which I also disagree with him in the
interpretation of this passage.

### Erastus's sixth objection

He wishes the term “church” to mean the civil Sanhedrin, or the Jews' own
magistracy, and from this infers that spiritual discipline or government is
not instituted in this passage.

**Reply 1.** But the very thing Dr. Erastus maintains elsewhere refutes the
foundation of this inference, for he contends that one and the same Sanhedrin
possessed both civil and ecclesiastical or spiritual jurisdiction. We will
discuss this in its proper place, when we reach the disputation concerning the
presbytery.

[Scan 74; printed page 52]

### Erastus's seventh objection

Concerning the words “Let him be to you as a Gentile and a tax collector,” he
says it must be noted that the text does not say, “Let him be to you a Gentile
and a tax collector,” but inserts the particle of comparison “as.”

**Reply 1.** But to what purpose is this observation, and what can possibly
follow from it against excommunication? Obstinacy certainly could not cause a
circumcised person to become uncircumcised, or someone who was not a tax
collector to become one. Christ therefore rightly added the particle “as,” as
though he said: However much this person may be a circumcised Jew—for he was
speaking of one brother causing another to stumble—nevertheless regard him as
a Gentile and profane person, and place him in the same category in which the
Jewish tax collectors are customarily placed by the Jews.

### Erastus's eighth objection

Dr. Erastus denies that this formula signifies excommunication. We will soon
consider the word “Gentile.” Concerning the tax collector he says: “I do not
remember reading that Matthew, Zacchaeus, or any other tax collector was
prohibited from religious sacrifices, solemn ceremonies, and sacraments.”

I have already answered that Jewish tax collectors could not have been
regarded as profane merely because they were contractors for the taxes. There
is therefore no doubt that the Jews were hostile to the tax collectors from
hatred of the Romans rather than for any other reason, even more than they
were to the Romans themselves. Thus, when Christ adds “as a tax collector,”
his expression does not diminish in force, as Dr. Erastus thought, but rather
intensifies. In the same way, a Christian who defected to the religion of the
Turks would today deservedly be more detestable to us than someone born a
Turk. “But,” Dr. Erastus says, “the subject is not apostate tax collectors.” I
concede that not all tax collectors were manifest apostates, and therefore
acknowledge that the Jews did not observe moderation in most cases. Yet the
fact that tax collectors were utterly detestable to the Jews suffices to
confirm our interpretation. In expressions of this kind, one sometimes
attends simply to what is customarily done, not always to what is justly and
moderately done. Nevertheless, the ancient Romans' own complaints against the
tax collectors' extreme greed and rapacity show that tax collectors deserved
to be abominated not only by the superstitious—from whom it is most inequitable
to take a rule rashly—but also by the truly religious. Livy writes of them in
book 45: “In settling affairs in Macedonia, Minucius proposed abolishing both
the leasing of the Macedonian mines, which yielded an immense revenue, and the
leases of rural estates. He understood that these could not be operated
without a tax contractor; and where there is a tax contractor, either public
law is empty or the allies have no liberty.” If this was the Romans' own

[Scan 75; printed page 53]

opinion of tax collectors, anyone may easily judge what the Jews deservedly
thought of their countrymen who joined them. This is why the two words “tax
collectors and sinners” are ordinarily joined in the Gospel history, and why
John the Baptist addressed them: “Collect no more than what has been appointed
for you.”

“But Christ counts Matthew and Zacchaeus among his own.” He counts them, of
course, as Jews condemned by no one. Moreover, Matthew had been changed so
completely that he even abandoned his tax table, and Zacchaeus had repented so
thoroughly that he restored fourfold.

“But Christ does not hesitate to eat with others as well.” Certainly: as
opportunity presented itself, he did so to bring to the Father those who had
entered the path of repentance. This does not conflict with excommunication,
but agrees with it perfectly, as the Apostle teaches.

“But the other Jews are not said to have prohibited Matthew or Zacchaeus from
the Temple or sacrifices.” They did not prohibit even the Lord himself—not
because they lacked the will, but because God's hidden power and fear of the
people restrained them, so much so that the Pharisees themselves sometimes
ate with him.

“But Christ also presents a tax collector praying in the Temple, undoubtedly
according to the accepted custom of that time.” We concede this concerning
Jewish tax collectors, whom the Jews, like many other things, were compelled
against their will to tolerate from fear of the Romans. Yet it is not even
probable that the Jews meanwhile sacrificed together with the tax collectors,
with whom they had a religious scruple against maintaining any association.

### Erastus's ninth objection

We now turn to the word “Gentile.” Dr. Erastus feels himself greatly pressed
by the established fact that the uncircumcised were barred even from the
Temple's inner court, where the altar of sacrifice stood, although sacrifices
were offered for them at their request. Transferring this passage to civil
jurisdiction, he explains it in three ways. First, he proposes the following
sense: “If someone who has been chastised by the church—that is, by words—for
trivial injuries continues to inflict injury, you are not obliged to admonish
him as before, but may proceed directly to the magistrate.” This
interpretation, he says, “is simple and by no means forced, but agrees with the
nature of the matter, custom, and Christian charity.”

**Reply 1.** I think entirely otherwise. This exposition must be derived either
from Christ's words or from the matter itself. But what can be found in his
words from which we might reasonably suspect that he makes any mention here
of trivial injuries, persistence in inflicting another injury, or approaching
some other tribunal? Christ does not say, “If he sins slightly,” but “If he
sins.”

[Scan 76; printed page 54]

We have already shown that the subject is not injuries of any kind, public or
private, but stumbling blocks. Nor did Christ say, “If he persists in
wrongdoing,” but “If he does not hear you”—whether alone or with witnesses—
“and the church.” One who does not comply with an admonition—that is, who does
not acknowledge the sin or make satisfaction for it—is said not to hear. This
is far less than adding injury to injury. Nor can regarding someone as a
Gentile signify summoning the unjust person before a Roman magistrate or any
other one.

If the matter itself is considered, then, as we have already said repeatedly,
a person can hardly be said to have forgiven an injury if, not content first
to reprove his brother and then to admonish him with witnesses present about
a private injury—especially a slight one, as Dr. Erastus contends—he pursues
the reluctant man all the way to the magistrate's tribunal. Christ soon
answers Peter's question about forgiving private injuries, not stumbling
blocks, and often commands it elsewhere. Indeed, there are many cities in
which no action is granted for slight injuries, and the jurists limit actions
for private injuries to a short period. Finally, to repeat once more what I
have already said often, the commands “Take,” “Reprove,” and “Let him be to
you” do not merely grant or permit something, but command it absolutely. They
therefore plainly refute this interpretation. A Christian who has suffered an
injury may indeed complain with a good conscience to a Christian magistrate
about the damage caused by it, provided he brings a pure spirit free from all
hatred. Yet true Christian charity will scarcely allow a judge to be
approached over slight injuries, and no one is compelled to appeal to the
magistrate even for the most atrocious injury if he prefers to forgive it and
completely pardon even an unrepentant offender.

### Erastus's tenth objection

Dr. Erastus's second exposition of this passage is as follows: “Let him be to
you as a Gentile and a tax collector” means that if the wrongdoer refuses to
abide by the judgment of the first Sanhedrin—for there were various Jewish
Sanhedrins of equal rank—the injured person will be permitted to
plead the case before other Sanhedrins.

I concede that the Jews had various councils of judges corresponding to the
gravity of the cases, to which Christ also alluded in Matthew 5:22. But I do
not know whether an appeal from one to another was permitted. And even if it
was, where in Christ's words is there even the slightest mention of the right
of appeal?

### Erastus's eleventh objection

Dr. Erastus expresses his third exposition in the following words:

[Scan 77; printed page 55]

“It may be said in the third place, and perhaps more correctly, that Christ,
taking account of the condition of his own time—when the Romans had
transferred to themselves a large part of the Sanhedrin's power, especially in
cases of injury and the like—teaches that the Jews ought not to litigate with
their brothers, lest they occasion offense, before attempting a settlement
before the judges of their own nation. For if a Jew had a dispute with a
Gentile or tax collector, he was compelled to bring him to trial before the
Roman governor. Therefore, Christ says, let that obstinate brother be to you as
a tax collector and Gentile—that is, you may plead your case against him before
a profane magistrate just as if he were a Gentile.”

**Reply 1.** Here I may set Erastus against Erastus. He argues at great length
that, even after Judea was reduced to a province, the Romans regarded the Jews
as autonomous to such an extent that even capital judgments, especially in
Jerusalem, remained in their hands. I do not now dispute how true this was at
the time when Christ lived and delivered this discourse, which was adapted to
that very time. I instead ask how it agrees with what he says here: that the
Romans had transferred to themselves cognizance of injuries and other similar
controversies arising between Jewish litigants on both sides—the very case
which Christ proposes in this passage.

**Reply 2.** Moreover, since it is sufficiently established how reluctantly
the Jews submitted to Roman rule, I ask how it is credible that any Jew,
especially one suffering a slight injury, as Dr. Erastus contends concerning
this passage, would choose to offend his whole nation by pursuing his right
before a Roman tribunal rather than forgive a slight injury even when the
offender had made no satisfaction. Or how is it credible that a person
convicted by witnesses and defeated in his case by the Sanhedrin's judgment
would obstinately prefer to incur the hatred of the whole nation voluntarily
rather than acknowledge and seek pardon for his fault and pay whatever fine
might have been imposed upon him?

**Reply 3.** I also ask whether, if these cases had to be pleaded before the
provincial governor, the Romans would have allowed the Sanhedrin—or the Jewish
magistracy, as Dr. Erastus interprets it—to arrogate this cognizance to itself.

**Reply 4.** Finally, I ask whether a case adjudicated before the Jews'
autonomous Sanhedrin could be appealed to the Romans. If anything of this sort
were done, would the conscience of either plaintiff or defendant and the
public tranquility thereby have been protected? Would not a door instead have
been opened to endless public disturbances?

**Reply 5.** Christ certainly did not propose this. Neither did Paul. He was
not treating a remedy for stumbling blocks, but forensic controversies,

[Scan 78; printed page 56]

“matters of this life.” Yet he expressly and without exception commands
Christians to surrender any right whatever rather than litigate before a
tribunal of profane persons. He accordingly commands that arbiters instead be
chosen to decide such controversies between Christians without the clamor of
a court. Much less, therefore, can I assent here to Dr. Erastus's claim that
Christ in this passage and the Apostle in the First Epistle to the Corinthians
treated the same matters, and that Paul explained more fully what Christ
taught here more briefly.

### Erastus's twelfth objection

Dr. Erastus also does not wish the words “binding” and “loosing” in Matthew to
be applied to the Sanhedrin's judgment, but to the person against whom the sin
was committed. Thus in this passage, he says, the person who retains the
injury is said to bind, because he renders his obstinate brother inexcusable;
and the person who forgives the injury is said to loose, because he gains the
injurious brother whom a brotherly admonition has brought to repentance. “For
unless,” he says, “the words ‘binding’ and ‘loosing’ are understood of the
brotherly resolution of private injuries, why does Christ afterwards add,
‘Again I say to you: if two of you agree on earth,’ and so forth?”

I answer that, in ordinary usage, the word “again” marks a repeated act and
signifies no more than “moreover.” Someone is said to speak again or once more
when he speaks even about the most different things, and sometimes even about
opposites. Nevertheless, I acknowledge that this saying of Christ, like the
preceding ones, also concerns mutual agreement. It is not the agreement by
which an injured person becomes reconciled to another, but that by which a
person who has somewhat separated himself from God and his brother by
offending his brother's conscience through some stumbling block is reunited
to God and the church. By these words Christ confirms his statement that a
judgment pronounced by the church on earth is ratified in heaven, using an
argument from comparison. For if the prayers of only two or three people
assembled and agreeing in God's name are by no means vain, much less will be
void what a lawful Sanhedrin of the church, assembled in God's name as the
Apostle says, lawfully determines from God's Word, whether binding or loosing.
But let us address the matter itself.

**Reply 1.** First, I answer that Dr. Erastus may search through the entire
Scripture, but will never find the terms “binding” and “loosing” attributed to
any except those exercising the public ministry. They are applied
metaphorically with reference to divine and spiritual power, for these are
judicial terms. Consequently, they do not belong to *idiōtai*—people who hold
no public office in the church and whom later ages called

[Scan 79; printed page 57]

laypeople. The right to teach, reprove, correct, and train in Christ's name and
by his authority—the first two of which concern doctrine and the latter two
morals—extends both to universal preaching in the assembly of the whole flock,
and to individual sheep as necessity requires, as well as to the presbytery's
particular censures. This is clear from express testimonies of God's Word and
from the examples of Christ himself, the prophets, and the apostles. Thus, as
Christ himself interprets it, to bind is to retain sins, while to loose is to
remit sins, and this by divinely granted authority. Who then would accept Dr.
Erastus's interpretation that binding means rendering someone inexcusable and
loosing means forgiving a private injury?

**Reply 2.** Dr. Erastus rightly teaches that in the public ministry remitting
and retaining sins is nothing other than announcing, in God's name and by his
command, that the sins of those who repent and believe are remitted, while
those of the obstinate and unbelieving are retained. As I have just said, this
is sometimes declared universally or indefinitely to the whole flock and
sometimes applied to individuals in ecclesiastical admonitions and censures.
But the matter stands very differently in private reconciliation and private
injuries. The former acts belong to God alone speaking through the mouth of
his chosen ministers. Of the latter acts, we can perform one: we can remit our
own right to our neighbor. But I do not know how the other—refusing to forgive
an injury—can possibly be attributed to the godly, especially in the private
and trivial cases which Erastus contends are treated here. To be sure, whenever
one private person duly reproves or consoles another from God's Word and with
Christian charity, and the other does not disdain to receive it, this is also
ratified in heaven. In this sense we must understand what we will shortly hear
from Theophylact and from Augustine's sermon on Matthew 16. Nevertheless, I
affirm that the words “binding” and “loosing” always signify a sacred and
solemn action.

**Reply 3.** Moreover, three things preceded in Christ's discourse: one
admonition in private between two persons alone; a second admonition made with
two or three witnesses present; and finally a report to the church. How then
does Dr. Erastus refer these words to the first two rather than to this third
act? If there are only two people, of whom one necessarily either looses or
binds and the other is loosed or bound, the plural verb

[Scan 80; printed page 58]

“you bind” or “you loose” certainly cannot fit a single person. Nor do these
terms adequately fit the second admonition, at which no judges were present,
but only witnesses. It remains, therefore, that the church—that is, the
presbytery—is said to loose and bind, as both the sequence of the context and
reason itself demonstrate. For what injustice would this be, I ask, for
someone to sit in his own case as both accuser and judge? Yet this must happen
if Dr. Erastus's opinion is valid. What if the fault belongs to the reprover
himself, as often happens? We must plainly acknowledge that a person unjustly
bound is not held by that bond. Yet if we follow Dr. Erastus's interpretation,
the accuser will nevertheless bind him—if not *de iure*, “by right,” as the
common phrase has it, then *de facto*, “in fact”—and will regard an innocent
person as a Gentile and a tax collector. How many quarrels, complaints,
stumbling blocks, and finally schisms will arise from this, far from thereby
serving the concord of private persons and the peace of the church!

If someone objects that the Sanhedrin may also judge unjustly, we will concede
that this can happen, as the Son of God himself experienced. For this reason,
properly constituted churches must also make provision to avoid this evil.
Nevertheless, unless the injustice is manifest, the presumption must favor the
Sanhedrin; and therefore, apart from human wickedness, a schism can scarcely
arise for this reason. Finally, we answer that, although it is impossible to
provide against every evil, the order once established in the church by the
Son of God must nevertheless be retained and preserved inviolate as far as
possible.

We will soon learn from how many testimonies it can be proved that this custom
which we defend was perpetual throughout the entire ancient Christian church.
That the Israelite Sanhedrin practiced the custom to which Christ referred in
these words is evident from Numbers 19, cited above. From it one can also see
what it means to bind: to declare someone constrained by the snares of sin and
uncleanness and finally to expel him from Israel, or from the assembly of the
Israelites. As a result, he was no longer regarded by the assembly as an
Israelite or an upright man, but as though uncircumcised and a tax collector—
that is, as someone wicked and impure. I will add what Elias Levita, a recent
but very learned writer drawing on a reliable ancient tradition, writes about
this in his *Tishbi*: “We Jews have three kinds of anathema—that is,
excommunication—namely, *Niddui*, *Cherem*, and

[Scan 81; printed page 59]

*Schammata*. *Niddui* is expulsion. We have said that this separation was
partly voluntary, when the unclean reported themselves and sought expiation,
and partly involuntary, if someone was condemned as unclean by the Sanhedrin.
Hence the water was called *Nidda*, that is, the water of expulsion or
separation, because it was dedicated to the expiation of such persons, joined
with a solemn confession of sin. If someone did not repent—that is, if he
neglected the prescribed expiation and showed himself disobedient to the
Sanhedrin's judgment, which Christ calls failing to hear the church—they
anathematized him by *Cherem*. This is to cut someone off from Israel, from his
peoples, or from the midst of the congregation, so that he is no longer
counted an Israelite but as a Gentile, as Christ says. If he still did not
repent, they subjected him to *Schammata*—that is, judged him liable to death,
namely eternal death. It is called *Schammata*, as though ‘death is there.’”
This anathema was perhaps irrevocable. Chrysostom admits no use of it in the
church except against doctrines. So far Levita. These observations greatly
confirm the preceding arguments, making perfectly evident the practice to
which Christ referred, and they can illuminate many passages in Paul.

### Erastus's thirteenth objection

Finally, Dr. Erastus produces two passages in support of his opinion, one from
Augustine and the other from Theophylact.

**Reply 1.** As for Augustine, we concede that in the sermon Dr. Erastus cites
he applies this power of binding and loosing to private reconciliation. As
often happens elsewhere in his sermons, this great man does not make the exact
interpretation of a particular passage his chief concern. Yet he presupposes
the church's judgment, to which a private person subscribes and thereby is in
some sense himself said to bind or loose his adversary. So great a man could
not have failed to see how dangerous this license of private judgments would
have been and how it would occasion disagreements rather than
reconciliations.

**Reply 2.** Accordingly, elsewhere he wrote much more correctly and aptly,
indeed as though inveighing against the opinion of those who, once the
gentleness of forgiveness was established, wished discipline to be
extinguished. Augustine says in reply: “The merciful gentleness of forgiving,
on account of which it was said, ‘Not only seven times,’ and so forth, does not
serve to leave wickedness unpunished”—which Dr. Erastus concedes to the
magistrate—“or discipline sluggish and asleep”—which Dr. Erastus denies is
entrusted to the church—“for that would do more harm than diligent and vigilant
punishment.” If we believe Augustine, or rather Christ himself,
excommunication is therefore not something tyrannical or oligarchic, nor is it
destructive to the church or introduced by an imprudent plan.

[Scan 82; printed page 60]

Augustine continues in the same passage: “For Christ gave the keys of the
kingdom of heaven to the church in such a way that he not only said, ‘Whatever
you loose,’ and so forth, but also added, ‘Whatever you bind on earth will be
bound in heaven,’ because the justice of punishment is also good. For his
words, ‘If he does not hear even the church, let him be to you as a Gentile
and a tax collector,’ are graver than if he were struck with a sword, consumed
by flames, or exposed to wild beasts. For there too he added, ‘Truly I say to
you, whatever you bind upon earth will be bound in heaven,’ so that it might
be understood how much more gravely punished is the one who is seemingly
abandoned unpunished.” Here is Augustine's judgment on this passage, plainly
and completely opposed to Dr. Erastus's opinion.

**Reply 3.** That Theophylact likewise thought nothing other than what I have
just said—namely, that private persons bind and loose when, subscribing to the
Sanhedrin's preceding judgment, they regard an obstinate adversary as a Gentile
and a tax collector—is evident from the very passage Dr. Erastus cites, though
not in full. These words precede it, confirming our opinion and completely
overturning Dr. Erastus's doctrine: “But if he does not hear even the church,
then let him be cast out, lest he impart some of his own wickedness to the
others as well.” Notice that Theophylact understood it as expulsion from the
assembly of the church itself. The same must be understood of loosing as of
binding. Otherwise his statements about private forgiveness or retention
could not stand. Unless someone who sins against his neighbor repents, he
remains bound before God even if the private person he injured forgives him a
thousand times. Conversely, one who repents and performs his duty toward his
brother is absolved before God even if the person he injured refuses to
forgive the wrong. This is enough concerning these objections of Dr. Erastus.

### The sixth argument for excommunication

The Lord forbids giving what is holy to dogs. By “holy things,” as the Apostle
interprets the term, are meant the mysteries, whose stewards are called
servants of Christ. The names of pigs and dogs of this kind fit none better
than those who, after first being admitted into the church, have been proved
by experience and judged by the church to be such. Therefore, the Lord forbids
giving his mysteries to such persons and consequently forbids dispensing the
sacraments to them. The major premise of this argument cannot be denied; the
minor is the point in dispute.

### Erastus's first objection

Dr. Erastus denies that “holy things” and “pearls” signify anything other than
the sound doctrine which people

[Scan 83; printed page 61]

fiercely resist. What reason does he bring for his opinion? This one: “No one
was ever more a pig or a dog than the Pharisees and Sadducees. Nevertheless,
the Lord did not cease to offer them eternal life, even when he lamented over
Jerusalem.”

But to what purpose is this? We do not cease to offer the Word of salvation
even to the excommunicated. Excommunication aims at nothing other than that
the spirit of the one who repents may be saved on the day of the Lord.

**Reply 1.** It is indeed very difficult to discern this distinction, since
dogs and pigs are sometimes changed into sheep through the preaching of the
Word, while many who bore the appearance of sheep degenerate into dogs and
pigs. Paul even commands that opponents be instructed gently in 2 Timothy
2:25. Thus obstinate rejection of doctrine, or hypocrisy more than adequately
exposed, reveals some indications of a pig or a dog. Yet these indications
remain so uncertain that the Lord fittingly compared the kingdom of heaven to
a dragnet which gathers everything. The sacraments must be judged differently,
and the reason for the distinction is evident. The Lord's Word is like good
seed which sowers cast with an uncertain outcome until it finally becomes
evident from effects joined with perseverance. The sacraments, however—and in
particular the sacrifices formerly and the Lord's Supper now—presuppose an
explicit profession of faith. For this reason the eunuch was not baptized by
Philip until he had made a confession of true faith, nor should adults be
admitted to baptism before becoming catechumens. Infant baptism has its own
distinct rationale, which this is not the place to discuss.

Suppose, then, that a catechumen seeking baptism is ready to utter a confession
of faith in words, but when he is also admonished to change his wicked life,
as the baptismal pledge attests—for example, to dismiss a prostitute or restore
another person's property—refuses to acknowledge his sin and promise a better
life. Would Dr. Erastus say that the sacrament of regeneration should be
imparted to one who asks for it so shamelessly? Would he not instead say that
the person should be repelled with disgrace? I concede that someone never
admitted into the assembly cannot be said to have been excommunicated. But
suppose this same person is already baptized and, because of his wicked life
and his refusal to dismiss the prostitute, is summoned before the elders and
convicted by them. He promises no repentance, but wallows in his filth, even
railing insolently against the presbytery itself, while nevertheless demanding
the Lord's Supper. Shall we not say that this pig and dog must be barred from
the Lord's Table,

[Scan 84; printed page 62]

where the holy thing is placed in our hands and the very Son of God, infinitely
more precious than any pearl, is offered for our participation? Peter formerly
barred Simon from the laying on of hands and the gifts of the Holy Spirit,
while nevertheless offering him another holy thing and pearl of the same
kind—the Word of repentance and salvation.

**Reply 2.** But what is the relevance of Christ's example, when he barred no
one absolutely or individually from hearing the Gospel, just as John the
Baptist baptized the very people he called a brood of vipers? John and Christ
were not serving in the office of exercising these ecclesiastical judgments
in the Israelite church. Paul shows what the church's governors must do
against convicted and adjudged dogs and pigs when, addressing their assembly
in the person of Timothy, he follows a vivid description of those dogs and
pigs with the verb *paraiteisthai*, “turn away from.”

### Erastus's second objection

Dr. Erastus next denies that the names “dogs” and “pigs” can mean those who sin
merely by a more dissolute life while otherwise professing the same religion
as we do. He says that they mean manifest enemies and persecutors of the
Gospel.

**Reply 1.** Nor do we judge that just anyone of an impure life should be
excommunicated, but only those who are unrepentant and do not hear the church.
Yet when manifest rabid dogs bark shamelessly at the pastors and flock within
the church itself, and pigs defile the Lord's assembly in which they wish to
be counted and ravage the Lord's vineyard, their external profession of faith
certainly does not help them. It instead makes them more culpable, because
they profess Christ with their mouths but deny him by their deeds.

**Reply 2.** Ishmael and Esau were circumcised and outwardly acknowledged the
true God with Abraham. Nevertheless, they were disinherited. Ishmael, though
the son of the slave woman, barked at the son of the blessing; Esau, a profane
pig, was preparing to tear apart a harmless little sheep. In their own times
both were living examples of ecclesiastical excommunication, whose author is
God and whose ministers are the people appointed to the task, such as Abraham
and Isaac in their households. They were also types of the same for future
ages—unless perhaps we deny that the houses of Abraham and Isaac were the
dwelling place of the church, or that the land of Canaan was a token of the
covenant and thus a type of the heavenly inheritance.

**Reply 3.** Indeed, do we not plainly see that, to reprove the uncleanness of
those who live in the church, Christ alluded to the legal impurities when he
metaphorically named pigs and dogs, the most unclean animals under the Law?

[Scan 85; printed page 63]

**Reply 4.** Finally, the matter itself and daily experience teach that it is
far easier to discern from deeds than from an oral confession who are sheep
only as far as words go but dogs and pigs in their impure morals. No one will
easily persuade me that the pastors and governors of the church ought to be
more concerned with an examination of faith and external profession than with
an examination of life in order to distinguish such persons rightly and truly
from the sheep—much less that they ought to be content with the former alone.
The prophet teaches us to distinguish in this way those who are truly to be
admitted into God's tabernacle and counted as citizens of God's city. Christ
himself will distinguish the sheep from the goats in this way on the last day.
Likewise, the prophets and apostles everywhere customarily seek from the
second table the outward evidence of true piety.

**Reply 5.** What more need be said? We say that the dogs and pigs living in
the church's bosom are also those with whom the Apostle warns the truly
faithful not even to eat, much less to open the sacred Table of the Lord to
them, once they have lawfully been declared such. We also say that they are
the very people whom Jude vividly describes and calls spots defiling the love
feasts—the common sacred meals then celebrated, to which the Eucharist was
joined, as is clear from Paul and Tertullian. Jude finally says: “After making
a distinction, have mercy on some”—namely, those who show themselves
teachable—“but save others by fear”—that is, by a more severe judgment, either
threats or even binding and delivering to Satan—“snatching them from the
flames.” For this is the end of excommunication: that the person who wandered
may be affected by a sense of sin and shame and return to the way, since, as
we cited from Augustine, the justice of punishment is also good. “Hate even
the garment stained by contact with the flesh”—that is, contact with something
as impure as these people, for he alludes to legal uncleanness.

Did Jude call them the spots of the love feasts because the pastors' negligence
was already so great that they did not expel them as they ought, just as the
Corinthians in a cruel mercy allowed that fornicator to remain among them?
Chrysostom wrote about this long ago: “Let us consider whether Jude's words
also contain those commands of the Lord: ‘Do not give what is holy to dogs,’
he says, ‘nor cast your pearls before pigs.’ But corrupted by love of praise
and the vice of boasting, we act contrary even to this command. We
indiscriminately publish the sacred mysteries to people who possess neither a
pure understanding nor a sound faith, and moreover are bound up in the stains
of sin.”

[Scan 86; printed page 64]

Shortly afterward, having spoken of people admitted to the Christian holy
things before they had even entered Christ's religion, Chrysostom finally adds
other matters that concern dissolution of life, and rightly so. For which is
more impure: the person who abuses holy things whose power he does not know,
or the person who knowingly and willingly contaminates them? “We violate the
force of this command,” he says, “not only in this respect but sometimes also
when we allow people we know to be of an impure life”—that is, neglecting the
church's judgment by which they ought in either case to be declared dogs and
pigs or Gentiles and tax collectors—“drunkards, rapacious, impure, and in every
way like pigs and dogs, to become participants in the divine sacrament.” By
this circumlocution he means the Lord's Table.

### Erastus's third objection

Dr. Erastus replies ironically that the good are not polluted by the evil.

**Reply 1.** Shall we suppose that Chrysostom and every ancient church, without
a single exception, which drove dogs and pigs away from sacred things, either
failed to see even this or agreed with the Donatists, whom they very sharply
reproved? Or, because we do the same, are we to be regarded as Anabaptists?

**Reply 2.** We therefore accept the antecedent of this enthymeme but deny that
the inference is valid: “The good are not polluted even if some evil people
receive the same Supper as they do. Therefore the evil must not be prohibited
from the Supper.” This is a manifest fallacy which treats as a cause something
that is not the cause. We do not bar the evil from the Lord's Table as though
they pollute it for the good, for we have learned from the Apostle that all
things are pure to the pure. We bar them because they pollute it for
themselves. The pastors' duty is to prevent such pollution so far as they can,
lest through their own negligence they too become guilty of allowing the
sanctuary to be polluted by another. The impure eat and drink judgment upon
themselves, and it is the pastors' duty to recall those who wander. This is
sometimes done by severity and even by temporarily withdrawing their food.
Finally, evil people contaminate the good by evil examples and corrupt the
lump with their leaven.

The sound argument would instead have been: “The evil do not pollute the
Supper for the good. Therefore, even if the good are mixed with the evil, the
Supper is nevertheless pure for the good.” But it is the elders' duty to
prevent this mixture for the reasons I have stated, as the apostles also
earnestly admonished and themselves practiced.

We also rightly count Simon Magus's rejection from the laying on of hands
among the clear examples of excommunication, although in one respect it was
extraordinary—namely, insofar as it was performed by the apostles through a
special inspiration of the Holy Spirit.

[Scan 87; printed page 65]

At that time this laying on of hands was also an express sacrament of the gifts
of the Holy Spirit, and Simon had already been baptized. There is no reason to
think it probable that Peter would have admitted to the Lord's Table someone
whom he barred from the gifts of the Holy Spirit in such a way that, unless he
became an entirely different man, Peter announced his imminent destruction.

### The seventh argument for excommunication

The exclusion by which Paul separated from both the Ephesian and Roman
churches the Jews who obstinately resisted the truth was likewise real and
terrifying. He obeyed Christ's command, and his action was already then
regarded as ratified in heaven when, abandoning their synagogues, he turned to
the Gentiles.

### The first objection

Someone may say that this was done not because of their morals or dissolute
life, but because they obstinately resisted Christianity.

**Reply 1.** I concede that it was. Yet those who profess the truth but deny it
so completely by their deeds that they are plainly stains upon the churches
seem even worse than those Jews. Is it therefore unlawful to bring them before
the church—that is, before those assembled in the Lord's name to whom this care
belongs—and there convict and admonish them?

**Reply 2.** If they persist in obstinately treating Christ himself and his
church with such contempt that they wish to be regarded as Christians merely
by professing what the demons themselves believe, and under this pretext
continue corrupting Christ's flock, would it be tyrannical to convict and
judge them, bar them from the sacraments which they manifestly mock, and
prevent them from corrupting the flock? Is it permissible to bar wolves from
entering the sheepfold, but unlawful to regard wolves that have entered as
anything other than sheep, provided they appear covered in sheepskins?

### The eighth argument for excommunication

I come to Paul's passage in which he ascends from a particular case to a
general proposition. The particular case is the condemnation of the incestuous
man, together with a grave reproof of the Corinthians because they had not
long since cast him out of their assembly. By apostolic authority he decrees
that they should finally do what ought to have been done long before. That is,
assembled in the name of Christ, from whom they had received this power to
promulgate the decree made by Christ himself, they are to deliver this impure
man to Satan, or remove him from their midst. This is by no means so that the
wretched man, driven from the Lord's house, should perish in Satan's kingdom.
It is so that, taught by his own suffering, he may utterly destroy that wicked
desire of the flesh and thus be saved on the day of the Lord, while the
Corinthian church is not infected by the contagion of such filth. Rather, once
it has purged this leaven, it may truly celebrate the feast of unleavened
bread, as was appointed for the Passover under the Law—that is, it may worship
God purely and holily when assembling both to hear

[Scan 88; printed page 66]

the Word and at the Lord's Table, and thus enjoy Christ, our true Passover.
The general proposition is that this procedure must be followed not only with
people as supremely wicked as this, but with everyone who is in reality
nothing less than what they are called—namely, Christians and therefore
sanctified in Christ. True Christians must abstain even from private
association with such people.

We judge and affirm that this is the true and genuine meaning of the chapter.
This passage has never been explained otherwise in the church, nor should it
be. Nevertheless, because Dr. Erastus raises many objections, we will resolve
them one by one before turning to his own interpretation.

### Erastus's first objection

“It is established,” says Dr. Erastus, “that the Apostle did nothing contrary
to or without Christ's command. It has now been shown that Christ excluded no
one, not even Judas.”

**Reply 1.** We concede both points but completely deny the inference he wishes
to draw. What prevents Christ from commanding something to be done which he
himself did not do in view of the circumstances and his office? He had not
come to sit in the Sanhedrin, but to judge the Sanhedrin itself. We have
already said more than enough about Judas, and indeed about this whole matter,
in the proper place.

### Erastus's second objection

“The Apostle does not write,” says Dr. Erastus, “‘Why did you not prohibit him
from the Supper?’ He writes, ‘Why did you not mourn?’ According to Augustine's
exposition, this means: Why did the mourners' grief not ascend to God, so that
he might remove this deed from your midst as he himself knew how?”

**Reply 1.** I hear what Augustine says in that passage. Nevertheless, in the
same place, 1 Corinthians 5:12–13, the Apostle repeats the command in these
words: “Remove that wicked person from among yourselves.” This demonstrates
with perfect clarity that the verb “remove” refers not to God, who is not
mentioned, but to the Corinthians themselves, who were obliged to remove the
man from the assembly of the church.

**Reply 2.** Moreover, who could ever think that the church was commanded to
offer such prayers even against its bitterest enemies?

**Reply 3.** Indeed, if it was ever permissible to offer prayers of this kind,
they certainly had a place only after the church had tried every other remedy
in vain, and excommunication first of all. In the very passage Dr. Erastus
cites, Augustine himself emphasizes that excommunication was employed in the
church as the ordinary final remedy to remove the evil, not from life, but
from the assembly of the church.

**Reply 4.** But why say more? It is clear that Augustine does not say the
Corinthians should have mourned so that the man might be removed, as Dr.
Erastus thinks, but so that

[Scan 89; printed page 67]

the deed might be removed. That is, they should first of all have asked the
Lord to cleanse this stain from the church in some way. Such a remedy ought to
be among the foremost for both public and graver private stumbling blocks. We
have an example of mourning of this kind, although feigned, in the history of
Naboth. What Erastus produces from Augustine therefore contributes nothing
against us. Anyone who tried to misuse Augustine's authority to abolish
excommunication would also do him a grave injustice, since Augustine expressly
defends it.

### Erastus's third objection

Dr. Erastus says in the third place that he does not dare affirm that the
Corinthian man was delivered to Satan, because Paul writes in his second
epistle, “This censure inflicted by the majority is sufficient for such a
man.” Erastus collects many examples to prove that the verb *epitiman* means
nothing other than to reprove and threaten.

**Reply 1.** Yet the Apostle wrote *epitimian*, “censure,” not *epitimēsin*,
“reproof.” According to the church's usage, you can rightly interpret the
former as a formal censure.

**Reply 2.** But suppose the word signifies mere reproof, and that the
Corinthians did not proceed to the publication of the excommunication itself
but suspended it until they could receive Paul's response after he was
informed of the incestuous man's very deep repentance. They might have feared
that he would be swallowed by grief, as Paul appears to indicate with the verb
*charizesthai*, “to forgive,” in 2 Corinthians 2:10. Nevertheless, this does
not affect the matter. Paul commands not that the man be rebuked, but that he
be delivered to Satan. Whether the Corinthians obeyed, or had not proceeded
that far for the reason just given, did they not adequately understand that
Paul sought nothing other than the incestuous man's salvation?

### Erastus's fourth objection

“Careful inspection of the Apostle's words,” Erastus says, “appears to teach
that he did not command that this filthy man be punished as though persisting
in his crime, but that he should be admonished as someone who had ceased to
sin in this way. For the text says, ‘the one who did this deed,’ not ‘the one
who does it,’ and ‘the one who perpetrated it,’ not ‘the one who is
perpetrating it.’”

**Reply 1.** Yet this does not refer to the continuation of the fornication,
but to the deed itself—namely, his taking his stepmother into his household.
Paul himself teaches this plainly when, at the beginning of the chapter, he
does not say “to have had a woman,” but “to have a woman.”

**Reply 2.** Moreover, if this were so, what kind of spirit would the Apostle
display? Would he wish a man who had ceased sinning to be removed from the
midst of the church—that is, as Dr. Erastus presently explains it, delivered
to Satan to be killed—when his repentance should instead have been
congratulated?

**Reply 3.** Finally, I cannot see

[Scan 90; printed page 68]

how Dr. Erastus does not contradict himself here when he contends that “to
remove from the midst” and “to deliver to Satan” signify nothing more in this
passage than admonition and threatening.

### Erastus's fifth objection

“But,” he says, “the Corinthians were affected with great sorrow because of
this epistle. Paul also offers many excuses for his decision that this man be
delivered to Satan; indeed, he says that he repented of it. Yet if this were
nothing other than temporarily barring him from the Supper until he showed
signs of repentance, why would the man who wished this practice observed in
the church and appointed elders for this purpose so anxiously excuse himself
for doing it? Why would he regret obeying Christ's command? And why would the
Corinthians not instead rejoice that the Apostle had opened the way for them
to perform this duty? Thus this must have been something different and greater
than being barred from the ceremonies.”

**Reply 1.** Yet shortly before this Dr. Erastus contended that the Apostle
commanded only that the man be admonished.

**Reply 2.** To address the matter itself, we are compelled to criticize
almost every statement in this argument. The Apostle had severely—and
deservedly—admonished the Corinthians for excessive indulgence and neglect of
duty. The amputation of one member from the body, however just, also grieved
them. As charity commanded, they were concerned for the wretched man's
salvation as well. These were the causes of their great sorrow, a sorrow
according to God. Dr. Erastus does not even touch upon them, intent only upon
wresting this passage from us by any means whatever.

**Reply 3.** I nowhere find Paul excusing himself as though he had sinned in
this affair. Nowhere, I say, do I find it. What I do find is that, when he saw
the Corinthians conceive such sorrow from that reproof and the incestuous man
so sharply tormented by a sense of his crime that they appeared now to need
soothing medicines, Paul softened the harshness of the earlier letter as much
as he could and should. Paul does not say that he regretted it like someone
conscious of having acted wrongly. He acted as true fathers are accustomed to
act when compelled to deal more severely with their dearest children. They
know they have done nothing beyond their duty, yet inwardly grieve as they
await the outcome and fear that perhaps they have somehow exceeded the proper
measure.

**Reply 4.** In sum, apart from the sentence on the last day, from which there
will be no place for delay or appeal, we cannot see what more terrifying thing
could be set before those who fear God than to be declared bound in heaven and
on earth in Christ's name. We judge true what we already cited from Augustine:
the sentence “If he does not hear the church, let him be to you as a Gentile
and a tax collector” is more grievous

[Scan 91; printed page 69]

than being struck with a sword, consumed by flames, or exposed to wild beasts.
But people whom God has intoxicated with a spirit of dizziness and
insensibility never perceive these things until far too late. There are all
too many such people today, if ever there were.

### Erastus's sixth objection

Dr. Erastus also produces Athanasius's exposition. Athanasius thought that
“to deliver to Satan” here and in other passages of the Apostle signified
something like what happened to Job, when Satan was granted a certain right
over him, although circumscribed by fixed limits. Erastus adds that Augustine
and Chrysostom did not reject this opinion.

**Reply 1.** Yet Athanasius expressly says that this man's soul—that is, his
life—was excepted, as occurred in Job's temptation. Augustine speaks
hesitantly, yet in such a way that everyone can see him tending toward the
opinion of those who refer the “destruction,” “ruin,” and “killing” of the
flesh to the mortification of the flesh. Chrysostom, however, expressly refers
it to bodily torment inflicted by Satan. But as we will soon see, Dr. Erastus
plainly and repeatedly insists upon the death of the body itself, as though
the man were commanded to be handed to an executioner not only for torture
but also for death.

**Reply 2.** That opinion of the fathers contributes nothing to the abolition
of excommunication. This is established by the fact that they referred the
Apostle's other words about removing the evil to the very discipline we
defend. Being cast out of the church and consequently tormented by the one who
reigns outside the church do not contradict each other. An evil spirit invaded
Saul after the Lord rejected him. The ancients also relate this concerning a
certain secretary of Stilicho whom Ambrose had excommunicated. Chrysostom
plainly joins both things—the scourge of Satan and expulsion from the church—
in these words: “He was cast out of the common assembly of the faithful, cut
off from the flock, stripped bare, and thus, being abandoned, exposed to the
wolves,” and so forth.

**Reply 3.** Let us therefore grant either that those delivered to Satan were
bodily possessed by Satan himself or that they were bound by Satan as though
with fetters through some other torment, as the Lord himself says of a certain
woman in Luke. This nevertheless occurred because they had been cast out of
the church. Moreover, it is established that no demonically possessed persons
were admitted to the sacred Table unless liberation had been obtained through
the church's prayers, although such people had not been delivered to Satan by
the church. Much less, therefore, were those whom the church had delivered to
Satan customarily admitted.

**Reply 4.** We leave it for all who carefully consider the particulars to
judge whether it is not much more fitting to refer this to Satan's snare. It
is established that all are held captive by this snare who

[Scan 92; printed page 70]

have either not yet been liberated by the Son or, after once being liberated,
are again delivered into that tyrant's hands because of their sins and cast
outside the church in which Christ reigns. This is not so that they may perish
in his prison, but so that, instructed by their own suffering, they may enter
again through the doors of repentance into the kingdom of true liberty.

### Erastus's seventh objection

Dr. Erastus next argues from the meaning of the noun *olethros*, which he says
signifies destruction, slaughter, killing, and death.

**Reply 1.** I would prefer to translate it “ruin,” by which something is
utterly destroyed and perishes. Its primitive form is *ollō*, which means to
destroy and erase completely. But to what purpose is this? The Apostle does
not simply say “for the ruin,” but adds “of the flesh,” so that we may know
what he wishes to perish and be erased by so sharp a medicine of delivery.
This was medicine both for the entire church, from which this putrefaction was
cut away, and for the Corinthian himself. He would have perished if the power
of this cautery had not amputated the lust by which his whole soul was already
ablaze.

**Reply 2.** Dr. Erastus rightly warns that we must not dwell upon this word
alone, but consider the whole phrase, “for the ruin of the flesh,” together.
Yet we think that he errs by omitting the element in which the controversy
lies and calling into doubt the element which is not disputed. We therefore
agree concerning the meaning of the noun *olethros*. Perhaps it was from one
of our writers that Dr. Erastus learned of the refutation of Athanasius's and
Chrysostom's exposition which I presented shortly above. But the whole
controversy lies in the noun “flesh.” Erastus affirms that it signifies the
body which the Apostle destined to be killed. We think that it denotes the
desires of the flesh which must be utterly mortified, and that both expulsion
from the church and delivery into Satan's hands, however understood, are
appointed to destroy them. But let us examine his arguments further.

### Erastus's eighth objection

“I nowhere else find *olethros* used for the extinction of the desires of the
flesh,” he says, “but for killing and other such things. In sum, it signifies
what we said before: ruin and final destruction.”

**Reply 1.** But would this meaning be changed if here too, as elsewhere, the
Apostle had written “the deeds of the body” or “the passions of sins” in place
of “the flesh”? When I say “destroy a man,” do I mean anything different by
the verb “destroy” than when I say “destroy a city”?

### Erastus's ninth objection

“But,” he says, “Paul nowhere else joined *olethros* with ‘of the flesh.’”

**Reply 1.** What then? Did Paul say everything he said twice in the very same
words?

**Reply 2.** Indeed, if I wished to press the testimonies which Dr. Erastus
cites here,

[Scan 93; printed page 71]

what would follow except that *olethros* brings with it the destruction even
of the spirit itself, and thus of the whole person? But this cannot apply to
the person whose spirit is to be saved.

**Reply 3.** Moreover, the very testimonies he cites plainly work against him,
unless perhaps we say that “to put to death,” “to make dead,” and “to kill”
signify something less than *olethros*. I certainly do not think so, unless
someone says that this follows from them.

### Erastus's tenth objection

Dr. Erastus also denies that Paul uses the word “dead” for someone in whom the
desires of the flesh have been abolished.

**Reply 1.** On the contrary, in Colossians 3:3, shortly before the very
passage he cites—“Put to death your members”—we read, “For you have died,”
which is entirely equivalent. Elsewhere, expressly discussing this very
matter, the abolition of the flesh and life of the spirit, Paul says, “The
body is dead because of sin, but the spirit is life because of righteousness.”
And how often is it said that those who live in Christ are dead to sin—that is,
that sin has been extinguished in them? We acknowledge that this must be
understood in such a way that the struggle still continues, but with a certain
victory.

### Erastus's eleventh objection

Dr. Erastus also presses the verb *paradidonai*, “to deliver,” which, he says,
does not signify admonishing someone to restrain the desires of the flesh, but
delivering one person to another for a definite purpose.

**Reply 1.** We do not dispute the verb “deliver” itself. We ask for what
purpose someone is delivered to Satan, which cannot be said of someone who is
a member of the church—that is, of Christ. Hence “to be delivered to Satan”
and “to be cast out of the church” mutually entail one another and are almost
one and the same. Nor do we say that someone is delivered to Satan, the master
of all wickedness, in order to learn something good from him. Rather, as Paul
says elsewhere of Philetus and Hymenaeus, someone condemned for blasphemy, for
example, is delivered so that he may learn not to blaspheme—that is, as the
saying goes, so that vexation may give understanding. We will say something
more about this shortly.

### Erastus's twelfth objection

“How,” he says, “can it seem probable to anyone that the Corinthians would
have experienced such grief if they had been commanded to do nothing more than
bar this man from the ceremonies for a time, until he displayed evidence of
repentance? Certainly no evil could thereby have come either to the church or
to that impure man for which they would be affected with such grief.”

**Reply 1.** The word “ceremonies” is certainly far too feeble. Sacraments are
not just any rites, but seals of our most intimate communion with Christ,
which is the foundation of our salvation.

**Reply 2.** Dr. Erastus also presupposes what we refuted earlier: that this
judgment of the church brings about nothing other than exclusion from the
external administration of the sacraments on earth. But Christ testifies

[Scan 94; printed page 72]

that what is bound in this way on earth will be bound in heaven. Thus, as we
said earlier from Augustine, a lawful excommunication by which someone is
reckoned profane and alien to Christ both on earth and in heaven is infinitely
graver than any capital punishment of the body. The sole exception is that
bodily life, once lost in this world, is not restored, whereas an
excommunicated person is not so excluded from the right to spiritual and
eternal life that he cannot be fully restored through repentance.

**Reply 3.** Finally, the Corinthians had to mourn not only because of the
amputation of one member. The most sorrowful lamentation of Christ himself
over Jerusalem and Paul's words, “Who among you is offended, and I do not
burn?” declare how grave is the experience of such Christian sympathy. Above
all, they had to mourn because of this stain branded upon the whole Corinthian
church. The fast recorded in the history of Naboth also bears upon this.

### Erastus's thirteenth objection

“But,” he says, “elsewhere the Apostle commands us to kill the deeds of the
flesh by the Holy Spirit. He does not wish Satan to kill them in us, but meant
a bodily punishment or temporal death.”

**Reply 1.** None of us has transferred the Holy Spirit's office to Satan.
But let the same Paul answer for us when he speaks of Hymenaeus and Alexander:
“I delivered them to Satan so that they might learn not to blaspheme.” Thus
some are delivered to the master of all wickedness and impurity for
discipline. Let us not be ashamed to learn how this must be understood from
the same Chrysostom whose opinion Dr. Erastus set against us shortly before.
“How,” Chrysostom asks, “does Satan teach them not to blaspheme? If he teaches
others, he ought much more to have taught himself. But if he has not yet been
able to teach himself, neither can he teach others. Paul does not say, ‘that
he may teach them not to blaspheme,’ but ‘that they may learn not to
blaspheme.’ Satan does not do this, but the outcome produces it. In the same
way, in another passage Paul commands concerning the man who had committed
fornication that such a person be delivered to Satan for the destruction of
the flesh—not so that the Spirit may save, but that the Spirit may be saved.
How then does this happen? Just as executioners full of innumerable crimes
punish others, so this is also said of the most wicked demon.” So far
Chrysostom.

**Reply 2.** Moreover, even if we grant that this delivery includes some
bodily torments, as we said earlier, they by no means overturn our opinion of
excommunication, because they follow it, as in the case of the demonically
possessed.

**Reply 3.** But what, I ask, is that bodily punishment or temporal death? If
we say that to be delivered to Satan is the same as being bodily tormented by
Satan

[Scan 95; printed page 73]

so that a person may thereby learn wisdom and, by the Holy Spirit's power,
repent and kill the flesh, that inward enemy, this certainly does not abolish
excommunication. It agrees with Chrysostom, who also writes: “And why did you
not punish them yourself, as Paul did Bar-Jesus and Peter did Ananias, but
delivered them to Satan? He says, ‘Not so that they may die, but so that they
may learn.’” So far Chrysostom.

But if, with Dr. Erastus, we prefer to say that Paul wanted this Corinthian to
be killed by Satan just as Ananias fell dead at Peter's words, Dr. Erastus must
consider what opportunity for repentance was left to the man. He must also
consider what entirely adequate excuse the Corinthians could have offered for
not obeying Paul, especially when Paul wished by this means to test the
strength of his apostolic authority among them. It is one thing to have been
ready to obey, but another actually to have obeyed and done the thing which,
as the Apostle had so sharply admonished them, ought to have been done long
before.
