### QUESTION II

#### What is the beginning of the seventy years of the Babylonian captivity?

Answer. Among chronologists and interpreters of Scripture, ancient as well as
modern, Protestant as well as Roman Catholic, there is great disagreement
about the true limits of the years of the Babylonian captivity, especially
about their beginning. We spoke about this matter at Questions Thirty-one and
Thirty-four of the preceding epoch, but here we have thought it proper to add
more.

Many weighty authors establish the beginning of the captivity at the
destruction of the city, in Zedekiah’s eleventh year and Nebuchadnezzar’s
nineteenth. Others, no fewer or less weighty, anticipate it by eleven years,
placing this boundary in Nebuchadnezzar’s eighth year, or Jeconiah’s first,
which was Jehoiakim’s eleventh. Finally, others—to whose calculation we add
our own—prefer Nebuchadnezzar’s first year, which falls partly in Jehoiakim’s
third and partly in his fourth.

We wonder less at the diversity of these men when we consider their
disagreement with themselves. I pass over Scaliger, whose disagreement with
himself everywhere the Jesuit Petavius pursues rather importunately, injuriously
wringing his nose until blood is drawn, with no other end than to detract from
the great glory of a very great man for the sake of his envious society. In
Book 6 of *On the Emendation of Times*, Scaliger indeed places the beginning of
this seventy-year period in Jeconiah’s first year, when Ezekiel was taken to
Babylon with the king and others, and not in Jehoiakim’s fourth, when Daniel
migrated to Babylon with others. Yet in Book 5 the same man holds that Daniel
and Ezekiel were taken to Babylon together, as though the captivities of
Jehoiakim and Jeconiah had been one and the same. It is remarkable that this
oversight escaped the hundred-eyed hands of the Argus-like and acute Jesuit
Petavius.

> Marginalia, printed p. 144: *Scaliger’s disagreement with himself, passed
> over by Petavius*.

That Nisbett borrows a “clever remedy” (σοφὸν Φάρμακον) from Polanus does not
excuse the error. He holds that Daniel and Ezekiel were taken to Babylon in
the same year, on
the ground that there was no captivity of Jehoiakim before his eleventh year,
which was Jeconiah’s first. As for the statement that Daniel’s first removal
occurred in Jehoiakim’s third year, he thinks it must be understood not of the
third year of his reign, but of the third year of Jehoiakim’s servitude, when
he had become tributary to the Chaldaeans—which was the eleventh and last year
of his reign. I called this a “clever remedy” (Σοφὸν Φάρμακον). For besides
the fact that Scaliger never mentioned this rabbinical invention, this evasion
contradicts the
express words of Scripture. For Daniel 1:1 states in explicit words that he
was brought to Babylon not in the third year of tribute,

[Printed p. 145 | IA leaf 175 | access PDF 175 | OCR object 174]

but in the third year of Jehoiakim’s reign. And even if we granted that the
captivities of Jehoiakim and Jeconiah fell in the same year, things that are
most distinct must not therefore be confused. Although Daniel had not gone to
Babylon before Jehoiakim’s eleventh year, he nevertheless would not have
migrated there together with Ezekiel, since the latter did not set out on the
journey until at least Jeconiah’s fourth month.

But what I wished to note about the disagreement of certain men with
themselves in this dispute concerned the English annotators, who in their two
editions hand down statements that conflict with each other. At Daniel 1:4 and
9:2 they hold that the seventy years of captivity began, not with the
captivity of Jeconiah and Ezekiel in Nebuchadnezzar’s eighth year, but with
Daniel’s, in Jehoiakim’s fourth and Nebuchadnezzar’s first. They prove this by
various arguments and also answer objections. Nevertheless, at Jeremiah
25:12 and 2 Chronicles 36:21, they note that the captivity began not in
Jehoiakim’s fourth year but in Jeconiah’s first, and they endeavor to establish
this by many reasons and to refute what they themselves noted on Daniel. It is
true that these learned and useful annotations proceeded not from the hand of
one man but from those of various men. Yet in a work of such value and so
greatly anticipated, they ought to have applied so exacting a file that, after
the labors of each had been reviewed by all, provision would have been made by
the common counsel of the colleagues to keep open contradictions from being
published—especially in the second edition, in which they professed to supply
the defects of the former edition and wash away its blemishes.

> Marginalia, printed p. 145: *The English annotators contradict themselves*.

As for the question before us, we intend nothing else here than to establish
the third opinion by several reasons and to resolve what is advanced for the
first, passing over the second for brevity’s sake.

That the seventy years of captivity must be begun from Nebuchadnezzar’s first
year, or Jehoiakim’s fourth, and not from Zedekiah’s eleventh, or
Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth, is evident, first, from the fact that almost
everyone establishes the end of the captivity without dispute in Cyrus’s
first year. But from Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth, or even his eighth, to the
Persian Empire of Cyrus, scarcely fifty-two years elapsed, certainly not
seventy. For many of the Jews who saw the later temple being founded had been
of an age suitable for seeing the former house when it was destroyed in
Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth year and for comparing it with the later one. That
age was, at minimum, eighteen years. Therefore, if the captivity is not to be
reckoned from Nebuchadnezzar’s first year and there were seventy years from
the destroyed temple to Cyrus’s first year, all those men must not merely have
lived to their ninety-eighth year, when, remembering the former house, they
wept over the foundations of the second; even at that very advanced age they
must also have had sufficient bodily strength to complete the very long
journey from Babylon to Jerusalem, and to do so on foot, for asses—much less
horses or camels—were not available to a sixth part of the people for riding.
If this is not absurd, it certainly will not escape the name of an unnecessary
miracle.

> Marginalia, printed p. 145: *The seventy years of captivity begin not from
> the destruction of the city in Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth year, but from
> Nebuchadnezzar’s first. Proof 1*.

Moreover, if we turn over sacred or secular writings as carefully as we can,
we shall never hammer out seventy years from Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth to
Cyrus’s first. If, with some, we understand Cyrus’s first year as the first of
his Persian kingdom and not of his monarchy, we shall discover that scarcely
thirty years elapsed between the stated limits in any history. Cyrus ruled
the Persians for twenty-two years before he subdued the Babylonians. Let us
grant that all those years coincided with the times of the Chaldaean kings
from Nebuchadnezzar’s line and thus were included within our seventy-year
period; yet even granting this, how shall we ascend to the desired total? No
one at all assigns Nebuchadnezzar more than forty-five years, and few more than
forty-three. Thus from his nineteenth to his forty-third only twenty-four can
be reckoned. Many assign only two years to Nebuchadnezzar’s son Evil-merodach,
and only six to Nebuchadnezzar’s grandson Belshazzar; all these do not exceed
thirty-two.

Ptolemy’s Mathematical Canon is a catalogue of all the Chaldaean, Persian,
Greek, and Roman emperors from Nabonassar to Antoninus, and has great authority
among all chronologists. If it is false, then, by the confession of many, the
greatest foundation of chronology—apart from the sacred writings—falls:
namely, Ptolemy’s observations of the stars. According to this Canon, after
Nebuchadnezzar there reigned at Babylon Ilvarodamus, or Evil-merodach, for two
years; Nericasolassarus, or Neriglossarus, for four; and Nabonidus (called
Darius the Mede by some and Belshazzar by others) for seventeen. All these do
not make twenty-four years, which, when added to those of Nebuchadnezzar stated
above, do not exceed forty-four.

But let us extend the calculation as far as any authors of any authority light
the way. According to the English annotations on Daniel 1:21, Nebuchadnezzar
will be given twenty-six years after his nineteenth; Evil-merodach two;
Neriglossarus four; Laboroso-ardechus nine months; Belshazzar seventeen years;
and Darius the Mede two. All these make only fifty-two, so that, even in the
largest number of all, eighteen are still lacking from seventy.

The second argument is taken from a comparison of Ezekiel 40:1, Jeremiah
29:10, and Daniel 1:1. Ezekiel teaches that not only his own captivity and
Jeconiah’s, but that of his people—namely, Judah and the people of God—preceded
the destruction of the city by at least eleven years, because the
twenty-fifth year of the captivity corresponds to the fourteenth year of the
desolation. And Jeremiah, immediately after Jeconiah’s migration, indicates to
all the captives then remaining in Babylon that their captivity would be
released after seventy years. This certainly would not have occurred before
eighty-one years had passed if the seventy years of captivity did not begin
before Zedekiah’s eleventh. Daniel affirms that his own and Jehoiakim’s
captivity occurred in Nebuchadnezzar’s first year, as Jehoiakim’s third was
ending and—as Jeremiah has it—his fourth was beginning. In Jehoiakim’s fourth
year Jeremiah indeed declares that Nebuchadnezzar would come to Jerusalem,
not simply, but in order to ruin the city and to impose upon the Jews and the
neighboring nations a yoke that was to be shaken off only after seventy years.
The prophet, I say, speaks in the year that was both Jehoiakim’s third and his
fourth, not simply of Nebuchadnezzar’s coming—for he was then present and
besieging the city—but of his coming for the purpose of accomplishing there
the predicted evils, most of which were to occur many years later and would
not cease before the seventieth year.

3. Third, we argue from Matthew 1:11–12. The Babylonian captivity and migration
is named from Jeconiah, not Zedekiah—indeed, from that Jeconiah who had brothers,
which many of the best interpreters understand of Jehoiakim, Jeconiah’s father.
But those who understand this passage of Jeconiah the son necessarily have the
captivity begin long before the destruction of the city, namely, from the time
when Jeconiah migrated, before he had fathered any children, for Matthew says
that he fathered Shealtiel after the Babylonian captivity.

4. Let the final argument be the testimony of ancient as well as recent men,
of Protestants as well as Roman Catholics: both those who in great number begin
this captivity from Nebuchadnezzar’s first year and close it in Cyrus’s first,
and those who reckon seventy years from the destruction of the temple to its
full restoration in the second year of Darius Hystaspes. For even the latter,
from Cyrus’s first year—which according to our opponents was the end of the
captivity—back to Nebuchadnezzar’s first as its beginning, must reckon with us
eighteen years before the desolation of the temple and the city.

Against our opinion, after the Jesuits Salianus and Torniellus, the learned and
godly old man D. Nisbett advances six arguments. The first coincides with the
fourth. The point of each is this: the beginning of the Babylonian captivity
must be placed at the most notable captivity; but the most notable captivity
was neither Jehoiakim’s nor Jeconiah’s, but Zedekiah’s.

> Marginalia, printed p. 146: *The opposing arguments of Salianus and Nisbett
> are resolved. 1*.

Answer. The minor premise can be disputed. It is certain that, when the city
was destroyed in Zedekiah’s eleventh year, the desolation and devastation were
greater than ever before, yet the captivity was not so great. For the number
of captives at that time did not rise to one thousand, nor did it exceed by a
hundred the final captivity through Nebuzaradan, which no one reckons. But,
leaving this aside, we deny the major premise. The Babylonian captivity of the
Jews had various stages: the first was in Jehoiakim’s fourth year; the second
in Jehoiakim’s eleventh, or Jeconiah’s first; the third in Zedekiah’s eleventh;
the fourth five years after the temple was destroyed, when more than seven
hundred of the remnant were taken to Babylon by Nebuzaradan. Granted that the
third stage was the most notable because of many circumstances, nevertheless
the beginning of the captivity must be taken not from the third stage, however
notable, but from the first.

> Marginalia, printed p. 146: *Four stages of the Babylonian captivity*.

2. Second objection. The seventy years of captivity were the time in which
the whole land of the Jews was desolated and enjoyed its Sabbaths, Jeremiah
25:11. But in Zedekiah’s eleventh year, and not before, the land began to be
desolated and to enjoy its Sabbaths. Therefore, in Zedekiah’s eleventh year,
and not before, the seventy years of captivity began.

Answer. Passing over the minor premise—even though five years after Zedekiah’s
eleventh year Judaea was hindered by many settlers from enjoying its Sabbaths
and from complete desolation—we distinguish the major as follows: “During the
seventy years of captivity the land was desolated and enjoyed its Sabbaths
with respect to the whole of itself” is false; “with respect to the greatest
part of itself” is true. Statements made by synecdoche are true of the subject
as a whole, even if not of the subject with respect to the whole of itself and
all its parts. That this commonplace distinction is rightly applied to the
argument before us we demonstrate from the text of Jeremiah, where Judaea is
no more desolated during those seventy years than Tyre, Egypt, and the
neighboring nations. Yet it is certain, as no one denies, that Tyre and Egypt
were not desolated for all seventy years, for the end of that desolation is
placed at the fall of Babylon and the beginning of the Persian Empire.
Moreover, it is evident from Ezekiel and Jeremiah that Tyre was not captured
until after a siege of thirteen years, and that after Jerusalem had been
devastated, whose calamity the Tyrians mocked. Thus the year of Tyre’s capture
could not begin before Nebuchadnezzar’s thirty-second and the thirteenth year
of the temple’s desolation; and not before captured

> Marginalia, printed p. 146: *The land kept Sabbath for seventy years, but not
> during all the parts of that seventy-year period*.

[Printed p. 147 | IA leaf 177 | access PDF 177 | OCR object 176]

Tyre was Nebuchadnezzar’s expedition into Egypt undertaken. Therefore, the
Egyptian and consequently the Jerusalem desolation that is called seventy
years cannot be understood of each and every one of the seventy years, but of
the greater part of that seventy-year period. This span was the monarchy of
Nebuchadnezzar and the Chaldaeans, which reduced the neighboring peoples to
servitude, these sooner, those later, but in such a way that the servitude and
desolation of them all ended at the expiration of that seventy-year period and
the end of the Chaldaeans’ dominion.

Tyre, Egypt, and the neighboring nations were desolated and served the
Chaldaeans for seventy years, just as the children of Israel dwelt in Egypt
for 430 years, Exodus 12:40. The former served until the end of that epoch and
portion of seventy years, which began before the servitude of the various
subjugated nations commenced, just as the latter sojourned in Egypt until the
end of that era of 430 years, which began from the promise made to Abraham,
long before the Israelites’ descent into Egypt.

Petavius, who is accustomed to strain out Scaliger’s gnats, here swallows
whole camels himself. When discussing the beginning of this seventy-year
period, he openly acknowledges in his *Rationarium* his own inconstancy and
disagreement with himself in his doctrine of times. He devises a Chaldaean
epoch distinct from the Jewish one, by which he would grant Nabopolassar two
years after the enthronement of his son Nebuchadnezzar, and would give
Nebuchadnezzar not merely forty-three years but forty-five. Indeed, from the
Canon of Ptolemy—whom he is accustomed to celebrate so highly—he removes a
full fifteen years from the last king of the Chaldaeans immediately before
Cyrus, substituting the seventeenth year for the thirty-second of Ptolemy and
the Chaldaeans, and thus manifestly overturning all Ptolemy’s celestial
calculations. Anyone with the inclination and leisure to pursue Petavius’s
oversights of this kind would find it quite easy to measure back to Petavius
himself, who very often deserves it, the measure that he too rashly measured
out to Scaliger many times.

> Marginalia, printed p. 147: *Petavius’s inconsistency*.

3. Nisbett’s third objection is this. The beginning of the Babylonian
captivity occurred when the tribe of Judah was taken captive to Babylon; but
this happened in Zedekiah’s eleventh year, Jeremiah 32:27; 2 Kings 25:21.

Answer. The cited passages supply a clear basis for applying to the major
premise the preceding distinction concerning the tribe of Judah with respect
to the whole of itself and with respect to its parts. In Zedekiah’s eleventh
year the tribe of Judah was taken into captivity with respect to its most
principal parts, but not with respect to all. Indeed, in the cited passage
Jeremiah distinguishes in the clearest words the captivity of Judah according
to its different parts into three points, of which Zedekiah’s captivity
constitutes neither the first nor the last, but only the middle—as though the
prophet expressly denied that this middle point was the beginning of Judah’s
captivity.

> Marginalia, printed p. 147: *The captivity of Judah has three points*.

4. Argument 4. The author calls it the primary and insoluble argument, yet he
himself solves it very easily. The Babylonian captivity, he says, began with
the Babylonian Empire. But the Babylonian Empire and Monarchy did not begin
before the capture of Jerusalem.

Answer. The author grants that the Babylonian Monarchy and Empire began before
Tyre was captured, and that it was not captured until a full eleven years
after the fall of Jerusalem—indeed, if we believe the whole of ancient
history, only after a siege of thirteen years. Moreover, as we said above,
Nebuchadnezzar did not move into Egypt until Tyre had been captured; and,
according to D. Nisbett, the Babylonian Monarchy did not begin before Egypt
was subjugated. Therefore, with his kind permission, this primary argument is
no argument at all. We do indeed grant that the years of captivity must be
begun with Nebuchadnezzar’s Monarchy, but it was established in his first
year, for then he broke both the Syrians and the Egyptians, just as his father
Nabopolassar had subjugated those nations before him—unless we wish
gratuitously to strip all authority from ancient history. The victories that
he afterward won over the Jews or Egyptians were nothing other than the
reduction of rebels and the reduction of those previously subjugated into
harsher servitude.

> Marginalia, printed p. 147: *When Nebuchadnezzar’s Monarchy began*.

5. For the final argument D. Nisbett advances testimonies, asserting that the
greater part of learned men stands with him.

Answer. If we count even those named by him, more will contradict him. For in
addition to Willet, whom he holds to begin our seventy-year period both from
Jehoiakim’s third year and from Jeconiah’s first, he grants us Scaliger,
Junius, Capellus, Brughton, Vatablus, and Lyra. But besides these, most of the
others whom we customarily esteem most highly in chronology stand with us:
Calvisius, Emmius, Ussher, Howaeus, and Isaaksonus. I also see Funccius,
Bucholcerus, Torniellus, and Salianus standing for Nisbett. But on the other
side, from our writers, stand Beroaldus and Alstedius; and from the Jesuits,
Petavius and Henricus Philippi. Among the ancients, Eusebius, Jerome,
Augustine, Theodoret, and others do begin the seventy years of captivity from
the destruction of the city. But since they place its end in the second year
of Darius Hystaspes, while reckoning far fewer than seventy years from Cyrus’s
first year to the destruction of the city, they seem to stand on our side
rather than on the opposing side.

> Marginalia, printed p. 147: *More chronologists stand with us*.
