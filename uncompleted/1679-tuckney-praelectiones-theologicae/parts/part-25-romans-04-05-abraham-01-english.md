## Romans 4:5

“But to the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies the
ungodly, his faith is reckoned for righteousness, according to the purpose
of God's grace.” — Vulgate

I well remember that at the very beginning of this work I resolved, and
publicly promised you, not always and continuously to keep you entangled
with me in the thornier thickets of controversy. I would sometimes range
over the more pleasant broad fields of Holy Scripture, which can produce
godly delight in a holy soul, dividing my work between treating
controversies and interpreting at least some passages of Scripture.

To fulfill that promise better, after undertaking with God's help to treat
the doctrine of justification and its controversies, and after discussing
the two preliminary questions at sufficient length—“What is the genuine
meaning of the word justification?” and “What works does Paul exclude from
our justification?”—before proceeding to other questions more particularly
concerning justification, let us allow the doctrine to bask for a little in
the freer field of Scripture. We will especially examine passages able to
carry a torch before the discussions to follow.

Since method requires us in approaching justification to begin from its
subject or object, which normally precedes the rest, and Paul's words “the
one who believes in him who justifies the ungodly” sufficiently identify
that subject, let us with God's help expound this apostolic text according
to our measure. To do this more successfully and fully, we will examine the
entire passage contained in the chapter's first five verses, bringing out
its analysis and sense both as to the words and the purpose and meaning of
Paul's argument, as far as God grants. Paul begins:

“What then shall we say that Abraham our father found according to the
flesh?”

[1679 printed p. 162 | IA leaf 170]

The Vulgate reads, “What then shall we say that Abraham our father found
according to the flesh?” Beza, “What then shall we say that Abraham our
father obtained according to the flesh?”

Verse 2: “For if Abraham was justified by works, he has boasting, but not
before God.”

Verse 3: “For what does Scripture say? Abraham believed God, and it was
reckoned to him for righteousness.”

Verse 4: “To the one who works, the reward is reckoned not according to
grace but according to debt.”

Verse 5: “But to the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies
the ungodly, his faith is reckoned for righteousness according to the
purpose of God's grace.”

The sum is this: Abraham was justified by faith, not works. Therefore all of
us are justified in the same way.

Paul established this conclusion in Romans 3:28 by an argument drawn from
the guilt of everyone, Gentile and Jew, which he had developed broadly and
fully through the preceding three chapters. He now joins to it a new
argument drawn from Abraham's example and case.

To begin farther back, in his debate over justification Paul appears to set
himself two closely connected matters to demonstrate and confirm. The first
and chief is that people are justified by faith in Christ, not works of the
law. The second is that justification is not peculiar to Jews but common to
believing Gentiles. The latter follows from the former. If justification
came by works of law, it would belong only to Jews, since they alone were
under law in the distinctive sense (Romans 3:19). But since it does not come
by works of law, access to it lies open to others besides Jews.

Since it comes by faith in Christ, even deeply sinful Gentiles can share it.
As regards Christ, he is God's salvation prepared before all peoples, a
light for revelation to the Gentiles and the glory of Israel (Luke
2:30–32). As regards the Gentiles themselves, God had already given them
faith, the condition and instrument of this justification, as appears in
Acts 13:48; 15:7; 21:25 and many other passages.

The Jews bore these two claims very badly and stubbornly denied them. They
wished to be justified by their works and sought to establish their own
righteousness from them (Romans 10:3). They scornfully abhorred the Gentiles
as idolaters thoroughly polluted by every kind of crime and kept them far
from fellowship in this grace (Acts 21:28). Paul therefore undertakes to
prove these two connected claims: that people are not justified by works of
law,

[1679 printed p. 163 | IA leaf 171]

and that Gentiles share this justification. In Romans 3:28 he first
concluded, “We conclude, therefore, that a person is justified by faith
apart from works of law,” and immediately adds the second point: “Is he the
God of Jews only? Is he not also of Gentiles? Certainly of Gentiles also.”

He follows precisely the same course in this chapter. In the first five
verses he confirms justification by faith rather than works with a new
argument, and in verse 9 and following establishes the second point: “Does
this blessing, then, fall upon the circumcision only, or also upon the
uncircumcision?” Thus he proves the same two things with a new argument.

Interpreters do not agree whether this is his second, third, or fourth
argument. Pareus calls it the third, adding it to two earlier arguments in
his analysis. Beza regards it as the fourth. The first comes from the guilt
of all people—Gentiles in chapter 1 and Jews in chapters 2–3 through verse
27. The second is that boasting is abolished by the law of faith rather
than works (3:27). The third is that if justification came through works of
law, God would be the God only of Jews under law, though he is also God of
Gentiles who share faith equally with Jews (3:29–30). The fourth, in this
chapter, comes from Abraham's example: he was justified not by works but by
faith, and therefore so are we all.

Learned interpreters thus differ when they distinguish and explain
Scripture, especially its analysis, according to dialectical rules—or
rather, the course of the discourse under its present division into
chapters and verses. I will make several general observations.

1. I piously confess and profess that God's most holy and wise Spirit wrote
Scripture with supreme reason and arranged everything by the best method
for his intended ends: producing, increasing, and exercising faith and
obedience in us. He did not disdain dialectical topics or various kinds and
rules of syllogism and argument, which he frequently employs implicitly and
sometimes completely and openly. Thus the Jesuit Veronensis asserts not
only boldly but absurdly that Scripture nowhere contains even one inference
from the principles of reason.

We grant sober philosophy entrance into theology's sanctuary provided that,
as Baron warns, it serves theology. We admit Vedelius's *Theological
Rationale*, though not Durandus's *Divine Rationale*. More than this, we
acknowledge the highest and truest reason in Scripture's innermost chamber.
The Holy Spirit, writing logically through his scribes, everywhere used not
only grammatical and rhetorical instruments for words and discourse, but
also logical discoveries of arguments, arrangements of axioms, and
syllogistic consequences for meaning, context, and sequence.

2. In certain biblical books, especially epistolary and argumentative ones,
and preeminently in Paul's Epistle to the Romans, above all in the discussion
of a sinner's justification in its earlier chapters, we encounter extremely
frequent, almost continuous, dense

[1679 printed p. 164 | IA leaf 172]

ranks of logical reasons, consequences, and connections. One clear sign is
that connective, inferential, causal, and similar particles occur at the
beginning of almost every verse, or at least every second one. Paul appears
to have written as though debating in the school of Tyrannus and to be
entirely occupied with syllogisms. Origen rightly said on this chapter that
Paul argues with dialectical art. The arguments, connections, method, and
whole discussion possess such orderly precision that Beza reasonably dares
say no profane author, however artfully and almost slavishly he followed the
rules of that art, supplies so exact an example of method.

3. As for Scripture's division into chapters and verses, I acknowledge it
as an ingenious and useful invention. Bale and Godwin attribute it to
Stephen Langton, archbishop of Canterbury; others to Cardinal Hugh; still
others leave the dispute undecided. It is certain that Jews derived it from
European Christians, whom Rabbi Nathan admits following in his concordance.

Long before this, the Jews divided the Law into *parashoth* and *sedarim*,
and the Prophets into *haphteroth*. Some attribute the former to Moses
himself, while others refer them to Ezra's time. Among our writers, Mercer,
Buxtorf, Schickard, Rivet, Hottinger, and others may be consulted besides
Jewish authors.

I confess the invention useful. It makes scriptural sentences and periods
easier to find and remember; and the subject matter and sequence of
narratives and arguments, provided the divisions are properly and carefully
made, can be excellently defined, distinguished, and explained by it.

Several cautions nevertheless concern our present subject.

1. The present division of biblical books into chapters and verses is not
very ancient. Earlier writers had another division of chapters, sections,
and titles, which may be seen in Sixtus of Siena's *Sacred Library*, book 3.
He testifies that the arithmetic division of chapters is recent and that
before five hundred years ago no passage of Scripture among either Hebrews
or Greeks was cited by number except the Psalms. Acts 13:33 mentions the
second Psalm, showing that numbered references to Psalms existed from the
church's beginning. Hilary says the Septuagint translators added numbers to
the Psalms alone, not the other biblical books. Elias Levita says the rabbis
held that the whole law was originally like a single verse or sentence. The
Cabbalists say Moses wrote it without points, accents, or verse divisions,
as appears in many ancient biblical copies. The same is true of the ancient
Codex Claromontanus of the New Testament which the celebrated Beza gave our
university,

[1679 printed p. 165 | IA leaf 173]

where it is still preserved among the archival treasures. Thus the present
division into chapters and verses is, first, not very ancient; second, far
less authentic or divinely authoritative, but entirely human; and third,
cannot be denied to suffer some human defects. It is sometimes inaccurate
and even confused and perverse, separating things most closely joined and
joining things far apart.

The Jews themselves have spoken and complained about confusion and mixture
in their *parashoth*. Maimonides and others tried to remedy it. Everyone
knows Junius and Tremellius slightly altered the common chapter divisions in
their biblical translation because they were inconveniently arranged and,
as our Rainolds rightly judges, “not without substantial reason.” The
Genevans did the same at Romans 2:1. The Roman Church has no basis for
faulting this, since, as Rainolds notes, Pope Sixtus V created a new division
of verses in almost every chapter of the Vulgate Bible printed at Rome in
1590, differing from every preceding division.

It is obvious to an attentive reader that our common verse division is very
unsuitable in several places. Two examples will suffice. In Isaiah 9 the
first words of verse 1 are torn away from the final words of the preceding
chapter, greatly disturbing the sense, as our learned Gejerus aptly and
correctly notes. Jerome and the royal Bibles properly join them. A similar
case occurs in Colossians 4:1, where instruction concerning masters is
forcibly torn from the teaching about servants at the preceding chapter's
end. It should be joined there, so that chapter 3 ends with it and chapter 4
begins with the general duties now found in verse 2 and following.

The common arithmetic division of chapters sometimes resembles our English
metrical version of the Psalms, in which parts are divided to be equal in
length or number of lines more than to cohere suitably in subject matter.
The Jews similarly take account of length when reading their legal
*parashoth*, twice joining two shorter ones so that the fifty-four portions
of law can be completed in fifty-two weeks and the reading of law ends with
each year.

This poor chapter division causes substantial confusion for a reader, above
all because he assumes each chapter contains a distinct portion of biblical
discourse. He is consequently diverted from a passage's true meaning, which
is elicited from the continuous sequence and connection of the preceding
chapter with the following one. I confess this frequently happened to me
while reading Scripture. I therefore thought it useful to warn you not to
consider each chapter an isolated member but to read it, or at least place
it, as one continuous course with the whole body of the sacred text and the
preceding and following chapters. Its meaning will then be fuller and
clearer,

[1679 printed p. 166 | IA leaf 174]

just as a bodily member is understood best when the whole structure of the
other members is seen together at one glance.

You will pardon me, hearers, for wandering somewhat farther afield while
pursuing a matter that, I hope, is neither unworthy of your attention nor
useless to know. It serves our present purpose by showing that although Paul
introduces a new argument in this new chapter, the argument arises from the
sequence of the apostolic discourse, not from the uncertain, sometimes
inconvenient, humanly invented chapter division.

4. My final incidental observation is that although Scripture was written
according to the best divine reason, and at least some books—especially
Paul's epistles and Romans above the rest—possess an extremely careful method
and artful connections of inferences and arguments, their analysis must
nevertheless proceed cautiously. We must not give over its arrangement to
over-subtle minds.

The breadth and majesty of the Holy Spirit's mind must not be confined
pedantically and slavishly within the narrow and often tortuous circles of
our methods and reasoning. He is the sovereign Spirit. It is treason to
subject a king's speech to a grammarian's rod; how much more to subject the
King of kings to rhetoricians, dialecticians, and methodists? He is utterly
free and speaks and acts with perfect freedom according to his pleasure,
dignity, and majesty. He surpasses our reason and will not be bound to the
minutiae of our arguments. “His way is in the sea and his paths in the great
waters, and his footprints are not known” (Psalm 77:19).

Scripture therefore follows not so much artistic method as the wisest
prudence and frequently uses a hidden method. Transpositions occur often in
the sacred text, giving rise to the celebrated rabbinic dictum, “There is no
before and after in the law.” Anyone who judges the chronology of prophetic
and gospel histories merely by their present order in the Bible will stray
very far from truth in many places.

For the same reason the Holy Spirit's scribes do not present their arguments
as punctually and distinctly as our Scholastics and methodists, but in an
oratorical or freer manner of speech, “as the Spirit gave them utterance”
(Acts 2:4). They often answer Christian questions or adversarial objections
that were familiar enough to them but less known to us, moving without
transitional markers and silently digressing.

Hence interpreters give the following rules for understanding Paul's
epistles: “We must not always and everywhere seek a connection of both
chapters and sentences in these epistles, because Paul wrote in epistolary
fashion, in which friends frequently write many familiar things to friends
without order and connection.” “Paul often answers tacit objections and, as
Jerome says in Letter 150, discusses by implication whatever someone could
object before it is raised. If another necessary question happens to arise
in an argument, he commonly digresses to it and afterward returns to his
chief purpose.”

[1679 printed p. 167 | IA leaf 175]

Thus transpositions are so frequent in Paul's epistles, especially Romans,
that they break and disturb the sequence of discourse, creating the greatest
difficulty particularly for analytical interpreters. According to their
different ability and disposition, their expositions and analyses therefore
usually diverge and sometimes directly conflict.

To end these prolegomena at last: although everyone recognizes Paul
introduces a new argument in this chapter, some call it his second, others
his third, and others his fourth. I regard it as the second.

We must now consider the subject or topic from which this second new
argument is drawn.

First, some observe that after Paul said in Romans 3:21 that God's
righteousness apart from law is attested by the Law and Prophets, he now
confirms both parts: from Abraham's example in verse 3, drawn from Genesis,
the first book of the Law properly so called; and from David's testimony in
verses 6–8, drawn from Psalms, numbered among the Prophets. This is true and
sound in itself and not foreign to Paul's purpose or sequence.

Second, Aquinas's observation should not be passed over. Explaining the
progress of Paul's discussion, he writes, “In chapter 3 Paul suppressed the
Jews' boasting in law; now he suppresses their boasting in circumcision.”

Third, some prefer this account: in the former argument throughout the first
three chapters, Paul proved that no one is justified by works performed
without faith—whether Gentile works in chapter 1, unbelieving Jewish works
in chapters 2–3, or works generally. In this chapter he now proves that not
even works after faith or arising from faith justify, such as Abraham's
works discussed here. I would not confidently affirm that this formally
constitutes Paul's new argument, though I readily call it quite probable. I
have already sought to prove against the Romanists that Paul excludes works
flowing from faith from justification, especially in this chapter.

Fourth and finally, the most natural view, followed by many, is that Paul's
former argument was drawn from reason, while this one is drawn from example.
In the former he proved all Gentiles and Jews guilty of violating law and
therefore subjected by law to death and condemnation, so that they cannot be
justified by works of law. In

[1679 printed p. 168 | IA leaf 176]

this argument he adduces the supreme example of Abraham, in whom the Jews
especially boasted. They did not doubt that his works justified him and were
ready to oppose his example to Paul's assertion of justification by faith
without works. Paul turns Abraham's own example against them and kills them
with their own sword.

Examples generally illuminate a matter greatly and, when parallel and
appropriate, confirm it strongly. The more evident they are, the more
effectively they convince even the senses, for examples place a matter most
plainly before the eyes. Because clear and truthful teaching calls for such
examples, Paul very wisely follows this course and illustrates the universal
doctrine already delivered by a particular example, giving it the first
place among his arguments. The prophets, Christ, and his apostles frequently
used this mode of argument. Paul uses the noblest example and the one most
effective for convincing Jews: Abraham.

Accordingly, as some carefully observe, in the debate over justification
James adduces Abraham's example once and Paul three times. Paul's argument
has this form:

All believing children must be justified in the same way that Abraham, the
father of all believers, was justified. Abraham was justified neither by
works preceding nor following his faith in the promised Christ, but by faith
in Christ alone, imputed to him for righteousness. Therefore all his
children are justified in the same way.

This is Beza's fuller analysis. More briefly: Abraham and all who will be
saved share the same mode of justification. Abraham was justified not by
works but by faith. Therefore every other believer who will be saved is
justified in the same way.

This is the general sum of everything contained in the chapter's first five
verses, which each verse arranges, explains, and proves with consummate art.
