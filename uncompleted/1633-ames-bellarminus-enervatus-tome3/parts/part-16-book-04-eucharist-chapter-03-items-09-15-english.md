9. Protestant reply. If a body could be in many places, by the same rationale
it could be in all places. But then being everywhere would not be peculiar to
God, nor would Christ's human nature be circumscribed by a definite place. Yet
the fathers unanimously confirm this against the Eutychian heresy and also
expressly affirm that no body can be wholly everywhere.

Bellarmine 1. The first difference between God and Christ's body

[1632 witness | printed p. 99]

with regard to existence in a place is that God is in fact and in reality
everywhere, while Christ's body is not in fact and in reality everywhere and
never will be.

Protestant reply. This difference does not remove the argument. For if
Christ's body can be everywhere, although it neither is nor will be, then
omnipresence is not peculiar to God by the nature of the thing.

Bellarmine 2. The second difference is that the deity cannot be confined to
one place, but Christ's body can be restricted to only one place. For it was
in only one place before the institution of the sacrament, and it will be so
again after the consummation of the world.

Protestant reply. 1. By this rationale that argument of the Christians is
completely enervated: “The Holy Spirit is everywhere; therefore he is God.”
For the heretics will answer that the Holy Spirit is indeed everywhere but
can be restricted to only one place. Nor can this answer be refuted in any
way if the same thing can be everywhere and nevertheless be restricted,
either before or afterward, to only one place. 2. It implies both a
contradiction and an exceedingly absurd chimera that one and the same thing
should be in potency for only one place and also for everywhere, yet actually
be neither in only one place nor everywhere.

10. Protestant reply. No temporal thing can be in two times at once;
therefore the same localized thing cannot be in different places either.

Bellarmine. Times cannot exist simultaneously, since they are always flowing,
but places remain.

Protestant reply. 1. The flow of time no more separates one time from another
than distance separates disjoined places.

11. Protestant reply. If one body is in different places, it will at the same
time be one and many, continuous and divided.

Bellarmine 1. A thing's essential unity does not depend upon the unity of its
place, since being one is prior to being in one place, but depends upon its
internal principles.

Protestant reply. 1. A thing's essential unity so depends upon its internal
principles that their undivided conjunction is necessarily required. 2. The
principles of a corporeal thing do not constitute one body unless they come
together into one place. 3. Among the principles of this or that body, the
common nature of body, which consists in dimensions, holds the primary place;
therefore those dimensions must be joined into one. 4. Although a thing is
one by nature before it is in one place, it is not so prior in time, nor is it
prior by nature to possessing that formal rationale upon which the unity of
place necessarily depends. 5. Unity of quantity and of corporeal place is
prior by nature to an organic human body. 6. Not

[1632 witness | printed p. 100]

everything that is prior can be actually separated from what is posterior,
as may be seen in a genus and its species. 7. It is most certain that a body
can no more be separated from one place than from place altogether. For it
was not place in the abstract but this place that was created together with
this body. Therefore, just as no body can exist without place, neither can it
exist without unity of place.

Bellarmine 2. Christ's body as it is on the altar is not continuous with his
body as it is in heaven. Yet it is not discontinuous from it or divided from
it. For being made continuous and being divided belong to things which are
many, but this body in heaven and on the altar is the same.

Protestant reply. 1. Being made continuous does not belong to things which are
many. 2. Being divided does not belong to things which are actually many
before division, but afterward. 3. Things separated by a great distance must
be divided. For what denies contiguity denies continuity much more; but a vast
distance lies between the body in heaven and the body on earth.

Bellarmine 3. By this argument the places themselves are proved to be
discontinuous and Christ's body to be divided from itself with respect to
place, but not with respect to its own substance or quantity.

Protestant reply. What is divided from itself with respect to place is also
divided with respect to the thing located. For a place is not a place except
with respect to the thing located, nor is it divided unless the located thing
is divided. Moreover, the formal rationale of a located body is substantial
quantity. Therefore, whatever is divided from itself with respect to place is
also divided with respect to substance and quantity.

12. Protestant reply. If one body is posited in multiple places, it will be
in a place and not in a place, because it will be in no place
circumscriptively or definitively—that is, so contained in one place that it
is not elsewhere.

Bellarmine 1. The same body can be in multiple places circumscriptively and
also definitively. For a body's being circumscriptively in some place
requires only that it be commensurate with that place, not that it is not in
another place.

Protestant reply. 1. In this subterfuge Bellarmine abandons Thomas and all the
Thomists. Among them, “to be in a place definitively” means nothing other than
that its mode of existing in one place is incompatible with being elsewhere
at the same time. But “to be in a place circumscriptively” means, in addition,
to be commensurate with that very place by means of quantity. Bañez, on Part
1, question 52, article 2. 2. To remove all sense and intelligibility from his
words, Bellarmine makes being in a place definitively something greater than
being there circumscriptively: “circumscriptively,” he says, “and also
definitively.” Yet all the scholastics say that angels are in a place
definitively but not circumscriptively, and it is altogether unheard of that
anything should be in a place circumscriptively and not

[1632 witness | printed p. 101]

definitively. 3. If a body three cubits in size can be commensurate with three
places at once, as Bellarmine maintains, then at the same time it can be
adequately commensurate with both three and nine cubits. But this is absurd.

Bellarmine 2. A body three cubits in size can occupy a space of nine cubits if
the same magnitude is placed in a location twice or three times, although it
is commensurate only with a place of three cubits.

Protestant reply. 1. If the same three-cubit body is posited in three places,
it will occupy a nine-cubit space in the same manner in which three
three-cubit bodies ordinarily occupy it. But those bodies are commensurate
with nine cubits; therefore this one body is as well. 2. If the same body can
be posited in three disjoined places, it can also be posited in three
contiguous places, and in such a way that continuity results. Now if the Roman
pontiff, a man perhaps three cubits tall, should occupy not only the place he
now occupies but also the next one and the one adjoining it, with no body or
place intervening, who would deny that he was commensurate with a space of
nine cubits?

Bellarmine 3. The same can be said concerning the definitive mode of existing.
For something's being definitively in a place requires only that it be
confined to some definite space, so that it is in that whole space and not in
a larger one, whether or not it is also definitively in another place.

Protestant reply. 1. Here Bellarmine confuses definitive presence with
circumscriptive presence. For, at least when a body is in question, to be in
one whole definite space and not in a larger one means nothing other than to
be commensurate with that place. 2. If something that is definitively in one
place can be in another, it certainly can much more easily be in a larger
place, for greater and lesser are more easily reconciled than one and
another. 3. The papists maintain that the same body of Christ is in a smaller
and a larger host, in a small chalice and a larger cup, or even—as some of them
hold—in an entire consecrated cask.

Bellarmine 4. We can say that, besides these two modes of existing in a place
(circumscriptively and definitively), there is a third: by sheer presence,
which is the manner in which God is in a place. In this manner those bodies
which by divine power are simultaneously in various places are in a place.

Protestant reply. Thus Bellarmine's whole dispute finally comes down to this:
that a body is maintained to be in a place in the manner in which God is.
This is both blasphemous and implies a more shameful contradiction than the
very one it serves to remove. For nothing except God is present in the manner
in which God is present; and there is a more manifest contradiction in a
body's being substantially present in a place in a mode that is not only
incorporeal but also divine than in its being present in two places.

13. Protestant reply. What begins to exist somewhere where previously it did
not

[1632 witness | printed p. 102]

exist either comes there by local motion or is generated there. What is moved
locally leaves one place in order to acquire another. But how can that which
already exists be generated anew? In no way, therefore, can something which
is in one place begin to be in another at the same time.

Bellarmine 1. The Lord's body begins to be on the altar where it was not
before, not by generation or local motion but by the conversion of the bread
into it.

Protestant reply. 1. The change of one body into another is not opposed to the
generation of that in which the change terminates. For whether the
matter of the former body remains and a new form is induced in it, or that
whole body is annihilated and a new one is put in its place, in that change of
substance there must be either some generation or some creation. 2. This
argument is advanced generally against Bellarmine's position that “the same
body can be in various places circumscriptively and definitively.” Therefore
this response does not suffice unless Bellarmine concedes that this cannot
happen except by a change of another substance into the one that is to occupy
the new place, which he will by no means grant.

Bellarmine 2. Similar examples are not lacking, for the rational soul begins
to be in matter added through nutrition, and God begins to be in a newly
created thing without generation or local motion.

Protestant reply. These examples are not similar. For the soul does not begin
to be in a new place by a total and adequate circumscription while retaining
the former place, much less does God. Nor is any substance changed into a
rational soul, much less into God.

14. Protestant reply. If the same body were in multiple places, many
contradictions would follow. For it could be above and below; remote and not
remote; moving and resting; approaching one place while simultaneously
receding from it; heated to the highest degree in one place and chilled to
the highest degree in another; or even wounded and killed in one place while
remaining well in another.

Bellarmine 1. Some theologians think that the positing of one body in
different places multiplies only relations, not absolute properties. Others
think that absolute properties are multiplied, and they proceed so far as to
say that the same man can be drunk in one place and thirsty in another, or
even die in one and live in another.

Protestant reply. By licking this monstrous offspring in different ways,
these eminent theologians have not reformed it but augmented it with various
new and unheard-of monsters.

Bellarmine, secondly. When one body is posited in different places, its
relations to those places will necessarily be multiplied; and the same body
will be above and below, remote and near, in motion and at rest. Nor is any
contradiction implied by these contrary relations, since they are

[1632 witness | printed p. 103]

under a different respect, namely, insofar as that body is in different
places.

Protestant reply. With such a remedy any contradiction could be defended,
for some different relation can always be devised by which opposites are
attributed to the same thing. But diversity of place is irrelevant to
avoiding a contradiction. It does not exempt a thing from those laws of
opposition: “to the same subject,” “in the same respect,” “in relation to the
same thing,” and “at the same time.” No logician ever added, “and in the same
place.”

Bellarmine 3. It seems that the same judgment should be made about actions
which terminate at places.

Protestant reply. According to this judgment of Bellarmine, therefore, it can
happen that one and the same man—Bellarmine, say, or Suárez—walks at the same
time from Rome toward Salamanca and from Salamanca toward Rome, and along one
and the same line. It would be delightful to see how, on meeting himself on
the road, he would greet himself, like the doubled Sosia in Plautus's
*Amphitryon*; how, once the greeting was performed, he would run into and
penetrate himself—for no one can yield place to himself, nor would this
doubled traveler need to do so; and finally how he would be affected when,
having arrived at Rome and Salamanca in precisely the same instant, he
learned from himself that throughout that whole time he had been resting
pleasantly in bed in both places and had dreamed certain monstrous things
about such a journey. But against this judgment of Bellarmine we set the
judgment of William of Auvergne, who was among the first and weightiest of the
scholastics: “Neither human imagination nor the intellect admits this
restlessness—indeed, this whirlwind and tempest of contrary motions.” *On the
Sacrament of the Eucharist*, folio 10.

Bellarmine 3. A contrary judgment should be made about the things received in
a located body, for these are not multiplied—for example, being heated and
being chilled, being ill and being well, living and dying.

Protestant reply. The same judgment must be made about these too, unless as
many miracles are invented as there are qualities or actions received in a
body. For by its own nature a body existing in two places can receive two
contraries if contrary agents are applied to it. And who will assure us that
this will be miraculously prevented so that it never happens? The papists who
concede that the same man can live and die simultaneously in different
places are certainly more restrained than Bellarmine in inventing miracles.

#### Second question

Also pertaining to the possibility of the real presence of Christ's body in
the Eucharist is the question whether such a body can be present and not
occupy a place.

15. Bellarmine. Three things belong to magnitude. 1. That it be

[1632 witness | printed p. 104]

extended in itself, with one part beyond another, and consequently that it
have a certain intrinsic position and an order and arrangement of parts; this
is essential to magnitude. 2. That it coexist with a place, or be commensurate
with a place, and have an extrinsic position in relation to the place. 3. That
it exclude another body from its place, or not allow another magnitude
together with itself in a place commensurate with it. The second can be
separated from the first, and the third from the first and second. With these
points noted, the common opinion of Catholics is that the whole magnitude of
Christ's body is truly present in the sacrament of the Eucharist, but has the
first condition, not the second and third. We also think that penetration of
bodies can be granted.

Protestant reply. This opinion sufficiently overturns itself. For 1. if the
kind of extension Bellarmine describes in the first condition is essential to
magnitude and body, then it must occupy space, since nothing can be extended
in this manner except in space; but the space of a body is its internal place.
2. The extrinsic place discussed in the second condition necessarily follows
when that same magnitude is considered in relation to an encompassing body,
if there is any such body. For one body cannot possibly be contained on every
side by another body without having an extrinsic position in relation to the
other body by which it is contained. 3. The third condition is plainly
inseparable, for it is just as certain that one magnitude excludes another
from the same commensurate place as it is certain that when one opposite is
affirmed, the other is denied. 4. An organic body, such as a human body,
requires a greater distinction in the position of its parts than any other
body. Therefore these fabrications are less probable concerning Christ's body
than they are concerning a body considered simply. 5. The penetration of
dimensions in two bodies cannot happen, because by that penetration the
dimensions of the two bodies would be the same, and thus two bodies would be
one. For a body, as Bellarmine acknowledges in this chapter 5, is nothing
other than extension in length, breadth, and depth.
