QUESTION XVI.

ON THE GOVERNMENT OF THE CHURCH.

Ought the government of the church to be monarchical? We deny against the
papists.

I. Up to this point the church has been discussed as considered with respect
to both its internal and external state. Now follows the third part of the
treatment, concerning its government. The first question, upon which nearly
all the others depend, concerns the nature of this government and is therefore
rightly reckoned among the principal questions, so much so that Bellarmine says
in the preface to this disputation that “the chief matter of Christianity is at
issue.” Condensing into a few points what others have pursued more fully, we
will elucidate the whole matter with as much brevity as possible.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

II. Concerning the state of the question, it must be observed: 1. It is not
disputed whether some government must be established in the church. For since
God is a God of order, not of confusion, and order cannot be had without
government, God must necessarily have prescribed in his house some manner by
which it would be governed. Hence the names *family*, *city*, *body*, and
*sheepfold* are attributed to it, things which cannot be well ordered without
some government. Rather, the question is what that government is like and of
what nature: monarchical or aristocratic?

III. 2. It is not asked whether some monarchical government has a place in the
church to which all people must be subject by the necessity of salvation. No
one denies that this is proper to Christ, because he was appointed by God the
Head and supreme King of his church, rules it *monarchically*, and is therefore
everywhere distinguished by the titles of Head, Bridegroom, and King, bearing
written on his thigh, “KING

[1847 vol. III printed p. 126 | IA leaf 160 | PDF p. 160]

OF KINGS AND LORD OF LORDS” (Rev. 19:16). Rather, the question is whether
besides Christ another head is given and another monarch must be acknowledged
among the rulers of the church to whom all are bound to be subject. The
adversaries affirm this; we deny it.

[Marginalia: The government of the church is proved not to be monarchical. 1.
From Luke 22:25–26.]

IV. The reasons are: 1. Because Christ utterly prohibited all lordship in the
church (Luke 22:25–26). For when the disciples had contended with one another
about the primacy, he restrained their ambition with these words: “The kings
of the Gentiles exercise lordship over them, and those who exercise authority
over them are called benefactors. But it shall not be so among you; rather, he
who is greatest among you should be like the least, and he who leads like one
who serves.” And Peter says (1 Pet. 5:1–2), “Shepherd the flock which is among
you, exercising care over it, not by compulsion but willingly, etc.; neither
as lording it over the allotted portions, but as those who are examples to the flock,
etc.” In both passages lordship in the church is prohibited and only ministry
and *service* are left.

V. Nor can it be said, 1. that in Luke “monarchy and lordship are not
absolutely forbidden, but only a mode of them which is not like political
lordship, or the tyranny of the kings of the Gentiles.” (1.) The apostles were
not contending with one another about the mode of primacy but about primacy
itself. Hence Christ’s answer must absolutely proscribe all lordship in order
to answer their question. (2.) If he had wished to prohibit only a certain
mode of lordship, he would not have removed the ambition which he wishes to
drive far from them, since other forms of primacy also nourish ambition. (3.)
From the usage of Scripture, the phrase “not so” conveys a simple and absolute
negation (Ps. 1:4; 147:20; Matt. 19:8), which in Matthew 20 and Mark 10 is
expressed, “It shall not be so among you.” (4.) If Christ had already given or
was going to give lordship and primacy to Peter, why does he not instruct the
apostles about it? Why does he not say that their duty is to subject themselves
to him? This certainly should have been said in this place so that he might
easily settle that contention. (5.) The *service* which he assigns to his own
is *incompatible* with monarchy.

VI. 2. It is no better to add that violent lordship is at issue, which is
designated by the words *to lord it over* and *to exercise authority over*.
(1.) Although the word is sometimes used for violent lordship, this occurs on
account of the subject under discussion, not from the force of the word, which
elsewhere conveys simple lordship (Gen. 1:28; Ps. 72:8; 110:2). (2.) Then Luke
uses the simple words *to rule* and *to exercise authority*. (3.) The apostles
were not disputing about violent lordship; therefore there was no need for
Christ to speak about it. 3. Nor is the objection valid that “Christ does not
forbid every primacy, because he adds, ‘he who is greater among you.’” For he
speaks not of a true greatness with respect to the thing, but of an imaginary
one with respect to aspiration and desire, which Matthew and Mark explain by
these words, “He among you who wishes to be first”; or it must be understood of
a primacy of order, not of jurisdiction and lordship. 4. Nor does Christ’s
example help anything here when he says in what follows, “But I am among you as
one who serves,” although he nevertheless possessed true lordship. For he does
not argue from an equal case but from the greater to the lesser. If I, who am
Lord, am nevertheless among you as one who serves, how much more fitting is it
for you, who are ministers and have no jurisdiction over one another, to be
humble and remove from yourselves every thought of primacy and lordship? That
Christ must be understood in no other way is gathered from John 13:14, where
he likewise prescribes humility to them: “If I who am Lord have washed your
feet, you also ought to wash one another’s feet.”

[Marginalia: 2. From Matthew 23:8–9.]

VII. Second, Christ proscribes the same absolute and *autocratic* authority
(Matt. 23:8–9, etc.): “Do not be called Rabbi, for one is your teacher,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 127 | IA leaf 161 | PDF p. 161]

namely Christ, and you are all brothers. Call no one father on earth, for one is your
Father, who is in heaven. Neither be called masters, for one is your Master,
namely Christ; but he who is greatest among you should be your minister.” Here
it is clear that Christ forbids the disciples all the names of mastery and
authority which the Pharisees ambitiously arrogated to themselves. He does not
absolutely and simply condemn the names *teacher*, *father*, and *master*,
which in a sound sense can be given to true pastors, for teachers are reckoned
by the apostle among ecclesiastical offices (Eph. 4), and he calls himself the
father of the Corinthians because he begot them in Christ through the gospel
(1 Cor. 4:15). Rather, he condemns them in a qualified sense, with respect to
the reality of the title, namely a mastery and authority from which no one may
appeal but in which all must acquiesce (Gal. 1:8). In this manner the rabbis
and teachers among the Jews attributed these names to themselves and wished
them to be attributed to them, so that they would be, as it were, teachers in
a school, fathers in a family, and masters in a household who prescribed laws
and commands to the faithful in the church according to their own pleasure,
which had to be obeyed absolutely and without examination. That Christ’s words
must be understood in this way is evident from the two reasons added by him.
The first is taken from the condition of the faithful, who, since they are
brothers, are equal among themselves, so that it is unfitting for anyone to
assume authority and jurisdiction over another: “For you are brothers.” The
other is taken from Christ. Since such authority belongs to him alone, it
cannot and ought not be communicated to anyone on earth. Hence it is
invincibly gathered that the unity of the church on earth is wrongly made to
consist in adherence to some visible head which unites the whole body in
itself, since Christ wished it to be joined in himself alone. He is the one
Teacher, Father, and Master under whose authority the entire church is united
as his school, family, and kingdom. It is an invasion of Christ’s rights to
wish to establish the mode of the church’s union in the authority of a mortal
man who is to be the teacher, father, and master of the church, and to set his
see as the infallible rule and arbiter of decisions of faith, as the
adversaries contend.

[Marginalia: 3. From 2 Corinthians 1:24 and 1 Peter 5:3.]

VIII. Third, the apostles remove all lordship from ministers: 1. Expressly,
when they deny that they exercise lordship over the faith of Christians (2
Cor. 1:24): “Not that we exercise lordship over your faith, but we are helpers
of your joy”; and they forbid this dominion to pastors (1 Pet. 5:3): “Not
lording it over the allotted portions, but being examples to the flock.” That is, they
are not to command the Christian people at pleasure in the manner of kings,
for the people are God’s possession or inheritance, as was said of the
Israelite people (Deut. 4:20; 9:29), because this belongs to Christ the *Chief
Shepherd*, the sole Prince of Pastors. 2. Implicitly and equivalently, when
they designate pastors by names which denote care and ministry but not
lordship or dominion, calling them ministers, servants of God, ambassadors,
messengers, stewards, and managers of the mysteries of God. They thereby teach
that pastors ought not arrogate to themselves the right to reign over
consciences, but only to cause Christ, the sole Prince and Monarch of the
church, to reign. “We do not preach ourselves,” says Paul (2 Cor. 4:5), “but
Jesus Christ the Lord, and that we are servants on his account.” From all
these passages taken together an invincible argument is drawn. For if God had
wished to erect some visible monarchy in the church, is it credible that he
would have forbidden all lordship to pastors so often and so expressly? Would
he not somewhere have declared that monarchy clearly and expressly, so that
no place for doubt or error might remain in so momentous a matter? That he did
not do so, even in those places where it especially should have been done,
will be clear from the following argument.

[Marginalia: 4. From Paul’s silence concerning a monarch of the church.]

IX. IV. If Christ had appointed some monarch and ecumenical pontiff in the
church, Paul should have made some

[1847 vol. III printed p. 128 | IA leaf 162 | PDF p. 162]

mention of him in those places where he enumerates the orders and ecclesiastical
offices instituted by Christ, because this was of the greatest importance.
Otherwise, if he had kept it silent, this would have occurred either from
ignorance, because he did not know it—who would say that of *inspired*
apostles?—or from negligence or malice, because he was unwilling to mention
it. But this would be to accuse him of bad faith and of *dereliction of duty*
for not fulfilling his office. Yet it is evident that Paul was completely
silent about him from the notable passage in Ephesians 4:11, where, recounting
the various orders of ecclesiastical offices, both ordinary and
extraordinary, he indeed mentions apostles, evangelists, and prophets among
the latter and pastors and teachers among the former, but he is utterly silent
about a monarch and ecumenical pontiff of the church. The same is true in 1
Corinthians 12, where the same enumeration occurs. Nor will he escape who says
with Bellarmine (book 1, chapter 9) that Paul mentions him when he says that
“Christ gave some as apostles, since supreme power was given not only to Peter
but also to the remaining apostles—to Peter as an ordinary pastor who would
have a successor, but to the others as delegates who would have no
successors.” For when the ecumenical pontiff is disputed, the issue is not
simply an infallible authority in the church, which no one can deny existed in
each and every apostle. Rather, it is one person alone who possesses that
authority in such a way that he admits no colleague and is, as they say, the
center, principle, and root of unity. But the apostle speaks of all of them in
the plural when he says that Christ “gave apostles,” not of some one individual
in whom plurality is reduced to visible unity. Add that the office of the
apostolate was temporary and extraordinary and had to cease, not ordinary and
perpetual.

[Marginalia: 5. From the fact that Christ is the Head and King of the church.]

X. 5. Christ alone is the Monarch, Head, and Bridegroom of the church, to
which he professes that he will always be present until the consummation of
the age (Matt. 28:20). But this is *incompatible* with a vicar, who supplies
only for one who is absent or powerless. Otherwise, the Monarch would not be
*alone*, the body would be two-headed, and the Bridegroom would admit a partner
to the marriage bed, which cannot be tolerated. This is confirmed from the
fact that Paul grounds the character of Head which Christ possesses in
relation to the church upon the relation of Savior, when he says (Eph. 5:23),
“Christ is the Head of the church, the Savior of his body,” so that no one can
be Head who is not also Savior. Hence he adds that “husbands ought to love
their wives as Christ loved the church and gave himself for it, etc.” So also
in 1 Corinthians 1:12 he condemns all those who, divided among themselves into
factions, were choosing either Peter, Paul, or Apollos as their head, saying,
“I am of Paul, I of Apollos, and I of Cephas. Is Christ divided? Was Paul
crucified for you? Were you baptized in the name of Paul?” That is, just as
Christ is one and is not divided into many, so the members of the church ought
not be split into parties under this or that minister, but ought to unite under
Christ, who alone was crucified for the faithful and in whose name we were
baptized. Thus he intimates that he alone is to be acknowledged as the Head
to whom we should adhere who died for us and in whose name we were baptized.
And indeed, as there is one body and one Spirit, so only one head must be
given, especially since Christ is Head of the church in the same way that a
husband is head of a wife, and he admits no vicar in the marital office. “The
one and only true head,” says Basil in the *Ascetical Works*. And Gregory I
says in *Letters* 4.36, “One King, one Monarch, one sole Head.” And since it
belongs to the head to impart life to the whole mystical body, everyone sees
that this belongs to Christ alone. The distinction between the head with
respect to internal power, which is proper to Christ alone, and the head with
respect to external direction, which belongs to the pope, is made in vain.
For the pope also claims internal power for himself by prescribing laws which
bind the conscience, while Christ rightfully possesses both.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 129 | IA leaf 163 | PDF p. 163]

XI. Nor is the distinction of the Head into the first and principal head and
a secondary and ministerial head to be admitted here, because it involves a
contradiction to speak of a secondary head, since *head* of itself denotes
what is first and supreme. Then it is monstrous to attribute two heads to one
body. Nor do the examples of vicegerents, who are appointed by a king as
rulers of provinces, prove this. For however much authority is attributed to
them, no one can without offense usurp either the name of the king or that of
head of the kingdom under the king. Nor, if the names *pastor*, *bishop*,
*apostle*, and *prophet* are rightly attributed both to Christ and to
ministers, does it follow that the name *head* can also belong to each. For the
former denote only a ministry, which can fall to servants, but the latter
denotes the supreme dignity and authority which is proper and peculiar to the
Lord alone. They are ambassadors, not vicars; ministers, not lords;
instruments, not heads. Ministers can discharge the duties of a pastor in
preaching and remitting sins, but never the duties of the supreme King. Just
as the best man never attempts the office of the bridegroom as bridegroom,
since he who undertook this would entangle himself in adultery, so faithful
ministers are friends and servants of the Bridegroom who ought to prepare the
church as a bride for Christ the Bridegroom alone and are therefore kindled
with God’s holy jealousy (2 Cor. 11:2). But without wickedness and sacrilege
they cannot claim that bride for themselves and call themselves her
bridegrooms and heads. Hence it is evident how blasphemously Bellarmine says
in *On the Roman Pontiff* 1.9: “And certainly, if the church which is on earth,
even with Christ excluded, is not inaptly compared to a bride, then even with
Christ excluded she ought to have one head.”

[Marginalia: 6. From absurdities.]

XII. 6. If besides Christ some other head of the church were given, then from
the perpetual relation between head and body the church would be called the
body of someone other than Christ. But this is unheard of in Scripture, which
everywhere calls the church the body of Christ just as it calls Christ its
head (Eph. 1:22; 4:11–12; 5:23; Col. 1:18), but nowhere calls the church the
body of the pope. Nor can the distinction between a principal and ministerial
head remedy this difficulty. For besides being false and *incompatible*, as
was just said—namely, contrary to the analogy of the human body from which the
metaphor is taken—it is also useless. For the difference between principal
and nonprincipal does not prevent a reciprocal relation from being
acknowledged within the proper kind of each thing. How indeed could he who can
be a reprobate and a heretic, and thus not even a member of the church itself,
be called the head of the church?

[Marginalia: 7. From impossibility.]

XIII. 7. It is impossible for the church dispersed throughout the whole world
to be ruled by one mortal man who can neither be present in every place nor by
himself see and know all that happens everywhere, but must depend upon the
report of others who can deceive him. If twelve apostles were necessary to
plant the church, how will one non-apostle be equal to governing and ruling
the church once it has been planted? And if a universal monarchy neither ever
has been given nor can be given, as the sounder political writers acknowledge,
how much less can an ecumenical episcopate be given which presides
*autocratically* over all particular churches? Ecclesiastical government is
not coercive and cannot, like political government, be subdelegated to vicars,
because ministry is a personal office which each person is bound to exercise
himself and cannot delegate to others in his place. Whoever dispenses the word
and sacraments to others ought to perform this as Christ’s minister and in his
name alone, not as the vicar of another man, especially because according to
the canonists a vicar cannot appoint a vicar.

XIV. Neither Christ’s promise that he will not fail the church nor his prayer
in which he willed to pray for Peter’s faith that it might not fail should be
pressed upon us here. For from this it cannot be proved that God ever made to
any see a promise of this kind which assures that it will be sufficient for
ruling all the churches of the whole

[1847 vol. III printed p. 130 | IA leaf 164 | PDF p. 164]

world. Thus, if he promises that he will build the church upon the rock, etc.,
neither does that church signify some one see, nor is being built upon the
rock the same thing as being sufficient to rule all the churches. Although we
admit that the government of the church rests not upon human prudence and
strength, but that suitable power is supplied by God to those called to it, it
is nevertheless certain that this power is measured, not immeasurable; it is
not proper to one alone but common to many. For Christ alone is the one to
whom God gives the Spirit *without measure*, so that each may draw grace upon
grace from his fullness. Hence it happens that individual pastors can scarcely
be sufficient for shepherding their own flocks—indeed, cannot even be
sufficient for it, for “who is sufficient for these things?”—so far are they
from being equal to ruling the whole world.

[Marginalia: 8. From the fathers.]

XV. 8. The fathers often oppose this monarchical primacy. Cyprian says in *On
the Simplicity of Prelates*: “The other apostles were what Peter was, endowed with
an equal fellowship of honor and power.” Jerome says in his letter to
Evagrius: “Wherever a bishop may be, whether at Rome, Eugubium,
Constantinople, etc., he is of the same merit and also of the same priesthood,
etc.” Gregory the Great says in book 6 of his *Letters*: “I confidently say
that whoever calls himself, or desires to be called, universal bishop or
priest is in his pride the forerunner of Antichrist.” And in book 4, letter 38,
speaking against John, bishop of Constantinople, who had seized the title
*ecumenical*, he had already said: “Certainly, when the apostle Paul heard
certain people say, ‘I am of Paul, I of Apollos, and I of Cephas,’ he was
utterly horrified by this rending of the Lord’s body, through which its members
were associating themselves in a certain manner with other heads, and cried
out, saying, ‘Was Paul crucified for you, or were you baptized in the name of
Paul?’ Therefore, if he avoided subjecting the members of the Lord’s body
individually to certain apparent heads outside Christ, even though they were
apostles themselves, what will you say to Christ, the Head of the universal
church, in the examination of the last judgment, you who by the designation
*universal* attempt to subject all his members to yourself?”

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions.]

XVI. The government of the church of the Old Testament cannot be brought
forward here. (1.) The church is now dispersed throughout the whole world and
cannot be ruled by one man, whereas formerly it was confined within the
borders of Judea and the worship of God was restricted to Jerusalem alone.
(2.) The high priest was indeed first in order among the other priests, but he
was never called their monarch and head who possessed supreme and
*unaccountable* authority over all, since he was subject to the censure and
jurisdiction of the great Sanhedrin and was bound to God’s law (Deut. 17:11).
(3.) Whatever authority he possessed, in it he was a type of Christ, the High
Priest of the New Testament, not of the pope. Hence by retortion we say that
in the Old Testament there was a high priest without any vicar who signified
Christ; therefore in the Christian church Christ alone is supreme and without
a vicar, and consequently besides Christ no supreme pontiff of all the faithful
is to be acknowledged.

XVII. The names of the apostles are said to be inscribed on the foundations
of the new Jerusalem (Rev. 21:14), not because in the proper sense they
themselves in their persons are its foundations, since the church is built
upon the foundation of the prophets and apostles, that is, their doctrine
(Eph. 2:20), and “no one can lay another foundation besides that which has
been laid” (1 Cor. 3:11). Rather, it is because they are its founders and
architects (1 Cor. 3:10), as the names of architects are customarily engraved
upon their works so that their labor may be held in happy remembrance. Thus
the remembrance of the apostles will be in eternal blessing.

XVIII. The likeness of body and head which the apostle uses (1 Cor.
12:14–15, 20–21) does not infer that just as in a

[1847 vol. III printed p. 131 | IA leaf 165 | PDF p. 165]

multitude of members there is one head which rules all the members, so also
there is one in Christ’s visible church. For the comparison with a body is
applied to the church in two ways: first, to signify the closest union of the
whole church with Christ, as
in Ephesians 1, 4, and 5 and Colossians 1. In this sense it is conceded that
there must be one Head of the church, namely Christ. Second, to illustrate the
union of the faithful with one another by comparison with the members in one
and the same body, which is treated in Romans 12 and 1 Corinthians 12. In this
sense, deny the consequence. For (1.) by this comparison with the human body
the apostle wishes to teach that this coherence of the members is assisted by
the varied distribution of God’s gifts. Therefore, he distinguishes the
members into superior and inferior: those which have received more gifts and
those which have received fewer. The former are compared to the more prominent
members of the body, among which the head also is found; the latter, to the
remaining members. Hence he concludes that just as the more prominent members
of the body cannot do without the service of the others, so in the church
those to whom more gifts have been granted cannot do without those to whom
fewer have been given. (2.) A universal Head of the church cannot be concluded
from this, but only a head in the church; that is, one who, although a believer
and Christian in the body of the church, holds the place of a head not in
relation to the church but in relation to the individual faithful who are in
the same church. (3.) This difference of members must not be located only in
ecclesiastical offices instituted by God for ruling and preserving the church,
but in any gifts whatsoever with which even laypeople can be adorned by the
Lord. (4.) Paul here calls it Christ’s body, not the pope’s.

XIX. The things which the adversaries bring forward concerning the excellence
of monarchical government do not help their cause. (1.) We acknowledge Christ,
not the pope, as the supreme Monarch of the church, and another besides him
cannot be given. The world will more readily endure two suns than one polity
will endure two monarchs. (2.) Those who hold that monarchical government is
the best in individual polities do not thereby prove that some supreme monarch
of all the polities in the world can be given. Therefore, granted that
individual churches are to be governed by one president for the sake of
avoiding confusion, it does not follow that the churches of the whole world
are to be subjected to the power of one man. (3.) A government which is more
noble simply and in itself is not always better or more useful for this or
that subject. There are peoples who cannot be ruled otherwise than
monarchically; there are others who cannot bear the rule of one. The issue
here is not monarchy in the thesis but in the hypothesis: whether Christ
instituted such a form of government in the church. This is denied. (4.) The
consequence from political to ecclesiastical government is invalid because
Christ entrusted to his ministers not *rule*, or lordship and magistracy properly
so called, but mere *service*, *stewardship*, and ministry.

XX. If Christ visibly ruled the college of apostles while he lived on earth,
it does not follow that after his ascension a visible head of the church had
to remain. For that college was not the whole church, and Christ is always
present discharging his office (Matt. 28:20).

XXI. There is no need that there be given one supreme prelate of the church
upon whom the care of preserving and propagating the whole body rests. For the
church can be propagated sufficiently without such a head, and in fact it was
propagated in this way. This task was never conferred upon some one man, but
each of the Christians, especially those who had been promoted to degrees of
ecclesiastical authority, kept watch over this matter, diligently seized an
opportunity when it was offered, or even sought one of their own accord.

XXII. When several rulers are appointed, several commonwealths are not always
established, but only when several are appointed who rule by their own supreme
right. But this does not occur in the church, for all rulers are servants of
one and the same Christ, whose flock each tends as far as in him lies. Nor does
this multiplicity of rulers divide the catholic church into many any more
than one kingdom is divided into many kingdoms when individual

[1847 vol. III printed p. 132 | IA leaf 166 | PDF p. 166]

vicegerents are placed over individual provinces, none of whom has dominion
over another. Hence Cyprian, speaking about the multitude of bishops in *On
the Unity of the Church*, says: “The episcopate is one, a part of which is held
entirely by each.”

XXIII. The unity of a Head and supreme Judge in whom all are bound to
acquiesce is indeed required for the unity of the church and the faith. But
this Judge and Head must not be sought outside Christ, who alone can and wills
to discharge this office. Nor is there need of a visible head besides him,
since he is perpetually present with his church both through his word and
through his Spirit.

XXIV. When Christ speaks in John 10 about “one sheepfold” and “one shepherd,”
he understands no other shepherd than himself, for throughout the whole
chapter he described himself under this figure as the good shepherd who laid
down his life for the sheep, knows them, and calls them. “I have other sheep,”
he says, “which are not of this sheepfold”—namely, the Gentiles who do not
belong to the Jewish church—“them I must bring”—namely, into the church
through calling—“and they will hear my voice, and there will be one sheepfold
and one shepherd,” namely, I myself, to whom the sheep belong. Nor, if the
church militant is under discussion, must a militant shepherd be understood,
because it is evident that Christ is also the shepherd of the church militant.
He is called “the great Shepherd of the sheep” (Heb. 13:20), and the faithful
are said to have returned “to the Shepherd and Bishop of their souls” (1 Pet.
2:25).

XXV. The one king and one head of whom Ezekiel 37 and Hosea 1 speak is no
other than Christ, who is called David because David was his type. The
interlinear gloss says: “And there will be one King over them all, Christ, of
whom it is said, ‘And of his kingdom there will be no end.’” Nor can Sanders
deny this. “I indeed concede,” he says, “that this one king and shepherd whom
Ezekiel foretells is Christ, who is the son of David according to the flesh
and the Lord of David according to his deity.” Kimchi, on the passage in Hosea,
understands the King Messiah.
