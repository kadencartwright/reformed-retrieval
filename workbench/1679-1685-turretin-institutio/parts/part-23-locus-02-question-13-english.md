QUESTION XIII

ON VERSIONS

Are versions necessary, and what should their use and authority in the church
be?

I. The question has two parts. The first treats the necessity of versions; the
second, their authority. As to the first, although the more judicious Roman
Catholics acknowledge the necessity and usefulness of versions, and hence
have themselves prepared various versions in various languages, nevertheless
not a few are hostile to them and condemn them as harmful and dangerous. Such
is Arboreus in *Theosophy*, book 8, chapter 4, who says that one source of
heresies is to turn the Sacred Writings into the common language. Asoto,
Harding, Bayly, and most of the Society of Loyola agree with him. They are
accustomed impiously and unjustly to assail the godly and holy endeavor to
translate the Sacred Writings as a curious invention (εὕρημα) of heretics
banished from orthodox religion, therefore useless to the churches, and
devised to sow heresies. Against them, the orthodox defend not only the
usefulness but also the necessity of versions and prove it by various
arguments.

[Marginal heading: Vernacular versions are necessary.]

II. 1. The reading and meditation of Scripture is commanded to people of all
languages. Therefore its interpretation in vernacular languages is necessary.
For since people have been divided into many languages, and those two
languages in which Scripture was first delivered are not familiar to all, it
cannot be understood by them unless it is translated. It is the same to have
said nothing at all and to say things which

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 112 | IA leaf 166 | PDF p. 166]

no one understands. But here, by the wonderful grace of God, it has come about
that the division of languages, which formerly was a sign of a curse, is now
an evidence of heavenly blessing; that which had been introduced for the
destruction of Babel is now employed for the building of mystical Zion.

III. 2. The gospel was preached in all languages. Therefore it can and ought
to be translated into all languages. The inference from the preached Word to
the written Word is valid, because the same rationale applies to both, and the
same reasons which moved the apostles to preach in vernacular languages prove
the necessity of versions. Although the apostles wrote in only one language,
it does not follow that Scripture should not be translated into others,
because the rationale of the original texts is one thing and that of versions
another. The original texts had to be written in only one language. Therefore
the apostles, as universal teachers of the church, had to write in no language
other than the universal and most common one, which at that time was Greek,
just as the Old Testament, which was intended for the Jews, was written in
Hebrew, their vernacular language. But when the use of the Greek language
ceased, versions were needed for the proclamation of the gospel.

IV. 3. There is the constant practice of the churches, according to which it
is certain that both the Eastern and Western churches had their own versions
and celebrated sacred rites in the vernacular language of the people, as is
evident from their liturgies. Why, then, should the same not be done today,
since the need and rationale for instructing the people are the same? Thus,
when there had been two memorable dispersions of Israel, one among the
Chaldeans and the other among the Greeks, and the people of God, by using
their languages, had almost forgotten the Hebrew language, the Targums, or
Chaldean paraphrases, arose from the former dispersion and the Greek versions
from the latter. They were prepared for the benefit of the less educated
people. As to the Targums, there were several. First comes the Chaldean
paraphrase of Jonathan son of Uzziel, a disciple of Hillel and contemporary of
Simeon, who lived forty years before Christ. When he saw that the pure Hebrew
language was gradually passing out of use and was employed only by the more
learned, he prepared a Chaldean version so that the common people would not be
deprived of so great a treasure. From him we have the version of the Former
and Latter Prophets. Onkelos, who flourished after Christ and was a
contemporary (σύγχρονος) of Gamaliel, added the translation of the Pentateuch
to it. A paraphrase of the Hagiographa also exists, but nothing certain is
known about its author. There also exist Syriac, Arabic, Persian, and Ethiopic
versions, but they are less used and known. As to the New Testament, there is
a Syriac translation. It is regarded as a most ancient work and is attributed
by some to the church of Antioch in its infancy.

V. These are followed by the Greek versions of the Old Testament, which also
are numerous. First and most celebrated is the Version of the Seventy, which
was made under King Ptolemy Philadelphus, king of Egypt, about three hundred
years before Christ. The second is that of Aquila of Pontus under the emperor
Hadrian in A.D. 137. He was first Greek in religion and afterward Christian;
when he was removed from the church because of his insane study of astrology,
he defected to the Jews out of hatred for Christians and translated the Old
Testament in order to corrupt the oracles concerning Christ. 3. The third is
that of Theodotion, who lived under Commodus in A.D. 184. Pontic by birth and
a Marcionite in religion, he afterward became a Jew and prepared a new
version in which he generally follows the Seventy. 4. The fourth is that of
Symmachus under the emperor Antoninus and Aurelius Verus, about A.D. 197.
First a Samaritan and soon afterward a Jew, he translated the Old Testament
in order to refute the Samaritans. To these are added two others of uncertain
authorship. One, the Jericho version, was found in a jar near Jericho under
Caracalla in A.D. 220. The other, the Nicopolitan version, was also found near
Nicopolis under Alexander Severus in A.D. 230. By joining all these together,
Origen composed his *Tetrapla*, *Hexapla*, and *Octapla*. The *Tetrapla*
contained four Greek versions

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 113 | IA leaf 167 | PDF p. 167]

set out in the same number of distinct columns: those of the Seventy, Aquila,
Symmachus, and Theodotion. In the *Hexapla* he had added two Hebrew editions,
one in Hebrew letters and the other in Greek letters. Finally, in the
*Octapla* two other anonymous (ἀνώνυμοι) Greek versions were inserted, the
Jericho and Nicopolitan, an edition which some call the seventh. They add as
the eighth that of Lucian the Martyr, who successfully corrected the preceding
versions; it was highly approved by the people of Constantinople. The ninth
was that of Hesychius, which the Egyptians and Alexandrians embraced. The
ancients said that the tenth was a Greek version made from Jerome's Latin.

VI. Various old Latin versions also circulate which were originally made not
so much from the original texts as from the Greek version. One, the more
common, was called the Itala, as Augustine notes in book 2 of *On Christian
Doctrine*, chapter 15. Two others were prepared by Jerome: one from the
Version of the Seventy, and the other, which he diligently corrected according
to the truth of the Hebrew and Greek text. The latter is believed to be the
present Vulgate Version, but over the course of time it was corrupted in
various ways. Hence various learned men—L. Valla, J. Faber Stapulensis,
Caetanus, Arias Montanus, and others—noted corrections to it. There are other,
more recent versions made in the preceding and present centuries, partly in
Latin and partly in vernacular languages. Because they are very well known,
nothing needs to be said about them. Hence it is evident that it was the
perpetual practice of the churches to use versions.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VII. The title of the cross was not written in only three languages in order
that those three languages might be consecrated for use in sacred rites, but
because at that time they were the most renowned and best known and therefore
the best suited for the report concerning Christ to be spread in every
direction throughout the whole world. This was God's purpose in that title.

VIII. The unity of the church is preserved not by unity of language but by
unity of doctrine, Ephesians 4:3. The first councils, notwithstanding the
diversity of languages, were lawfully convened and held with benefit.

IX. The majesty of Scripture arises more from its meaning than from its words.
If those three languages seem to increase its majesty, this occurs accidentally
from the superstition of the unlearned common people, not in itself.

X. We do not deny that after those three languages ceased to be vernacular,
they were often retained in assemblies of the more learned, so that
ecclesiastical business might be conducted in them and according to them and
controversies might be decided. But they did not have the same use among the
peoples and in sacred worship, where the faith and devotion of each person is
to be exercised in such a way that one understands, according to one's
capacity, what is being done.

XI. Although we do not deny that during the captivity the Hebrew language was
variously corrupted among the common people through interaction with
foreigners and that many Chaldean and Syriac words came into it, it does not
therefore follow either that the text was changed in any way or that it was
not understood by the people to whom it was inculcated. For Zechariah, Haggai,
and Malachi wrote in the pure Hebrew language, which they would not have done
unless the people understood it. Then, from Nehemiah 8:8, it is gathered that
Ezra read the book of the law before the entire multitude, which is said to
have been attentive to it. It could not have been attentive if it had not
understood. And if Ezra with the Levites is said to have interpreted what he
read, this is to be referred to the exposition of the meaning and of the
matters themselves rather than to the translation of the words.

XII. Although versions are not formally authentic with respect to the manner
of expression, they ought not on that account to have any less standing in the
church. For if they are accurate and agree with the original texts, they are
always materially authentic and authentic with respect to the things
expressed.

[Marginal heading: What is the authority of versions?]

XIII. From this it is evident what the authority of versions is. Although
their usefulness for the instruction of the faithful is great, no version can
or ought ever

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 114 | IA leaf 168 | PDF p. 168]

to be made equal to the original, much less preferred to it. 1. No version
has anything of importance which the Hebrew or Greek source does not possess
more fully. For in the original texts not only the matters and sentences but
the very words were dictated immediately by the Holy Spirit, which cannot be
said of any version. 2. It is one thing to be an interpreter and another to
be a prophet, as Jerome says in the preface to the Pentateuch. A prophet, as
one inspired by God (θεόπνευστος), cannot err; but an interpreter, as a human
being, regards nothing human as alien to himself, for he is always liable to
error. 3. All versions are streams, but the original text is the fountain
from which they flow. The latter is the rule; the former are the thing
regulated, which has no authority except human authority.

XIV. Nevertheless, all authority cannot be denied to versions. Here a
twofold divine authority must be carefully distinguished: an authority of
matters and an authority of words. The former concerns the substance of the
doctrine, which constitutes the internal form of Scripture. The latter
concerns the accident of writing, which is its external and accidental form.
The source possesses both, because it is inspired by God (θεόπνευστος) both
as to the matters and as to the words. But versions possess only the first,
because they are expressed in human, not divine, words.

XV. Hence it is evident that versions as such are not authentic and canonical
in themselves, because they were prepared by human industry and effort and
thus, under that relation (σχέσει), can be liable to error and admit
corrections. Nevertheless, they are authentic with respect to the doctrine
they contain, which is divine and infallible. Thus, as such, they do not
formally ground divine faith with respect to their words, but materially ground
it with respect to the substance of the doctrine expressed in them.

XVI. The perfection of the reality and truth, to which nothing can be added
and from which nothing can be taken away, is one thing; the perfection of this
version is another. The former is an entirely divine work and is absolutely
and in every way self-authenticating (αὐτόπιστον). Such perfection is in the
Word expressed in versions. The latter, however, is a human work and is
therefore liable to error and correction. Great authority can indeed belong
to it, but only human authority, which consists in faithfulness and conformity
to the original text, not divine authority.

XVII. The certainty of the conformity of versions to the original is
twofold. One is merely grammatical and belongs to the human science which
recognizes the conformity of the words in the versions with the words of the
original. This belongs to the more learned, who know the languages. The other
is spiritual and belongs to divine faith with respect to the conformity of
the matters and doctrines. This belongs to individual believers according to
the measure of Christ's gift, in accordance with Christ's saying, “My sheep
hear my voice,” John 10:27, and Paul's saying, “The spiritual person judges
all things,” 1 Corinthians 2:15. Therefore, although an unlearned person is
ignorant of the languages, he will not cease to gather the faithfulness of the
version with respect to the matters themselves from the analogy of faith and
the connection of the doctrines themselves: “If anyone wills to do his will,
he will know concerning the doctrine whether it is from God or whether I speak
from myself,” John 7:17.

XVIII. To be conformed to the original is one thing; to be equal to it is
another. Every version, provided it is faithful, is indeed conformed to the
original because the same doctrine in substance is set forth there. Yet it is
not therefore equal to the original, because the manner of setting it forth
is human, not divine.

XIX. Although any version prepared by people liable to error cannot be
regarded as divine and infallible with respect to its terms, it can very
properly be regarded as such with respect to the matters when it faithfully
renders the divine truth expressed in the original texts. In the same way,
the Word which a minister of the gospel preaches does not cease to be divine
and infallible and to ground our faith, although it is proposed by him in
human words. Thus faith does not depend upon the interpreter's or

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 115 | IA leaf 169 | PDF p. 169]

minister's authority, but rests upon the truth and authenticity (αὐθεντίᾳ) of
the matters themselves which are in the versions.

XX. If a version contained the pure Word of God in divine words, there would
be no place for its correction, just as the original texts therefore neither
can nor ought to be corrected, because they are inspired by God (θεόπνευστοι)
both as to the matters and as to the words. But because a version sets forth
the Word of God for us in human words, it follows that it can admit correction,
not with respect to the doctrine itself, which remains the same in both, but
with respect to the terms themselves, which, especially in difficult and
obscure passages, can be rendered differently by different people according
to the measure of Christ's gift.
