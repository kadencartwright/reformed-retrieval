QUESTION XXVII.

ON THE IMMUNITY OF CLERGY.

Are ecclesiastical persons exempt from the jurisdiction and subjection of the
civil magistrate? We deny this against the Romanists.

I. This question also is debated between us and the Romanists. For just as the
pope, out of sheer ambition, arrogates to himself autocratic power and
jurisdiction over the church, so he wishes all clerics to be entirely exempt
from every power and yoke of secular princes and to be wholly enslaved and
devoted to himself.

[Marginalia: The opinion of the Romanists.]

II. The Romanists agree among themselves on some points and disagree on
others. They agree on exemption and immunity. Hence that statement of Emanuel
Sa in the *Aphorisms*: “A priest cannot commit the crime of high treason
against a king, because he is not his subject.” But they disagree about the
nature of the right: is it divine, human, or mixed? Some wish only human right
to obtain; others a mixed right, such as Bellarmine. Still others contend for
divine right. This is the opinion of the canonists, who maintain that clerics
are simply and absolutely exempt from civil power, in temporal as well as
spiritual matters, in personal as well as real obligations, in criminal as
well as civil causes—and indeed by divine right. Boniface VIII says in the
chapter *Although*, *On Assessments*: “By divine and human right clerics and
their goods are free from secular power.” The Council of Trent, session 25,
chapter 20: “Was the immunity of the church and of ecclesiastical persons
instituted by God’s ordinance and by canonical sanctions?”

[1847 vol. III printed p. 220 | IA leaf 254 | PDF p. 254]

III. Bellarmine, book 2 *On Clerics*, chapter 28, embraces his opinion in five
propositions. The first is: “In ecclesiastical causes clerics are free by
divine right from the power of secular princes.” The second: “Clerics are in no
way exempt from the obligation of civil laws which do not conflict with the
sacred canons or with clerical office.” The third: “Although clerics are bound
to observe civil laws, they nevertheless
cannot be punished by a political judge or in any way brought before the
tribunal of the secular magistrate.” The fourth: “The goods of clerics, both
ecclesiastical and secular, are free, and rightly ought to be free, from the
taxes of secular princes.” The fifth: “The exemption of clerics in political
matters, at least with respect to goods, was introduced by human, not divine,
right.” Yet, contradicting himself in an appendix to his second volume, he
defends the immunity of goods by divine right. He evidently did so to win the
favor of Sixtus V, who was offended by what Bellarmine had said about the
subjection owed by right to Caesar by pontiffs in civil matters and came close
to placing Bellarmine’s books on the index of prohibited books, as Barclay
observes in chapter 13 of *The Power of the Pope*.

[Marginalia: And that of the orthodox.]

IV. We think the following distinction must be made. There are two kinds of
subjection of subjects by which they are under the rule of a magistrate,
whether faithful or unbelieving. One concerns persons in criminal and civil
causes. The other concerns property, in paying taxes and bearing other civil
burdens. We judge clerics to be bound by both, at least by divine and natural
right, no less than other subjects, although by privilege they are exempt
almost everywhere from personal burdens because of the labors of the sacred
ministry, and in some places even from taxes.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

V. From this it is clear: (1) The question is not about subjection with
respect to ecclesiastical office, which is occupied with preaching the word,
administering the sacraments, and exercising ecclesiastical discipline, nor
with respect to ecclesiastical causes which concern the conscience and can
lawfully be determined only from the word of God and the authority entrusted
by God to the church, such as controversies of faith. For in these spiritual
ecclesiastical acts they are not subject to the magistrate. The magistrate can
and ought to prescribe to them no other form of teaching, reproving errors and
sins, and administering the sacraments than that prescribed in the word of
God. Indeed, in these matters the magistrate, as a member of the church,
stands as a sheep in relation to the pastor. Rather, the question concerns
subjection with respect to political office, or political causes, which
concern the affairs of this life and the external peace and tranquility of
the commonwealth. In these we say that ministers are bound no less than other
citizens, from whose number they are not removed when enrolled among
ecclesiastics. And it is fitting that they observe the laws all the more
scrupulously because their life is more conspicuous, since as examples to the
flock they must shine before it by the example of a holy life.

VI. (2) The question is not about privileges which, by the kindness and
liberality of Christian magistrates and princes, can be granted to clerics,
many of which were plainly given by emperors. Rather, it concerns an immunity
belonging to them by divine right. (3) The question is not about personal
burdens. We acknowledge that these should not be imposed, lest they hinder the
exercise of ecclesiastical office and detract from either the dignity or the
authority of sacred office. Hence in the law *Generally* and the law
*Presbyters* in the Code *On Bishops and Clergy*, Justinian enacted “that
presbyters should be exempt from personal public duties which are discharged
by the exercise of care and labor.” For if they were bound to these, they
could not help but be drawn away from their office, and the dignity of the
priest would be confounded, as is said in the law *Nor by Honor*, if he had to
serve as a soldier or watchman and discharge other duties that are purely
civil. Except the case of utmost necessity, if the country is in extreme
danger. But the question does concern real burdens with respect to the goods
they possess. It concerns not so much sacred and ecclesiastical goods which,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 221 | IA leaf 255 | PDF p. 255]

when consecrated to the church, have first been discharged and freed from
every burden of taxation—although in a case of necessity they can be made
liable for extraordinary taxes—as it concerns secular goods which they have
acquired by purchase, inheritance, marriage rights, and other means. From
these, what is owed must be paid to the magistrate, for property passes with
its burden, “and real burdens follow the property and whoever possesses it,”
the law *Emperors*. Nor are such goods exempt from the jurisdiction of the
political magistrate because they are possessed by clerics. For the fact that
clerics serve the church does not make them cease to be citizens and parts and
members of the commonwealth, nor does it keep them as such from being subject
to the political magistrate or their privately held goods from being subject
to the burdens of the commonwealth.

VII. Finally, the question does not concern a special, partial, and limited
immunity, which is a matter of human right descending to the ecclesiastical
order from the kindness and privilege of pious princes, by which its members
in certain cases are exempted from public burdens and the duties of subjects
with respect to persons or property. Rather, it concerns a general, total, and
unqualified immunity belonging by divine right, such as the Romanists ascribe
to clerics with respect to both persons and property. This we deny.

[Marginalia: Immunity is overthrown. 1. From the general command of obedience.]

VIII. The reasons are as follows. (1) From the general command of obedience
and subjection, which extends to everyone without exception. The passages are
Romans 13:1, “Let every soul be subject to the higher powers”; and 1 Peter
2:13, “Submit yourselves to every human ordinance”; and Titus 3:1, “Remind
them to be subject to rulers and powers.” Christ himself wishes “what is
Caesar’s to be rendered to Caesar,” and what he spoke with his mouth he soon
took care to fulfill by his deed. Several arguments lie within these passages.
(1) There is the universal term *every soul*, that is, by synecdoche, every
person, as frequently elsewhere. For the wish to take “soul” as meaning an
animal man, as some have thought, is so absurd that it needs no refutation.
(2) The passage does not concern just any power but secular power, which bears
the sword for the protection of the good and the punishment of the wicked,
and to which tax must be paid. (3) All who shake off this yoke declare war on
God and nature by resisting God’s ordinance, and they loosen and dissolve
public tranquility and the fabric of human society. (4) There is an immediate
antithesis between civil magistrates and subjects. But clerics do not belong
to the order of magistrates, and therefore must be counted among the subjects.
(5) When Paul and Peter write to all the saints, they cannot exclude ministers
of the church, unless the latter are to be struck from the number of the
saints. Chrysostom, homily 13 on Romans at the beginning: “The apostle shows
that these obligations are imposed on everyone, priests and monks, not merely
secular persons. He declares this immediately in the very opening when he
says, ‘Let every soul be subject to the higher powers,’ even if he is an
apostle, an evangelist, a prophet, or whoever else he may be. For this
subjection does not overturn piety. And he does not simply say, ‘Let him
obey,’ but ‘Let him be subject.’” Theophylact asserts the same on this passage,
and Bernard, letter 42 to the Archbishop of Sens: “Let every soul be subject
to the higher powers. If every soul, then yours also. Who exempted you from
the universal statement? If anyone attempts to exempt you, he attempts to
deceive you.”

IX. It is objected in vain: (1) “Every soul ought to be subject to higher
powers while they are higher powers. But princes are no longer higher powers
with respect to clerics, because the princes themselves have in part subjected
clerics to their own bishops in all civil and temporal causes, and the supreme
pontiff has exempted all clerics from subjection to princes.” This begs the
question and assumes what must be proved: namely, that princes have exempted
clerics from their power or ought to have exempted them, and that the Roman
pontiff could exempt them from the power of princes. We deny this, because he
can enact nothing against the divine right which binds them to

[1847 vol. III printed p. 222 | IA leaf 256 | PDF p. 256]

obedience and subjection. Although princes coming to the Christian faith
subject their scepters to Christ and to spiritual power, it does not therefore
follow that the ministers of spiritual power are exempt in temporal matters
from temporal power. Only this can be inferred: princes are subject to
spiritual power in spiritual matters, not that they lose their political
authority over ministers in temporal matters. (2) It is no better here to
distinguish “Christians from priests, on the ground that these things are said
to them as Christians, not as priests.” For things that should be joined are
absurdly separated. If a priest is a Christian, he must always be subject,
although in the exercise of his office and with respect to spiritual matters
he is immune. And if the character of baptism and the right of evangelical
liberty in Christ do not release him from obedience to the king, much less
does the clerical character. For the church’s liberty is said of faith and the
soul before it is said of temporal matters. (3) It is objected that this is to
be understood of “a soul, which can be subjected, whereas one power cannot be
subjected to another power, and thus neither can ministers, who possess
power.” A power cannot be subject to a power in the same order, but nothing
prevents those who possess spiritual power in the ecclesiastical order and in
the forum of conscience with respect to spiritual matters from being subject
in the political order and with respect to temporal matters to political
power. This is to say nothing of the fact that the ministers’ power is not
autocratic and supreme, but subordinate and ministerial.

[Marginalia: 2. From the example of Old Testament priests.]

X. 2. From the example of Old Testament priests, who were subject to the
political magistrate in both civil and criminal causes. There can be no
difference in the principle, because Bellarmine admits in proposition 5 that
“clerics now are the same as priests once were.” Moses is set over Aaron as
God (Exod. 4:16) and corrects him when he falls into sin (Exod. 32:21). The
Levites were subject not only to Aaron but also to Moses, who was the civil
prince of the whole people. Nor can it be objected that Moses was also the
high priest. For although before Aaron’s consecration he could occasionally
discharge the priesthood outside the order, once Aaron had been anointed and
consecrated Moses entrusted that whole office to him in God’s name and no
longer performed any of its parts, but only those of leader and lawgiver. So
David established the orders and offices of the Levites (1 Chron. 23 and the
following chapters). Solomon removed Abiathar, who was guilty of sedition,
from his office (1 Kings 2). Abiathar did not resist or complain that the
divine law was being violated. It is said in vain either that “this deed is
related but not approved,” because the same answer can be returned to every
example the adversaries bring from the Old Testament to establish their own
opinion; or that “Solomon acted here as a prophet, not as a king.” For it
indeed belongs to prophets to pronounce the judgments of God, but to punish
with exile or another more serious penalty belongs only to the magistrate. Nor
can this be inferred from the statement that Solomon did this “so that the
word of God might be fulfilled,” because God fulfills his word through princes
as well as through ministers, and indeed even through the impious, such as
Judas and others. To the same point belongs the fact that Jehoshaphat commands
the priests to reform public worship (2 Chron. 17), Joash reproves the
negligence of the high priest Jehoiada (2 Kings 12), Hezekiah avenges religion
corrupted by the priests (2 Chron. 29:30–31), and Josiah burns the bones of
the priests upon the altars and purges the temple (2 Kings 22–23). Nor can it
be replied that it is no wonder if in the Old Testament spiritual power was
subject to secular power because temporal promises were then in force. For if
that reasoning holds, the adversaries cannot deduce divine right from the Old
Testament.

[Marginalia: 3. From Christ’s example.]

XI. 3. From the example of Christ, who subjected himself to the magistrate in
both personal and real matters. With respect to his person, this is clear from
John 19:11, where Christ acknowledges Pilate’s power over him and submits
himself to it: “You would have no power at all over me unless it had been given
to you from above.” Here it is evident that

[1847 vol. III printed p. 223 | IA leaf 257 | PDF p. 257]

civil power over Christ had been divinely established, to which in that state
of humiliation he had to be subject through obedience even to death. Those who
wish this subjection furnished by Christ to be one of fact and not of right
plainly contradict Scripture, which testifies that Christ, as he was made of a
woman, was also made subject to the law (Gal. 4:4). But this law subjects man
to human order, indeed an order divinely instituted. Therefore Christ, as Son
of Man, was subject to the magistrate both by right and in fact. I acknowledge
that he was not subject by a necessity of nature and insofar as he was a
divine person, and that he could have exempted himself from it. But because he
willed to become man, as such he had to be subject to human laws. Again, if in
fact he willed to undergo that judgment, why do clerics exempt themselves from
it and refuse to follow their Lord’s example? Nor is it any better to say that
“the power given over Christ was not judicial power, but permission.” For
Christ had to speak of the same power of which Pilate had said, “Do you not
know that I have power to crucify you and power to release you?” This can only
be conceived as judicial power. Therefore mere permission, such as was given
to the devil and the Jews to afflict Christ, must not be posited here. He
conducts himself very differently toward Jews inflicting violence and toward
Pilate judging him, for he recognizes less sin in the latter than in the
former. Indeed, although he presents himself to be judged by Pilate, more than
once he withdrew himself from the hands, stones, and fury of the Jews. Hence
Bernard, letter 42 to the Archbishop of Sens: “Even then he was speaking
through himself and experiencing in himself what he proclaimed in the church
through the apostles: there is no power except from God.” Before him Augustine
said on this passage: “Pilate’s power over Christ was from God, according to
that word of the apostle: there is no power except from God.”

XII. The same argument is confirmed from the assessment or tribute paid to the
collectors (Matt. 17:25) through Peter, both for Christ and for himself, which
everyone acknowledges as a sign of subjection. For although Christ, as Lord of
all, was not bound to pay tribute, nevertheless, because he was not yet
acknowledged as such, he willed the assessment to be paid both for himself and
for Peter in order to avoid scandal, lest he be regarded as a despiser of
God’s law. This was either because Peter alone was present and not the other
apostles, or because he alone had been asked, since he lived at Capernaum. But
if Christ did this, much more ought his ministers to do it; and if tribute had
to be paid for Peter, by what right do pontiffs, who boast that they are
Peter’s successors, maintain that they are immune from it? It does not help to
say, “Christ paid tribute because he willed to, not because he had to.” Christ
willed to pay tribute in such a way that at the same time he also willed to be
obliged to pay it, and he was obliged in the state of emptying in which he
lived. As he had to be circumcised, baptized, subjected to Pilate, and die, so
he had to pay tribute. He was indeed absolutely free as the Son of God, but in
this case he was not free because he had made himself a servant. Jerome
explains it so on this passage: “As the son of kings he did not owe tribute,
but he who had assumed the humility of the flesh had to fulfill all
righteousness.” In this way he wished to give an example and teach what all
his people should do in this matter, so that everyone belonging to Christ
might judge that he should do what Christ did, and do it all the more because
he sees Christ doing this for us and as our example, though considered in
himself he otherwise had no obligation to do it. Moreover, however this
tribute is understood—whether as the imperial tribute, or poll tax, which
Augustus imposed on the Jews and whose amount was the didrachma, from which
the Jews exempted themselves by rebellion but which Vespasian restored after
Jerusalem was captured, as Josephus observes in *The Jewish War*, book 7,
chapter 26 (this is the opinion of Jerome and various Romanists, including
Jansenius, *Harmony*, chapter 69, Salmeron, Barradius, and others, and it seems
to be confirmed from what follows, where the kings of the earth exacting taxes
are discussed); or as the tribute which God imposed upon the sons of Israel
for the use of the sanctuary (Exod. 30), as Hilary,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 224 | IA leaf 258 | PDF p. 258]

Bellarmine, Baronius, and others thought—the force of the argument always
remains. For from the fact that he paid it to the temple, it can be inferred
that he also submitted to forensic laws. The tribute was paid to God as the
proper king, but the one who was that king’s son could exempt himself from it.

[Marginalia: 4. From Paul’s deed.]

XIII. 4. From Paul’s deed, because he appeals from the Jerusalem Sanhedrin to
Caesar (Acts 25:10): “I stand before Caesar’s tribunal, where I ought to be
judged, etc. I appeal to Caesar.” Anyone to whom a person appeals is
acknowledged by that person as his superior and judge, and he submits himself
to that person’s power. Bellarmine replies in vain, “Paul appealed to Caesar
because Caesar was his judge in fact, even if not by right.” For Paul speaks
not only of fact but also of right when he says “that he *must* be judged
before Caesar.” Where Paul had to be judged, there he was subject to judgment
by right. For the apostle would never have said—much less done—that something
contrary to right must be done. Things done in a legal action but not by right
must be understood as done against right and by injury. “He who acts against a
duty of justice,” as Aquinas has it, “sins mortally” (*ST* 2a2ae, q. 61). It
belongs to the duty of justice toward each person that the right of a superior
be preserved unharmed, which a person does not do when he substitutes someone
else for the true judge. This is confirmed from the fact that God ratified the
appeal. Sending an angel, he consoles Paul while endangered by the storm: “Do
not fear, Paul, for you must stand before Caesar.” He certainly would not have
mentioned this if, contrary to right, the apostle had constituted Caesar his
judge in place of Peter. Hence Bellarmine, recognizing the emptiness of the
response, adds: “Second, it can be said—and better, following Albert Pighius—
that there is a distinction between pagan and Christian princes. For at the
time when princes were pagans, the pontiff was not their judge, but on the
contrary was subject to them in all civil causes no less than other men.
Therefore Paul rightly appealed to Caesar and acknowledged him as judge when
he was accused of sedition and stirring up tumult.” But the adversary cannot
escape in this way either, because then the condition of Christian princes
would be worse than that of pagans. Yet the gospel does not abolish political
orders and, as Bellarmine himself says, “The Christian law deprives no one of
his right.” It would do so if princes lost part of their jurisdiction and
power through their conversion to Christ. What is more, Bellarmine himself
rejects this response elsewhere: “I do not approve,” he says in the
*Recognition of His Works*, “what I said with Albert Pighius, that St. Paul
appealed to Caesar as his lawful prince. For if the reason for the exemption
of clerics is that they are ministers of Christ, who is the Prince of the
kings of the earth, they are certainly exempt by right not only from the power
of Christian princes but also from that of pagans.” In this way, evidently,
he wished to win favor with Sixtus V, who had taken so badly what Bellarmine
had said—that by right the pontiffs were formerly subject to Caesar in civil
causes—that he intended to mark all Bellarmine’s books with the censor’s pen.
Hence Bellarmine adds third that the question of which Paul was accused
concerned faith and therefore did not belong to the political magistrate, nor
did Paul subject himself to Caesar. But Paul sufficiently shows that he was
accused before Caesar not only on account of religion but for other causes.
The Jews were prosecuting him as guilty of various crimes (Acts 24:5), and
especially of sedition: “We found him *stirring up sedition*.” Again, before
Festus and Caesar Paul does not directly dispute about the faith but about the
violence inflicted upon him, namely, that it was not lawful for the Jews to
inflict violence upon him for preaching the gospel. The question is not
whether Paul is a heretic or not, but whether the Jews should have contrived
and attempted such things because of his preaching. “If I have committed any
wrong or done anything worthy of death, I do not refuse to die.” Thus he asks
to be judged not on whether the things he said are true, but whether because
of them he deserved death, whether he was

[1847 vol. III printed p. 225 | IA leaf 259 | PDF p. 259]

injurious to the Jews, a profaner of the temple, a violator of the law, and an
enemy of Caesar. Therefore he appeals to Caesar not merely as judge of the Jews so that
they may be restrained, but so that he himself also may be judged concerning
the crimes charged against him by the Jews.

[Marginalia: 5. From the status of clerics.]

XIV. 5. From the status of clerics, who are citizens and parts of the
commonwealth, and who therefore must be either magistrates or subjects, since
these are the two constituent parts of the commonwealth. Since, therefore,
they are not numbered among the magistrates, they must be numbered among the
subjects, because “the commonwealth is not in the church, but the church in
the commonwealth,” as Optatus says in book 3 *Against Parmenian*. Bellarmine
acknowledges this in book 1 *On Clerics*, chapter 28: “Besides being clerics,
clerics are also citizens and certain parts of the political commonwealth.
Therefore as such they must obey the laws and the magistrate, because it is
not fitting for the members to disagree with the head.” So Medina, in the
chapter *On Restitution*, question 15: “It is not contrary to reason that men
devoted to God’s service and spiritual functions should be subject in
temporal and civil matters to the secular magistrate. For just as a secular
prince, as a Christian and in spiritual matters, is a sheep and son of the
priests, so conversely priests, as citizens and in temporal matters, can be
called sheep and, as it were, sons of secular princes.”

[Marginalia: 6. From the disadvantages.]

XV. 6. From various disadvantages and absurdities. Nothing more ruinous and
unjust can be conceived than this complete exemption of clerics. For what
greater injustice is there than to give the choicest goods of the commonwealth
to be consumed by those who not only bear no burdens and do not assist the
commonwealth from those goods from which they ought to expect their own
safety, but do not even acknowledge as their benefactor the commonwealth from
which they first begged all their possessions? What greater plague than to
have within your own bosom not merely those against whom, if they injure you,
you dare not even mutter, but also those who have pledged allegiance to a
foreign power and are bound at another’s nod to take up arms against you? So
speaks Arnisæus in book 1, chapter 2, *On the Subjection and Exemption of
Clerics*. What is more unjust than to share with others the goods and
advantages of the commonwealth and yet refuse to bear its burdens with them,
so that there is hardship for everyone else but relief for them alone? Love
for country does not permit those who possess so many and such great goods to
sit idle and withdraw themselves from burdens. Finally, if the evangelical
law frees no one from debts contracted beforehand—neither filial debt,
financial debt, the debt of servitude, nor marital debt—why would it exempt a
person from subjection and obedience to the magistrate? For it is gratuitously
assumed, not proved, that the bond between debtor and creditor, servant and
master, or son and father is greater, because this obligation, as founded in
divine right, is indispensable.

[Marginalia: 7. From the testimony of Romanists.]

XVI. 7. From the testimony of the pontiffs themselves and of various
Romanists. Gregory, bishop of Rome, “acknowledges the emperor as his lord,”
book 3, letter 61. He calls himself “the unworthy servant” of the emperor and
there also “confesses that he owes him obedience,” book 4, letter 31; and in
book 2, letter 65, he acknowledges that “power was given to the prince not
only over soldiers but also over priests.” Leo IV, writing to Lothair,
acknowledges this, as appears in chapter 9, distinction 10, and the chapter
*Adrian*, distinction 63. He also confirmed this right to Otto in the chapter
*In a Synod*, in the same distinction. The same Leo IV asks to be judged by
Emperor Louis and promises that he will submit to his sentence, chapter “If
we have acted incompetently,” section 2, question 7. Nor can it be said with
Bellarmine that he “subjected himself to the emperor’s discerning judgment,
but not his coercive judgment.” For one who asks that a matter be settled by
lawful judgment, as Leo says, requests not only a determination but also its
execution. Not a few Romanists favor us here, especially those who teach that
clerics are free by divine right from secular power in ecclesiastical and
spiritual matters, but in other matters only by human right, such as Francisco
de Vitoria in *On Ecclesiastical Power*, Domingo de Soto on book 4,
distinction 25, Juan

[1847 vol. III printed p. 226 | IA leaf 260 | PDF p. 260]

de Medina in the *Code on Restitution*, Covarrubias on the law *Practice*,
question 31, Du Moulin, Alciati, Báñez, Albert Pighius, Jansenius, and Marsilius
of Padua.

[Marginalia: 8. From the constitutions and deeds of the emperors.]

XVII. 8. From the constitutions and deeds of the emperors. It is evident that
the emperors, mindful of their power and conscious of their calling, subjected
clerics to political laws. This is clear from both codes and from the
*Novels*, where they command many things of clerics: law 26, *We Decree*, in
the Code *On Bishops and Clergy*; and law 10, *All*, in the Code *On the Laws
and Constitutions of Princes*: “Let everyone live according to the laws, even
if they belong to the divine household.” *Novel* 123, chapter 8: “Let no
bishop be brought unwillingly before a secular judge unless the prince has
commanded it.” Gratian omitted the final words, as well as the word
“unwillingly,” in the chapter *No One*, question 1, because they cut the
throat of the Roman cause. Refer here to law 6, *Those Bound*; law 7, *If
Anyone by Consent*; *Novel* 3 at the beginning; *Novel* 6, chapter 8; and so
forth. From the preface to Justinian’s edict written to the archbishops and
patriarchs, it is evident that although Justinian granted a hearing to
bishops, he reserved to himself supreme cognizance and appeal. Valentinian,
in the *Letter to the Bishops of Asia* reported by Theodoret, book 4, chapter
7, writes that “upright bishops obey not only the laws of God but also those
of kings.” From canon law it is evident that emperors once had power to
coerce pontiffs and clerics, as King James of Britain proves in the preface to
his admonition to Christian princes. Histories testify that Christian
emperors frequently judged and deposed pontiffs. Constantius sent Liberius
into exile; Justinian sent Silverius; Otto I deposed John XXII and installed
Leo VIII; Henry III ordered Gregory VI and Clement II to be ordained;
Constantius gave judges to Athanasius when he was accused concerning the death
of Arsenius; and the envoys of the emperor Constans prosecuted Stephen,
bishop of Antioch, as guilty of public violence under the Cornelian law *On
Assassins* and condemned him in the palace. Baronius cannot deny this in the
*Annals*, volume 7, year 558.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XVIII. It is not absurd for one and the same man to be a sheep in the
ecclesiastical order and with respect to spiritual matters, and a shepherd in
the political order and with respect to temporal matters. Therefore, just as
in matters pertaining to Christ’s kingdom it is fitting for princes to be
subject to sacred ministers, who act as ambassadors for Christ, so in matters
pertaining to earthly life ministers must undertake the prince’s commands. It
is well known that in a naval fleet an admiral obeys the shipmaster in matters
concerning the governance of the ship, while the shipmaster in turn is subject

XIX. The political power and the ecclesiastical power of ministers cannot be
called collateral. For collaterality obtains only between equal powers, but
the power of ministers stands far below political power, because it is only
ministerial and subordinate, whereas political power is directive and
principal. Nor should magistrates be called subjects of ministers, but of
Christ who sent them; ministers, however, are properly subject to magistrates
as lords.

XX. A superior ought to be exempt from the rule of an inferior in the matters
in which he truly is superior. But the clergy are not superior in civil
matters, whether these concern persons or property, but only in spiritual
matters with respect to the mysteries of the word and sacraments and their
administration.

XXI. It is one thing for sacred ministers to enjoy certain privileges granted
to them by princes; it is another to possess complete immunity by divine
right. We grant the former, but not the latter; nor can a perpetual and
universal right be inferred from certain particular examples. Bellarmine
himself, book 1 *On Clerics*, chapter 7, response to argument 7, says: “The
examples of Scripture do not make something a matter of divine right, but
God’s precepts are what do so.” The exemption given by Joseph to the Egyptian
priests (Gen. 47) was not immunity from every subjection to royal jurisdiction
in political matters and taxes, but only

[1847 vol. III printed p. 227 | IA leaf 261 | PDF p. 261]

immunity from a particular tax, namely, payment of a fifth part of the produce
of fields which had not been sold to the king, since grain was supplied to
their owners from the royal storehouses. Moreover, no divine command but a
human institution is related here.

XXII. Artaxerxes forbids tribute or tax to be imposed upon the priests and
other ministers of the temple (Ezra 7:24), because they lacked fields and
lived from sacrifices and offerings. It does not follow that no poll tax could
rightfully have been exacted from them if the need of the commonwealth had
required it and the king had so resolved.

XXIII. The constitutions of the emperors and the *Novels* of Justinian by
which Bellarmine defends his opinion do not help his cause. They show that the
exemption of clerics was a human privilege granted by the indulgence of the
emperors rather than acquired by any divine right. Indeed, even according to
Bellarmine, Justinian exempts clerics not in criminal causes but only in civil
ones. And above we saw that the emperors imposed this subjection upon
ministers.

XXIV. We grant that among almost all nations priests were immune from certain
burdens and taxes, but we deny that they were immune from every jurisdiction
of the magistrate. It is one thing that the highest dignity was customarily
accorded to them, yet within their own order, which we admit. It is another
that they possessed power in temporal and civil matters, which we deny. Hence
Gelasius, in the chapter *There Are Two*, distinction 96, assigns sacred
authority to pontiffs and royal power to princes.

XXV. If princes subjected clerics to bishops, they indeed did this from zeal
for piety, because the offenses of ecclesiastics could not be investigated by
ordinary ministers—especially if these were unbelieving and hostile to the
church—without scandal to the weak and injury to religion. But insofar as
this immunity was sanctioned by perpetual law and obtained simply and
absolutely, when it ought to have had force only temporarily and in a certain
respect, this must be attributed to the superstition of princes and ambition
of bishops. But the Roman pontiff ultimately subjected to himself the bishops
along with the clerics and the princes themselves along with the bishops,
shook the yoke from his own neck, and put it upon theirs. This must not be
thought to have been done by divine or human right, but to have proceeded from
the tyranny of Antichrist foretold in the Scriptures themselves.

XXVI. It is gratuitously claimed that this subjection degrades the
ecclesiastical order, destroys its authority, and takes away the liberty
necessary for reproving political rulers. Subjection in political and temporal
matters does not take away authority in spiritual matters. The high priest
freely resisted King Uzziah when he took the censer, although in political
matters the priest was subject to him.

XXVII. Bellarmine maintains in vain that clerics are bound by civil laws only
*directively*, not *coercively*; this is a mere deception by which he attempts
to put on a false appearance. For what kind of subjection to law is there if
we cannot be compelled to obey? The sinew of law is the sanction of a penalty.
If that penalty cannot be inflicted, the law is empty, and thus loses all
force and authority. An obligation is empty without a power of compelling in
the superior and a necessity of obeying under the sanction of a penalty in
the inferior. Therefore Paul imposes this subjection both “because of wrath,”
that is, fear of punishment, and because of conscience.

XXVIII. It is one thing to speak of complete exemption; it is another to
speak of certain privileges granted by the beneficence of princes. Justinian
in *Novels* 79, 83, and 123 does not speak of complete and universal immunity,
but only orders in civil causes that clerics first approach the bishop, lest
they be drawn away from their duties or be forced to be absent from their
churches because of an action in court. But he subjects them in criminal
causes to the public courts of the empire, as is evident from those laws.
Although he granted the bishops a hearing in ecclesiastical causes, he
nevertheless reserved the supreme right and cognizance to himself. If bishops
received

privileges from princes, they belonged to the jurisdiction of princes, because
one who receives a privilege from another acknowledges that person as his
superior.
