# On Ecclesiastical Ceremonies (5 April 1558)

*A fresh translation from the Latin advice at CO X/1 211–213. The title and
page notices are editorial, not words silently attributed to Calvin.*

[CO X/1, p. 211]

As far as ceremonies are concerned, this general axiom must be maintained:
because the church has been burdened by their immense accumulation, and the
clarity of the gospel almost buried, those who are content with a sparing and
sober use of them act most wisely. Next, in the mysteries which are called
sacraments, diligent care must be taken that the pure and genuine institution
of the heavenly Master is not corrupted by human inventions. Especially where
there is freedom of choice, nothing is better than simply to maintain the form
prescribed by Christ, as it has been handed down to us from hand to hand.

If antiquity is raised as an objection—although those who are excessively
devoted to custom and accepted practices boldly use this shield to cover every
corruption—the refutation is easy. The ancients themselves adequately
testified by their grave complaints that they did not approve everything
devised by human judgment. Augustine, especially in writing to Januarius,
laments that even then the church had been oppressed by an unjust and
intolerable servitude because of excessive rites. Yet scarcely a tenth part
had then been constructed of that labyrinth in which the world is now held
entangled for the ensnaring of souls.

Next, although many ceremonies received long ago have been met with silence
and toleration, the distinction established by Paul must be maintained: we
must not permit the clear light of the gospel to be obscured by legal figures
(Galatians 4:9; 5:2). If no place is granted to the shadows of the law, how
much less ought human fabrications to obtain this license?

Now, if we examine carefully and thoroughly what so powerfully drives people
to fabricate ceremonies, we shall discover that they have all flowed from
this source: each person has dared to fashion a new worship of God. Yet God
not only repudiates every fabricated worship but also severely abominates it;
with him obedience is worth more than every sacrifice (1 Samuel 15:22). And
rightly so, because whatever human beings arrogate to themselves in this
matter is taken away from his command. We must therefore hold that, as soon
as people desire to worship God according to their own judgment, whatever
they contribute from themselves is a foul profanation. But lest I enter a
vast forest, I come down to the particular case. I have said that the lawful
use of baptism and the sacred Supper must be sought from nowhere other

[CO X/1, p. 212]

than Christ's institution and command. The only-begotten Son of God willed
that the mystery should be performed with water. Human beings chose to add
chrism and exsufflation. If Christ's authority is sacred and inviolable to us
and receives the reverence it deserves, who does not see that baptism was
defiled by those foreign trifles? It was then so corrupted by mere mockeries
that it pollutes rather than cleanses all those who mingle those gross
sacrileges with the washing.

I do not see why a dispute needed to be raised over mixing water and wine in
the sacred Supper. Christ testified by the symbols of bread and wine that his
flesh is food for us and his blood our drink (John 6:55; Matthew 26:26). If
wine is scarce, it will be in no way absurd to dilute with water the little
that is available, so that there may be enough for the whole church. But if a
religious obligation is imposed, as though the mixture were necessary, this
is not free from superstition. For the church is improperly and no less
rashly bound by a new law beyond Christ's command, and a mystery is childishly
sought in a matter of no significance.

They want the cup to contain water mixed with wine because water and blood
flowed from the Lord's side (1 John 5:6), as though the author's purpose had
been to teach any such thing, rather than to give a pledge of spiritual
drink. Nor is Cyprian's testimony rightly twisted in this direction. He never
contended that the wine in the sacred Supper must be diluted; against the
water-drinkers, he asserted that by Christ's command wine must be drunk there.
There were some who, under the pretext of abstinence, excluded from the
heavenly mystery wine which the common people abuse intemperately. Since,
then, his whole argument teaches that Christ's blood is represented in the
sacred Supper not by water but by wine, the things he says incidentally about
the mixture are not rightly drawn into the defense of water.

Add to this that the unlearned are mistaken about the verb *to mix*, which he
repeatedly uses, when they always take it to mean *to dilute*. According to
accepted usage among ancient and competent Latin writers, it means rather
*to pour*. Indeed, elsewhere, when wishing to exhort the godly to unity, he
reminds them that the sacred cup consists not of many drops of water but of
many grapes, just as the mystical bread consists of many grains (1 Corinthians
10:17). I nevertheless do not deny that Cyprian affirms the mixture of water
and wine, but on an insubstantial pretext: that the wine signifies Christ's
blood and the water the people. If anyone delights in this subtlety, he
deserves to feed upon wind.

Let us, however, bid farewell to the curiosity of the human mind, which has
always been excessively wanton, and hang upon the lips of the one who is the
sole author of the Supper. Indeed, we see how Cyprian himself, in a memorable
statement, sends us away from himself to Christ when he forbids following
custom in offering water alone. Instead, he says, one must ask whom the
fathers followed whom others wish to imitate. “For if Christ alone,” he says,

[CO X/1, p. 213]

“is to be heard, we must not pay attention to what anyone before us thought
should be done, but to what Christ, who is before all, first did. For we ought
not to follow human custom, but God's truth.” Among modest and peaceable
readers, this is surely a full and solid determination of the case: human
opinions are brought into order, custom, however ancient, is renounced, and
Christ's authority alone stands preeminent.

Farewell. 5 April 1558.

## Translator's notes

1. The internally written *Nonis Aprilis* is 5 April. Neither a place nor an
   addressee appears in the surviving CO main text.
2. *Caeremoniae* denotes ecclesiastical rites; *cultus* in the argument about
   invention is broader “worship.” Calvin allows restrained use of ceremonies
   but treats Christ's sacramental institution as the controlling boundary.
3. CO identifies Augustine's Januarius discussion as Epistle 119. That
   reference is editorial apparatus and is not inserted into the Latin body.
4. *Lymphatici* permits a polemical play on water and frenzy. “Water-drinkers”
   states the immediate eucharistic issue without importing the secondary
   sense into the translation.
5. The discussion of Cyprian turns partly on the semantic range of *miscere*:
   Calvin argues that its occurrence need not mean dilution, while expressly
   conceding that Cyprian elsewhere supports a mixed cup.
6. The English was translated afresh from `latin.txt` and checked against the
   facsimile. The Beaty–Farley translation was not consulted or used for
   wording.
