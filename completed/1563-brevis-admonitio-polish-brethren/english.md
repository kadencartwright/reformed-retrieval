# A Brief Warning to the Polish Brethren

1563

This is a fresh English translation of John Calvin's complete Latin prose in
*Ioannis Calvini opera quae supersunt omnia*, vol. IX (Corpus Reformatorum
XXXVII), pp. 633–638. The source title page is reproduced at CO IX 629–630 and
the intervening opening is blank. The translation was made from the normalized
Latin and checked against the page images. No modern English translation
supplied its wording.

The full source title means: *A Brief Warning by John Calvin to the Polish
Brethren, Lest by Imagining a Threefold Essence in God in Place of Three
Persons They Fashion Three Gods for Themselves*. The title is publisher's
paratext rather than the opening of Calvin's argument. CO running heads,
old-edition page locators, and comparative French footnotes are not translated
as Calvin's Latin prose.

## Translation

### [CO IX, p. 633]

I examined with the bitterest grief the table recently published in Poland
which made Christ and the Holy Spirit gods other than the Father. For some
time, and not without cause, this concern had kept me anxious: that Stancaro's
importunity might sweep away brothers less practiced in Scripture, so that in
trying to avoid one absurdity they would fall into another still fouler one.
What I feared has therefore happened, and a sad example has disclosed how
harmful a plague contention is when the aim is to defeat an adversary rather
than simply to defend a good cause.

Stancaro's gross delusion was deservedly repudiated by the Polish brothers.
But while they guarded themselves against one subtlety of the devil, another
impostor slipped in, Blandrata, worse than Stancaro; and he abused this
occasion to spread an error no less detestable. Satan, of course, has been a
shape-shifter from the beginning. Even now he transforms himself in various
ways and insinuates himself cunningly, as though fighting against himself, so
that he may ensnare the wretched in different errors. Stancaro's delusion has
already been refuted by us solidly and lucidly. I will now ask and exhort our
brothers who have been deceived by the cunning of a few men at least not to
refuse to attend to my warning with composed minds. If they are teachable
enough to come to their senses, it will not, I hope, be difficult for me to
bring them to recoil from the impious invention that they embraced too rashly
and thoughtlessly.

The principle set down in the table is false: that Scripture always speaks
distinctly about the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. It is clear from many
passages that God is mentioned sometimes comparatively and sometimes without
distinction. If I establish that there are two modes of speaking, that whole
empty speculation will immediately vanish—that the Father of our Lord is
[alone] the one and true God. I certainly acknowledge that when the Father and
the Son are mentioned together, the Son is distinguished from the Father. But
lest the table-maker congratulate himself, I deny that the distinction of
which he boasts so ignorantly is

### [CO IX, p. 634]

perpetual.

I will begin with the first passage, which he shamefully corrupts: “Jehovah
your God is one God” (Deuteronomy 6:4). Because no comparison between the
persons is made here, I say that the name of the one God is wrongly restricted
to the Father. For if Jehovah, who has being from himself, is the Father alone,
it will follow that the Son is not Jehovah. How could one then transfer to the
Son what is asserted about the Father alone? We must now determine whether
Christ is Jehovah or not.

Isaiah saw the God of hosts sitting upon the throne (Isaiah 6:1). It cannot be
denied that he was that same Jehovah whom Moses declares to be the one God.
What, then, does John say? “Although Jesus had performed so many signs before
them, they did not believe in him, so that Isaiah's saying might be fulfilled.”
Then, after quoting the passage, he adds: “Isaiah said these things when he saw
his glory and spoke about him” (John 12:37). It is a worthless quibble to
object that Christ's glory was shown to the prophet because Christ is the
image of the Father. For this cannot be wrested from us: if Isaiah spoke about
Christ, what he says about Jehovah applies to Christ himself.

Again, to prove that we must all appear before Christ's judgment seat, Paul
introduces another statement of the prophet: “As I live, every knee will bend
before me” (Isaiah 45:23). Certainly it is the God of Israel who speaks in the
prophet, the one whom Moses had testified to be the only God. Yet according to
Paul this designation applies to Christ (Romans 14:11; Philippians 2:10). He
is therefore the same one who swears by his own life that he will not give his
glory to another. They will try in vain to escape by saying that God will be
judge in the person of Christ because he has given all judgment to the Son.
Paul takes it as an acknowledged truth that what the prophet says about God's
supreme dominion truly applies to Christ.

By similar reasoning Paul proves from the same Isaiah that Christ is a stone
of stumbling to the Jews, although the prophet makes that declaration only
about the one God (Isaiah 8:14; Romans 9:33; 1 Peter 2:7). He would likewise be
wrong to apply to Christ what David affirms about the supreme God unless we
acknowledge that Christ is comprehended within that deity. David addresses
the one God in these words:

### [CO IX, p. 635]

“You ascended on high” (Psalm 68:19). Paul teaches that this was fulfilled in
Christ (Ephesians 4:8). Moreover, if anything is proper to God, it is marked by
singular designations in these words: “You, Jehovah, founded heaven and earth
in the beginning” (Psalm 102:26); and again, “Worship him, all you angels”
(Psalm 97:7). Yet the apostle claims these things for Christ (Hebrews 1:10).
It therefore follows that the name “God” there designates the whole divine
essence, which Christ and the Spirit share with the Father.

Accordingly, whenever the maker of the table thrusts his distinctions upon
me, the answer will be ready: when Christ is compared with the Father, the
persons are rightly distinguished; but when God is named without
qualification, the Son is by no means excluded. And certainly, if being life
belongs to the Father alone, then John's statement will be false: “This is the
true God and eternal life” (1 John 5:20). I ask whether he introduces a
different true God from the one celebrated in Moses and the prophets.

Add also that, although Malachi speaks particularly about Christ, he
nevertheless ascribes to him what it is not lawful to transfer away from the
true and only God of Israel. For to whom was the temple on Mount Zion
dedicated? To whom were sacrifices offered? To worship there a divine being
other than the true God would certainly have been a sacrilegious profanation.
Yet Malachi, speaking about Christ, says, “The ruler whom you seek will come to
his own temple” (Malachi 3:1). Indeed, because he is named Jehovah in Jeremiah
(23:6), he has always had to be worshiped together with the Father.

In this dispute we must weigh carefully and with pious attention what the name
“Jehovah” signifies. By this designation the God of Israel not only separates
himself from all creatures, but also shows that essence is to be sought
nowhere except in him alone. When the prophets share this name with Christ,
they demonstrate clearly enough that he is the one who spoke this way in
Moses. Otherwise, not only would Stephen have violated the lawful pattern of
prayer when he prayed to Christ (Acts 7:59), but the whole church would also
be guilty of the same impiety.

The God of Israel, whom the maker of the table wants to be the Father alone,
ranks invocation of his name above every sacrifice and claims this honor for
himself as the chief sacrifice (Psalm 50:14). What, then, does Stephen do? He
directs his prayer to Christ. Ananias, moreover, says in Luke that all the
pious invoked Christ's name (Acts 9:14). How will these things agree with one
another—that Christ is rightly invoked, and yet he is not that God who through
David demands this sacrifice of praise? The fiction that Scripture everywhere
speaks distinctly about the Father and the Son has therefore been overthrown,
since it often declares the one deity of both under the single name Jehovah.
When the Son is joined to the Father, however, it is no wonder that each is
assigned his own person.

Nevertheless, while the table-maker affects an imposing heap of material, he
distorts some passages badly. I know that

### [CO IX, p. 636]

the ancients interpreted this saying of Moses in this way: “The Lord rained
from the Lord” (Genesis 19:24). But since the name Jehovah is expressed in
both clauses, the sense would in no way fit that the Father rained from the
Son. Rather, to display God's dreadful vengeance more clearly, Moses repeats
the name of the eternal God a second time. Likewise, when it is said that God
made man in the image of God (Genesis 1:27), a distinction between the persons
is foolishly sought.

As for the statement taken from the first chapter of John, the Son is indeed
distinguished from the Father because he was with God (John 1:1). But when it
is added that the Word was God, it is detestable sacrilege to invent a new or
different deity. The table-maker would also have done better to leave alone
this passage from the Psalm: “By the Word of the Lord the heavens were
established,” and so forth (Psalm 33:6). It is not that I wish to pursue him
if he cites Scripture less than aptly, but that he should not parade his
cleverness before us with such insolence. He has likewise failed to grasp
Paul's meaning in the statement he produces from First Corinthians:
“There are distributions of ministries, but the Lord is the same”
(1 Corinthians 12:5). In many passages he quibbles childishly, but in the
process he betrays his poison.

I acknowledge that Christ does not say, “I and the Father are one” using the
masculine *unus*, but “one” using the neuter *unum* (John 10:30), because his
purpose there was not to call himself one God with the Father. The table-maker,
however, captiously infers from this that Christ is a God other than the
Father. Similarly, when John says, “These three are one” (1 John 5:7), he uses
this form because he is not expressly discussing essence or deity. But this
man, whether maliciously as an impostor or ineptly as a trifler, applies to the
designation “God” what was said for an entirely different purpose. In one word
he denies that he establishes three gods, but he cannot escape this absurdity
as long as he denies that Christ is one God with the Father. For if he is not
the same God and yet is God, he is therefore another God.

I will speak more plainly. If the Father has his being from himself, the Son
has his being from the Father, and the Spirit from both, do not three essences
emerge, and is not the one essence thereby torn apart? Certainly, if this
man's invention is accepted, no essence is left to Christ other than some
abstraction or other. Thus they boast that the Father's essence, which gives
fecundity to all, brought forth the Son from itself in the beginning so that
it would not be sterile. Here again I implore the brothers to guard themselves
prudently against hidden snares. If Christ's deity is placed separately from
the Father's deity, a plurality of gods is introduced. This deception is
exposed by the man's own words when he calls it a dream if the Son and the
Father are fused into one. For according to him, “fusion” is nothing other
than simple unity of essence.

### [CO IX, p. 637]

I am not, however, putting forward my own conjectures. We know the workshop in
which the diabolical error with which Blandrata infected Poland was
manufactured. He, Matteo Gribaldi, and a certain man named Valentino Gentile
were truly one. Two of them were not ashamed to profess publicly what I have
said: that there are numerically several gods. Whatever disguises they use to
cover themselves, their shamelessness has been caught fast. For if the Son is
the same God as the Father, it follows that he has always been the God of
Israel, a rank of honor from which they openly exclude him.

As for the phrase “God from God” in the Nicene Creed, although the expression
is harsh, its sole purpose is to say that the distinction between the persons
is no obstacle to there being one essence. The statement must therefore be
resolved this way: although he is from God, he is nevertheless God, and indeed
true God. It is even probable that, because the holy fathers feared deceit,
they deliberately caused this to be added: “true God from true God.” That this
was certainly the council's intention is gathered from the Athanasian Creed,
whose author presided there and directed the entire proceeding. I greatly
marvel that those who parade the creed disdainfully reject its sure and
suitable interpreter. I will not now examine the statements they advance
from other ancient fathers, because I believe that I have already done this
in my refutation of Gentile.

I now come to the second head of this question. I acknowledge that Christ
everywhere distinguishes himself from the Father: namely, insofar as he bears
the person of the mediator. I rightly warned somewhere, and indeed have
repeatedly insisted, that the things Christ declares about himself in the
Gospel of John apply neither to his human nature nor to his divine nature
separately, but must be referred to the office enjoined upon him by the Father.
Thus when he makes himself one with the Father, his purpose was something far
different from raising us to a naked speculation about his hidden essence.
Rather, he argues from the end: that we will be one with the Father because
the Father dwells in him.

I therefore do not hesitate to repeat what I wrote long ago: “To understand
properly how Christ and the Father are one, take care not to strip him of the
person of the mediator. Instead, consider him as he is the head of the church
and join him to his members. The context will thus stand best: lest the Son's
unity with the Father be ineffectual or useless, its power must spread through
the whole body of the pious.” Conversely, when the Son is subjected to the
Father as though inferior, there is no reason to dispute too curiously about
how this befits God manifested in the flesh. God's marvelous design, which
was not badly expressed by the word “dispensation,” ought to be enough for us.
Christ's statement, “The Father is greater than I,” was restricted to his
human nature; I, however, do not hesitate, with reference to the whole
composite,

### [CO IX, p. 638]

to extend it. For although Christ is eternal God, when he descended to us he
began to be the one between God and us. For this reason
the Father is called the one God: because Christ, clothed in flesh, presides
over the church in such a way that he has God as his head, as Paul teaches
(1 Corinthians 11:3). In this same way God governs us mediately through the
hand of his Son until we are brought to the full union of glory, in which the
Son will be subjected to the Father so that God may be all in all.

In accordance with this dispensation Christ himself prayed on earth; and now,
relying on him as our advocate, we pray to the Father in his name. There is
nothing forced or thorny in this doctrine. Christ comes forth to us only as
the mediator who came from God; yet he has God dwelling in himself, and all
the fullness of deity resides in him, so that he may at last lead us to God,
when we will enjoy fully the intimate and immediate vision, as they call it,
of the deity which now shines in an image.

Let Stancaro now shout that we are Arians. The emptiness of this slander will
immediately vanish when we acknowledge that what is rightly applied to the
person of the mediator does not fall within the divine nature. Yet when we
acknowledge three distinct hypostases in the one essence of God—so that with
respect to Christ, his Father is the one God, while Christ himself is the image
of the invisible God—there is nothing in this doctrine akin to Sabellius's
error.

But let those who take such delight in perpetual distinction see to it that
they do not mix and confuse everything. By restricting the name of deity
everywhere to the name of the Father, they soon tear it into parts when they
wish it to be shared by the Son and the Spirit. The cause of their error is
that they observe no difference between two forms of knowledge: first God's
majesty is described to us without distinction, and then he manifests himself
in three persons. So true is the unity we assert that when Christ is separately
called God, the Father's deity is included at the same time. When Thomas
exclaims, “My Lord and my God” (John 20:28), he recognizes the mediator and
then transcends higher. Likewise, when Paul declares that Christ is “God
blessed forever” (Romans 9:5), he does not imagine a second or produced deity,
but understands that the deity which is one applies to Christ. Nevertheless,
when the Son and the Spirit are included under the name of God, no cloud is
drawn over them to prevent the three persons from shining forth and each from
retaining his own property.

Would that the Polish brothers thoroughly ponder these things among themselves
with the modesty and sobriety that is fitting. And may those who now exult,
drunk on the novelty of this invention as though on new wine, not refuse to
taste afresh doctrine that has been well clarified and matured.

## Translator's notes

1. The “table” was an antitrinitarian schematic circulating in Poland. The CO
   prolegomena associate it especially with Giorgio Biandrata and say that it
   represented the Son and Holy Spirit as gods other than the Father. The
   table itself is not reproduced with this edition.
2. “Stancaro's delusion has already been refuted by us” refers to the earlier
   dispute over Francesco Stancaro's teaching that Christ mediates only
   according to his humanity. Calvin's *Responsum ad fratres Polonos quomodo
   Christus sit mediator ad refutandum Stancari errorem* is printed at CO IX
   333–342.
3. The Latin at p. 633 lacks “alone” in *unum esse et verum Deum patrem Domini
   nostri*, but the immediately surrounding argument shows that Calvin rejects
   the exclusive restriction of “the one and true God” to the Father, not the
   designation of the Father as God. Square brackets mark the supplied word.
4. *Iehovah*, retained as “Jehovah,” is central to the argument. Calvin treats
   it as the name of the self-existent God of Israel and argues from prophetic
   and apostolic applications of Old Testament passages to Christ.
5. The sentence beginning *Neque enim nobis excutietur* is compressed. The
   idiom means that Calvin's inference cannot be shaken or wrested from him:
   if Isaiah spoke of Christ's glory, what Isaiah said of Jehovah applies to
   Christ.
6. “Essence” translates *essentia* throughout. Calvin contrasts one common
   divine essence with three distinct persons or *hypostases*. His opponents'
   account of the Son's derivative being, he argues, produces three essences
   and therefore three gods.
7. At p. 636 Calvin contrasts masculine *unus* with neuter *unum*. English
   “one” does not display that grammatical distinction, so both Latin forms
   are named explicitly in the translation. The early French version expands
   the contrast as “the Father and I are not one [person], but the Father and
   I are one [thing].”
8. “From both” translates *ab utroque*, Calvin's concise reference to the
   Spirit's procession from the Father and the Son. The translation preserves
   the phrase without expanding it into a separate argument.
9. “Fusion” translates *conflatura*, language attributed to the table-maker.
   Calvin's point is polemical: what the opponent condemns as fusing Father and
   Son is, Calvin argues, merely the orthodox confession of one simple divine
   essence.
10. “Two of them” translates *duos*. The immediate Latin does not expressly
    identify which two among Biandrata, Gribaldi, and Gentile Calvin means.
11. The clause about the Athanasian Creed translates Calvin's own historical
    claim that its author presided at the council and directed its proceedings.
    It should not be quoted as this project's independent historical finding.
12. “My refutation of Gentile” is Calvin's *Impietas Valentini Gentilis detecta
    et palam traducta*, printed at CO IX 361–420.
13. “The whole composite” renders *totum complexum*: Christ considered in the
    complete mediatorial union, rather than either the human or divine nature
    in isolation. Calvin applies “The Father is greater than I” to Christ in
    that mediatorial capacity.
14. “The one between God and us” translates *medius*. It can mean one who
    stands in the middle and thus a mediator; the following sentences explain
    it through Christ's incarnate office.
15. “Two forms of knowledge” renders *duae notitiae species*. Calvin
    distinguishes passages that describe divine majesty without personal
    comparison from passages in which God makes himself known in the three
    persons.
16. The conclusion continues its wine image: *mustum* is new wine,
    *defaecata* means cleared from the lees, and *concocta* can mean digested,
    matured, or thoroughly worked out. “Well clarified and matured” preserves
    the contrast between intoxicating novelty and settled doctrine.
