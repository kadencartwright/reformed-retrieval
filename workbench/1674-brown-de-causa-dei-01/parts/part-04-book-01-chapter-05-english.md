## CHAPTER V

### On created natural right

Having now explained in some measure uncreated natural right, so far as we
judged necessary for our purpose, it follows that we should likewise indicate
briefly what created natural right is.

“The just,” says Aristotle in *Ethics*, book 5, chapter 6, “is something
analogous.” *Right* states a relation to the law from which it proceeds. For
although it often denotes the same thing as law—as in *the right of nature*,
*the right of nations*, and *civil right*, that is, a law prescribed by nature,
a law employed by nations, and a civil law—properly it signifies something
resulting from law and belonging to a person by reason or force of law.

Thus *the right of nature* is that which belongs to each person through the law
of nature. It is properly called the *right of a person*, and by some an
*active right*. Grotius defines it in *The Law of War and Peace*, book 1,
chapter 1, section 4, as “a moral quality belonging to a person for having or
doing something just.” Lessius, *On Justice and Right*, book 2, chapter 2,
doubt 1, defines it as “a lawful power for obtaining some thing,” and so
forth. Matthew Martini, in his philosophical lexicon under the word *right*,
defines it thus: “A necessity adhering to a person, or a right power, to do,
omit, or permit something.”

This created right is either *obligatory* or *permissive*. The former is that
which imposes an obligation upon the creature to avoid this as an evil,
shameful, dishonorable, and unlawful thing, and to do that as a good,
honorable, and lawful thing. For this right takes away the liberty of doing
whatever one pleases, and restrains, limits, or diminishes it. Thus natural
liberty is not absolute but moderated and restrained, and is now nothing other
than the natural faculty of doing whatever one pleases unless something is
prohibited by right. Here prohibition embraces commands no less than
interdicts, for one is prohibited from violating a command and commanded to
obey a prohibition, as Selden says in *On Natural and Gentile Right*, book 1,
chapter 4, page 46.

Hence this obligatory right produces in the things or acts themselves the
distinction between evil and good, or between shameful and honorable, and in
the persons upon whom this right is conferred or imposed it produces an
obligation to avoid evil or shame and to do good, honorable, and just things.
This right is, as it were, *what is commanded*.

*Permissive right*, however, states no obligation. It concerns acts which are
neither prohibited as evil and shameful nor commanded as good and honorable,
but are indifferent and properly permitted as lawful. By this right one has a
lawful power to do or omit something among these permitted things. This right
is, as it were, *what is just*.

This natural obligatory right differs little or nothing from the law of
nature. For, as has been said, it has the force of law, binding one to
obedience by avoiding the one and doing the other. A clear and distinct
explanation of it will therefore be of great use to us in what follows.

[1674 logical printed p. 50 | IA leaf 68 | PDF p. 68]

We need not inquire at length whether there is such a right, since it is
sufficiently established by the practice of all nations, even barbarous and
savage ones, which establish certain laws and institutions by which they are
governed, proposing rewards to the good and inflicting punishments upon the
wicked. For whence, unless from this right, comes that distinction between
evil and good, and the sense of obligation both to flee evil and pursue good?
It is also established by the terrors of conscience which torment even those
accustomed to live alone after the manner of beasts, according to Juvenal:

*Whatever evil example is committed displeases its own author. This is the
first vengeance: that no guilty person is acquitted when he himself is judge.*

“This is nature,” says Cicero, “which restrains from injury and torments the
impious even in the greatest solitude; which compels the wicked and daring,
however great they are, either to deny the crime or, when they have confessed,
to invent some cause so that they may seem to have acted justly.” And in *For
Roscius*: “Each person is especially tormented by his own fraud and error;
each is driven mad and afflicted by his own crime; his evil thoughts and the
consciousness of his mind terrify him. These are the wicked person's
unceasing, domestic Furies, which day and night exact penalties from the most
criminal.” And in *On Ends*, book 2: “Wickedness never allows him to breathe,
never to rest.” Again in the same place: “The guilty pay penalties not so much
by judgments as by anguish of conscience and the torment of fraud.”
Tarquitius the Epicurean likewise says in Cicero, *On Ends*, book 1: “We think
wickedness should be avoided not only because of the disadvantages that befall
the wicked, but much more because it never allows the person in whose mind it
dwells to breathe or rest.”

Seneca testifies to the same thing in epistle 97: “The evils of crimes are
scourged by conscience, whose greatest torment is that perpetual anxiety
presses and beats upon it.” And epistle 105: “Conscience does not allow one to
attend to anything else, but repeatedly compels one to look back upon
oneself.” “He who fears punishment pays it.”

The story of Bessus in Plutarch is well known. Many years after killing his
father, he knocked down a swallow's nest with a stick and crushed the chicks
underfoot. When the bystanders accused him of inhumanity and savagery, he gave
as his reason that for too long they had falsely accused him of parricide. He
thereby furnished a sufficiently clear suspicion of his crime, was arrested,
and, after the matter was examined and proved, was punished.

The same is evident in tyrants who think themselves sufficiently exempt from
human penalties. Thus Tacitus, speaking of Tiberius in *Annals*, book 6, says:
“His crimes and outrages had themselves turned to his punishment. Nor was it
in vain that the most eminent master of wisdom was accustomed to affirm that,
if the minds of tyrants were laid open, gashes and blows could be seen; for as
bodies are torn by lashes, so the mind is torn by cruelty, lust, and evil
counsels. Neither fortune nor solitude protected Tiberius from confessing the
torments of his breast and his own punishments.” Indeed Tiberius himself
addressed the Senate in a letter: “What shall I write to you, conscript
fathers, or how shall I write it, or what shall I not write at all at this
time? May the gods and goddesses destroy me worse than I feel myself dying
daily, if I know.” See Suetonius, *Tiberius*, chapter 61. Historians also
testify of Jugurtha that after he had committed innumerable crimes, neither
day nor any night was peaceful for him—

[1674 logical printed p. 51 | IA leaf 69 | PDF p. 69]

—nor did he place sufficient trust in any place, mortal person, or time. He
feared citizens and enemies alike, looked around at everything, and trembled
at every noise, and so forth.

Hence Juvenal, satire 11: *Whose mind is cold with crimes, while the heart
secretly sweats with guilt.* And satire 13: *Yet do you think those have
escaped whose mind, conscious of a dreadful deed, holds them thunderstruck and
falls upon them with a silent blow, while an unseen torturer lashes the soul?
The punishment is severe and far crueler than those invented by stern
Caedicius or Rhadamanthus: night and day to carry one's own witness in one's
breast.*

Whence come these wounds of conscience and those avenging Furies, when those
who are burdened by the memory of no evil deeds are not tormented by them,
unless from this right born, nourished, and brought up with us? Why did the
nations living in paganism believe God to be the avenger of crimes and
therefore institute sacrifices to expiate sins, unless because they believed
transgressors liable to punishment? Whence comes fear of punishment to be
inflicted, if not from consciousness of an obligation imposed by some law by
the Deity?

Just as God's right discussed above is independent and borrowed from no one,
so this right is altogether dependent upon God, from whom human beings have
their being, all their perfections, and whatever they possess, and who by the
best right is their Legislator and Governor. Therefore this right is called
natural, not so much because it draws its origin from nature as because it
arises from the very Author of nature, as some think. Hence Plato traces the
origin of right to heaven and wishes *dikaion* to be, as it were, *diaion*,
that is, “running through.” This is true not only of obligatory right but also
of permissive right. For he who prescribes an obligatory right to avoid these
things and do those things at the same time grants power to do or omit the
other things at pleasure; he wills these permitted acts to be lawful just as
he wills the others to be unlawful or necessary.

When some say that this right proceeds entirely from each person's natural
reason, or that it is what natural reason prescribes to all—namely, reason
which is right, simple, and not depraved by evil custom—they do not mean that
the right depends upon reason's prescription and has no other origin. Rather,
they indicate that this right is so closely connected with our reason and so
clear in itself that it can very easily be perceived by right reason uncorrupt
by prejudices and evil custom; or, once perceived, approved without any long
deliberation and embraced as the rule and measuring line of morals, just as if
it had been prescribed by reason itself and drawn its origin from it.

When Ulpian says that right is “the art of the good and equitable,” it is
certain that he defines not so much right as the science of right. What follows
confirms this, when he adds—

[1674 logical printed p. 52 | IA leaf 70 | PDF p. 70]

—“For this reason someone deservedly calls us priests, for we cultivate
justice, profess knowledge of the good and equitable, separate the equitable
from the inequitable, and discern the lawful from the unlawful,” and so forth.
When he adds, “This study has two positions,” that is, parts or species, he
speaks similarly. Certainly, since right is itself rather the good and
equitable, it cannot be called the art of the good and equitable. No art is
occupied with itself, and every science differs from what falls under it. A
house is one thing and the art of architecture another; numbering is one thing
and arithmetic, or the art of numbering, another.

Justinian calls justice “the constant and perpetual will to render to each
person his own right.” Cicero in *On Invention*, book 2, defines it a little
differently: “a habit of the mind which preserves the common welfare and
renders to each person his dignity.” Or, as elsewhere, it is “equity rendering
right to each according to each person's dignity.” Let the jurists consider
which is to be preferred; it matters little to us. It seems rather harsh and
far-fetched to say that by “constant and perpetual will” Ulpian or Justinian
designates a habit of the will, or a virtue residing in the will, by which a
person is disposed readily to render to each what is just and equitable. Ames
rejects it for this and other reasons which may be seen in *On Conscience*,
book 5, chapter 1, sections 1–3.

They commonly distinguish justice into *general* and *special*. General
justice is occupied with every virtue, or contains in itself all the virtues
that concern morals, such as wisdom, fortitude, and temperance. Hence Zeno is
said to have called justice prudence in rendering to each his own, temperance
in receiving, and fortitude in enduring. Others say it can be understood in
two ways: either insofar as it is the bond of all virtues in relation to laws,
so that it is nothing other than the rectitude seen in all virtues insofar as
they conform to laws, and for this reason is called *legal justice*; or insofar
as it is a virtue primarily and in itself in relation to another, to whom
something is owed by the prescription of laws, and consequently in relation
to oneself, whether within society or outside it. Even then it is called
general justice, because it perfects a person with respect to the good of the
whole.

In the former sense they say that justice is nothing other than the virtue of
obedience owed to laws. In the latter sense they say it is the virtue according
to which each person performs something provided by the laws to which he
submits. According to them, a person must first submit himself to the laws and
then render obedience to them. Because he does this, they say it follows that
he conducts himself toward God, his neighbor, and himself in the manner the
laws prescribed. They hold that general justice taken in this latter way is
what Justinian defines.

*Special or particular justice* is, as they say, that which is exercised in
human societies in transactions and in keeping faith regarding things
contracted. Others say it is that according to which the will inclines to
render each person's right according to equality. They hold that it regards
another in the stricter meaning, so that there is no justice of a person
toward himself, a father toward a son, or a master toward a servant. By
equality here they understand both commutative equality, which directs one
private person toward another, where equality must be preserved between what
is given, furnished, and received; and distributive equality, which directs a
public person, or one who stands in the place of a public person, toward a
private one, where proportion must be preserved between the things distributed
and the conditions of the persons among whom the distribution is made.

[1674 logical printed p. 53 | IA leaf 71 | PDF p. 71]

When they limit this justice to another, however, they seem to understand it
far too narrowly. For, as Ames says in the place cited, sections 5–6, every
virtue in its own way is ordered to the rectitude and perfection of its
possessor; and from the very fact that a just person renders another his own,
it necessarily follows that he makes himself fit to do this, and therefore
preserves his own right for himself with moderation.

As they divide justice, so they divide right into *general* and *special*.
They hold the former to be that which exacts all the precepts of all the
virtues, and the latter that which contains the prescriptions of special
justice. They hold it to concern another's goods, because no one can be just
or unjust to himself.

They lay down three precepts of right as common rules of justice—or, as others
not improbably judge, as so many foundations of jurisprudence, from which the
other precepts and rules of that discipline are deduced like conclusions from
their premises, or to which the other hypotheses are referred as to theses:
*to live honorably; not to injure another; to render each his own*. Ames, in
*On Conscience*, book 5, chapter 1, section 19, considers this enumeration very
confused and imperfect, for reasons not to be despised which may be seen
there.

Indeed, the jurists divide into various parties in explaining these precepts
or principles. Some refer all three to the one, *to live honorably*. Others
oppose them to three furies—lust, wrath, and avarice. Others wish all three to
be related to one's neighbor. Others hold them to be precepts of general
right, and wish the first to embrace all the honorable qualities of our life
and to admonish us to conduct ourselves courageously, temperately, kindly,
courteously, moderately, meekly, truthfully, and in a manner altogether
agreeable to every virtue. They hold that the remaining two pertain to special
right as its proper subject matter, the first concerning the correction of
offenses and the second fidelity in contracts.

Those who wish these three to be related to one's neighbor, and this right to
be occupied only in fellowship, civil society, or another's affairs, become
involved in certain difficulties. For they themselves say that this right
draws its origin from nature, or rather from the Author of nature, and is
nothing other than nature's voice indicating what is just and unjust,
honorable and dishonorable. They thereby extend right much farther. Do they
not also define jurisprudence as “knowledge of divine and human things, the
science of the just and unjust”? Who, then, will deny that this right also
embraces and requires the things which regard God, so that the creature
renders to God what is God's and does not injure his honor, but rather honors,
fears, loves, reveres, trusts, and cleaves to him, serves him, and complies
with him in all things? Is this not nature's own voice? Furthermore, who will
deny that a person is debtor to himself and, when he is bound to render
another his right and to render to each his own, is also bound to render his
own to himself? When he is bound not to injure another, is he not much more
bound not to injure himself? And how can anyone be said to live honorably who
does not cultivate piety toward God and is injurious to himself?

Perceiving this, others follow another manner of interpretation. They say the
first precept regards a person in relation to himself, insofar as he ought to
be well disposed in accordance with the virtues so that he may seek another's
good; and that the duty of a person, even one established in society, is in
his own manner to—

[1674 logical printed p. 54 | IA leaf 72 | PDF p. 72]

—consult nature and live agreeably to it according to natural honorableness,
which dictates not only what is lawful but also what is expedient, honorable,
and becoming. Thus he is rendered fit to furnish help to another and injure no
one. Much less, by force of this precept, ought he to injure himself—not only
because no one is lord of his own members, but because by the efficacy of this
precept he is bound to seek his own good and preserve his own right whole and
uninjured with moderation, so that he may at length care for his neighbor. For
since each person is nearest to himself, and love of oneself is the rule and
measure of the duty to be shown one's neighbor, how will one who has not
learned to spare himself will or desire to benefit others? Moreover, one who
does not live agreeably to the first virtue will become a useless member of
the commonwealth.

They say that the worship of God also ought to be referred to this first
precept. For although nature's judgment concerning God and his worship is very
weak and has been greatly obscured since the fall unless it is more fully
illuminated by the revealed word, nevertheless some rays and traces of the
divine Deity remain. No nation is so savage that worship of the Supreme Deity
has not imbued its mind. Nor should anyone be said to live honorably unless he
lives piously and holily and abstains from crimes, including those committed
against himself.

Right is divided by the jurists into *public* and *private*. Public right is
that which regards the condition of the Roman commonwealth; private right is
that which concerns the advantage of individuals, for some things are useful
publicly and others privately. Public right consists in sacred things,
priests, and magistrates. Some divide private right into three parts because,
as they hold, it is collected from natural precepts, those of nations, or
civil precepts.

This division displeases some because, as is evident, it is not a division of
right in general, but only of the civil right of the Romans, which regarded
partly the public advantage and partly that of private persons. It also
displeases some because it implies that only private right is composed of
natural, gentile, or civil precepts, as if the same could not be said of public
right, which acknowledges the same sources. For traces of public right occur
among brute animals no less than likenesses of private right: bees follow
their king, gather around him, fly about him, and by natural instinct furnish
him fidelity; cranes go out in ranks, form a battle line, and post a guard.
Moreover, religion toward God is of natural, gentile, and civil right, while
it also concerns public right. Some indeed say that there is no public right
of either nature or nations, because whatever is public is established by
laws and customs, and every public right is civil, since *public* is as it were
*popular*. I doubt, however, whether this is rightly said, since administrators
of commonwealths ought to care for religion and its exercise, though they may
not handle sacred things.

The division of right into *natural*, *gentile*, and *civil* is celebrated,
though it does not appear altogether sound to some, as we shall afterward
say. Let us nevertheless see how the more common doctrine explains it, so that
at length we may speak more distinctly of natural right, whose nature we are
now investigating.

Civil right is that which each people establishes for itself and which is
proper to a city or people governed by the same laws and customs, such as the
civil right of the Athenians, Romans, or Quirites. It is called civil because
it was established for their sake alone, differing in no other respect, as
some hold, from that—

[1674 logical printed p. 55 | IA leaf 73 | PDF p. 73]

—which is called the right of nations, except that the one belongs to its own
citizens and the other is common to all human beings. Each regards the
advantage of those whom it rules and directs to this as their end the whole
force of its institutions. The origin of both is also the same, though the
right of nations is a little older.

They divide this civil right into that which is properly such—namely, what
derives its origin and invention from a city, is not common to all cities, and
is not borrowed elsewhere—and that which is called civil only by approval or
particular application, such as natural and gentile right received and
approved by this or that particular city. Some say, not without reason, that
there is no merely civil right, that is, a civil right resting upon neither
natural nor gentile right. For every right properly and lawfully so called is
founded upon some natural or gentile right; and a law in a city which wholly
departs from natural and gentile right should be called not a law but a
tyrannical rule. What is not right and just is not right; and in morals nothing
is right which does not agree with right practical reason, while what agrees
with right reason pertains to natural right.

Civil right is also divided into *written* and *unwritten*, though all do not
agree in explaining this. Some hold that written right is right reduced to
writing by public authority, and unwritten right its contrary. But according
to others this is wrong, since writing contributes nothing to the essence of
right and customs are sometimes written, indeed by public authority. These
explain the division thus: written right is sanctioned right, because it is at
once and together enacted by the one who makes laws. For, they say, among the
Latins the word *write* in the matter of laws signified nothing other than to
enact; and among the Greeks *nomizein* often means to enact or sanction laws.
This sanctioned or written law had three species: law, plebiscite, and decree
of the senate. Unwritten right, however, is right without sanction, or custom,
which according to Cicero is “right expressed by antiquity without a law,” or
not sanctioned.

The right of nations is called by Justinian that “which natural reason has
established among all human beings and is observed equally by all, and which
all nations therefore employ.” Again he calls it “that which is common to the
whole human race; for, as use required and human necessities arose, human
nations established certain rights for themselves.” The jurists do not wish
this to be understood as if every nation without exception employed it. For
certain things said to be of the right of nations are not equally observed
among all nations. They say it suffices that most nations, if not all—or at
least the more civilized and humane—employ this right. Although some nations
are so savage that they do not observe the right of enemies, the sanctity of
embassies, and the like, this right can nevertheless properly be called the
common law of all or the common right of the human race. The perverse custom
of this or that particular nation can detract nothing from common consent.

Nor do they wish it understood so that whatever was devised and discovered by
one people, and afterward imitated by all peoples more by its pattern and
example than by natural reason, should be called the right of nations. Rather,
once it is known, it is immediately approved by all through a sure judgment of
nature, not merely as something done but as rightly, justly, and suitably done
for the common advantage of all. Thus, concerning war, the right of war, the
introduction of—

[1674 logical printed p. 56 | IA leaf 74 | PDF p. 74]

—servitude, and so forth, no one who heard of them failed to judge it
equitable to repel force by force, oppose arms with arms, kill the vanquished
whom one had defeated, and serve the victors. For nature has implanted and
persuaded each person that these things are done by right and are necessary
for the common safety of human beings.

The right of nations is distinguished from civil right in various ways.
First, they call the right of nations what is approved by the highest consent
and equally observed, agreeably to natural reason, among all or most nations,
or at least the more civilized ones. Thus it was introduced by reason common
to all and by a kind of tacit consent of all, for the honorableness,
necessity, and advantage of the human race. Civil right, however, was
introduced or approved by only one people or perhaps one or another nation,
and that by wise persons or legislators agreeably to the character and
condition of this or that particular people, for its common safety.

Second, the right of nations is never founded on civil right and is sometimes
made civil by approval, because it precedes civil right. Civil right, however,
is often founded on the right of nations. For the right of nations, as common
to all, is frequently approved by civil right insofar as it is received into a
city and made that city's right.

Furthermore, the right of nations is derived from no civil right, nor does it
follow it as a conclusion follows its premises or a hypothesis is fitted to a
thesis. But civil right flowing from the right of nations—and also from
natural right—is considered either as a special conclusion from more general
premises or as a special determination and accommodation of the right of
nations as a general axiom.

Finally, some explain the distinction by saying that they are distinguished
not by order of time but by the reason of their nature. It can happen that
many things not noticed until later ages ought to be referred to the right of
nature or nations. For whatever is of such a kind that, once known, all
approve and embrace it as fruitful and useful to themselves must be assigned
to the right of nature or nations, provided it has any force of right. But
what does not benefit all, and concerning which it does not matter to the law
of nature or nations whether it is done this way or another, must be regarded
as proper to the city by whose institution it was established.

Because elsewhere Justinian, in the section *On the Division of Things*, calls
the right of nations what “the nature of things brought forth together with
the human race,” they deem it necessary for reconciliation to distinguish the
right of nations into *primary and secondary*, or *primeval and subsequent*.
They confound the former with natural right, and accordingly assign some
things to the one and others to the latter. Yet some think that the authors of
this division wished not so much to divide the right of nations itself as to
mark the ages and progress of the things invented by it. For since all of it
proceeds from the fountain of nature and is nothing other than what each
person's reason prescribes to be done or not done, nothing in it can be prior
or later. They wished to say instead that the things which flowed from this
right for the common advantage were not all invented at once and at the same
time. First came those which human beings employed from the beginning, in the
so-called golden age, when they still lived agreeably to nature and were not
yet depraved by false opinions; then came those received into use in the age
they called iron.

They commonly assign to primary gentile right the things assigned to natural
right, but to secondary gentile right the prohibition of marriage between
brothers and sisters, dominions—because in the beginning goods were held in
common—the rights of embassies, wars—

[1674 logical printed p. 57 | IA leaf 75 | PDF p. 75]

—captivities, servitudes—although by natural right all are born free—and
contracts such as purchase, sale, letting, hiring, partnership, deposit, loan,
and so forth. Some, however, refer certain of these to natural right.

Justinian calls natural right that “which nature taught all animals,” not
because it was not proper to the human being alone, as certain jurists speak,
but because it seems to concern only the animal faculty and is therefore
thought common also to beasts. On this ground they distinguish it from the
right of nations, which human beings employed when joined and associated in
larger societies. The first rude and rustic human beings employed the former
when scattered in woods and mountains and possessing no society besides the
family, in which there were none but husband, wife, and children; they then
had no laws other than those concerning the union of male and female and the
procreation and education of children.

The defenders of this definition do not indeed hold that everything nature
taught even beasts is right. They wish only that to be called natural right
in which other animals also seem to have been instructed, so that by a force
of their own nature they are carried into it just as human beings are by their
implanted and inborn reason. Thus they say that appetite for drinking and
eating is not right, but that the lawful union of male and female is, and so
forth, because human beings are moved by the example of beasts to conduct
themselves justly in these things. Beasts are invited by natural instinct
alone in these matters toward justice—not that we can regard them as just,
but because from them are drawn the beginnings of natural right. For if these
things appear among wild and tame brutes—that they unite for the sake of
generation, love and nourish what is begotten, and do this with a certain
likeness of human solicitude—how much more does nature command it to a human
being?

To these three enumerated matters they add this as likewise conceded by this
right: that we may repel force with force. We see this implanted even in the
senses of beasts, so that if violence is offered them there is none without
anger, a spirit impatient of injury, and a ready eagerness to defend itself if
harmed. This, as Cicero says in *For Milo*, is “not a written law but one born
with us, which we did not learn, receive, or read, but seized, drew, and
expressed from nature itself; for which we were not taught but made, not
instructed but imbued—so that if our life fell into any ambush, violence, or
weapons of robbers or enemies, every honorable means of securing safety would
be available.”

But because the definition given seems to imply that right is also common to
beasts, and consequently that they too are endowed with reason—since, as
Cicero says in *Laws*, book 1, chapter 19, “to those to whom reason was given
by nature, right reason also was given; therefore law too, which is right
reason in commanding and forbidding; and if law, then right also; but reason
was given to all, and therefore right was given to all”—and since this seems
absurd to the jurists, they assert that the emperor meant natural right to be
that which is so innate in us that we can notice its traces and likeness even
in brutes. Others apply this remedy: natural right is understood either as the
natural right of the human being and brute insofar as the human is considered
as an animal, or as the natural right of the human being insofar as he is a
human and rational creature. They hold that it is defined here in the former
way, namely insofar as it is taken for a certain divine power implanted by the
Creator himself in created things—

[1674 logical printed p. 58 | IA leaf 76 | PDF p. 76]

—or for a certain instinct of nature common to all animals endowed with
sensory life. It merits neither praise nor blame and is not determined by
moral circumstances. According to this instinct, and thus according to a
certain sensory reasoning which philosophers call estimative, animals are
positively stirred toward their own preservation and aversion from what is
harmful and destructive.

Thus nothing can be attributed or applied to this natural right except what
regards the preservation of each living thing: either of the species, through
the propagation of its like, to which belong the union of male and female and
the procreation and education of offspring; or of the individual, when the
spur of nature incites each to its own defense and protection, repelling injury
and averting harm. Accordingly, if human beings do something by reason and
brute animals perform it by the guidance, impulse, and incitement of their
nature, they are said to have acted by right. In no other way do approved
authors, by a certain analogy, attribute character, virtues, and vices to
brutes. Insofar as the effect is regarded, certain outward likenesses and
traces of virtues and vices appear in brutes, though they are neither rewarded
nor properly subject to punishment. They therefore hold that Ulpian here
indicated only a certain inclination and impulse of nature.

They similarly say that this natural right is distinguished from the right of
nations in that it belongs to human beings—to all and each without exception,
including those who lead a solitary life or who, separated from others in the
wilderness, pass life like cattle—and thus can be called innate right, not
established or named from the use and custom of nations. The right of nations,
however, is what natural reason indeed dictates among human beings but has now
been transferred into use. It does not always belong to all and each human
being, but sometimes to certain nations, and is established and named from the
use and approval of nations—not because nations once assembled together, but
because successively it seemed good to most of them tacitly, so that it is
regarded as though it were a decree of the whole world.

This is the opinion of certain jurists concerning this division of right.
Connanus, however, enters upon another method. We shall describe his opinion
briefly and then reach our purpose.

Following all the ancients and jurists except Ulpian, as he affirms, he divides
right into *natural* and *civil*. He again subdivides natural right into that
which is the law of just and unjust, mutable at no time or place, and that
which is moderator of those advantages necessary for living. The one aims at
this alone, that we live holily, piously, and justly; the other, that we live
conveniently and suitably.

The former is truly and properly called natural right, which natural reason
prescribes to each person. It is eternal and perpetually consistent with
itself. From it as from a fountain flow those precepts which invite us to
justice, whose sum is: *to live honorably, to injure no one, to render each his
own*. All these, as he speaks, are directed to that rule handed down from
heaven: *Do not do to another what you do not wish done to yourself.* By this
rule and the judgment established by it, the distinction of just from unjust
is handed down. If it is removed, every method of conducting life is removed;
if it is observed, we cannot stray from equity. Neither commands of peoples,
constitutions of princes, nor sentences of judges can break or change this
natural right, because it cannot be done without injustice.
The other natural right cannot be broken or changed, because this cannot be
done without injustice.

[1674 logical printed p. 59 | IA leaf 77 | PDF p. 77]

The other right is occupied with utility. To it belong dominions, kingdoms,
wars, servitudes, manumissions, contracts, and other dealings of this kind by
which we are rendered more fit to share one with another and communicate
among ourselves. It is not ineptly called the right of nations because it was
established not so much by nature itself as by human judgment. Nevertheless it
is a part of natural right and is contained under its name, because unanimous
agreement of all nations in every matter, confirmed gradually and tacitly by
customs rather than institutions or laws, is deemed a law of nature.

This can be changed while natural right remains safe. Some peoples are said
still not to know dominion over things, all things being common among them. If
someone prohibited purchase or lease and abolished by law every use of money,
Connanus says that he does not see what inequity the law would possess. It
would indeed detract something from the advantage of its citizens, but nothing
at all from that simple and equal right of nature. He therefore says that
natural right can be changed in some matters—those devised by the consent of
all for the usefulness of human life through natural judgment—but not in those
which contain the distinction of just and unjust.

To the first and proper natural right he refers the education of children and
fidelity in contracts. Unless we do the latter we injure another and do not
render each his own. These are laws of nature, closely joined to the rule that
no one ought to be enriched by another's loss. He also refers to it the
repulsion, and indeed avenging, of force. “Natural right,” says Cicero in *On
Invention*, book 2, “is what not opinion but a certain inborn power brings to
us, such as religion, piety, gratitude, vengeance, respect, and truth.” Defense
protects us from future and imminent injury; vengeance avenges injury already
done and received, but with no other intention than that the offender, having
impunity set before him, should not become bolder in sinning afterward.

Connanus does not hold that dominion over the things which one took for
oneself from heaven, sea, and earth was from nature, but only possession or
use, so that as long as one preserved it for his use no other had a right to
take or use it. Likewise no one could disturb another from the land he had
taken for dwelling or cultivation while he possessed it, lest he overturn the
possession nature granted to the first occupier, which could not be done
without injury. Thus among those first natural persons, he says, each had his
own possession but not dominion. The common consent of all later devised the
right of dominion for the greater custody of things and provision against
future necessity. He says that first and simple nature knew neither lawful
marriages nor definite children, but that all were common—though I do not see
by what authority.

He says that those things belong to the right of nations, or secondary right
of nature, which human reason afterward devised when insinuating itself into
human societies and friendships and seeking advantages suitable for living,
yet without departing from nature. Such are those contracts which all nations
approved because they were convenient and almost necessary for commerce. And
because human society could not be reconciled or preserved unless there were
some necessity and constancy in transactions, they devised contracts and,
having devised them, added the force of obligation—

[1674 logical printed p. 60 | IA leaf 78 | PDF p. 78]

—to them. He calls civil right that concerning which at the beginning it
mattered nothing whether it was done in this or that way, but after it has
been established it does matter; or what does not benefit all, and concerning
which it does not matter to the law of nature whether it is done thus or
otherwise. This must be considered proper to the city by whose institution it
was established. Thus Cicero in *On Duties*: “What is civil is not immediately
also gentile; but what is gentile ought also to be civil.”

Having premised these things, let us now approach our principal work and see
what can properly be called natural right for us and what cannot. Various
rules or indications are proposed by various authors according to which they
think judgment should be made, but these cannot please in every respect, and
some overthrow all justice and piety.

There are, first, those who imagine a state of liberty and war, as they call
it, of human beings established outside human society. In it all things are
lawful against all, and the norm of every matter and good—or of right and of
things justly done—is one's own preservation or defense of life and limbs.
Thus Hobbes in *On the Citizen*. But who does not see how horrible, atheistic,
impious, and hostile to all society this opinion is? It imagines a state in
which it wishes the human being to be without law and subject to no law of
God. But by what argument can this be proved? Who, weighing everything in a
just balance, will deny that God is Rector of all, governs this world by his
omnipotence, and rules rational creatures by laws? We proved this above and
confirmed it by firm reasons. Who, then, will say that a human being has been,
or can now be supposed to be, placed in a state in which he is released from
divine laws and his will stands to him for reason and law, so that whatever
pleases him is lawful?

Second, on this account he will at least make us equal to beasts in that state
and affirm that beyond the sensuality or animality common to us with brute
animals we shall have nothing. Lactantius, book 6, speaks far otherwise: “In
all animals which lack wisdom we see nature reconciling each to itself. They
injure others in order to benefit themselves, for they do not know that it is
evil to injure. But a human being, because he has knowledge of good and evil,
abstains from injury even to his own disadvantage.”

Third, in that state the human being will either be endowed with reason or
not. If not, he will be a mere brute; and when or how will he become a rational
animal? If he is endowed with reason, will he not use it? Ought he not to act
according to reason and its prescription? But what reason will prescribe such
things for him to do?

Fourth, is the human being in every state created in God's image and bound to
restore that image now lost by the fall, and consequently to employ all means
necessary to this end and avoid all things opposed to it? If this cannot be
denied, how can we imagine a state in which everything is lawful? Will all
things, even contraries and contradictories, equally advance this end?

Fifth, can a human being be in any state in which he is not bound to seek
God's glory and propose God to himself as his ultimate end? If this cannot be
said, do all human acts—just or unjust, honorable or dishonorable, equitable
or inequitable—have the same connection with God's glory and the human being's
highest good? Who will affirm this? By what right, then, can all things be said
to be equally lawful?

Sixth, the good which human beings in that state are bound to seek is either
one and the same, or diverse and manifold according to the number of human
beings placed in that state. The latter can be said with no reason, since all
have the same nature and the same—

[1674 logical printed p. 61 | IA leaf 79 | PDF p. 79]

—natural rule. If the former is said, that good must have another measure than
the sensual appetite, which is so varied and manifold.

Seventh, the notion of justice is so inscribed and comprehended in each
person's mind that no one, even if he lived alone after the manner of beasts,
could be ignorant of the force of right. Orestes can be one witness among many:
consciousness of his mother's murder drove him to madness as if with burning
brands.

Eighth, in that state parricide, patricide, indiscriminate intercourse, and the
like would not be crimes. Ninth, a human being in that state would lack all
religion and thus be independent of God, at least morally. Tenth, all the
arguments proving that natural law is implanted in human consciences establish
the contrary. By this natural law a human being has seeds of good and evil
inscribed in him and inborn sparks which he cannot extinguish completely and
which possess their force in whatever state he may be.

Next, the rule taken from Ulpian's definition of natural right—namely, that it
is what nature taught all animals—is not altogether safe. Although in
creatures there is some trace, likeness, or shadow of reason, or rather an
instinct or inclination common to them with human beings, through which they
do many things which a human performs by rational judgment, and perhaps even
do some things more agreeably to reason than certain human beings do, yet
right properly so called cannot be ascribed to them, since they lack reason.
How, then, can their actions, which arise from some propensity and inclination
rather than from any right wholly concerned with lawful and unlawful, describe
for us the true nature of natural right or indicate which things belong to it?
Indeed, those very propensities, insofar as they are natural and common to the
human being and brute, do not receive the name of right, but only insofar as
they assume the character of lawfulness or unlawfulness, obligation or
permission.

It is true that God aggravates human sin by this very comparison of human
beings with beasts whenever a person does something against those natural
inclinations common to him and brute animals, as may be seen in Isaiah 1:2,
Jeremiah 8:7, Romans 1:26, 2 Timothy 3:3, Jude 10, and frequently elsewhere.
But it can by no means be proved from this that the proper character of right
has a place in brute animals, since no reason distinguishing good from evil,
nor will or choice of one thing before another, inheres in them.

Connanus rightly says in *Commentary on the Law*, book 1, chapter 6, section
3: “It is regarded as absurd to join beasts with human beings in a society of
right because they possess certain likenesses of it in begetting and
educating. By that reasoning it would be necessary to make them participants
in piety and every virtue, because they have a certain likeness of these
things—and, in the opinion of some, a greater likeness than of right and
justice. We therefore do not grudgingly communicate to them the praise of all
other virtues except right and justice, because the other virtues are turned
toward oneself, while justice embraces those with whom one lives. But beasts
can have no such society among themselves or with a human being, and therefore
no communion of right. Thus we may call serpents prudent, horses brave, and
turtledoves chaste, but no living things except human beings just. For they do
whatever they do for their own sake, while justice performs its entire office
for others. Indeed, those virtues of which they are thought to possess a kind
of seed are neither true nor constant in them.”

Since they are not said to act by reason, so they are not taught by mind or
intelligence, but rather by craft or fear, as someone speaks.

[1674 logical printed p. 62 | IA leaf 80 | PDF p. 80]

Those to whom nothing can properly be commanded or enjoined are not capable
of right. But nothing is given to beasts by command. We know that there is in
them, as in all other creatures even those lacking sense, a certain
inclination or power directed in a definite way according to what agrees with
the nature and end of the things themselves. In this respect all can be said
to have a law imposed upon them, Psalm 148:6; Job 38:10–12; Jeremiah 33:20,
25. But properly no law is prescribed to them so that they are without excuse.

Beasts cannot do injury; therefore they are not the subject of right. The
consequence is perfectly clear, and the antecedent cannot be denied, for they
are not obligated to such duties under any penalty. It is true that God
commanded beasts also to be killed in a certain case, as in Leviticus 20:15–16
and Deuteronomy 13:15–16. But this no more proves that beasts were properly
punished than the destruction to which inanimate things also were devoted for
human sin in the latter passage proves that inanimate things were properly
punished. In those cases the killing of beasts was commanded more to deter
human beings from such wickedness and otherwise convict and punish them than
to punish the beasts themselves properly. Augustine, *Questions on Leviticus*,
question 74, and after him Thomas, I–II, question 105, article 2, reply 9, say
that cattle were ordered killed because, contaminated by such wickedness, they
kept alive the memory of the deed.

The goring ox was to be stoned, Exodus 21:28, not to exact punishment from the
ox but from its owner who negligently guarded it, and so that the beast would
not kill anyone again. Genesis 9:5, “I will require your blood at the hand of
every animal,” sufficiently declares how greatly God hates homicide, since he
wills even beasts which kill a human to be killed—not because they violated
any right, but because they were instruments of so great a crime. The command
was therefore given because of the horror of the deed.

Third, the device some invent to establish the matter does not satisfy:
namely, that all those things whose traces appear in brutes pertain to this
natural right. Traces of very many evils and vices appear in beasts, as do
traces of other actions which no one refers to this natural right. Therefore
there must be some other, prior rule by which those traces in brutes are to be
examined and according to which judgment is to be made concerning the acts.
The question then recurs concerning that prior rule. Hence this account cannot
satisfy us.

Furthermore, it would follow that religion toward God should by no means be
referred to natural right, because no trace of it at all is visible in brutes.
Lactantius says in *On the Wrath of God*, book 7: “If a likeness of all things
usually ascribed to the human being is found also in dumb animals, it appears
that religion alone is that of which no trace or suspicion can be found in
dumb animals. For proper justice belongs to religion, which no animal attains;
the human being alone commands and the rest have been subjected to him.” And
Cicero says in *Laws* that no animal except the human being has any knowledge
of God. Even if Pliny's account that elephants venerate the sun and moon were
true—which is scarcely credible—it would prove idolatry, rather than religion
toward God, to be of natural right.

Who, however, will deny that the internal religion by which each person
worships and venerates God is of natural right, since it is older than every
right or—

[1674 logical printed p. 63 | IA leaf 81 | PDF p. 81]

—at least as old? Was care for marriage, procreation, or the education of
offspring earlier than veneration of God himself, who created and governs all
created things? Is not this notion of God fixed in every mind? Must not this
religion toward God be numbered among the first things of nature?

The same can be said of obedience to parents, which is nearly as ancient as
marriage itself and the education of children, though it belongs only to human
beings; and also of liberty, which is not communicated to beasts. They have
displayed no example of our liberty and seem born for human use and service.

Nor will paternal, maternal, or similar intercourse belong to the right of
nature. Whatever ridiculous things may be related about camels fleeing
intercourse with their mothers and about the horse of a Scythian king which
was brought to intercourse with its covered mother and, when it finally saw
her, threw itself over a precipice—concerning which see Selden, *On Natural and
Gentile Right*, book 1, chapter 5—it certainly remains as Ovid sang in
*Metamorphoses*, book 10: “Other animals couple with no distinction. It is not
held shameful for a heifer to bear her father on her back; the horse's daughter
becomes his mate; the goat goes among the flocks he fathered; and the bird
conceives from the very seed by which she herself was conceived.”

Selden speaks best in the place cited: “Why, finally, are norms of human life
or examples of natural right to be sought from camels, bees, turtledoves,
storks, elephants, and certain other animals—even if it were granted that
these species, according to the cited authors, always shrink from certain
deeds, though the matter is often very doubtful—rather than from so many
commonwealths of brutes, or the countless herds of the rest, of which nothing
of the kind is reported? Or, more strongly, why from all these together, even
if as much were true of them all, when among them there is truly no trace of
lawful and unlawful, obligation and will? There is no reason at all why the
customary acts of any one species should be preferred in this inquiry to the
acts of another—and we know these to differ in many ways—much less why, as
some do, we should go hunting for natural rights from a few very obscure
examples of individuals. This would plainly be just as if one wished to learn
the constant causes and effects of natural things from various prodigies and
omens.”

The claim that natural right regards human beings only in a solitary state
and outside political society must be rejected in the same way. It is indeed
true, as shown above, that solitary human beings living in the countryside
with spouses and children are subject to natural right. But outside that state
the same natural right retains its force. Duties are changed—or rather,
different duties are required from a person when he forms part of a city than
were required when he formed only part of a family—but natural right also
exerts its force here. Nature, which taught a person natural duties toward
God, himself, spouse, and children, teaches how he ought to conduct himself in
a larger society toward God, himself, and neighbors, whether kindred,
relations, or other nearby persons. Nature, which taught the human being, who
is a sociable creature—

[1674 logical printed p. 64 | IA leaf 82 | PDF p. 82]

—to associate with others both to avoid solitude and to provide for his safety
and other necessities, also taught that even in that state piety must be
exercised toward God, as is confirmed by the example of almost all nations;
and justice toward others, namely by keeping fidelity in contracts, injuring
no one by deed or word, and doing to others the same things one wished done to
him by them, according to Christ's saying in Matthew 7:12 and Luke 6:31. From
this saying Christ indeed draws that part of natural right which embraces
duties toward one's neighbor.

Fifth, when the jurists say that primeval gentile right—which is the same as
natural right—is what natural reason establishes among all human beings and
what is equally observed among all nations, they seem to imply that judgment
concerning natural right must be made from the customs and use of all, or at
least most, nations. Thus Cicero says, “The consent of all nations must be
regarded as the law of nature.” But this rule is not sufficiently firm. How
will the use or customs of absolutely all nations be known to us? How will we
learn how many and what all those nations are? Some ancients speak of seventy
in all, but who can demonstrate with certainty which they are and whether
their number has not increased since that time? Much less can anyone clearly
delineate all their customs.

If the rule is restricted only to known nations, we shall derive nothing
certain, because not a few are found to be savage. According to Aristotle,
*Politics*, book 8, chapter 4, some, such as the Achaeans and Heniochi, were
accustomed to kill and eat human beings. Even if it is further limited to the
more civilized nations which use laws, who does not know that many naturally
good things are not commanded by civil laws? Nature commands fidelity to be
kept, and this is the foundation of justice, as Cicero writes in *On Duties*,
book 1; yet many examples can be given in which civil laws do not compel human
beings to keep faith. General justice, the mother and parent of every virtue,
desires constancy and truth in all our words and deeds. Do political laws
require all this?

Connanus says in book 1, chapter 6: “For perfect and consummate justice it is
not enough that we injure no one unless we also increase the advantages of,
and bind by our benefits, those whom we can. Nor is it enough that we return
another's property unless we do it freely and voluntarily. But civil and
gentile right regards the outcome of the matter and establishes no punishment
for thoughts, however evil and wicked, if they lack effect. Their end is the
preservation of human society; and unless that is injured by a deed, neither
an evil mind nor an evil and criminal spirit can harm it. Thus we see what is
done deceitfully and maliciously go unpunished by the laws and decree not only
of civil societies but of all nations unless fraud is joined to guile—that
is, unless guile brings fraud and loss upon another. Yet if we examine these
things by nature's weights and recall them to the rule of general justice,
those who sin in intention are as guilty as those who sin in effect. The
simplicity, purity, and holiness of nature requires one thing, the custom of
nations and cities another. The former requires you to benefit each person if
you can, think nothing impious or criminal, and fulfill every promise not
joined with shame. The right of nations or civil right requires only that you
not retain another's property, do nothing criminal which harms by deed or
example, and not break faith to another's destruction.”

Is it not also certain, and observed by most, that scarcely any head of
natural right exists which has not been violated by some nations, by their
customs, and indeed by their laws?

[1674 logical printed p. 65 | IA leaf 83 | PDF p. 83]

See Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book 1, chapter 4, and book 4,
chapters 15–17.

“Moreover,” says Selden in the place cited, book 1, chapter 6, page 80, “it
cannot be unknown to anyone who examines the celebrated customs and
institutions of the Persians, Assyrians, Egyptians, Greeks, Romans, and other
ancient nations that peoples and nations embraced customs sometimes similar
to one another and sometimes altogether contrary—even concerning matters in
which natural or common human right is chiefly occupied, such as intercourse
with blood relatives, exposure of children, human slaughter, and marriages—not
so much through contemplation of just and unjust, lawful and unlawful, or good
and evil as through what is contained under the names pleasant and unpleasant,
useful and useless; and that they likewise changed, revoked, and innovated
them at pleasure and from blind, rash lust, that mistress of the mind.”

From this very thing, as the very celebrated Selden observes in the same
place, Carneades and others, as Connanus notes in book 1, chapter 1, section 3,
contended that there is no natural right, but that right comes solely from
opinion and not from nature: it is not the same among all, but varies with
customs and is often changed among the same people at different times; there
are so many and frequent abrogations, derogations, and substitutions of right,
and so many conflicts and disagreements of laws with one another. Selden says
this would certainly seem sufficiently agreeable to reason if the measure of
natural right were sought only from the customs and use of nations. A
philosopher or jurist would approach the matter no less wrongly and fruitlessly
than a physician who prescribed things conducive either to preserving health
or curing diseases by what was to be learned from common human use or from the
solemn luxury of banquets. Just as regard is there had for pleasure, luxury,
and pomp, not for protection or cure—which must be sought from foods and
medicines agreeing with their own nature, not that of the caterer or cook—so
in the customs and institutions of many nations it is customary to regard not
so much the simplicity of natural right as lust itself, that almost mother of
the just and equitable.

Who does not know that the customs and usages of various nations likewise
vary even in the same matters we number among natural things? Among the
Lacedaemonians thefts of food were permitted which elsewhere were always
prohibited. The Cilicians lived by plunder, the Massagetae held wives in
common, and the Persians once married their daughters—to mention no more
examples. Perhaps someone will say these were not the more civilized nations.
I would readily concede it and would say that it thereby appears clearly that
the customs or practices of nations are by no means a certain rule from which
we can judge this natural right. For it is evident that the nations themselves
and their customs must be judged by some other rule, and must be called more
or less civilized according to their agreement or disagreement with it.

Who also does not know how great the force of custom is, so that it is regarded
as a second nature? As it influences human manners by its own force, it is
thought to be from nature. No right or power in governing human beings is more
potent than custom when it has acquired strength by antiquity; it is therefore
called king of all, because it alone reigns, rules, commands, and forbids. It
is well known that Darius once asked the Greeks under his rule—

[1674 logical printed p. 66 | IA leaf 84 | PDF p. 84]

—for what sum of money they would be induced to eat the bodies of their dead
parents after the Indian manner, and received the answer that no money could
induce them to do it. He likewise asked the Indians for what sum they could be
induced to burn their dead parents after the Greek manner, and received the
answer that they would sooner suffer the most extreme things.

Everyone has a natural judgment concerning a benefit received. One who burns
with desire for his own preservation cannot fail to think of the duty to be
shown to the person by whom he was preserved when destitute of every means.
But, I ask, among how many or what nations is such a thing confirmed by laws?
This can be established by many other examples. Seneca says in *On Anger*,
book 2: “Who is he who professes himself innocent by every law? Even if it is
so, how narrow is innocence which is merely good according to law! How much
more widely does the scope of duties extend than the rule of right! How many
things do piety, humanity, liberality, justice, and fidelity require! All are
outside the public tables. But we cannot satisfy even that most narrow formula
of innocence. Some things we have done, some thought, some wished, and some
favored; in certain things we are innocent because we did not succeed.”

Connanus, book 1, chapter 7, section 7, gives an example in which civil right
seems greatly to conflict with natural right, not only in that part concerning
honor and reputation, but also in that which contains the force of equitable
and inequitable: “Usucaption and prescription transfer a thing from its just
owners to strangers, which is most inequitable if we are willing to hear the
voice of nature, which, while it commands that each receive his own, chiefly
commands that we injure no one. Nature,” he says shortly before, “commands us
to live honorably; the honor of life consists in constancy of words and deeds
and also integrity and purity of thought and mind. But the right of nations
did not reduce all these things to necessity; it only prohibited anyone from
injuring another. Thus it established no punishment for thought and exacted
fidelity not in every promise, but in those in which, besides words, earnest
money or something of the kind intervened, or which rested upon the authority
of the contracts we call nominate. Civil right afterward devised the bond of
stipulation, in which it added much to gentile and natural right: to gentile
right, because it introduced an obligation of words which did not exist by
that right except in nominate contracts; to natural right, because even if a
stipulation is fraudulent and harmful to the promisor it nevertheless binds
him. There is no room for repentance, even if that repentance brings no
disadvantage to the stipulator but perhaps diminishes something of his profit,
which was otherwise by natural right.”

Selden says in the place cited that it is a true argument from the posterior
to draw sometimes, and not inopportunely, from the customs of the more
civilized and illustrious nations—as we unanimously dignify them with these
names—as from the votes of approved men, especially when inquiry concerns
certain heads of permissive natural right. From this it becomes manifest what
they judged, or at least seemed to judge, concerning the matters about which
their customs, as generally permitted or lawful, are occupied. For whatever
human beings agreed was permitted by any right, they must have judged, or
pretended to judge, to be permitted also by natural right if they had any
reason for it, and that nature did not obligate to the contrary—that the thing
was not restricted by any prohibition of universal nature, which is constant
with itself. Thus those who dispute or inquire concerning that permissive
right sometimes collect the votes of other nations. Yet meanwhile what those
same nations established concerning some heads of obligatory natural right,
or concerning the simple reason and universal use of natural permission—

[1674 logical printed p. 67 | IA leaf 85 | PDF p. 85]

—cannot even be adequately conjectured from them. For although teachers of
the disciplines, formed differently, sometimes assert in scholastic and
theoretical disputations, and perhaps even in forensic ones, that this or that
head belongs to natural right among nations of this kind, nevertheless in
conducting and deciding public affairs every nation we know uses either only
its own civil and positive right, or the right it has with other nations with
which it has treaties, commerce, or wars, or its own right—whether imperative
or intervening—which varies according to circumstances. It is clothed with
natural right no otherwise than with overlaid garments. In each class certain
natural rights are not rarely allowed to be exercised, but they are so mixed,
involved, and united into one mass, so to speak, with civil, positive, and
imperative right that no mortal can distinguish the latter from the former
solely from the nations' own use or customs and derive from them their opinion
on the matter. We very frequently see numerous civil and positive formulas of
both obligation and permission added to natural ones, whatever these may
finally have been.

Sixth, some hold that our natural reason is the rule and measuring line of
this natural right. Thus they hold that natural right denotes rules or certain
common principles of honorableness to the knowledge of which a human being is
carried, as it were, by the weight of rational nature and rushes by a certain
impulse if he examines himself and searches out his reason. They hold nature,
that is, right reason, to be the fountain of every law and custom and the end
to which they are referred. Hence Cicero says in *Laws* that “there is one
right by which human society is bound and which one law establishes, which
law is right reason in commanding and forbidding.” Afterward: “We can
distinguish a good law from an evil by no other than the natural norm. Not only
right and injury are judged by nature, but altogether all honorable and
shameful things. For common understanding makes things known to us and begins
them in our minds, so that honorable things are placed in virtue and shameful
things in vices.”

It is indeed true that there are certain practical principles so clear and
evident, and as it were known through themselves, that human reason cannot be
altogether ignorant of them. They are so intimately joined with human nature
or reason that, at least as to the habit, the most perverse person can never
extinguish them, as is clearly evident from Romans 1. Yet as to practice
conformed to these principles, it is sufficiently established that it can
sometimes be removed altogether. Human reason not altogether corrupted and
vitiated by evil custom can also readily draw out certain other things
proximately deduced from these first principles. But not a few others which
belong to this right must be deduced from them more remotely, and in discerning
these human reason—at least that of everyone—is not keen and ready. Thus human
reason, now depraved in many ways, will be an uncertain rule concerning these.

This is further evident from the various and conflicting opinions of the
philosophers concerning good and evil, and from the many sects of philosophers
who professed to order all things according to the dictate of reason. Each
sect defended its own moral principles and rejected and condemned the dogmas
of the others.

How many absurd and utterly unlawful things condemned by natural right were
regarded by some as most lawful and approved? Maternal intercourse, and that
of brothers with sisters, was lawful to Zeno, Chrysippus, and the Persian
Magi; among some, intercourse between males was thought lawful. Thus theft,
sacrilege, and adultery were lawful to the philosopher Theodorus—

[1674 logical printed p. 68 | IA leaf 86 | PDF p. 86]

—the Philosopher. Plato wished to introduce community of wives, almost
promiscuous intercourse, and universal community of goods. At Sparta the
promiscuous use of wives was confirmed by law. I pass over the opinion of
Carneades mentioned above, which Archelaus and Aristippus also defended,
according to Selden and others.

Hence it appears that this reason is not a sufficiently firm norm of right.
One person, judging according to it as he supposes, holds these to be the
truest principles of morals and the conclusions inferred from them legitimate
and firm. Another, who seems no less possessed of reason and likewise follows
its dictate, lays down far different if not contrary principles, deduces far
different conclusions from the principles laid down, or at least does not
acknowledge the former person's conclusions as so clear, but remains doubtful
and does not approve them by his consent. Indeed, some do not blush to affirm
that there is no rule of actions besides force, as Brennus did formerly and
Hobbes with his followers does now.

Perhaps someone will say such people do not follow the dictate of right
reason. I would not deny it. But I wish further to ask how we can know and
judge which of the two—the affirmer or denier—misuses right reason? Some prior
rule must necessarily be given according to which judgment is made concerning
these various, distinct, or contrary opinions. That prior rule, whatever and
of whatever kind it is, will be a safer and far more excellent norm and
measuring line of right.

It is certain that natural right is a perpetual and invariable law and has the
force not only of directing but also of obligating. The dictate of reason only
persuades or directs with regard to circumstances, occasions, and the like;
when these are changed it persuades to new things and sometimes even to
contrary and worse ones.

Who does not know that the perverse blindness of the human mind is now so
great that it often fails to know even the best-known things and judges most
wickedly and pronounces absurdly concerning acts about which others possess a
right and sound judgment? Connanus says in book 1, chapter 1, section 6: “Among
human beings some see more clearly, some hear more distinctly, and others
smell more keenly; scarcely anyone does not possess something peculiar in his
senses. Yet the same and single force of senses is not inborn in all, but
prevails in each according to the measure of its faculty. Similarly, the human
mind is often itself blinded by many errors and cannot always distinguish true
from false. It is generally deceived in this not so much by uncertainty in the
things themselves, of which there is none, as by weakness of judgment and
ignorance. It must not therefore be thought that nature is changed; though it
is one and simple, it is diverse and manifold to minds differently affected.
Those who have greater ability and learning judge everything more rightly;
those deficient in both or either stumble very often. Right itself is naked
and open in itself, consistent with itself, and defined from the very beginning
of its kind by eternal law; but it is difficult and arduous to accommodate it
to the uses and customs of human beings, which are various and uncertain.
Hence judgment concerning it is not the same, and reason dictates one thing
to one person and another to another. No one ought to marvel that this happens
in matters so obscure and remote from certainty, since the senses themselves,
whose judgments are so clear and certain, very often err.” I know that he
speaks here particularly of the cause of various and distinct civil laws, but
his reasons can well be applied here, as is evident.

Furthermore, natural reason only discerns and perceives—even when most right—

[1674 logical printed p. 69 | IA leaf 87 | PDF p. 87]

—what is just or unjust as its object concerning which it judges. What is just
or unjust, good or evil, is such before reason perceives it: reason perceives
it because it is such; it is not such because reason perceives it. Finally,
there are certain things concerning human beings themselves about which, even
after so many disputations and observations, it is still doubted what the law
of nature pronounces.

Seventh, the rule some assign—that those things are of natural right which are
immutable—is not sufficiently safe unless it is rightly explained. We heard
Connanus above say that the right of nations, which is a part of natural right
and contained under its name because unanimous agreement of all nations in
every matter, confirmed gradually and tacitly not by institutions or laws but
by customs, is deemed a law of nature, can be changed in some measure by human
beings. “Some peoples,” he says, “are still said not to know dominion over
things, all things being common among them. If anyone prohibited purchase or
lease and abolished altogether every use of money by law, I do not see what
inequity that law would possess, since it would detract something from the
advantage of its citizens, but nothing at all from that simple and equal right
of nature. The same can much more be said of not consigning captives to
servitude, which has now been abrogated by Christian customs.”

By this reasoning he refutes the opinion of those who thought there was no
natural right because they saw this gentile right, which is also customarily
called natural, changed, although nature is stable, firm, and free from all
change. He confirms his statements by Aristotle's authority. Having said that
legal right consists of matters concerning which at the beginning it made no
difference whether they were done thus or otherwise, Aristotle adds: “Some
think all things are of this kind, since what is natural is immutable and has
the same force everywhere, just as fire burns both here and among the
Persians. But we see those rights changed. Yet it is not altogether so, but in
a certain respect—although among the gods perhaps nothing is of this kind,
among us certainly some things implanted by nature are changed, though not
all; nevertheless, one thing is established by nature and another not by
nature.”

I judge that a distinction must here be made between things which are *purely
natural* and those which should rather be called *mixed natural*. The latter
properly pertain to secondary gentile right. Although they can be called
natural insofar as they contain something derived from nature and approach,
though more remotely, nature or the first natural principles, nevertheless
they have many things mixed or intimately joined with them which draw their
origin from mutual human consent; therefore they can be called mixed natural
things. As to these, it can be conceded that human beings can change them when
the advantage and utility of a people require it, as the examples Connanus
adduced prove.

But as to purely natural things, I do not see how human beings can change
them. They pertain to obligatory natural right, and this obligatory right is
really the same as the law of nature. God alone is the author of the law of
nature, and from him alone that law possesses all its obligating force. Who
will say a legislator's law can be changed by the subject? By what reasoning
will it be proved that human beings have power to change any law of God, the
supreme Legislator?

Nevertheless, although none of the things reduced to pure natural right can
be changed in any way by a human being, we know that some of them have been
changed by God—whether the change was made by abrogation of law—

[1674 logical printed p. 70 | IA leaf 88 | PDF p. 88]

—derogation, interpretation, equitable relaxation, mere change of the matter,
alteration of circumstances, or whatever other name the change receives. God
commanded Abraham to sacrifice his son Isaac, permitted Adam's sons to marry
their sisters, and commanded the Israelites to despoil the Egyptians. Hence,
I say, it is established that natural right was changed by the good pleasure
of God the Legislator alone. The very act which, if done by anyone else without
God's command, would have violated this natural right—and which, if committed
at another time by the same persons without God's command, would have been by
no means lawful—was here lawful and indeed praiseworthy, since commanded by
God, the Author of nature and supreme Lord.

Natural things are indeed often called immutable. Thus Justinian, *Institutes*,
*On Natural Right*, in the section *But Natural*: “Natural rights, which are
observed equally among all nations, having been established by a certain
divine providence, remain firm and immutable.” Very many others say the same.
But this is chiefly true with respect to human beings, not with respect to God,
who can change the grounds of things at pleasure and remove the obligation
here and now, as has often been declared above.

Having rejected these methods as by no means suitable for indicating this
natural right to us all, it remains that we indicate briefly how we can come
to knowledge of it.

Since this natural right is a right assigned by God himself to the human race
in its very creation, knowledge of which was inscribed and deeply impressed
upon the human mind, a human being in the original state could readily perceive
and recognize both the general principles and the conclusions to be gathered
from them and applied particularly to this or that case. He did this by
perceiving and seeing the order of things established by God and observing the
other internal and external objects from which this right is drawn.

It is now certain, however, that the natural light with which we were imbued
and endowed in the primeval state has been extinguished in great part, and our
reason depraved, corrupted, and vitiated in many ways. Thus we are blind in
the very light and perceive even the most obvious things quite indistinctly
and confusedly. Especially do the things which most nearly concern God's
worship and our souls escape the sight of our mind's eyes, though when more
distinctly revealed, confirmed, and explained they can be known in some
measure, and even then not without the greatest labor as to many things.

In the original state these things were most readily understood from the book
of nature, the entire order of things, the relation of God to the human being
and of the human being to God, and other objects of that kind which it is not
necessary to enumerate here. Now no distinct, evident, or saving knowledge of
all of them can be possessed without the special aid of the Spirit leading us
through the word revealed in Christ into all truth. It is therefore manifest
that we must go to the Scriptures, the most perfect rule of morals as well as
faith, and draw from them, under the Spirit's guidance and teaching, all
knowledge necessary to be known concerning the divine will: both the things
called natural right with respect to God and those which are of positive right
but are natural and moral to us.

From Holy Scripture, therefore, as the most perfect and certain summary of
all, we can recognize which precepts are universal, obligate all, and remain
perpetually in force, and which do not; or which are of natural—

[1674 logical printed p. 71 | IA leaf 89 | PDF p. 89]

—or moral right, because they are indicated everywhere there. All are reduced
summarily to the ten heads we call the Decalogue. Everyone concedes that all
its precepts except the fourth and second are of natural right. As to the
fourth, God willing, we shall afterward prove both that it is natural right
for us and that it obligates us no less than the others. Indeed, at least some
of the precepts contained in the second table bind and morally obligate us.
As to the second, our theologians abundantly prove against the Papists that it
is of natural right.

No one who remembers that we can come to a right knowledge of our duty in no
other way will doubt that we must necessarily recur to Scripture to see our
present obligation to moral duties. We hate the things which ought to be loved
and most conduce to our eternal salvation, Colossians 1:21; Amos 5:10; Micah
3:2; John 15:18, 24–25; 17:14. Our corrupt affections exercise dominion and we
are carried to destruction, indeed rush willingly into it, Galatians 5:24;
Colossians 3:2, 5; Ephesians 2:3. Our reason is so covered with darkness that
we are said to be and walk in darkness, indeed to be under the power of
darkness, Colossians 1:13; 1 Thessalonians 5:4–5; Matthew 6:23; Luke 11:34;
Ecclesiastes 2:14; John 8:12; 12:35, 46; 1 John 2:9, 11; 1 Peter 2:9; Acts
26:18; Job 1:5.

Perverse education and corrupt customs daily increase this perverse ignorance
and apostasy from our highest good. Even if we granted that our natural reason
could understand the things of God—which both Scripture denies, 1 Corinthians
2:6–7, 11, 14; 1:18–21, 23, and daily experience proves false—who does not
know that many are so carried away by their affections that they reject the
guidance of natural reason? Romans 1:26–27; 1 Corinthians 11:14; 2 Peter 2:12;
Jude 10.

What else sounds in Cotta's complaint in Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*,
book 3, page 103? “It seems to me that the use of reason in the greatest
wickedness has been employed with no small measure of reason. Not only is the
stage filled with these crimes, but much more is common life: each person's
house feels them, the forum, senate-house, field, allies, and provinces feel
them. Just as one acts rightly by reason, so one sins by reason. The former is
done by few and rarely; the latter often and by many, so that it would have
been better if no reason at all had been given us by the immortal gods than
that it should be given with such destruction. Therefore, if the divine mind
and will cared for human beings by giving them reason, it cared only for those
whom it endowed with good reason, whom we see—if there are any—to be few,” and
so forth.

Seneca's saying in epistle 42 confirms the same: “A good man can neither be
made nor understood so quickly. Perhaps that other Phoenix is born once in
five hundred years. Nor is it wonderful that great things are produced at a
long interval; fortune often produces mediocre and common things in a crowd.”
But one who knew what a good man was would not yet believe himself to be one,
and perhaps would despair that he could become one.

Even that book of nature which our reason consults and reads can now reveal
God's will only obscurely to us unless the Spirit, teaching and leading us,
makes us fit to understand the things said there. From it we shall never learn
many things supremely necessary without supernatural illumination. What, I
ask, will teach us the origin of our natural depravity and ignorance? What
method of amending all these things will it delineate for us? What certain
means of reconciliation will it prescribe, and what will it teach us about
the true worship of God? Will it dictate anything about our true redemption—

[1674 logical printed p. 72 | IA leaf 90 | PDF p. 90]

—or indicate any duty to be performed in a right and due manner? How vain,
therefore, to resort here in order to know what is moral for us and what is
not! No one who has read or heard their writings and opinions can fail to know
how greatly even those regarded as the wisest erred when they unfolded only
this book of nature. Some of the more prudent thought there was some
retribution of actions after death, but it is everywhere sufficiently evident
how ineffective this opinion was to correct their morals and lead them to
follow even right reason in moral matters. Though some taught many things
well, it is abundantly evident how contrary their practice was.

Cicero's saying in *Tusculan Disputations*, book 2, page 252, sufficiently
confirms this: “Do you think these same things prevail among the very persons
by whom they were invented, disputed, and written, except very few? How many
philosophers are found so morally formed and so constituted in mind and life
as reason requires? Who regards his teaching not as an ostentation of
knowledge but as a law of life? Who obeys himself and submits to his own
decrees? One may see some of such levity and boasting that it would have been
better for them not to have learned; others greedy for money, some for glory,
and many slaves of lusts, so that their speech conflicts wonderfully with
their life—which seems to me most shameful.”

Who will say that those who were ignorant of themselves and of natural
depravity are suitable judges of divine law? “You err,” says Seneca, “if you
think vices are born with you; they came afterward and were introduced.” We
must therefore go “to the law and to the testimony,” Isaiah 8:20, if we wish
to be certain of the duty prescribed to us by God.

But because Scripture contains other things besides those of natural right,
a difficulty will remain. Since Scripture presents both equally to us, our
mind will remain doubtful and unable sufficiently to distinguish the latter
from the former unless some rule is given.

It must therefore be said that things of natural right are distinguished from
others by this mark: they possess a certain fittingness and agreement with
rational nature and thus agree perfectly with everyone's nature when it is
not vitiated or corrupt. Scripture frequently indicates this when it speaks of
such things. It confirms it when it limits other precepts to a predetermined
time and at length declares them abolished; and when it declares them given to
some particular person, family, or nation rather than to the whole human race.
For in this way it sufficiently testifies that they do not agree with rational
nature as such; otherwise they would be common to all human beings at every
time. The rational soul of everyone is the same at every time, and actions
which possess this proportionate fittingness and agreement with rational
nature always possess it and possess it with the rational nature of every
human being without distinction.

It is certain that our reason illuminated by Scripture's light can perceive
more things commanded by the law of nature than if it lacked this aid. From
the polytheism and other wicked customs of the pagans who professed to live
according to this law, it is sufficiently evident how little this natural
light, when destitute of Scripture's light, profits in perceiving all the
requirements of the law of nature. Scripture will therefore be a much clearer,
more distinct, fuller, and plainer rule than our natural light, by which we can
know what the law of nature requires and does not require. In this corrupt
state, that law can be recognized more by its force of condemning than of
directing. Dr. Hoornbeeck speaks excellently in *Practical Theology*, book 5,
chapter 1: “In nature indeed—

[1674 logical printed p. 73 | IA leaf 91 | PDF p. 91 | ends before CAP. VI]

—God has also indicated most fully and perfectly in Holy Scripture what is
good. To this we must now have recourse altogether, after reason has been
obscured by the fall and greatly depraved. Since the light of Scripture has
arisen, it is fruitless afterward to place such trust in the darkness of
reason that we are instructed by it rather than Scripture concerning what is
just. It would be otherwise if reason or nature had remained excellent. But
now, since we have fallen into such a state that we neither see rightly nor
will what is right, this very reason must be taught from elsewhere lest it err
in a work so necessary.” See what follows.
