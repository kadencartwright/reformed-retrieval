QUESTION XV

ON THE VULGATE VERSION

Is the Vulgate Version authentic? We deny, against the Roman Catholics.

I. The question is not whether the Vulgate Version is useful and sometimes
even renders the truth exceedingly well, which no one denies. Nor is it
whether it is ancient and was used in the church for a long time, which is
also acknowledged by everyone. Rather, the question is whether it possesses
authentic truth, so that it is to be made equal to the original texts and
preferred to all other versions. We deny this; the Roman Catholics affirm it
from the Council of Trent, session 4, decree 1: “If anyone does not receive as
sacred and canonical these books in their entirety, with all their parts, as
they have customarily been read in the Catholic Church and are contained in
the Old Vulgate Edition, let him be anathema.” And shortly afterward: “Moreover,
the same Holy Synod, considering that no small benefit can accrue to the
church of God if it becomes known which one of all the Latin editions of the
sacred books in circulation is to be held as authentic, ordains and declares
that this same old and common edition, which has been approved in the church
itself by the long use of so many centuries, is to be held as authentic in
public readings,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 119 | IA leaf 173 | PDF p. 173]

sermons, and expositions, and that no one may dare or presume to reject it on
any pretext.”

II. It is true that the Roman Catholics hold differing opinions about the
meaning of the council's canon. Some maintain that no comparison was made
between this version and the original text, but only between it and the other
Latin versions in circulation. These include Bellarmine, Serarius, Salmerón,
Mariana, and very many others, who judge that it can even be corrected and
emended from the original texts. Others, however, maintain that it was
declared authentic absolutely, so that no better version can be given, that it
is to be preferred to all editions in any language whatsoever, and indeed
that the original codices, as corrupt, are to be emended from it. These
include Cano, Valencia, Gordon, Gretser, Suárez, and others. Yet anyone who
weighed the words of the canon more carefully would readily recognize that
they incline toward the latter opinion. For if it cannot be rejected on any
pretext, then neither can it be rejected on the pretext of the Hebrew codex,
as Hart openly pronounced in his conference with Rainolds. Hence Cardinal
Ximénez, in the preface to the Complutensian Bible, declares that he placed the
Latin version in the middle between the Hebrew and the Greek, like two thieves
on either side, with Jesus in the middle—that is, the Roman or Latin church.
Nor does Mariana win credence from his associates when, in his book on the
Vulgate Version, he says that it possesses no less authority than the original
texts where it agrees with them, but where it differs it must be excused.
Accordingly, the Expurgatory Index struck out the words “where it agrees with
the original texts.” And Luis de Tena, *Introduction to Scripture*, book 1,
distinction 6, section 3, argues against Mariana: “If the Vulgate were
authentic,” he says, “insofar as it agrees with the original texts, and faulty
when it differs, then it would not be authentic absolutely. Nor does it
receive, so far as we are concerned, greater certainty through the Tridentine
decree than it possessed before, since even before this decree it possessed
authority under the aforesaid condition, namely, agreement with the original
texts. Therefore, if the Council of Trent so decreed, it defined nothing new,
but the dispute remained as it was before.”

[Marginal heading: The Vulgate Version is not authentic.]

III. We, on the other hand, although we esteem the Vulgate Version as ancient,
nevertheless deny that it is authentic. 1. It was produced by human industry
and does not have a God-inspired author (θεόπνευστον), which an authentic
edition requires. For whether its author is held to be Jerome, as the Roman
Catholics maintain, or someone else before Jerome who composed the version
called the Itala and the Vulgate, or Sixtus V and Clement VIII, who corrected
in many ways the old version used in the church, none of them was God-inspired
(θεόπνευστος).

IV. 2. It was authentic neither before the council's decree nor after it. It
was not authentic before, because it contained many errors which various
Roman Catholics freely censure: Nicholas of Lyra, Paul of Burgos, Driedo,
Jerónimo de Oleastro, Cajetan, and others—especially Isidore Clarius, who says
that he observed eighty thousand errors in the Latin Vulgate. Sixtus of Siena,
at the end of book 8 of his *Bibliotheca*, says: “We too candidly confess that
many errors were emended by Jerome in the old translation and that, likewise,
in this new edition of ours there are found some mistakes, solecisms,
barbarisms, many things rendered with little suitability and in less than good
Latin, things interpreted obscurely and ambiguously, as well as some things
added, others omitted, and some transposed, altered, and corrupted by the
fault of copyists—things which Pagnino, Oleastro, Vatablus, and Cajetan judged
in their interpretations and expositions.” Certainly, if Pope Leo X had
previously judged it authentic, he would not have commissioned Sante Pagnini
of Lucca to prepare a new version because he had seen that the celebrated
version of Jerome, through human carelessness and the injury of time, had in
great part either perished or been corrupted, as Sixtus of Siena reports in
book 4. Nor can it be called authentic after the council, because the council
could not make authentic what was not authentic before. For just as it cannot
make a noncanonical book canonical but can only declare it so, neither can it
make a version authentic. This belongs to God alone,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 120 | IA leaf 174 | PDF p. 174]

who can confer divine authority upon any writing. The council can only declare
that a version is faithful and conforms to its original text; or, if errors
have crept in, correct them and command its use in the public offices of the
church.

V. 3. In many places it departs from the original texts. Clement VIII concedes
this concerning the Sixtine edition, for although it had been pronounced
authentic by the council and, in Sixtus's judgment, most thoroughly emended,
Clement nevertheless undertook to emend it. Two years later he brought it back
to the anvil, restored some things expunged by Sixtus, and altered and
corrected many things. This is evident from the comparison of copies in Thomas
James's *Bellum Papale*, where, in addition to innumerable variations, he
produced nearly two thousand readings which Sixtus had confirmed by apostolic
authority against the Hebrew and Greek truth, but which Clement recast by the
same authority and brought back into conformity with the original texts.
These cannot be ascribed to a fault of the press, as Clement alleges. For who
would believe that thousands of printing errors crept into an edition which
Sixtus had prepared with such great diligence? The preface itself also teaches
that the Clementine edition, which Clement ordered to be regarded as authentic
after the Sixtine, abounds in various faults: “Therefore, Christian reader,” it
says, “with the approval of the same pope, receive the Vulgate edition of Holy
Scripture, corrected with as much diligence as was possible. Although it is
difficult, in view of human weakness, to affirm that it is perfect in every
respect, there is no room to doubt that it is more emended and purer than all
the others which have appeared up to this time.” For if it is difficult to
affirm that it is perfect in every respect, and it was only purer than all
those which had appeared up to that time, then it is not denied that more
correct editions can appear thereafter, nor could the council have called it
the most thoroughly emended. But this very thing appears more clearly in what
follows, where it is expressly said that in the old Vulgate translation of the
Bible some things appeared to require alteration but were deliberately left
unchanged. Bellarmine, who had been among the correctors, does not conceal this
either. In a letter written to Lucas Brugensis, which he himself mentions, he
says: “I would have you know that the Vulgate Bible was not corrected by us
with the utmost accuracy, for with deliberate intent and for just reasons we
passed over many things which seemed to require correction.”

VI. 4. Many Roman Catholics long ago acknowledged that the Vulgate Version was
filled with various errors: Erasmus, Valla, Pagnino, Cajetan, Oleastro,
Forerius, and Sixtus of Siena. Various celebrated interpreters confess it even
today: Salmerón, Bonfrère, Serarius, Masius, Muys, and very many other most
learned men, who repeatedly appeal from it to the original texts.

VII. 5. Many passages have been wrongly rendered and have furnished either an
occasion or support for the gravest errors. Thus Genesis 3:15 has “she shall
crush,” so that it may be referred to the Blessed Virgin, although the
original reads הוא, “it,” namely, the seed. Genesis 14:18 has “For he was a
priest” instead of “And he was”; and Genesis 48:16 has “let my name be invoked
over them” instead of “let my name be called in them.” Psalm 99:5 has “Worship
his footstool” instead of “worship at the footstool,” if the ark is meant, or
“in the footstool,” that is, in the temple, which is his footstool.
Ecclesiastes 9:2 has “all things are kept uncertain for the future” instead of
“all things are before them.” John 14:26 has “he will suggest to you all things
whatsoever I shall say to you,” in order to insinuate unwritten (ἀγράφους)
apostolic traditions. But the Greek reads otherwise, πάντα ἃ εἶπον ὑμῖν, “all
things which I said to you.” Hebrews 11:21 has “worshiped the top of the rod,”
contrary to Paul's meaning, προσεκύνησε ἐπὶ τὸ ἄκρον, “worshiped upon the
top of the staff,” that is, as Beza translates it, “leaning on his staff.”
Hebrews 13:16 has “by such sacrifices God is merited” instead of “is pleased.”
First Corinthians 15:10 has “not I, but the grace of God with me” instead of
“which is with me.” Other passages can be added in which it errs either by
subtraction or addition. Thus, after the words of Romans 11:6, “But if by
grace, then it is no more of works; otherwise grace is no more grace,” an
entire sentence found in the Greek is absent: “But if of works, then it is no
more grace; otherwise work is no more work.” In Matthew 6:4, 6, and 18, the
words “in

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 121 | IA leaf 175 | PDF p. 175]

public” which occur in the Greek are absent three times. In chapter 15:8 the
words “this people draws near to me with its mouth” are absent. In chapter
20:22 the words “and to be baptized with the baptism with which I am
baptized?” are absent. In 1 Corinthians 6:20, after “in your body,” the words
“and in your spirit, which are God's” are absent. It also errs more than once
by addition. Luke 10:1 has “he appointed also other seventy-two,” but in the
Greek it is ἑτέρους ἑβδομήκοντα, “other seventy.” Acts 9:29 has “he also
spoke to the Gentiles and disputed with the Greeks” instead of “he spoke and
contended against the Greeks.” Romans 4 has “from works of the law,” while the
Greek has only “works.” There are more things of this kind which readers can
encounter. See further Whitaker, Chamier, Amama, James, and other writers of
ours who have noted the errors of this version.

VIII. 6. Whatever that version may be which they maintain was composed partly
from the old version, called the *Itala* by Augustine in *On Christian
Doctrine*, book 2, chapter 18, and the Vulgate by Jerome himself on Isaiah 49,
and partly from Jerome's new version, it cannot be authentic. For neither was
the Vulgate God-inspired (θεόπνευστος), since otherwise Jerome would not
have been permitted to correct and interpolate it, nor can the new version
which grew out of both be regarded as such, by Jerome's own admission.

IX. 7. The council's decree canonized an edition which did not yet exist but
which had to appear only forty-six years later. For the decree was adopted in
1546, while the work was finally completed and published in 1590 by Sixtus V
and then two years later by Clement. But how could the council approve and
declare authentic an edition which it had not examined and which had not even
yet been made at that time?

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

X. Although the Hebrews and Greeks had their own authentic texts, it does not
follow that the Latins must also have an authentic one, because the reasoning
is not the same for both. For it is certain that the Hebrew text of the Old
Testament and the Greek text of the New proceeded from prophets and apostles
inspired by the Holy Spirit. But no one will say that the authors or promoters
of the Vulgate Version were likewise God-inspired (θεοπνεύστους).

XI. Long use of a version, when joined with reason, can confer authority upon
it, but cannot bestow authenticity upon it such that it is never lawful to
depart from it on any pretext. For such authenticity depends not upon long use
but upon divine inspiration. Furthermore, whatever the use of this version
may have been, it prevailed only in the Latin church, not in the Greek and
Eastern church.

XII. The true and proper ground of an authentic edition is not the testimony
of the Fathers, the practice of the church, or the confirmation of a council.
For even according to Bellarmine, book 1 of *The Word of God*, chapter 10, the
church does not make books authentic but declares that they are such.
Therefore, a version which was not authentic in itself cannot be declared such
by the church.

XIII. It is not necessary that the common people, who are ignorant of the
Hebrew and Greek languages, hold the Vulgate as authentic in order to be able
to know whether they are reading Scripture. For they can recognize the truth
of Scripture no less from the vernacular versions which they read and
understand than from the Vulgate which they do not understand.
