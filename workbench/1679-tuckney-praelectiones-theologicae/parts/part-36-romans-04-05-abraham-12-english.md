Second, although Scripture does not expressly say that we are justified by
Christ's righteousness credited to us, it is enough if equivalent
statements occur there from which this follows by necessary consequence. To
omit many, I shall offer only two or three.

The first is 1 Corinthians 1:30: “From him you are in Christ Jesus, who was
made for us wisdom from God, righteousness and sanctification and
redemption.” The prophet predicted that the coming Lord would be called our
righteousness in Jeremiah 23:6, and the apostle says that he was made
righteousness for us. This occurs through crediting.

Bellarmine indeed denies this for two other reasons. I pass over Becanus's
childish answer: he admits that “Christ was made our righteousness” but
denies that “Christ's righteousness was made our righteousness,” as though
Christ could become righteousness for us otherwise than through his
righteousness.

Bellarmine denies it for two reasons. First, “Christ is the efficient cause
of our righteousness, just as he is elsewhere called our strength,
patience, and salvation, and in this very passage our wisdom and redemption,
because he redeemed us and made us righteous and wise.” Second, Christ is
called our righteousness “because he satisfied the Father for us and so
gives and communicates that satisfaction to us when he justifies us that it
may be called our righteousness and satisfaction. Although we are called
and are righteous through righteousness inherent in us, we do not thereby
satisfy God for our faults and eternal punishment. Rather, inherent
righteousness together with the remission of fault and eternal punishment
is the effect of Christ's satisfaction, conferred and applied to us in
justification. In this way it would not be absurd to say that Christ's
righteousness and merits are credited to us, since they are given and
applied as though we ourselves had satisfied God—provided one does not deny
that inherent righteousness also exists in us, and that it is a true and
absolute righteousness to which, by God's just judgment, glory rather than
punishment is owed.” He adds Bernard's words: “If one died for all, then all
died, so that the satisfaction of one might be credited to all, just as
that one bore the sins of all.”

As to the first argument: Christ was made righteousness for us in the same
sense that he is called our strength, peace, and salvation, and in this
passage our wisdom and redemption—not by crediting but by efficiency. He
effectively redeemed us and reconciled us to the Father; and in the same way
that he makes us strong and wise, he makes us righteous, efficiently and not
by crediting.

I answer:

First, it is true that Christ is most rightly called our wisdom, strength,
and peace. He can even be called righteousness in the same way insofar as he
efficiently makes us wise and strong, and righteous with respect to the
inherent righteousness which he creates and infuses in us by his Spirit. In
that sense he is called our wisdom and sanctification in this passage.

Second, if the passage said that he was made righteousness to us in that
sense, righteousness would be the same as sanctification, which is added
there as something distinct from righteousness. Bellarmine craftily omitted
it. He says, “Christ is here called our righteousness in the same way that
he is called our wisdom and redemption.” Bellarmine, why did you not add
“and our sanctification”? Paul added it: “Christ was made for us wisdom,
righteousness, sanctification, and redemption.” You omitted sanctification
entirely because you knew well that on your account it would be the same as
righteousness and thus create a pointless repetition in the list. Well done,
good man—you took sufficient care!

Third, since righteousness and sanctification are distinct here, Christ was
made each for us in its own, distinct manner. Estius wants them all spoken
in the same causal sense, namely as meritorious. We do not deny this if we
speak generically. But if we consider the several items particularly and
individually, although all generally flow to us from Christ's merits, each
does so in its own order and manner. Bernard says, “He was made for us
wisdom in preaching, righteousness in the absolution of sins,
sanctification in conduct, and redemption in suffering.” Or rather, he is
wisdom by efficiently enlightening us through his Spirit, and sanctification
by efficiently making us inherently holy. But he is redemption
meritoriously, the merit of his obedience being credited to us, and likewise
righteousness by the crediting of his mediatorial righteousness and
obedience.

Nor is it foreign to Scripture's usage to join several things in one list
when they are not all attributed in the same manner, but each in its own
proper way. Thus 1 Corinthians 6:11 says, “You were washed, you were
sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus and by the
Spirit of our God.” After first saying generally that they were washed, Paul
then identifies kinds of this washing in sanctification and justification.
Yet it is not the same washing in both, but one proper to each: in
sanctification, washing from sin's stains and filth; in justification, from
its guilt. In the former, grace and inherent righteousness are infused; in
the latter, Christ's righteousness and merits are credited, as Bellarmine
himself admits.

We understand these passages this way because it is the true nature of the
matter and parallel passages prove it. Indeed, even in this very passage,
although Roman commentators proscribe credited righteousness, they openly
acknowledge the substance of the thing. Thus

[1679 printed p. 225 | IA leaf 233]

Cornelius a Lapide says, “Christ satisfied the human debt by the most just
price of his blood and merited righteousness for us. He was therefore made
righteousness for us, because Christ's righteousness—that is, his
satisfaction—is ours as though we had satisfied God. Hence theologians say
that in justification Christ's satisfaction is applied to us, so that on
its account sins are forgiven and grace infused.” What else, I ask, do these
words express than the crediting of Christ's righteousness, by which in
justification we are acquitted of sin's guilt solely through crediting
Christ's merit, and in sanctification are made inherently holy?

Bellarmine acknowledges the same in the second part of his reply. He admits
that “Christ was made righteousness for us because he satisfied the Father
for us and gives and communicates that satisfaction to us when he justifies
us, so that it may be called our satisfaction and righteousness,” and that
in this way “it is not absurd to say that Christ's righteousness and merits
are credited to us, since they are given and applied to us as though we
ourselves had satisfied God.” He thereby most openly betrays his own cause
and confirms ours. Luther or Calvin could have said nothing more suitable
in support of justification by credited righteousness. When we press
credited righteousness, we have never meant anything other than that
Christ's satisfaction is so applied to us in justification to remove the
guilt of sins as though we ourselves had made satisfaction. Bellarmine
admits there that this does not occur through inherent righteousness. It
must therefore occur through righteousness thus credited to us.

Truth is indeed mighty and will prevail: it forced this confession from the
mouth of its sharpest adversary. Bellarmine grants it liberally but wants it
understood “provided that, besides this credited righteousness, inherent
righteousness in us is not denied.” Let him be free of that fear. We affirm
that sanctification is always joined to justification, and love to faith,
and prove it against them. We have never denied and never shall deny
inherent righteousness in those who are truly justified. He adds his
familiar claim that “this inherent righteousness is true and absolute
righteousness, to which by God's just judgment not punishment but glory is
owed.” This is utterly false on many counts, but since it concerns other
controversies and not the present one, we now leave it aside.
