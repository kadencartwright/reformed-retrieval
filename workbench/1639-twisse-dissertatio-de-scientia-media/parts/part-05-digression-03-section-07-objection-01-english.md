DIGRESSION THREE

SECTION SEVEN.

Arguments are raised against the preceding conclusions and their reasons.

[Marginalia: Navarr.]

But against these conclusions and their reasons it is argued, first: it is probable that an
intellect can have intuitive knowledge of a singular thing through the mere extrinsic termination
of that same singular thing. Thus, if the intellect has a concept of a universal thing and a
singular instance of that same universal becomes present, the intellect will know that singular,
not indeed by forming a special concept of that singular thing, but through the singular thing's
extrinsic termination. Therefore, without any change on the part of the knowledge which God has of
a future thing—even if such knowledge is called abstractive—it can become intuitive merely because
the thing known becomes present and extrinsically terminates the divine cognition; or at least each
position can be affirmed with equal probability.

This is confirmed: God is said newly to will something which he did not will before, and this is
because of a change in the thing willed. Therefore, he will similarly be said newly to know
intuitively or understand what he did not know before, and this because of a change in the thing
known; consequently, because that thing is placed in the nature of things, it will be said to be
known intuitively by God.

Response. Although the opinion presupposed by the argument, which is Cajetan's, has some
probability, and although it be granted that without a substantial change of the concept by which a
universal and specific nature is conceived, merely because a singular of that nature becomes
present to the intellect, that knowledge is called intuitive knowledge of that singular;
nevertheless, it must by no means be granted that this knowledge becomes intuitive without some
change of the aforesaid concept. Indeed, a presence in relation to the intellect necessarily
intervenes which is not merely through extrinsic denomination, but requires some change—namely, an
accidental one—on the part of the concept. For it is probable that, while a concept remains in its
substantial being, it can undergo a change when some syncategorematic term supervenes, without any
special representation, but solely through a certain peculiar mode of that same concept. Thus, in
the present case, the character of intuitive knowledge is derived not from a change of the concept
through a new representation, but from a new mode of a certain concept. In the present case,
therefore, it is impossible that the knowledge in God which is abstractive, or the knowledge of
simple intelligence, should become intuitive in time. For it is necessary at least that some change
intervene on the part of the concept, such that God newly knows the presence which he did not know
before.

To the confirmation it is answered that, since a thing is understood insofar as it has being in
the intellect, it is not said to be understood anew unless it newly has being in the intellect,
which does not occur by mere extrinsic denomination. Nevertheless, because God's will is ordered to
perfecting the thing which God wills, solely from the perfection of such a thing God is said newly
to will or love it.

[Marginalia: Respons.]

We attribute to God the cognition which is called simple intelligence, and we also attribute to God
the cognition which is called vision. By the cognition of simple intelligence he knows the
quiddities of all things and their necessary combinations, as well as possible and impossible
combinations of things. By the cognition of vision he is said to know, from among possible things,
those which are future, present, and past. All things of this sort, however, are singular. Yet we
must not judge that something occurs in God like the different faculties in a human being by which
he understands and sees. Rather, by his one intellect he contemplates all things and sees all
things. Nevertheless, because the objects differ, we conceive that same cognition of God under
various terms. We call one the simple intelligence which contemplates universals and possibles,
and another the cognition of vision, by which he beholds singulars, not as possible but as future,
present, or past—that is, as things either to be produced or already produced outside their causes.
Navarrete appears also to call the former cognition abstractive, insofar as it abstracts from
existence, and the latter intuitive, as distinct from abstractive knowledge. It is also beyond
controversy that the abstractive cognition of simple intelligence is naturally prior to intuitive
cognition. The former is natural and necessary; the latter is free and presupposes the free
determination of the divine will, or at least is most closely joined to it. We hold this intuitive
cognition to be eternal and never newly added to God. For although temporal things, with respect to
their actual production outside their causes, succeed one another at intervals of time, nevertheless
their intuitive cognition is not newly added to God because of their new production. Otherwise the
condition of divine cognition would be conformed, very unbecomingly, to the condition of imperfect
human cognition, which never beholds things before they actually exist outside their causes. For
our sight is acted upon by actually existing objects, and our sense deals only with singular things
of the kind which do not exist before they are actually produced outside their causes. Nor do I
know anyone who calls this into controversy. Yet from the structure of this first argument found in
Navarrete, and from Navarrete's response to it, one may infer that some have thought this intuitive
cognition is attributed to God only by extrinsic denomination arising from the new presence of
things in their actual existence. Nor indeed do I doubt that by anthropopathic language and by
conforming it to the likeness of human cognition it can be attributed to God in this way, just as,
following Scripture, we are accustomed to attribute eyes and ears to God. It is as though God,
although he knows future and past things, is nevertheless not said to behold things except when
they are present and for as long as they are present. And just as cognition of future things is
attributed to God after the manner

[Book I, printed p. 66; BSB canvas 00092]

of foreknowledge, and cognition of past things after the manner of memory—for Augustine himself and
also Scripture speak in this way—so cognition of present things is attributed to God after the
manner of vision or sight. Yet the comparison between cognition of future things and cognition of
present things differs greatly from the comparison between cognition of a universal thing and
cognition of a singular thing. For in the former comparison, cognition of a singular thing occupies
both members: in one it is considered as future, and in the other as present. Nor do I see how
cognition of a singular thing does not add some new concept to cognition of a universal thing. It
would be much more probable to say that the concept of a singular thing—Socrates, for
example—considered as present adds nothing to the concept of the same person as future. For the
concept is not only of the thing with respect to its universal essence and its own existence, but
with respect to its future existence. The futurity of that existence is indeed distant from the
presence of the same thing, yet less distant than a universal is from a singular.

The confirmation proceeds more clearly and suitably. It is most certain that God no more wills
something anew than he understands something anew; and if he willed something anew, he would also
understand that same thing anew. Yet he is said to will anew because he newly produces an effect;
but just as he had decreed from eternity that the effect would be, so also from eternity he foreknew
that it would be.

As for Navarrete's response, I do not see what purpose is served by the distinction of a concept
into substantial and accidental. That distinction concerns only the thing conceived, not our act
of conception formally, which is not merely of one genus but also of one species, although the
things conceived by us differ in species.

Again, in my judgment, although a new presence of the thing may intervene with respect to the
intellect by being placed in coexistence with it, nevertheless, unless the intellect depends in its
act of understanding upon the object presented, this presence contributes absolutely nothing to
constituting an intuition which arises anew in the intellect. Hence the presence of things in time
contributes nothing to God's intuitive cognition. Nor can I sufficiently marvel that the presence
of things in eternity which you invent contributes anything to God's intuitive cognition, since it
is a presence not absolutely speaking, but only in a qualified sense, while the presence of things
absolutely speaking contributes nothing. It would have been more honorable to hold that God's
intuitive cognition depends upon what is nobler rather than upon what is less noble. Perhaps you
will say: “Yet you admit only the presence of things according to their intentional being, and upon
this you found God's intuitive cognition.” Certainly; but this intentional being of things is in
truth not so much the being of the things as God's own creative essence. From this, however, we say
that things become knowable in the class of future things, since they have been decreed by God.
Once that is posited, it is necessary that the intellect, according to its infinite condition,
contemplate and comprehend those same things.
