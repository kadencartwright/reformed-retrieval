CHAPTER III. On the orders of ministers.

The third question concerns the distinction of clerics, or ministers of the
Church.

THESIS I.

1. Bellarmine. *We differ from our adversaries concerning the number, the
antiquity, and the office of clerics.*

Protestants. The first and principal disagreement is sophistically concealed
here. For we differ especially from the Pontificians in this: they number human
creatures among the ministers of the Church, whereas we acknowledge no
ecclesiastical ministers except those whom Christ instituted and the Church
commended in the Scriptures. Of those seven orders which they thrust upon us,
there are five of which they themselves admit that no express mention is found
in Scripture. The Jesuit Filincius, *On Cases of Conscience*, part 1, tract 9,
chapter 1. Yet since 1. all ecclesiastical ministers ought to be constituted by
God in the Church, 1 Corinthians 12:28, given by Christ, Ephesians 4:11, and
constituted by the Holy Spirit, Acts 20:28; 2. they ought to receive from the
Holy Spirit gifts suited to their offices, 1 Corinthians 14:4; 3. they ought to
discharge ministries directly dependent upon Christ the Lord, verse 5; and 4.
they ought to exercise operations with God himself working in them, verse 6;
therefore those who arrogate this to themselves, that they constitute new
ministers in the Church, ecclesiastical ministers properly so called, these
men—1.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n299 | printed p. 82]

sin against God, Christ, and the Holy Spirit, whose place and throne they do not
fear to occupy. II. They wrong those creatures of their own whom, bereft of
every suitable ability and hope of blessing, they thrust into the greatest
tasks, as if their feet had been cut off or their legs broken. III. They inflict
the gravest injury on the Church of God when they admit those administrations
which are both useless to it and also displeasing to its Lord and bridegroom,
Christ. Moreover, Bellarmine contends in chapter 1 that ministers are clerics
*because they are the Lord's lot, and the Lord is their lot.* Yet scarcely can a
greater presumption be conceived than to prescribe to the Lord whom and what
sort of people he will have for his lot and inheritance, and to whom he himself
will be a lot.

2. Bellarmine. *An ecclesiastical order is taken either properly, when it is
conferred by a bishop with a certain sacred and solemn rite and is directed to a
certain ministry to be rendered in the divine sacrifice; of this kind there are
seven: priest, deacon, subdeacon, acolyte, lector, exorcist, and doorkeeper; or
generally, when someone is in any way dedicated to divine service, even if it
is not directed to sacrifice; and in this way monks, virgins, widows consecrated
to God, and those who labor in burying the dead are called orders.*

Protestants. I. It is an absurd fiction derived from the idolatry of the Mass
that an ecclesiastical order is defined by its relation to the Eucharist rather
than to baptism or the preaching of the word. II. Durandus refuted that fiction
long ago, in book 4 of the *Sentences*, distinction 24, question 6, when he shows
that by this rationale the episcopate would be an order properly so called,
because, namely: *Episcopal power, which ordains a minister for the consecration
or confection of the sacrament of the Eucharist, has a nobler and nearer
relation to it than the power of the minor orders, which disposes the people
only for its reception.* III. But being directed to the Eucharist under the
aspect of a sacrifice properly so called makes those seven Pontifician orders
seven degrees of spiritual sacrilege. IV. A bishop ought not to be included in
the definition, because according to the Pontificians the minor orders can be
conferred by a presbyter or abbot by commission or delegation. Filincius,
chapter 5. V. *Those sacred rites* by which these orders are conferred (if you
except at least one) are a ridiculous sport, and notwithstanding those rites,
the orders themselves are divided by the Schoolmen into *sacred* and *not
sacred*. Bonaventure on book 4, distinction 24; Durandus, *Rationale*, book 2,
chapter 1. VI. The Pontificians do not have those seven orders in the
celebration of their sacrifice except when it is celebrated with the Pope, who,
as Durandus says in the same place, *when conducting the solemnities of the
Masses, has six orders with him, among whom neither the exorcist nor the
doorkeeper nor even the lector*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n300 | printed p. 83]

*are named, but bishops and cantors perform their functions.* Yet Durandus
learnedly observes a sacrament and perfection in that number of six, in the
same place. VII. Bellarmine offers no cause or reason why various ones of those
orders which he calls *proper* are not to be numbered among the *general*, so
that they are general *offices*, not degrees of a determinate ministry. VIII. If
orders are generally those who *are in any way dedicated to divine services*,
then all Christians have sacred order and belong to the clergy. Certainly
hypodeacons, lectors, and doorkeepers were no more ordained in former times than
deaconesses, or, as they were called by some Greeks, presbyteresses, for hands
also used to be laid upon them, Council of Chalcedon, canon 14. Indeed, hands
were laid even upon all who were baptized, as Jerome testifies against the
Luciferians: *Do you not know,* he says, *that this is the custom of the
churches, that hands are afterward laid upon the baptized?*

3. Bellarmine. *Clement, Constitutions, book 8, sets forth the rites of nearly
all the orders. Ignatius, in the Epistle to the Antiochenes, greets six of these
orders.*

Protestants. I. Bellarmine himself, in his book on ecclesiastical writers,
admits that those books of Clement are *apocryphal, of no account, disapproved,
corrupted, and depraved*, and that Ignatius's Epistle to the Antiochenes *did
not come into the hands of Eusebius and Jerome.* Furthermore, Ignatius greets
*cantors* and *laborers*, who are not orders, and passes over the whole order of
*acolytes* without greeting it. II. The Pontificians themselves admit that
these orders did not exist in the early Church. Durandus, *Rationale*, book 2,
chapter 1: *Only priests and deacons are named by the Apostles; the other orders
were added afterward because of the multitude of believers.* Bonaventure on
book 4, distinction 24, numbers 67–68: *In the primitive Church the orders were
not explained and distinguished as they are now.* In his *Reportatio* on the
same distinction, Scotus affirms both that there were not so many orders in the
beginning and that they are not now necessary. *In the primitive Church, and so
forth. But now there is not such great devotion in receiving the body of Christ
that it is necessary to have so many ministers. Indeed, four orders are now
conferred at once, but at an intermediate time, and so forth.* III. Among the
ancients in the second century, mention is indeed made of these names. It does
not appear, however, that those minor orders were held to be determinate degrees
of ecclesiastical ministry, much less of ministry pertaining to sacrifice
properly so called. Rather, certain offices general in themselves were freely
imposed by the Church upon particular people, who in this way were trained in
some manner for truly divine ministry.

4. Bellarmine. *The office remains. Let us speak of the individual orders.*

Protestants. It is pointless and ridiculous anxiously

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n301 | printed p. 84]

to dispute about the offices of the minor (as they are called) and nonsacred
orders, because I. they are not lawful degrees of sacred ministry, as has now
been sufficiently declared. II. Among the Pontificians themselves no account is
taken of the offices in their theatrical ordination. *Every day we see,* says
Bonaventure on book 4, distinction 25, number 34, *little children promoted to
the minor orders who neither know what order is nor what is given to them
there.* III. It is known that in most Pontifician churches a petty mass-priest,
together with the keeper of the church and perhaps some boy summoned to take
part in the childish game, performs the offices of all these orders when Mass
is celebrated in the common manner. IV. Among them all the lower orders are
commonly entrusted at once to one and the same man, without any thought on the
part either of the one conferring them or of the one receiving them about ever
undertaking the offices annexed to them. When a nobleman is to be made a bishop
by leap, they place the bell ropes, keys, and so forth in his hands, commanding
him to observe the times for ringing, to open and close the church at the proper
time, to guard its furnishings faithfully, to open the book for the lector, to
light the candles, and so forth, and also to obey the deacon, even though at the
same time this same man is elevated above all these offices to the episcopate.
Thus those offices have been reduced to bare spectacles of the emptiest
ceremonies and childish games. These matters therefore being omitted, let us
briefly see what Bellarmine has concerning deacons and presbyters.

On deacons.

5. Bellarmine. *It belonged to deacons—besides the office of collecting
offerings and administering the goods of the Church—also to attend upon and
minister to the sacrificing priest, and to distribute the Eucharist to the
people.*

Protestants. I. Ministering to the one sacrificing and distributing the
Eucharist do not belong to the essence or in any way to the institution of
deacons, as is apparent from Acts 6. *It is clear in every respect,* Cajetan says
on that passage, *that the seven men to be elected were deacons, that is,
ministers not of the altar but of tables. It did not belong to deacons of the
altar to devote themselves to prayer and the word of God; on the other side of
these men it is said, “But we will continue steadfastly in prayer and the word
of God.” And on 1 Timothy 3: We read of no other deacons in Holy Scripture than
those instituted in the Acts of the Apostles as ministers of the Church's
temporal goods.* II. It cannot be proved either from Scripture or from the most
ancient ecclesiastical writers that deacons performed any such thing. III.
Deacons could attend a minister administering the Supper, just as now in some
Reformed churches they commonly do by a general office, not by a determinate
ordination. IV. The distribution of the Eucharist in the time of the Apostles
neither required nor admitted the work of a deacon or any determinate minister
intervening between the one blessing and the communicants. *I would believe,*
says

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n302 | printed p. 85]

Cajetan on Matthew 26, *that just as one disciple gave the cup to another, so
they passed the dish with the holy bread successively from those nearer Jesus
to those more distant. They received it from Jesus not from hand to hand but
from the table.*

6. Bellarmine. *It is not credible that the Apostles would have used the laying
on of hands if they had wished to entrust only the ministry of tables to the
deacons.*

Protestants. I. Care for the poor, widows, orphans, and strangers, the
distribution of charity, and the preservation of the Church's peace were not as
cheap in the eyes of the Apostles as they are in those of the Pontificians. II.
Bellarmine himself in chapter 7 restricts the office of these deacons to tables
alone, for he answers as follows concerning their election, lest it be an
example of electing pastors: *This passage is not relevant, for we are treating
the election of pastors who are set over the government of the Church, but
these deacons were set over tables.*

Bellarmine. *Previously widows ministered at the tables, yet they were not
ordained by a fixed rite.*

Protestants. I. This is asserted gratuitously and without any proof, *that
widows ministered before.* II. Those who ministered before did not have a stable
ministry committed to them by the direction of the Holy Spirit and the election
of the Church. III. Hands were formerly laid even upon the deaconess widows who
ministered to the poor and strangers. Council of Chalcedon, canon 14.

7. Bellarmine. *It belonged to deacons to read the Gospel at the time of the
sacrifice.*

Protestants. This was not always done by deacons from the beginning, nor is
there anything in it which cannot be done by a presbyter or even by any grave
believer skilled in reading.

8. Bellarmine. *It belonged to deacons to baptize, preach, and so forth, as the
examples of Stephen and Philip teach, Acts 7 and 8.*

Protestants. I. To preach in some manner, as occasion arises, and even in the
same manner in which Stephen and Philip are said to have done it, belongs to
every believer according to the measure of gifts he has received. *All the
disciples, when scattered, evangelized the word of God,* Acts 8:4. A captive
woman preached among the Iberians, and the king, not yet baptized, did the same
after being instructed by that woman, Rufinus, book 10, chapter 10. Edesius and
Frumentius, still private men, were preachers to the Indians, Theodoret, book 1,
chapter 24; Rufinus, book 10, chapter 9; Nicephorus, book 1, chapter 23. Origen,
not yet having received the laying on of hands, taught in the Church itself,
Eusebius, book 6, chapter 20. His deed was defended by Alexander, bishop of
Jerusalem, and Theoctistus of Caesarea by the distinguished examples of
Evelpius, Paulinus, and Theodorus, who

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n303 | printed p. 86]

did the same with approval, in the same place. II. Philip did not baptize as a
deacon by ordinary power, but by a singular revelation, as is openly gathered
from Acts 8:26. Furthermore, that Clement whose authority Bellarmine praises in
this very cause and this very chapter, *Constitutions*, book 6, chapter 7, says
that this Philip was Philip the Apostle, not the deacon.

9. Bellarmine. *Yet it must be observed that all these things, and especially
the office of preaching, belong properly to bishops, so that not even
presbyters may preach unless by the bishop's mandate or consent.*

Protestants. I. It is therefore to be observed that deacons were not instituted
to preach, baptize, and so forth, for if they were ordained to perform those
things which belong properly to bishops, they would be ordained as bishops, not
deacons. II. It is also to be observed that papal bishops are not bishops,
because they are not occupied in those things which belong properly to bishops.
III. It is manifestly false when it is asserted here that no presbyter may
preach except by the bishop's mandate and consent. For everywhere in the New
Testament the office of preaching is committed immediately by God himself to
presbyters, but nowhere to any ordinary bishops distinct from the order and
degree of presbyters. Later indeed, as Jerome says to Nepotian, *it was the worst
custom in certain churches*—not all—*for presbyters to be silent in the presence
of bishops*, that is, in the same assembly where the bishop preached daily. But
this too is attributed by the same Jerome to the bishops' *envy and pride*, as
it also is by canon law, distinction 95, chapter 1; and to Rusticus, Jerome says
that in this matter bishops *were puffed up with the envy of diabolical
temptation because they were unwilling for presbyters to do what God commands;
that they were most unjust, and wished to rule alone in the Church.* Gratian
transfers all these things into pontifical law, distinction 95. IV. If this
whole matter were granted to Bellarmine—namely, that presbyters preach and
baptize by the mandate and consent of bishops—it would still not follow, on
Bellarmine's judgment, that this office belongs properly to bishops and not to
presbyters. For even the use of the keys which presbyters have in the internal
forum, according to the Pontificians, depends upon the mandate and consent of
those who possess external jurisdiction, Bellarmine, *On Penance*, book 4,
chapter 22; yet the office itself does not belong properly to them.
