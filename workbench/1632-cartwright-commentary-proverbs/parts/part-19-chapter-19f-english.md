V. 21. There are many thoughts belonging to each person; but the counsel of Jehovah will arise.

In קום (*will arise*) there is a metaphor transferred from bodily things to spiritual ones, by which it represents the firmness and completion of that thing which is determined from God’s will and counsel.

The purpose is that only those counsels will be firm and established which have been undertaken and instituted in agreement with God’s word.

The expression *counsel of God* is taken in two ways in Scripture: sometimes for God’s secret will, which he communicates to no mortal, as is evident from Isa. 40.13–14 and Rom. 11.34, and Isa. 46 (*all my will shall be done*). This will can no more be resisted than the course of the sun or of rivers can be retarded. At other times it is taken for God’s revealed will, as in the preceding verse. And therefore it must be thought that the expression *counsel of Jehovah* is to be read in this verse in the same sense, as though this verse had some affinity with the preceding one, so that by this Proverb he might kindle us to embraces of the divine word and add spurs toward consulting God in the word in every undertaking.

As often as the ungodly take counsel about doubtful matters, so often they admit into counsel either the reasoning of their corrupt or perverse reason, or custom, or the experience of former times, with all mention of the divine will either wholly thrust away, as being unknown to them, or certainly set after counsels arising from elsewhere. Many things also come into the minds of the godly, because they carry flesh and corruption about with them, which savour and smell of the old Adam. See Ps. 13.3. They too not rarely follow those counsels of the flesh after abandoning God’s salutary counsel. See Gen. 13.10 and 1 Sam. 27.1.

And therefore, so that we may not fear for ourselves because of the crafty counsels of the ungodly; then, so that we may examine all our counsels and thoughts by the rule of the word and divine counsel, let us set that Proverb before our eyes. It is confirmed by many testimonies of Scripture as well as examples. For in Isa. 8.10 the ungodly are commanded to enter upon counsel; but it is added that it will be rendered void and scattered, because God is on the side of his people. By contrast, in Ps. 1 it is said that all things which the righteous undertake will prosper in their hands; and Ps. 33.10–11 embraces both things: both the prosperity of counsels that have been instituted according to God, and the scattering of the counsels of the ungodly. Examples, moreover, occur everywhere in the books of Samuel, Kings, and Chronicles. For although the ungodly sometimes flourish and bloom, although they advance and are promoted by their impious counsels, as in Ps. 73 and in the example of the citizens of Ai and the Benjamites, nevertheless that momentary joy is converted into grief not long afterward. Just as, by contrast, matters sometimes turn out with little prosperity for the godly who institute the course of their life from God’s word, so that God may take trial of their faith and patience; for them it is well in the end, 1 Sam. 4, 5, 6.

V. 22. The desire of a man is his kindness; and a poor man is better than a man of falsehood; [or than a nobleman who is a liar.]

In תאוה (*desire*) there is a Hebraism for that which a person ought supremely to desire

[1632 witness | PDF p. 417 | printed columns 808-809]

and it is a metonymy of adjunct. In רש (*poor*) there is a synecdoche of species for every person who is destitute of the advantages of this life which aboundingly present themselves to his companion; and טוב (*better*) and איש (*than a man*) are likewise Hebrew, and the trope is קראו אדם.

The purpose is to spur men toward beneficence to the needy.

When he says that a man’s desire is kindness or beneficence, he indicates what a man ought to desire, not what everyone seeks. For what Isaiah pronounced at 1.23 obtains almost everywhere: namely, that all pursue the corruptions of gifts, and that it almost comes about that the richer a person is and the better his station, the more especially he is accustomed to raise his mind toward receiving gifts—to such a degree that he brings himself to suppose that in some measure an injury is done to him unless he is honoured and increased by frequently receiving gifts. Against this, it is established in this Proverb that the zeal of every person ought to be kindled and incited so that he may be kind and liberal toward others. Thus, if riches and revenues are to be desired by a man, they are especially to be desired for this reason: 1. so that he may acquire wisdom, Prov. 17.16; then, as is taught in this place, so that he may be beneficent toward others, especially those who are of the household of faith. Hence the rich are admonished to *do good and be rich in good resources*, 1 Tim. 6.18. [The Latin prints *bonis opibus*.]

This doctrine is wonderfully confirmed by the memorable saying of Christ, *it is more blessed to give than to receive*, Acts 20.35. Because this doctrine appears paradoxical to men, it must be sought a little more deeply, and the reasons on which it rests and stands must be gathered. And 1. kindness and liberality are commended by this very fact: that by them we imitate the heavenly Father, who, liberal toward all, receives nothing from anyone. What example can be more august or praiseworthy than this? For if the world is to be fashioned according to the example of an earthly King, how much more ought all to conform themselves to imitation of the heavenly King? And if someone accounts it praiseworthy to imitate the virtue of an earthly parent, much more to bear the image of the heavenly one. Here, if someone should perhaps object that Christ, the ruler and heir of heaven and earth, lived on what belonged to others, since he borrowed lodging from elsewhere when no house was available to him in which to lay his head, Matt. 8.20; and that provision and travelling expenses were furnished to him and his disciples by certain wealthy matrons, Luke 8; and that his example—since he is also a man—ought to be kept before our eyes rather than God’s, whose ways are unsearchable: let him receive the reply that Christ, who became poor for us, had to undergo this condition, 2 Cor. 8, since the desert of our sins necessarily drove him down into this vile and despised condition; whereas another condition awaits us, that, insofar as it is granted, we should endeavour to be a support to others from the blessing with which God has blessed us.

The second reason is that in this way a person gathers testimony for himself that he remains within obedience to God, since it is expressly said in the law that the one who remains in obedience to God will be so heaped with riches by God that he can both disburse to others and lend, Deut. 21; Lev. 26. To this must be referred what the Prophet sings in Ps. 112.5: that every righteous person gives graciously and lends. Moreover, what a great and extensive benefit it is for a man who, because of the multitude and gravity of his sins, wavers concerning God’s goodness, to bear God’s grace sealed inwardly in his mind! Hence also the Apostle in 1 Tim. 6.19 affirms that the beneficent rich, by their beneficence, lay up treasure for themselves in heaven, so that they may attain eternal life. See Matt. 6.20. Not because there is any merit of good works, as the Pontificians falsely and foolishly gather from passages of this sort, but

[1632 witness | PDF p. 418 | printed columns 810-811]

because God rewards this beneficence freely and from the good pleasure of his will. And these are the chief reasons, as they are referred to everlasting life. There are also others which carry no small weight for either avoiding the disadvantages of this life or pursuing its benefits.

And indeed, with respect to avoiding evil, this applies: one who receives gifts makes himself in some measure the servant of the person by whose beneficence and largesse he was aided. For if one who has received a loan binds himself with the bond of servitude, as at Prov. 22.7, much more one who receives a gift. From this it also follows that the one who is liberal and beneficent preserves his freedom among men and renders others bound to himself.

Another advantage of this life is that he gains many friends for himself, by whom this life is fortified and confirmed. Hence comes Prov. 19.6, where Solomon prefers a liberal man to a man who is honoured and established in authority but is not likewise munificent. For it is not denied that many suppliants approach such Magnates with knees bent so that they may obtain their causes; but he affirms that those who are thoroughly liberal are the ones who finally gather the most friends.

Finally, *it is more blessed to give than to receive*, because by giving one is increased very greatly in riches. Though this may seem πρῶτον ὕστερον (*first-last*) rather than the very thing which is proposed for discussion in this place, yet it rests upon both the firmest testimony of Scripture and is confirmed by many experiences, Prov. 11.24: *There is one who scatters and is increased the more, while one who withholds more than is right thereby brings on poverty.* Likewise in Ps. 112 and 2 Cor. 8 the righteous man is compared to a husbandman who cheerfully and copiously scatters seed through the furrows of his fields. By this means an answer is made to those who will object that wealth is diminished by giving lavishly, or even that the fountain itself is drained. So long as a person disburses with conscience toward God, riches will not fail him. For, as we learned a little before, one who is beneficent toward the poor gives to the Lord on loan. For it must not be understood that the liberal man gives indiscriminately to everyone, but (as it is in Ps. 112) *he administers his affairs with judgement and with discrimination applied*. For it is one thing to be liberal, another to be prodigal and rashly pour out everything.

From these things it is clear in what place the voluntary poverty of Monks, friars, and the entire company of the religious profession of the Pontifical Church ought to be held, and how shameful and foul is that in which they glory most of all, since they profess to enter upon that course by which they live on the labour of others, to such a degree that they renounce their patrimonies so that they may live on the alms of the Church.

In the latter part of the Proverb another doctrine is proposed, yet one deduced from the Proverb: namely, that the condition of the poor man who is destitute of the advantages of this life but possesses the will to do good is preferable to that of a man who excels in dignity and riches but, either through the appearance of feigned poverty, denies especially the supports of this life, or, if he bestows them, nevertheless does it neither benevolently nor cheerfully, but grudgingly and with a reluctant mind. For each is comprehended by the word *falsehood*: both the one who pretends poverty though he is rich, and the one who bestows grudgingly and not from his heart.

And therefore, just as *it is more blessed to give than to receive*, so it is more blessed (through want) to receive than either to deny your service to a needy person, or to render it not from your heart and heartily. Therefore there is no reason for the poor man to be too cast down by his poverty, or to envy the rich, especially the avaricious and tenacious, since his condition is preferable to theirs. And at the same time it is fitting for him to consider that his poverty has fallen to him not without God’s fatherly providence, because, however

[1632 witness | PDF p. 419 | printed columns 812-813]

inclined and ready a mind he may bear in poverty to oblige others, nevertheless he does not know whether he would have the same mind if means for giving had been available to him, seeing that he has recesses and hiding places in his mind of which he himself is unaware and ignorant.
