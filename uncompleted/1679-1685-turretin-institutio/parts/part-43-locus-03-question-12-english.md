QUESTION XII

ON THE KNOWLEDGE OF GOD

Do all things fall under God’s knowledge, even singular things and future
contingencies? Affirmative against the Socinians.

I. Among the communicable and positive attributes, which affirm some
perfection of God, there are three principal ones by which his immortal and
most blessed life is active: intellect, will, and power. The first belongs to
the directing principle, the second to the commanding principle, and the
third to the executing principle.

II. The inquiry into God’s knowledge belongs to the consideration of his
intellect. Two things above all must be attended to concerning this knowledge:
its manner and its object. Its manner consists in his knowing all things
perfectly, indivisibly, distinctly, and immutably, and in this way it is
distinguished from human and angelic knowledge. He knows perfectly, because
he knows all things through himself or through his own essence, not through
species abstracted from things, as creatures ordinarily do. This is both
because those species exist only in time together with the things themselves,
whereas God’s knowledge is eternal, and because it can have no cause outside
himself. 2. He knows indivisibly, because he knows all things intuitively and
noetically, not discursively and dianoetically by reasoning and inferring one
thing from another. And if God is sometimes represented as inquiring or
reasoning, this is said not properly but in a human manner, with Scripture
lisping with us in order to assert God’s perfect and certain knowledge. 3. He
knows distinctly, not because he gathers the diverse predicates of things by
diverse concepts, but because he sees all things most distinctly in one
intuition, so that nothing, however minute, escapes him. 4. He knows
immutably, because with him there is no shadow of change. And just as he,
remaining unmoved, causes all things to be moved, so with unchanged knowledge
he sees the various vicissitudes and changes of things.

III. The object of God’s knowledge is both God himself, who knows himself most
perfectly in himself, and all things outside God, whether possible or future,
according to their various orders and states: with respect to quantity, great
and small; with respect to quality, good and evil; with respect to
predication, universals and singulars; with respect to time, past, present,
and future; and with respect to state, necessary and free or contingent.

[Marginal heading: Does God know singular things?]

IV. The question here is raised especially about singular things and future
contingencies, which some would wish to remove from God’s knowledge. As for
singular things, Averroës denies that they are known exactly by God. This is
also attributed by some to Jerome in his commentary on Habakkuk 1:1, when he
regards it as absurd and unworthy of the divine majesty to bring it down to
the point of knowing how many gnats are born or die at each moment and what
multitude of bedbugs and fleas exists on the earth, and so forth. This opinion
would certainly be highly injurious to God if it were pressed precisely. But
those rather harsh words can be softened if they are understood
comparatively, so that he intends nothing other than to remove such things
from the special care which God exercises toward human beings and thus to
teach their privilege over those creatures. He seems to intimate this when he
adds: “Nor let us be such foolish flatterers of God that, while dragging God’s
power down to the lowest things, we are injurious to ourselves by teaching
that the same providence extends to rational and irrational beings.”

V. Scripture is certainly so clear on this point that it removes every
occasion for doubt from us. For why would Christ say that the hairs of our
heads are numbered and that not one sparrow falls to the ground without the
will of the

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 188 | IA leaf 242 | PDF p. 242]

Father? Matthew 10:29, 30. Why would Paul assert in Hebrews 4:13 that all
things are naked and laid bare (τετραχηλισμένα) before him and that no
creature is not manifest in his sight? Why would Psalm 147:4 intimate that God
numbers the multitude of the stars and calls each one by name, unless God
beheld singular things? And if God created and governs them, why should he not
also know them?

VI. The divine intellect is no more demeaned by knowledge of a base thing
than the sun is defiled by a foul thing. And if God’s power was not demeaned
when he created them, why would his knowledge be demeaned by beholding them?
Indeed, since all things, even the greatest, are very small before him when
weighed against his infinity, as Isaiah says so excellently in chapter 40:15,
and are as nothing, knowledge of all things would have to be taken away from
him if we removed the smallest things from his knowledge.

[Marginal heading: State of the question: the knowledge of future
contingencies.]

VII. Another question of greater importance concerns future contingencies,
the knowledge of which the Socinians attempt to take away from God so that
they can more easily assert an indifference of free choice immune from every
necessity, including that which God’s foreknowledge is ordinarily said to
impose upon it. For although they not infrequently seem to attribute to God
full knowledge of all things, including future things, as does Crellius in
book 1, chapter 24: “Under the name of God’s wisdom in this passage we include
the knowledge of all things,” and so forth; and shortly afterward: “As for
future things, the clearest proofs in the sacred Scriptures show that God
knows them also”; nevertheless, elsewhere they openly take from God the
knowledge of future contingencies because they do not think these belong to
the class of knowable things, so that either he does not know them absolutely
or knows them only indeterminately and probably. Socinus, *Lectures*, chapter
11: “Since, therefore, there is no argument and no passage of Scripture from
which it can be clearly inferred that God knew before they occurred all
things that occur, we must conclude that this foreknowledge of God should by
no means be asserted by us, especially since there are not a few arguments
and sound testimonies from which it appears that it must be denied
altogether,” and so forth. Thus Crellius, book 1, chapter 24, says: “Those who
say that God determinately knows future contingencies act without
consideration, because otherwise he would not be omniscient, whereas it is
rather because he is omniscient and never errs in knowing things that he does
not conceive future things determinately,” and so forth. And Smalcius,
*Against Frantzius*, disputation 12, speaks still more clearly: “How, indeed,
can that be true which destroys itself and conflicts with the sacred
Scriptures or does great violence to them? To say that God knows things which
in no way exist, such as future contingencies, is to contradict oneself; for
what is knowable is not properly contingent, and what is properly contingent
cannot be known.” The orthodox, on the contrary, maintain that future
contingencies fall under God’s infallible knowledge.

VIII. For the state of the question, note first that *contingent* can be said
in two ways: either with respect to the first cause, insofar as something can
be produced or not produced by God—and thus all creatures are contingent with
respect to God, because he could have created none if he had willed—or with
respect to secondary causes, which can produce or not produce an effect and
are therefore distinguished from necessary causes. Here we are treating
future contingencies in the latter sense, not the former.

IX. Second, a thing can be considered either with respect to the certainty of
its event or with respect to its manner of production. A future contingency
denotes both: as future, it denotes the certainty of the event; but as
contingent, it denotes the manner of production. It has the former from the
decree of the first cause and the latter from the constitution of the
secondary cause.

X. The question, therefore, is whether future things—not just any future
things or those which have a necessary cause, for these are not in dispute,
but contingent things, which have a free and indifferent (ἀδιάφορον) cause—
fall under God’s infallible knowledge, not logically with respect to the first
cause, in which sense even the most

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 189 | IA leaf 243 | PDF p. 243]

necessary things are contingent because they can exist and not exist, but
physically with respect to a contingent secondary cause; and whether he knows
them not merely indeterminately and probably but determinately and most
certainly. The Socinians deny this; we affirm it.

XI. The arguments are: 1. Because Scripture everywhere claims such knowledge
for God. John 21:17: “Lord, you know all things”; and 1 John 3:20: “God is
greater than our heart and knows all things.” It is vain to reply here that
“all things” means all things that are knowable, whereas future contingencies
are not knowable. For this is the first falsehood (πρῶτον ψεῦδος), the very
thing that had to be proved rather than assumed. We, however, say that they
are knowable, not as determinate in themselves but in God’s decree.

XII. Acts 15:18: “Known to God,” James says, “from all eternity are all his
works.” If all works, then also free and contingent ones; if known from
eternity, then also those which are future. Nor should it be said: 1. that the
passage concerns not every work of God or of human beings, but only the work
of which James was speaking, which pertained to the calling of the Gentiles.
For the universal term admits no restriction, but shows that James proceeds
from genus to species, from the thesis to the hypothesis, in this sense:
because whatever God does in time he decreed and foreknew from eternity, he
therefore decreed and foreknew that the Gentiles would be enrolled in the
communion of the church without observing the Mosaic law. 2. Nor should it be
said that this concerns works which exist, not those which neither existed
nor exist nor are certainly future, as future contingencies are. For although
they do not exist with respect to real being, they nevertheless exist with
respect to known being. Although they are not certainly future with respect
to secondary causes, they nevertheless are certainly future with respect to
the first cause, by which they have been determined. 3. The calling of the
Gentiles was so much God’s work that it also had to be the Gentiles’ work,
because through his grace they had to respond to God’s calling. Therefore, if
God certainly knew that calling, he had to know future contingencies.

XIII. Hebrews 4:13: “All things are naked and lie inwardly open to his eyes.”
If all things, then also the thoughts and voluntary actions of human beings,
not only those which actually exist but also those which are future. God sees
these not only in their causes but also in his decree. Hence he is said to
“understand our thoughts from afar” and to “know our sitting down and our
rising up”; “before a word is on my tongue, you know it completely,” Psalm
139:1–7. Therefore he knows not only the word which actually exists but also
the future word. And Jeremiah 1:5: “Before I formed you in the womb, I already
knew you.” Isaiah 48:8: “I knew full well that you would act treacherously.”
Ezekiel 11:5: “Every one of the things which come into your spirit, I know.”

XIV. 2. Because God predicts future contingencies, he therefore knows them;
for unless he knew them infallibly, he could not predict them with certainty,
as he does everywhere. Hence Tertullian, book 2 *Against Marcion*: “God’s
foreknowledge,” he says, “has as many witnesses as he made prophets.” “He
declares the end from the beginning,” Isaiah 46:10 says, “and from ancient
times the things which have not yet been done.” And chapter 41:23: “Declare
the things which will happen afterward, that we may know you are gods.” Thus
God repeatedly distinguishes himself from false gods and creatures. Examples
of predictions occur everywhere in Scripture. What will the adversaries
reply here? That those predictions are based only on probabilities, as they
impiously say? But who could bear the blasphemous assertion that God’s
infallible knowledge is merely conjectural, and that his Word, which is
firmer than heaven and earth and of which not even a jot can fail before all
things occur, rests upon mere fallible probabilities? That these predictions
concern things which God decreed to do? But there is no future thing which God
has not decreed either to do, if it is good, or to permit, if it is evil; nor
can he foreknow these things except because he decreed them. That they are
admonitions rather than predictions? But the passages themselves

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 190 | IA leaf 244 | PDF p. 244]

indicate the contrary, for a prediction is a straightforward narration of a
future thing, whereas an admonition is an exhortation to a virtuous work or a
dissuasion from its contrary. And if some predictions can serve to exhort
human beings, it does not follow that they are not predictions. That this can
be said of some future things, but not all? Yet why will it be said of some
but not all? No reason for the difference can be given.

XV. 3. Because the most perfect nature of God requires this. For if knowledge
of future things is a perfection in creatures, much more will it be so in
God. Furthermore, because God is omniscient, which he would not be unless his
knowledge extended to all things—not only past and present but also future,
both contingent and necessary. 2. Because he is the knower of hearts
(καρδιογνώστης), which could not be said of him unless he knew most
thoroughly not only the thoughts, counsels, and affections of the heart, but
also its very inclinations and dispositions before they issue into act,
Jeremiah 17:9, 10; Job 34:21; John 2:24, 25; Acts 1:24; and Revelation 2:23.
3. Because he is omnipresent, present everywhere and inwardly to all creatures
and moving and actuating them before they move or act themselves.

XVI. 4. The decree of providence necessarily carries this knowledge in its
train, because just as whatever occurs in time God immutably decreed within
himself either to effect or permit, so he must infallibly foreknow all of it.
The reason is that God’s foreknowledge follows his decree; and just as the
decree cannot be changed, so his knowledge cannot be deceived. Psalm 115:3:
“Our God is in the heavens; he has done whatever he willed.” Romans 11:36:
“From him, through him, and to him are all things.” Ephesians 1:11: “He does
all things according to the counsel of his will.” Nor can these passages be
referred only to the things which God himself does and not to the things which
must be done by human beings and which God placed within their own choice, as
Socinus wishes. For all things are equally subject to divine providence, nor
does the freedom of human actions prevent them from falling under his
knowledge and decree, as will be stated later.

XVII. Although it is difficult to comprehend the manner in which God
certainly knows future contingencies, the thing itself, which is confirmed by
so many passages of Scripture and arguments, must not therefore be denied.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XVIII. The principal foundation of divine knowledge concerning future
contingencies is neither the nature of secondary causes, which is assumed to
be indifferent (ἀδιάφορος), nor simply the divine essence as imitable by
creatures and as capable of producing them, which is the foundation of the
possibility of things, but the decree alone. Through it things pass from the
state of possibility to the state of futurity, in which God sees them as
predetermined and certainly future. And because God’s decree concerns not
only the thing but also its manner—that is, that the thing should come about
according to the nature of its cause, necessarily if the cause is necessary
and freely if it is free—God sees things in the decree not only as certainly
future but also as certainly future contingently.

XIX. There cannot be certain and infallible knowledge of things whose truth
is not determinate, if they are absolutely and in every way indeterminate.
But future contingencies are not such. For although they are indeterminate
with respect to the secondary cause and in themselves, they are not
indeterminate with respect to the first cause, which decreed their futurity.
Although their truth is indeterminate with respect to us, who cannot see
toward which side the free secondary cause will incline itself, it is not so
with respect to God, to whom all future things are presented as present.

XX. It is not inconsistent (ἀσύστατον) for the same thing to be called
possible and impossible at the same time, but according to different respects
(κατ’ ἄλλο καὶ ἄλλο): possible with respect to the power or secondary cause
considered in itself and in the divided sense, but conditionally impossible
upon the hypothesis of God’s decree and foreknowledge. Thus it was possible
for Christ not to be crucified if God had willed, but impossible because of
the decree. Therefore, what in the composite sense and upon the supposition of
God’s decree concerning the thing’s futurity is impossible not to occur could
nevertheless, in the divided sense and with that decree set aside, possibly
not occur.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 191 | IA leaf 245 | PDF p. 245]

XXI. It is one thing for a thing to be able to occur or not occur—that is, for
it to be possible or not to be future upon the hypothesis—and another for a
thing to be able to be future and not future at the same time. The former
denotes only simultaneity of potency, insofar as the thing could occur or not
occur. The latter implies a potency of simultaneity, because it supposes that
something can be and not be at the same time. A contingent thing is so called
in the first way, not the second, which is inconsistent (ἀσύστατος).

XXII. There is a contradiction between these two propositions, “a man will
walk” and “a man will not walk,” but not between these, “a man will walk” and
“he can refrain from walking,” because the first speaks of the truth of
futurity and the latter of the truth of possibility. Thus these two
propositions do not conflict with each other and can both be true at once:
“Adam will certainly fall,” and “Adam could have refrained from falling,”
because the former speaks of the futurity of the thing from the decree and
the latter of the possibility of the thing from the disposition of the
secondary cause.

XXIII. The infallibility and certainty of the event do not destroy the
contingent nature of things, because things can occur necessarily with respect
to the event and yet contingently with respect to the manner of production.
If there is foreknowledge of future contingencies, all things will occur
necessarily by a conditional necessity of consequence and infallibility, not
by an absolute necessity of the consequent. A distinction therefore always
remains between necessary and contingent things: necessary things have an
intrinsic necessity because they arise from proximate necessary causes and
are such in themselves; but contingent things, although they have an
extrinsic necessity with respect to the event, nevertheless come about by
contingent causes in accordance with their own nature.

XXIV. Freedom of the will is indeed overthrown by physical and coercive
necessity, which conflicts with the formal nature of freedom, but not by
hypothetical necessity and the infallibility of the event, for the same thing
can in this respect be both free and necessary. Hence, although human actions
are free because they are done willingly and with a prior judgment of reason,
they do not cease to be necessary by reason of God’s decree and foreknowledge.
God’s foreknowledge does indeed imply the infallibility of futurity and of the
event and a necessity of consequence, but it does not imply coercion or
violence, nor does it take away the will’s intrinsic freedom.

XXV. God’s infallible foreknowledge does not imply that God is the cause of
sins, because God foreknows sins as certainly going to occur—for example, the
selling of Joseph and the betrayal of Christ—not as things to be effected by
him insofar as they are sins, but as things to be permitted and then ordered
by him. This manner no more makes him guilty in his foreknowledge and decree
than in the execution, because neither the decree nor the foreknowledge
brings intrinsic necessity upon a human being, but only extrinsic necessity
with respect to the event.

XXVI. God does not test human beings out of ignorance in order by that means
to know something which he did not know before, but out of most wise
providence in order to reveal to others what was previously hidden from them.
Thus he tested Abraham, not in order to learn by this trial his faith and
obedience, which he had previously not known, since God had already professed
in Genesis 18:19 that he knew Abraham’s piety, but in order to make his
remarkable faith known to the world and the church. Nor, if Genesis 22:12 says
that God then knew Abraham feared him—“Now I know that you fear God, because
you have not withheld your only-begotten son from me”—must this be understood
absolutely of knowledge which God obtained, but transitively of knowledge
which he gave to others, as similar verbs are often used in Scripture.

XXVII. Although God testifies in Genesis 18:21 that he wishes to descend to
see whether the outcry of Sodom which had come to him was true, it does not
follow that he had previously been ignorant of the character and extent of
that city’s wickedness, because he had already said, “The outcry of Sodom and
Gomorrah had become

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 192 | IA leaf 246 | PDF p. 246]

great,” and so forth. Rather, this is said anthropopathically
(ἀνθρωποπαθῶς), to intimate that he fulfills the duties of a good and exact
judge and neither pronounces nor executes anything rashly, just as a careful
judge personally investigates the matter so that he does nothing
precipitately.

XXVIII. God is said to expect grapes from the vineyard in Isaiah 5:4, not
because he did not know what was going to happen, but this is said in a human
manner because he earnestly commanded his people to devote themselves to good
works, and the nature of the gifts bestowed was such that he ought no less to
expect fruit from them, had they not fallen upon ungrateful soil, than a
diligent farmer ordinarily expects fruit from a properly cultivated vineyard.

XXIX. When God conceives future contingencies as certainly future, he does
not conceive them otherwise than they are. Rather, he knows them in relation
to the decree as necessarily going to happen and determinate, while in
relation to their causes he knows them as indeterminate and contingently
future. God therefore foreknows them both in himself and in his decree as the
first cause, and thus they are necessary because of the immutability of the
decree and the infallibility of his foreknowledge; and also in the secondary
causes, upon which they proximately and immediately depend, which are in
themselves indeterminate, and thus they are contingent.
