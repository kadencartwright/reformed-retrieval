12. Suárez. *The second difference is that the will to concur is not of itself
determined toward an evil act, but indifferent; whereas the predetermining will
is of itself fixed upon the evil act. Therefore, by virtue of it, the evil act
falls within GOD’s will, and at least virtually and implicitly so does its
malice, since GOD, by his choice alone, resolved to will that act rather than
its good, and through that will determined the human will. But by virtue of the
former concurrence, the evil act does not fall within GOD’s absolute will;
only its permission does.*

[Marginal reference: Suárez, at the same place.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 450; IA PDF page 507; BSB canvas 502]

I reply, 1. Although GOD’s will to concur is not of itself determined toward
the act, it does not follow that, once it is now determined to effective
concurrence, it is not the cause of the evil act. No one would deny that Saul
was the cause of the slaughtered priests of GOD, even though he committed it
after being stirred and determined by Doeg’s persuasion and advice. Absalom was
truly the cause of the violation of his father’s concubines’ chastity, although
he committed that crime after being enticed and induced by Ahithophel’s
abominable counsel. Suppose someone is indifferent toward a certain crime,
perhaps killing his father—or even that his mind recoils from such villainy,
because the sting and force of an awakened conscience rise up against his
committing it. If he nevertheless murders his father, moved and induced by the
persuasion and counsel of a wicked man, he truly is the cause of that parricide
and is rightly guilty of so great a crime. Nor do definiteness or
indefiniteness, determination or indetermination, contribute anything toward
either preventing or advancing the true nature of causality. GOD’s will to
concur toward an evil act does not cease to be the cause of the evil act
because it is of itself indifferent toward the act, for the cause’s
determination is not of the essence of a cause, but only a condition required
for causing in actual causation. Likewise, the sun, because it is an
indifferent and universal cause of natural effects, would not of itself be the
cause of such effects, since it does not actually produce such effects except
insofar as its indifferent universality is restricted and particularized by
the natures of sublunary patients and of these lower things. But this is
absurd. 2. Nor is GOD’s predetermining will of itself fixed upon evil *as evil*,
but upon it *as an act*. And so the Jesuits’ indifferent concurrence, in actual
causation, is fixed and limited to an evil act. By virtue of GOD’s
predetermining will the malice of the act in no way—not even virtually and
explicitly—falls within GOD’s will, because the malice follows the act
accidentally and insofar as the sinner is subject to the law. 3. Although GOD,
from a pure principle, with a holy form and a most just end, fixes his willing
upon the act underlying malice rather than upon the good, why, I ask, will he
thereby be the cause of the malice any more than if he fixes his willing upon
the evil act, not by his choice alone, but because, moved by the wicked will, he
fixes it upon that evil act? The whole matter comes down only to this: in our
account God will antecedently be the author of sin; in yours he will
subsequently be its author. That is, the Jesuits and Arminians say, *in our
account it is true; in yours it is false*—and yet-*

[Marginal note: The indetermination and indifference of GOD’s will by which he
may concur or not concur toward the act does not free GOD from true causality
of the act, as Suárez says.]

[Marginal note: GOD’s will is not determined toward the act insofar as it is
evil and culpable, but toward the act that is evil, contrary to Suárez’s
fantasy.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 451; IA PDF page 508; BSB canvas 503]

*—yet both our mode of concurrence and yours in GOD are truly causative. 4. If
only permission follows by virtue of the Jesuit concurrence, GOD is just as
truly a permitter, not an effector, of supernatural acts as of evil acts. Yet
for them, to permit a free act is abandonment and punishment. Thus God abandons
every believer and penitent and leaves him to himself whenever he effects in
them both willing and doing. O Jesuit and Arminian grace—what are you?

13. Suárez. *The third distinction is that the will to concur is not of itself
and absolutely efficacious, but sufficient, and has its effect together with
the cooperation of the human will. But the predetermining will would be
efficacious of itself; and therefore, by reason of it, GOD would truly be said
to cause us to perform evil acts, which must by no means be said.*

[Marginal reference: Suárez, at the same place.]

I reply. If the Jesuit means that GOD’s concurrence in act is not efficacious,
he means that efficiency is not efficiency, which is absurd. I wish the same
to be understood of GOD’s actual will to concur. If he means that concurrence
in potency, or the conditional will to concur, is not efficacious without the
prior cooperation of the created will, this is the deep abyss of Pelagianism,
because on this account God wills to convert and save as many as are willing to
convert and save themselves. He effects willing and doing in us not according
to his good pleasure, but ours. Indeed, since the Jesuit denies the efficacy of
divine concurrence toward all free acts, whether good or evil, he denies that
the whole efficacy of grace is from God and says it is wholly from free choice.
What is Pelagianism, if this is not? 2. The predetermining will is efficacious
in such a way as gently to advance the will’s free motion. Nor does GOD, by
reason of it, truly effect evil acts *as evil*, but *as acts*; and in this same
way, according to Jesuit concurrence, he cooperates together with our will in
evil acts.

[Marginal note: By denying the efficacy of divine concurrence, Suárez removes
all efficacy of grace.]

14. Suárez. *By reason of general concurrence GOD cannot be called the author
of sin, both because GOD’s concurrence is of itself offered indifferently, and
because that entire real action which is from GOD, insofar as it is from him,
could remain without the true character of guilt or moral evil. Thus, if a
person performed the same real act with an invincible reason or through natural
inadvertence, it would lack the malice of sin, and GOD would furnish the
numerically same influx. For this reason he cannot be called either the per se
physical cause of sin, as is self-evident, or its moral cause, because he does
not move or predetermine toward it. Nor can he be called its accidental or
indirect moral cause, because he is not bound to deny his concurrence; nor can
he be called its accidental physical cause, because-*

[Marginal reference: At the same place, no. 20.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 452; IA PDF page 509; BSB canvas 504]

*—because deformity is not necessarily joined to his physical causality as it
is his. But if he predetermined the will toward such an act, GOD would have to
be called its moral cause, as one freely moving and drawing a person into sin,
and also its accidental cause, as one giving a motion not required by virtue of
general concurrence.*

I reply, 1. That GOD’s concurrence is indifferent does not prevent it from
being truly causative, and therefore does not prevent God from being the true
cause of the act. Otherwise the sun would not be the cause of generation and
decay among these lower things. 2. But we deny that *the whole real action of
GOD, insofar as it is from him, can remain without the true character of guilt
or moral evil*. The proper conclusion would be that God is not the physical
cause of the act of sin underlying the malice. But rather the contrary
consequence is true: because GOD’s entire predetermination can remain without
the true character of guilt—namely, if God had not imposed a law upon humanity
(which perhaps involves no contradiction)—God could have granted to our acts
all the prior predeterminations which he now in fact furnishes, if the subject
is general predetermination in the order of nature. In that case no guilt would
follow those predeterminations. Thus the Jesuit’s reasoning absolves and frees
our predetermination from all guilt. 3. What the Jesuit says—that *for this
reason GOD cannot be called the per se physical cause of sin*—is a marvelous
prodigy. For he means (as I know well enough) that GOD is not the physical
cause of the act of sin *as an act*; the Jesuit professes that he is speaking
of sin in this way at the beginning of his book on concurrence. But if God is
not the per se physical cause of the act of sin, he will be its accidental
physical cause. Yet the first cause is the accidental cause of no positive
being. If, however, he means that a per se cause is here opposed not to an
accidental cause but only to a cause through another, then, so far as I am
concerned, it can stand—without injury to the Jesuit’s reputation, learning, or
faith—if God is called the cause of sin not through himself but through the
intervention of free choice. 4. He proves that *GOD is not the moral cause of
sin, because he neither moves nor predetermines people toward sin*. But we
gladly accept this, for GOD’s predetermination moves toward the act, not toward
the act’s malice. 5. This reason too is empty: *GOD predetermines the will
toward the act*, namely the entitative act; *therefore, he is the moral cause of
the malice*. For more is required for someone to be the moral cause of sin than
a predetermining influx of the act, especially one that is merely physical. It
is required not only that he physically produce the act, but that, while pro-*

[Marginal note: The cause of the act is not thereby the cause of its malice.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 453; IA PDF page 510; BSB canvas 505]

*—ducing the act, he be subject to a legal obligation, or an
obligation proportionally corresponding to it, not to perform the act or to
perform the contrary act. But this obligation by no means constrains God not to
predetermine the created will.

15. And therefore Suárez adds: *It also does not follow that the will to concur
is a virtual will of the malice, but only of its permission, because that will
is of itself indifferent and inefficacious. But if it were predetermining, it
would be a virtual volition of the malice, because it would be of itself fixed
and efficacious.*

[Marginal reference: Suárez, at the same place.]

I reply, 1. If the will to concur is only a *virtual volition of the act*, the
will to concur toward supernatural acts will be only a *virtual will* of
supernatural acts. Empty, then, is that will of GOD by which he so earnestly
and vehemently seeks, wills, entreats, and begs for the obedience of the elect.
2. What is a *virtual will* of the permission of sin? Namely—unless I am
mistaken—it is nothing other than God’s willing to concur in virtue of the
human will. That is, GOD wills to concur toward the act of sin if the human will
is willing to cooperate. But that is nothing other than God’s willing sin
conditionally, if a person should will it. Yet to say this is egregiously to
sin, or rave, against GOD’s holiness. 3. Because the will to concur in God is
indifferent and inefficacious, God is not thereby exempted from causality. For
if an indifferent and inefficacious cause does not possess the character of a
cause on account of its indifference and inefficacy, then GOD’s will by which
God would will all mortals to be saved is not the cause of people’s salvation
and conversion. Indeed, the Jesuits’ and Arminians’ internal grace—which moves
the will by an entirely persuasive, or at least indifferent, motion—will not be
the cause of conversion. Indeed, GOD’s will to concur toward supernatural acts
will thus be merely permissive and virtual, for such a will is inefficacious
and indifferent toward conversion or non-conversion, whichever created free
choice is pleased to produce. And thus the Holy Spirit is not the cause of our
conversion, faith, repentance, love, and so forth. 4. It is marvelous how the
Jesuit proves that *GOD’s predetermining will is the virtual cause of malice*.
It is because, he says, *it is of itself fixed and efficacious*. Observe here
that although GOD’s will is the efficacious cause of the evil act, it is not
thereby the efficacious cause of the malice annexed to the act, but only its
virtual cause. Yet a virtual cause possesses the nature of a cause only very
improperly. Therefore, in bad-*

[Marginal note: According to Jesuit tenets, the will to concur toward all acts
is merely permissive and idle.]

[Marginal note: According to the Jesuits, the fact that GOD’s concurrence is
inefficacious does not prevent it from being causative of sin.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 454; IA PDF page 511; BSB canvas 506]

*—bad faith the opponents prove from our predetermining motion of acts, which
we attribute to God, that God is properly the principal efficient cause of sin
and human beings are mere instruments. Here we see the opponents’ bad
conscience. Then by this reasoning the human will, which is *of itself the fixed
and efficacious cause of sin*, will be only the virtual cause of sin and a cause
so called improperly.

16. Again, Suárez says: *For the same reason GOD cannot, by reason of his
universal and general concurrence, be said to cooperate toward evil: both
because his cooperation of itself abstracts from malice, and because he ought
not to deny it but to grant it on account of the general rationale of
providence and other greater goods. But the case would be different with
predetermination, because by virtue of it malice would follow infallibly, since
it would predetermine the moral act as such and with all its circumstances.*

[Marginal reference: At the same place.]

I reply, 1. GOD’s concurrence is said to abstract from malice only because it
is general and does not necessitate toward the evil act. But in this way GOD’s
concurrence abstracts from the act’s supernatural goodness, and God would not
cooperate toward good, for concurrence toward good acts is also general and
non-necessitating. Nor can this concurrence be said to abstract from malice
because God does not will malice, recoils from it with his holy will, and
counsels the good. For this proves that GOD’s approving will abstracts from
malice, which we piously teach and embrace with open arms; but, on Jesuit
teaching, it does not prove that concurrence itself does not cooperate toward
evil. For although God detests the act’s malice, he nevertheless truly concurs
toward the act by the act of causing proper to the first cause. Otherwise there
would be a positive and entitative action of the creature without the
Creator’s cooperation, which is absurd. So one who provides aid toward the
death of an innocent man through the persuasion and determination of an
adviser or commander—as Pilate did in the Savior’s death—is nevertheless truly
said to cooperate in the killing, although his will recoils from that death and
he engages the authors of the slaughter with persuasion, pleas, and arguments
to free the innocent man. His concurrence does not abstract from malice,
although his will abstracts from it in some manner. Thus God truly concurs
toward the act as first cause, although GOD’s holy will detests the act’s
malice. Nor do we teach that GOD concurs toward malice, for properly speaking
no one either performs or cooperates in malice, but only in the act. In this
sense our predetermination abstracts from every ma-*

[Marginal note: Jesuit concurrence is equally causative cooperation toward
evil, according to the opponents’ tenets; yet so is predetermination. And
predetermination equally abstracts from malice; yet so does that concurrence.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 455; IA PDF page 512; BSB canvas 507]

*—malice, because GOD’s holy predetermining will detests malice. But neither
does universal concurrence abstract from malice in the sense of exercising no
causality, from the office of first cause, toward producing the act. To abstract
from malice in this way would be for God not to act at all, which is foreign to
the causation proper to the first cause. 2. If GOD ought not deny general
concurrence because of the general rationale of providence and other greater
goods—namely, I say, for the manifestation of the glory of avenging justice and
sparing mercy—then I reply, 1. He ought not deny predetermination for the same
reasons in order to prevent the existence of malice. If that indifferent
concurrence is causative, as it is, and makes GOD the cause of the act, and
thus, according to the Arminian logic used against us, involves GOD in
partnership in the crime, no reason and no good, however great, can be a
sufficient reason for GOD to give such concurrence. For God would thus do evil
so that good might come from it, which is blasphemous. 2. The Jesuit concedes
that, by furnishing his concurrence with evil acts, God intends greater goods.
That is, with Calvin he intends the glory of justice and mercy, and therefore,
in order to attain such a glorious end, in some manner—namely permissively,
though not approvingly and effectively—he wills sin to occur. Therefore, the
Jesuits prefer to pass over in silence what those goods pre-intended by God in
the permission of sin are rather than present Calvin’s opinion as their own. 3.
Nor does malice follow by the causative force of predetermination. 4. GOD
predetermines moral acts insofar as the acts are positive, and that is not
absurd.

17. Next comes the Jesuit Théophile Raynaud, arguing against predetermination as
follows. *If GOD’s predetermination supervenes upon a person at the crossroads,
doubting whether to sin or not, and inescapably drives him into willing the
base thing, he will necessarily sin and the fall will be imputed to GOD. Thus
Suárez.*

[Marginal reference: Raynaud, distinction 4, question 3, article 5, no. 345.]

[Marginal reference: Suárez, opuscule, book 2, *On Concurrence*, chapter 2, no.
12.]

I reply. If GOD’s predetermination supervened with a force driving a person to
evil against the nature of his freedom, the fall would indeed be imputed to
God. But because predetermination gently draws the doubter, willingly, into the
act, nothing evil can rightly be imputed here to GOD, who exists above every
law and who furnishes his predetermination from his free good pleasure, which
is the supreme law for him. 2. Predetermination acts by a gentle physical
drawing; it does not coerce the doubter morally or legally.

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 456; IA PDF page 513; BSB canvas 508]

Raynaud. *GOD from eternity hates the material element of sin insofar as it is
the foundation of malice. Therefore GOD does not predetermine toward evil
acts.* I reply. God does not predetermine toward the act *insofar as it grounds
malice*, but toward the act *that grounds malice*.

[Marginal reference: Raynaud, at the same place.]

Raynaud. *I do not attribute to GOD a conditional volition of the material
element of sin. Nor does GOD say, “If you wish to sin, I will that you sin,”
but only, “If you wish to sin, I do not will to prevent you,” or, “I will not to
prevent you.” Once our determination is foreseen, GOD decrees to join his
cooperation to it. The love of God by which he loves physical acts does not
terminate in the act insofar as it is the foundation of malice*—because in this
respect it regards the creature alone—*but insofar as it is his cooperation, in
which respect malice does not follow upon it.*

[Marginal reference: Raynaud, at the same place, no. 351.]

Theodor Smising speaks thus. *GOD concurs as a cause toward the physical
material element of sin, but not toward its moral material element. Otherwise
he would accidentally and positively will the moral privation that is formally
sin, because, just as a privation is not formally causable except accidentally
by reason of the positive thing in which it is grounded, so neither is it
formally willable.*

[Marginal reference: The Jesuit Smising, theological disputations, tome 1,
treatise 3, disputation 3, *On the Divine Will*, question 3, no. 91.]

I reply, 1. God wills that to occur which he foreknows will necessarily occur
from his non-prevention, given that he is the universal Governor of all things.
A head of household wills that to occur which he sees happening in his house
and *does not prevent*, though he could prevent it—although he does not will it
culpably and under the evil mark of imputability, unless it is an evil that he
is bound to prevent. 2. What the Jesuit imagines God saying—*if you wish to sin,
I do not will to prevent you*—implies that, concerning sin’s entrance into the
world, God has no act of providence besides the act that demons have concerning
good acts and good angels concerning evil acts. For they have a bare act of
non-prevention concerning both good and evil acts. But it is known that GOD’s
non-prevention concerns the nonexistence of multiple worlds. Pure
non-prevention is mere unconcern as to whether sin exists or not; unconcern is
opposed to providence and is unworthy of God. For since God wills the glory of
his justice and mercy with a positive volition, not bare unconcern, if he
positively wills the end, he cannot be unconcerned about the means. 3. The moral
material element is a good and a being; therefore God concurs toward producing
it no less than toward the physical material element. And because the moral
results from the physical, God would still have to be said accidentally to will
the malice—or rather, to will it with a permissive will and in a qualified
sense.

[Marginal note: Smising’s claim is false: that sin, because it is a moral
privation, is not willable per se except by reason of the positive act. For the
agent who is subject to law and freely sins wills and chooses sin per se.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 457; IA PDF page 514; BSB canvas 509]

18. Raynaud. *Some say that GOD is not the cause of sin because he concurs as a
wholly physical and necessary cause, and accordingly as if he were not free,
nor as the cause of freedom and morality. Thus Soto, Cano, and Vázquez. But,*
says the Jesuit, *GOD cannot fail to be a free cause in sin, because he freely
laid down for himself, by a free decree, the law of concurring. Yet this matters
little, for GOD ought not to have laid down such a law necessitating himself to
concur with acts of sin. Then, that GOD does not transgress the law by
concurring with the act proves that GOD does not sin, but it does not prove
that he is not the cause of sin.*

[Marginal reference: At the same place, no. 160.]

Alfonso Curiel says that some argue against Soto and Cano as follows. *GOD
freely concurs toward the act of sin. Therefore he concurs in the manner of a
moral cause, because to act freely and deliberately is to act morally. It is
confirmed: by a moral cause is meant either one that acts freely, one that acts
in subjection to moral rules, or one that acts by means of advice, command, and
exhortation. If the first, the intended conclusion is obtained. If the second,
it follows that GOD is not the moral cause of good operations either, because
he does not concur toward these as one subject to a law or rule. If the third,
it follows that the created will is not the moral cause of its own acts,
because it does not concur toward them by advising or commanding. It is
confirmed, secondly: the real positive thing which is the formal element of sin
and serves as its substrate is intrinsically and essentially something moral.
Therefore it is referred back to God as the first moral cause.*

[Marginal reference: Curiel, 1–2, question 79, article 1, section 7.]

I reply, 1. I judge the opinion of Soto, Cano, and Vázquez to be most true.
Although God does not concur toward the material acts of sin as a brute and
wholly physical agent, as fire heats, he nevertheless acts as a physical cause
advancing and imparting whatever physical entity exists in the material act of
sin. I do not thereby deny that God freely, and in a certain sense morally,
concurs toward acts of sin. Nor does God cease to be a free agent because he
laid down for himself an eternal law of concurring, for he created the world
most freely and yet freely laid down for himself the decree of creating the
world, upon the supposition of which God cannot fail to create the world. 2. If
God only causatively advances the act’s physical entity without any subjection
to law, from the free dictate of his eternal wisdom alone, he neither sins nor
is the cause of sin. For to act physically in an act of sin is in no way to be
the cause of sin under a mark worthy of imputation. 3. It cannot be denied that
God is a natural agent in concurring toward either good or-*

[Marginal note: GOD is a moral cause insofar as he is quasi-physical in
concurring with moral agents.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 458; IA PDF page 515; BSB canvas 510]

*—or evil acts, but in this sense: that he acts from the dictate of his infinite
good pleasure, which is essentially good, not in subjection to a law and moral
precepts; and God is the first moral agent. Yet he is called a physical and
quasi-necessary, non-free agent for three reasons. 1. Because he ordinarily and
always exerts influx, as physical agents do. 2. Because he is no more subject
to the law and rule of morals—unless his eternal good pleasure is taken as his
law—or liable to the malice of imputability than a physical agent is. Wheat
stolen by robbery and theft, while it grows into ears of grain, could no more
be called guilty of the theft. 3. Because God advances the physical entity
through the holy causation of the first cause, detesting the morality of
malice, loving the morality of goodness, and working efficaciously but freely
through grace.

19. Raynaud. *Whoever is the cause of the material act of sin is the cause of
sin. But GOD is such a cause. Therefore.* Raynaud replies by denying the major
premise. *For if someone caused a positive act in sleep or madness, he would
not be the cause of sin. For someone to be formally the cause of sin, he must
cause the act under that aspect in which malice springs from it. For someone to
be the cause of a vital and supernatural act, and thus to be denominated a
vital and supernatural cause, it is not enough that he be a supernatural cause
of the aforesaid acts. Otherwise a habit would be a vital cause and the
intellect a supernatural cause, which no one would say. I say that GOD causes
the evil act specifically and materially, not formally and reduplicatively;
for GOD concurs toward the act in the same manner when it is good as when it is
evil. The will sins by not following the dictate of reason; GOD does not sin,
because his wisdom dictates that he ought to cooperate*—toward the act of
malice—*and therefore his cooperation is the execution of a rightful claim and
ought not to be imputed to him as a fault. This is clear in one who gives
another a sword under the right of defending his own life; in that case the
cooperation toward the killing is not vicious.*

[Marginal reference: Raynaud, distinction 4, question 3, article 5, no. 361.]

[Marginal note: Raynaud imagines GOD to be the author of sin according to the
Jesuits’ tenets.]

I reply, 1. What the Jesuit requires in one who is formally the cause of
sin—namely, in one who is formally denominated a sinner, for only the sinner
himself is formally the cause of sin—does not prove that God is not the cause
of sin, namely of its material act. For the habit of faith is truly the cause
of the vital act, but it is not a vital cause of faith. Thus, although God is
not formally the cause of sin, it by no means follows from this Jesuit
reasoning that he is not the cause of sin at all. For by equal reasoning the
Jesuit could prove that the Devil and evil counselors are not causes of the sin
toward which they solicit people. Why? Because they are not for-*

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 459; IA PDF page 516; BSB canvas 511]

*—mally its causes. 2. Raynaud offers a similar argument against Soto. If a servant could not
execute some crime unless his master were summoned into partnership in the
crime, and the master strove with all his power to draw the servant away from
his wicked plan, but, making no progress, nevertheless joined his cooperation
to the servant, the master would be a joint cause of the crime with the
servant.*

[Marginal reference: Raynaud, at the same place, no. 360.]

Here God joins his cooperation to the wicked act, although he prohibits,
detests, and dissuades that act. If the Jesuit says that *God joins his
cooperation to the act not under that aspect in which malice springs from it*,
this does not prove that God is not a joint cause of the act that is evil, as
we teach, but only that he is not the cause of the act *insofar as it is evil*,
which we detest as impious. Then by equal right let us say that *GOD does not
predetermine toward the act under that aspect in which malice springs from it*.
Our position then will not involve God in partnership in the crime. 3. That
right of GOD by which he concurs is GOD’s mere good pleasure, by which God,
above every law, holily concurs toward wicked acts. If one human being
furnished concurrence to another for a crime, he would be a joint cause of the
crime, because he is not endowed with the privilege of such a right. Therefore
immunity from law is the reason why God predetermines toward wicked acts and
yet neither sins nor is a cause or joint cause of sin. 4. Raynaud will not
persuade his fellow Jesuits Diego Ruiz, Suárez, and Gregorio de Valencia that
the outcome of the action, as it is from the will, is sin, while, as it is from
God, it is not sin. For the Jesuits impute this to the Calvinists as impious—or
so they say.

20. Next comes Guillaume Gibieuf. *The material element of sin,* he says, *is
unsuitable to rational nature. 2. It disposes one toward sin. But GOD neither
disposes nor prepares us for sin. Therefore he does not determine us toward the
act of sin.*

[Marginal reference: Gibieuf, *On the Freedom of GOD and the Creature*, book 1,
chapter 20.]

Gibieuf. *The law and motion of the first cause conspire toward one thing; but

and the internal inscription of the law in the heart conspire. GOD moves or
determines no one toward evil.*

[Marginal reference: At the same place, no. 3.]

I reply. It is false that GOD’s motion and the law conspire in the same
governance. The law forbids the killing of an innocent man, yet by GOD’s
definite counsel the Jews killed CHRIST. 2. The law inclines people by a mor-*

*—al and resistible motion; but the motion of the first cause is irresistible.
3. In the elect, the law and GOD’s motion often conspire—not that general
inclination of the first cause, but the special bending of the heart by GOD the
Father in CHRIST toward that to which the law engraved upon their hearts by
divine grace inclines, Jeremiah 31. 4. GOD’s motion leads into the ultimate end
either as formally loved, as it does in the elect—and this is the Holy Spirit’s
special motion—or moves into the ultimate end only materially—and this is a

and GOD’s motion by no means conspire toward this end, for the law urges the

manifestation of justice. This is not the end of the law.

Objection. *The first orderer does not determine toward an act of disorder; the
first actuality does not move toward acts subject to privations, such as
malice. Therefore GOD does not move toward evil acts.*

I reply. The first orderer does not determine toward disorder formally: true,
if we speak of effective and approving ordination; false, if the subject is
permissive ordination. 2. The first orderer most holily *ἀγαθύνει τὸ κακόν*, as
Dionysius says: he makes evil good, and for this end he permissively wills
evil. Indeed, Boethius says that he so orders it *that, if you consider the
disposing providence, you perceive that nowhere is there any evil*. Indeed,
Augustine says: *Just as in singing, the insertion of silences at certain most
measured intervals, although they are privations of voices, is nevertheless
well ordered by those who know how to sing.* So here the very existence of sin
adorns the universe with a certain order. Shadows adorn paintings, pauses a
song, blackness a beautiful image, clouds the day, and vinegar makes sweetness
savory. Hence the order of the first orderer is marvelous: he wills the very
disorder to occur.

21. Here the opponents also contend that God cannot predetermine toward
material acts, since the material acts themselves are prohibited by GOD’s law.
Jacob Arminius depicts the matter thus. *In every sin,* he says, *it cannot be
said that there are an act and a disorder. For sometimes it is the act itself
that is prohibited, while sometimes it is not the act itself but a
circumstance of the act. Thus eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and
evil was prohibited, not some circumstance of that eating. And therefore the
eating itself was thereby un-*

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Antiperkins*, p. 730.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 461; IA PDF page 518; BSB canvas 513]

*—due, unlawful, and disordered—indeed the very disorder itself—once the law
forbidding the eating was posited. Otherwise, in and of itself, apart from that
law, the eating is a natural act having no disorder in itself. But once the law
prohibiting the eating is posited, that eating cannot be considered naturally
good in such a way that a disorder is added to it on account of which it must
be omitted. For it must be omitted in itself and for its own sake, because it
is forbidden by divine law and because to eat itself is to sin. The entire
disorder consists in the fact that this act of eating has a place in the number

The Remonstrants speak thus. *On this account GOD would not have punished our
first parents because they ate of the forbidden fruit, but because they ate it
wickedly; and David, not because he lay with his neighbor’s wife, but because
he lay with her wickedly. Let the Calvinists show where in the world villainies
and crimes are found that were not wickedly done.*

[Marginal reference: Remonstrants, synodical writings, article 1, pp. 251,
253.]

Grevinckhoven speaks thus. *I do not deny that an evil act contains something
which GOD could will when considered in itself and apart from a relation to the
violation of a law that has been enacted—namely the entity of the act in
itself. But once the law is posited, I deny that GOD in any manner or respect
wills the act prohibited by the law.* So too the petty Aberdeen doctor James
Sibbald runs with the adherents of the Arminian sect among us. (Thus, not long
ago, Arminianism gained strength in Scotland under the antichristian
episcopate.)

I reply, 1. The doctors, academy, councils, and scholastics never raised a
dispute concerning that famous and sound distinction between the act and the
malice annexed to it. But the Lord has made foolish the Jesuits’ and Arminians’
very metaphysics. For, first, if the act and the disorder are not to be
distinguished but are one and the same, then the act would be essentially
disordered and would be the disorder itself, which is plainly Manichaean. Snow
is white, but snow is not whiteness itself. A form or privation is predicated
concretely of the acts in which it inheres, but by no means abstractly. This is
not an essential predication—*the eating from the tree of knowledge is
disordered*—but plainly an accidental one. Therefore the eating will be one
thing and the disorder of the eating another. Hence Scripture distinguishes
works from the wickedness of works: Isaiah 1:16, *Remove the evil of your
actions*; Hosea 9:15, *Because of the evil of their actions I will drive them
out of my house*; hence Isaiah 65:2, *They walk in a way that is not good*; and
Galatians 5:19, *the works of the flesh*. Is not the work therefore one thing,
and the flesh or corruption adhering to the work another? 2. If the act itself
were the very disorder, the eating would be essentially and per se evil. But
this is false even for Arminius himself, since he admits that eating in itself
is a natural act having no disor-*

pseudo-bishop of Aberdeen, James Sibbald defends against Jacob Arminius that

printed in the marginal note.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 462; IA PDF page 519; BSB canvas 514]

*—der in itself. But a legislator’s prohibition does not remove the nature of
the natural act, nor does it annihilate it or transubstantiate it into disorder
itself. Such a metamorphosis would be marvelous even to Ovid. Therefore, if the
act’s naturalness and entity remain when GOD’s prohibition is added, the
disorder is one thing and the eating itself, or the act of eating, another. 3.
If act and disorder were not distinct in every sin, God could not have
commanded Abraham to slay Isaac without the mark of inconstancy and mutability.
For if a father’s slaying of his son is in and of itself the disorder itself,
God could not have commanded the disorder itself. The slaying would thus be
willed by GOD, because he commanded it, and not willed, because it is the
disorder itself. 4. The act is a real and positive being; secondly, the proper
effect and terminus of the first cause’s causation; and thirdly, a physical
being. But disorder is a privative being; secondly, not caused by God, for God
does not sin and is not the cause of sin; and thirdly, disorder is essentially
a moral being and a violation of the divine law. According to the Jesuits and
Arminians, by indifferent influx GOD caused, from the office of first cause,
the acts of the hand, lips, palate, and throat of Adam touching and eating that
fruit, for these were entitative and physical actions. But God, from no office
of first cause, caused the disorder and malice of these acts. 5. If the act
itself were prohibited per se, then the contrary act, which underlies
supernatural goodness, would also be commanded per se and would itself be
obedience per se; and the vital act in the act of believing, and the
supernaturalness and supernatural goodness in that act, would also be the same
thing. But this is repugnant to common sense. From the law of contraries, the
reason for the connection is altogether clear.

2. To Arminius’s arguments I reply. 1. Although the act itself is forbidden, it
in no way follows that act and disorder are not distinguished. Snow itself is
white; does it follow that snow is whiteness, or that snow and whiteness are
not distinguished? 2. It is true that the physical and entitative act is
formally forbidden by GOD. I acknowledge that the physical act must be avoided
because GOD prohibits it; but it does not follow that they are not
distinguished. Rather, the contrary follows. 3. What Arminius says is false:
that *the entire disorder consists in the fact that this act of eating has a
place in the number and order of human actions*. For the disorder did not
formally consist in the physical act of eating being numbered among human
actions. Suppose that God had not enacted such a law—*You shall not eat of this
fruit of the tree of knowledge*. That physical act-*

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 463; IA PDF page 520; BSB canvas 515]

*—would have had a place among human actions, and yet there would have been no
disorder at all in the number of human actions. Rather, the whole disorder
consisted in the act’s existing, not simply and insofar as it lies on the
physical plane, but in its existing after God had already testified to Adam
that he morally detested and disapproved that action. That is, it existed on
the moral plane and after it had already been prohibited by the legislator. 4.
Arminius says that *the act was not to be omitted because of an added disorder,
but was to be omitted in and for itself because it was forbidden by divine
law*. He is selling trifles. For the act’s being omitted because of its
disorder is precisely the same thing as the act’s being omitted because it is
forbidden by law and because it is disordered on the moral plane. Thus he
favors us. This proves that because the act itself is prohibited, the act and
the disorder added to it in relation to GOD’s prohibiting law are prohibited,
formally and primarily, and that Arminius, unknowingly and unwillingly,
distinguishes the act itself from its disorder—the physical entity from the
moral entity. For what, I ask, is eating’s being omitted because it is
forbidden by the law? Certainly, it is nothing other than eating’s being
omitted because it becomes morally disordered, and because the legislator
says, “By my wise good pleasure I judge that eating to be inexpedient, morally
evil, and disordered.” 5. I do not know whether the Remonstrants reveal
ignorance or a lust for contradiction in this childish argument. They say that
crimes are always done criminally. But what then? Therefore in every sin the
act is not distinguished from the disorder of the act. If they had used a
syllogism here, it would doubtless have been a syllogism in the fourth figure
and out of its mind. For they fit the malice itself, in the abstract, to the
entitative act of David’s adultery, in the concrete, which is to fit square
things to round. Malice itself cannot fail to be done maliciously, but the act
of loving one’s neighbor’s wife nevertheless has in itself a physical act
distinct from the disorder. Otherwise every disorder would not be grounded in
some entitative act; privation would not be in a deprived subject; evil would
not inhere in good; and God would not have circumscribed entitative acts by a
legal limitation. But this is to tear down immovable principles. To imply that
lust is not done lustfully and a crime not criminally would imply that one lust
is done lustfully and another not lustfully. 6. But Sibbald stole the following
from Arminius and Grevinckhoven for his barren sermon: *From our opinion it
follows that God says, take care lest while-*

*—while you commit adultery, kill, and lie, you act wickedly and violate GOD’s

“Take care that the physical action of begetting be nothing other than chaste
and fitting the marriage bed; let it not be defiled by incest or adultery.” For
by his law God circumscribes, determines, and limits our acts, which in
themselves are natural and indifferent, so that they may be holy and fitting
his law. Therefore GOD commands that the act be done free from disorder. 7.
This little Arminian argument supposes that malice consists in an undue mode and
end, and that this distinction between act and disorder obtains only in
prohibited circumstances of acts. But we are not such blockheads as to say that
the malice in eating from the tree of knowledge consists only in an undue mode
and end of eating and in prohibited circumstances. Indeed, the malice of eating
is in the act itself with respect to the substance of the act, insofar as the
act expresses a moral relation to GOD’s law. Indeed, we maintain that this
distinction obtains in every kind of sin. 7. Grevinckhoven’s claim is also
false: *Once the law is posited, GOD in no manner or respect wills the

in the malice adhering to the act, we concede it. For God can in no way approve
malice; that Holy One of Israel cannot fail to hate all impiety, as the
Scriptures abundantly testify. But if we speak of GOD’s physical volition by
which physical being pleases God—the physical complacency by which he pursues
all physical beings—it is more utterly false than false that God in no manner
or respect wills physical acts that subsist on the physical plane and in the
sphere of positive beings. For by such a volition he wills all physical acts,
just as he wills heaven, the sun, the moon, the sea, the earth, the birds, and
the fish to exist in the nature of things.

Nor need we dwell upon Grevinckhoven’s statement that *the law immediately

23. Diego Ruiz has similar statements. *Although the most formal and primary
reason for prohibiting is the malice, nevertheless what is prohibited, as the
thing prohibited, is the substance of the action, to which the malice is
necessarily and inseparably joined. And if the material action were not
prohibited, we would not be bound to avoid it. It is confirmed: when acts of
the virtues are commanded, by that very fact whatever would make the act itself
impossible is prohibited. But the material actions of sins, however much they
are abstracted from the formal character of malice, make the contrary commanded
act impossible. Therefore, when acts of the virtues are commanded, by that
very fact the material acts of sins are prohibited. Thus the command to love
GOD prohibits the material action of hatred of GOD.*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, *On the Will of GOD*, disputation 33, § 4, nos. 18

I reply, 1. The evil act is both that *by which* and that *which* is prohibited.
For because the entitative act is forbidden, malice and prohibited *ἀνομία*
(lawlessness) formally result from it. I do not sin formally by producing the
act insofar as it pertains to the physical plane, but because it so seemed good
to God that the act should be forbidden, from this comes malice. Nor is malice
the formal reason for prohibiting; on the contrary, prohibition is the formal
reason for malice. 2. Whenever an act of virtue is commanded, it is not
necessary that a contrary act incompatible with the virtuous act be formally
prohibited, either as that *by which* or that *which* is prohibited. It is
enough that the contrary act must be avoided by the physical obligation by
which we are bound to avoid whatever infallibly leads to the formally
prohibited act. Nor, because we are bound to avoid the material act on the
moral plane, is it rightly concluded that the material act on the physical
plane must be avoided by a moral obligation, except on the supposition of the
given divine law. Thus neither does it follow that it is what is prohibited,
whether as that *by which* or that *which*. The material action of hatred of
GOD essentially includes both the entitative act and its malice—namely, from
the object, GOD, who is hated—and therefore the instance is irrelevant. It is
also false that merely entitative acts make the contrary act impossible. This
is not proved unless the subject is an impossibility in a qualified sense, on
the supposition that the act of eating from the tree of knowledge already
exists. There can-*

[Chapter XXVII, Part B; printed page 466; IA PDF page 523; BSB canvas 518]

*—not be abstinence from that act while this hypothesis stands, because
everything that is, when it is, necessarily is. But absolutely Adam could have
abstained. Nor does the act of eating render the act of not eating, or
abstaining, incompatible except when the act of eating already exists.

Ruiz, at the same place. *In prohibitions the sense is not: Take care lest,
while producing an action by which you employ GOD as witness to a proposition
which you know to be false, moral malice should follow that action. For in no
way can it happen that malice does not follow an act of this kind. Therefore
the material action itself is prohibited. Likewise, the gospel prohibits the
vital actions toward which the natural person is inclined. Romans 8: the deeds
of the flesh are to be mortified.*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, at the same place, nos. 20, 21.]

I reply, 1. Because those prohibitions are addressed to people subject to the
obligation of the law, such a sense is fictitious. For it has this sense: “Take
care lest, while you perjure yourself, perjury follow upon the act of
perjuring.” For supposing that a person is subject to the law, by the very fact
that he produces an action in which he employs GOD as witness to a proposition
which he knows to be false, he formally perjures himself. But suppose someone
not subject to the law; then such a person would sufficiently take care that
malice not follow upon the materially false statement. 2. The inference,
therefore, is null, for the denied proposition is not concluded. It follows
only that the material action is prohibited on the moral plane, which we teach.
And this proves excellently that the act itself is prohibited because it so
seemed good to God, while malice results from the prohibition. 3. The gospel
prohibits vital actions—who denies it? But what had to be proved was that
actions are prohibited on the merely physical plane.
