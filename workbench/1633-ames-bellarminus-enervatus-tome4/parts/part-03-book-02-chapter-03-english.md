## Chapter III. On Original Sin

Although many other things occur in Bellarmine which are presented in such a
way on this subject that they are deservedly censured by our theologians,
there is nevertheless only one weighty controversy about which he enters into
a proper disputation with our theologians. The question is whether the
corruption of nature, and especially concupiscence, in and of itself and from
its own nature, such as it is found even in the baptized and justified, is
properly sin. The Roman Catholics deny it; we affirm it. Here, however, it
must be carefully ob-*

[Printed p. 248 | Google Books PA248 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*served—something that is not usually observed—that although the Roman
Catholics are accustomed to pose this question only about the justified, they
nevertheless openly hold the same thing about those who, after baptism, are
unrighteous because sin rules over them. For they teach that through baptism
a man is so released from the debt of original righteousness that
concupiscence which afterward reigns does not possess the character of
original sin. Bonaventure, on book 2, distinction 32, sections 6–7.
Since, however, concupiscence exists in precisely the same manner in all the
unrighteous, it clearly follows that in this cause the Roman Catholics fight
for the Pelagians, who simply deny original sin.

### Bellarmine's arguments

#### Thesis II

Bellarmine. James 1. *Four things are distinguished by the Apostle:
concupiscence, suggestion, delight, and consent. Concupiscence is not called
sin, but its offspring: concupiscence gives birth to sin.*

Protestant reply. I. The entire argument is ridiculous, because its force
rests upon these axioms: “What is not called sin by James in that passage is
not sin. What gives birth to sin is not sin.” II. The conclusion is foreign to
the matter and plainly Pelagian. For since James affirms these things
generally about all who are tempted—“each one is tempted”—if he teaches that
concupiscence is not sin, he teaches this no less about the unbaptized and
unbelievers than about the faithful and baptized. III. Concupiscence is said
to give birth to sin in the manner in which the inward rectitude of the soul
(which is righteousness) gives birth to righteousness, that is, to a
completed act. From that act the name and nature of the whole movement and of
its principle are most customarily and properly predicated.

Bellarmine. 1. *Why, then, did James distinguish the sin of the deed from sin
when it has been completed?*

Protestant reply. He does not distinguish them as different sins. Rather, sin
simply committed must be distinguished from sin ruling without repentance.

2. Bellarmine. *In Romans 7:17–18 Paul teaches plainly enough that
concupiscence in a justified man is not properly sin capable of making him
guilty.*

Protestant reply. I. By this the first argument is overturned. In that
argument concupiscence was denied to be sin because in one passage it is not
called sin. But in this chapter the same concupiscence is called sin ten
times, and once in these very words cited by Bellarmine: “the sin which dwells
in me.” Is not an argument drawn from what is said stronger than one which
depends upon what is not ex-*

[Printed p. 249 | Google Books PA249 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*pressed in explicit words? II. The question is changed sophistically, for in
place of this conclusion, “it is not properly sin,” another is substituted:
“it is not properly sin capable of making one guilty.” For the incapacity to
make one guilty with respect to a justified man, whatever sort it may be,
does not depend upon the nature of concupiscence, which alone is being
disputed, but upon God's grace and mercy. III. It is false that the Apostle
teaches this: that concupiscence is not properly sin capable of making one
guilty.

Bellarmine. 1. *He says that the movement of concupiscence is not his own
work, and on this account cannot be imputed to him.*

Protestant reply. I. He does not say that it cannot be imputed to him, but
intimates that through God's grace it is not imputed. II. He does not mean
absolutely that this movement is not his own. For in verses 15 and 19 he
acknowledges that he perpetrates it, does it, and performs it, and in that
respect “is subjected to sin,” verse 14. Rather, he means that comparatively
it is not his work, and according to that regenerated part which effectually
dissented from it.

Bellarmine. 2. *The Apostle adds that evil of this kind is not in himself but
in his flesh.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine adds this. For the Apostle plainly says the
contrary: “the sin which dwells in me”; “in me,” that is, “in my flesh,” and
so forth.

Bellarmine. 3. *Although in many passages of divine Scripture “flesh” without
doubt means all the powers, both the higher and the lower, corrupted through
sin, nevertheless in Romans 7 we deny that “flesh” is taken for the whole man,
and assert that it is taken for only the other part, which comprises the lower
powers.*

Protestant reply. “Flesh” in Romans 7 must necessarily be taken for the proper
seat of evil concupiscence. The Scholastics themselves, however, acknowledge
that concupiscence exists in all the powers of the soul, so that the will
itself is corrupted, and consequently the other powers. Alexander of Hales,
part 2, question 105, member 7, article 3. Bonaventure, on book 2, distinction
32, also testifies in explicit words that concupiscence, as it is taken in
Romans 7, exists even in the “rational power,” that is, in the will. He also
adds that “concupiscence is formally in the rational part.”

Bellarmine. 4. *If the whole man were meant by “flesh,” Paul would not truly
say, “It is not I who do it.”*

Protestant reply. On the contrary, unless the whole man in one respect were
meant by “flesh,” and the whole man also, but in another respect, by “spirit,”
Paul would not truly say, “I do it, and I do not do it,” verses 15 and 17. The
whole man is the principle *which* acts in an operation both evil and good;
but the principle *by which* he acts in an evil operation is corruption, and
in a good operation, grace.

Bellarmine. 5. *What is the purpose of that qualification, “in me,” that is,
“in*

[Printed p. 250 | Google Books PA250 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*my flesh,” if the evil about which he speaks is in the whole man?*

Protestant reply. Its purpose is to show in what respect even a man sanctified
by grace is still carnal and evil of himself.

Bellarmine. 6. *In explicit words the Apostle distinguishes the flesh from the
mind.*

Protestant reply. The corruption called flesh is distinguished from the mind,
not simply, but insofar as the mind consents to the will of God and is hindered
in its good purpose by corruption, which (while the mind wills the good)
appears especially in the appetite for carnal things.

Bellarmine. 7. *“Flesh” in chapter 7 is the same as “mortal body” in chapter 6
and “body of death” in chapter 7, verse 24.*

Protestant reply. It plainly means the same as “body of death” and “body of
sin,” that is, the old man, chapter 6, verse 6, but not the same as “mortal
body.”

Bellarmine. 8. *The outward man is the corruptible body, and the inward man is
the rational soul,* 2 Corinthians 4:16.

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine previously conceded that in many passages the
whole man is meant by “flesh,” but denied that this was true in Romans 7. Now,
however, from the sense which these words have in one passage, he concludes
that the same must necessarily be admitted in Romans 7. II. In 2 Corinthians
the subject is not the inward man acting morally, as in Romans, but the man
who is suffering.

Bellarmine. 9. *When “flesh” is used in a bad sense, this word is employed only
about the ungodly.*

Protestant reply. The Apostle testifies that this is openly false in verse 14:
“I am carnal”; and Galatians 5:17: “The flesh lusts against the Spirit, so that
you do not do whatever things you would.”

Bellarmine. 10. *If concupiscence is in the will, it is in that will in which
by its movement it precedes the judgment of reason, in which manner it is not
capable of sin properly so called.*

Protestant reply. Original sin precedes the judgment of reason and the evil
act of the will as a cause precedes its effect. Bellarmine therefore concludes
most beautifully that original sin is not sin properly so called, either in
the justified or even in the unregenerate.

3. Bellarmine. Romans 8:1. *There is no condemnation for those who are in
Christ Jesus; that is, in the justified there is nothing worthy of
condemnation, or there is no cause of condemnation.*

Protestant reply. Although there is no condemnation for those who are in
Christ Jesus, there is nevertheless desert and a cause of condemnation.
Otherwise there would be no matter in them for absolution or for remission to
be continued and renewed. There is no condemnation for such persons on
account of the mercy of God, who removes liability; but in them there is a
cause of condemnation on account of the demerit of both original and actual
sin,

[Printed p. 251 | Google Books PA251 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

the confession of which is necessary to escape condemnation. The Master of the Scholastics
answers in nearly the same manner, book 2, distinction 23: “Original sin is
remitted in baptism, in that its liability is discharged and its dominion
is taken away; it passes away in liability but remains in actuality.”

Bellarmine. 1. *Liability is properly called sin, or the form of sin.
Therefore, when it is removed, nothing remains which possesses the true
character of sin.*

Protestant reply. That liability which is removed by remission is neither
properly sin nor the form of sin. For it is not prohibited by God's law, nor
is it contrary to the law, but it exists by force of the law. Nevertheless, it
is called sin because of that inseparable connection which (by the common
law) it has with sin; and by some it is called the form of sin, just as proper
attributes are often put in place of forms.

Bellarmine. 2. *Liability is inseparable from sin worthy of eternal
damnation. For it is not merely an ordination to punishment, but a worthiness,
or deserving, of punishment.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine rightly observes here that liability has, as it
were, two parts: worthiness of punishment and ordination to punishment. The
true solution of this sophism depends upon that distinction, because the
worthiness of punishment is not removed while sin remains, but ordination to
punishment is removed in the remission of remaining sin. Liability,
therefore, is inseparable with respect to worthiness of punishment; but with
respect to ordination to punishment, which arises from the law, it can be
separated through evangelical grace.

Bellarmine. 3. *A relation cannot be separated from its proximate foundation;
and liability is a relation whose proximate foundation is sin.*

Protestant reply. I. The Roman Catholics separate a relation from its
foundation when they teach that liability to punishment remains after guilt
has been remitted. II. As liability signifies an actual ordination to
punishment, its proximate foundation is God's justice pursuing sin with
vengeance. This foundation, however, does not remain after the grace of
remission in Christ.

Bellarmine. 4. *It is certain that God never remits liability while sin
remains, because God's judgment is according to truth.*

Protestant reply. The contrary is most certain: concerning what does not
remain as the object of remission, it cannot be said that it is remitted.
Therefore, sin remains while remission remains. Now, when God remits, he does
not judge that sin does not exist, but by grace he judges that it is not to be
pursued with vengeance, and this judgment is true.

Bellarmine. 5. *That particle “therefore” (“there is therefore no
condemnation,” and so forth) signifies that this proposition is deduced from
the preceding words, in which Paul had said that concupiscence is not imputed.
But involuntary movements are not sins and cannot be imputed to a man.*

[Printed p. 252 | Google Books PA252 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. The deduction of that conclusion in Romans 8:1, “there is
therefore now no condemnation,” properly flows from what the Apostle had
taught in chapter 5 about justification to life through Christ, which removes
condemnation to death—a doctrine he had vindicated from objection in chapters
6 and 7. This is also clearly indicated in chapter 8, verse 34: “Who is he who
condemns? It is Christ who died,” and so forth. The basis of the deduction
which Bellarmine establishes from chapter 7 was refuted above.

Bellarmine. *In baptism we are freed from original sin.*

Protestant reply. In the celebration of baptism, liberation from every sin
and from every punishment of sin is sealed; but the actual effect of this
liberation itself is not completed in one moment.

Bellarmine. *Concupiscence is an effect and punishment of original sin;
therefore, it is not original sin itself.*

Protestant reply. Habitual concupiscence is not an effect or punishment of
original sin dwelling in the same man, but is the same thing as original sin.

Bellarmine. *The reasoning concerning death and concupiscence is the same; but
death is the punishment of original sin.*

Protestant reply. I. Death is the punishment of sin, whether original or
actual: “In the day you eat of it, dying you shall die.” II. The reasoning
concerning concupiscence is not the same, for we do not read it said or
written in the law: “In the day you sin, lusting you shall lust.”

6. Bellarmine. *If a man were not born from Adam and yet had the concupiscence
which he now has, concupiscence could not properly be called sin in him.
Therefore, concupiscence is not properly sin in and of itself and from its own
nature.*

Protestant reply. I. From the same supposition it could be proved that
concupiscence is not a punishment, though Bellarmine has just now laid this
down as certain and ascertained. II. By an impossible supposition we say that
if a man were not born from Adam, yet were subject to the law and possessed
concupiscence contrary to the law, it should properly be called sin.

Bellarmine. *It would be something altogether natural.*

Protestant reply. I. Why more than original righteousness, which Bellarmine
denies was natural to Adam, though he concedes that Adam was created with it
in *On the Grace of the First Man*? II. Speaking consistently from a false
supposition, it would not be absurd for sin to be natural.

7. Bellarmine. *The first movements of concupiscence which the mind resists
are not sin; therefore, neither is the propensity toward such movements. The
antecedent is proved because movements of this kind are not in our power.*

Protestant reply. I. This twist is introduced on behalf of the Pelagians to
prove that there is no original sin at all, because its first movements

[Printed p. 253 | Google Books PA253 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

are not in our power. II. Those first movements are of the same character as original
sin, which Lombard in book 2, distinction 23, teaches to be necessary “because
it cannot be avoided.”

8. Bellarmine. *If concupiscence is true sin and always remains in a man,
however justified, it follows that Christ did not truly redeem and free us
from sins, but only imputatively.*

Protestant reply. I. Truly and imputatively are not opposites. 2. If evil
concupiscence were always going to remain in the justified, then something of
this kind would follow. But we say that when its liability has been removed,
concupiscence is actually weakened and remains in the justified only until
they are brought into the perfect possession of those goods to which a right
has been obtained for them by Christ.

### Our arguments

9. Protestant. Romans 6 and 7. The concupiscence which is in the regenerate
is often called sin.

Bellarmine. 1. *It is called sin improperly, by the figure of metonymy,
because it is the cause of sin if it is obeyed.*

Protestant reply. I. It cannot even be conceived how something existing as an
inward habit could be the cause of sin and not be sin. An inward cause
inclining one to act well is good. II. It is said ambiguously and
sophistically to be the cause of sin “if it is obeyed.” For if obedience is
taken strictly, all natural and animal inclinations, even in the first
creation, were on this condition causes of sin and, according to Bellarmine's
rhetoric, could have been called sin. III. The Apostle himself shows plainly
enough in Romans 6 and 7 that concupiscence is sin properly so called. For he
not only repeatedly calls it sin, but calls it “not good, evil, to be hated,
warring with the law of God, to be crucified, mortified, and destroyed, and
making the one in whom it exists miserable.”

Bellarmine. *These things are rightly said about concupiscence, although it is
not properly sin, because it is a certain corruption inclining toward evil.*

Protestant reply. If a moral corruption warring with the law, for the
crucifixion and mortification of which Christ himself died on the cross, is
not properly sin, then Scripture has not explained to us what the proper
character of any habitual sin is.

10. Protestant. Romans 7:7. The Apostle openly testifies that concupiscence is
sin prohibited by the law.

Bellarmine. *Concupiscence is taken there for the voluntary act of coveting,
which is prohibited by the law.*

Protestant reply. I. An act does indeed seem to be meant there, but not only
one voluntary in the sense Bellarmine intends, for even the first acts of
indwelling sin are openly desig-*

[Printed p. 254 | Google Books PA254 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*nated there. II. In the same sense in which actual concupiscence is called
sin, that indwelling sin from which it proceeds is also so called in verses 7
and 8.

11. Protestant. Romans 7:14. With respect to indwelling sin, the Apostle
complains that he is carnal, subject to sin, and unable to observe God's
spiritual law accurately.

Bellarmine. *Paul does not mean to signify that the spiritual law is not kept
by the justified insofar as is needed to avoid sin, but that it is not kept up
to a certain perfection, which is the end of the precept and therefore is not
properly in the precept.*

Protestant reply. I. This gloss directly contradicts the text, for the text
says that the law is not observed by Paul because he is subjected to sin.
Bellarmine affirms that the law is kept insofar as is needed to avoid sin. II.
The inward end and perfection of every precept is properly contained in the
precept itself. But that observance which excludes every corruption of the
flesh and of sin is the inward perfection of the spiritual and perfect law.

12. Protestant. Romans 7:16. By not willing concupiscence, Paul consented to
the law. Therefore, the law did not will—that is, prohibited—concupiscence to
exist in Paul.

Bellarmine. 1. *If we extend the precept “You shall not covet” to involuntary
movements, they are contrary to the law only as it indicates the end, not as
it commands the means, and therefore they are not sins.*

Protestant reply. The Apostle explicitly explains himself about the deed,
which is the commanded means: “What I hate, this I do.”

Bellarmine. 2. *If we restrict that precept to voluntary movements alone, we
reply that Paul's consent with the law does not consist in Paul and the law
willing or not willing the same thing, but in Paul willing or not willing what
the law commands him to will or not to will.*

Protestant reply. Here we have a sophistical distinction without any
difference between its parts, for the law neither wills nor does not will
anything at all except what it commands men to will or not to will.

Bellarmine. 3. *It could be said more easily that the law does not will
concupiscence as an object and, similarly, that Paul's mind does not will it
as an object. In the same way, if someone killed a man entirely without
intending to, he could truly say, “What I did not will, I did; and therefore I
consent to the law which says, ‘You shall not kill,’ because it is good.” Yet
it ought not thereby to be inferred that the precept “You shall not kill”
extends to entirely involuntary homicide.*

Protestant reply. This can indeed be said more easily than truly or soundly.
For I. A certain appearance of a distinction is most ineptly put before the
unwary between willing or not willing something as an object and some other,
I know not what, manner of willing something—as if anyone willed anything not
as an object, and as if what someone wills were not for him an object of
willing. 2. The comparison devised cunningly enough to create a false appear-*

[Printed p. 255 | Google Books PA255 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*ance does not fit. For because Paul did not will concupiscence, he proves from
this that he consents to the law in the good which it demands, because the
evil of concupiscence and the good of the law are opposed. But no such
opposition is found in the example Bellarmine adduces.

14. Protestant. Romans 7:23. Concupiscence is called a law waging war against
God's law and making man captive to the law of sin.

Bellarmine. 1. *Many things which are not sins oppose the law, such as a man
who sins, an unjust law,* and so forth.

Protestant reply. Nothing can oppose the divine law without sin. A man opposes
it insofar as he sins; an unjust law, insofar as it is the vicious act of a
sinning legislator. Therefore, if concupiscence opposes God's law, someone
sins in it. Who, however, would that be besides the man who covets? 2. Inward
concupiscence, as something belonging to man, is said to oppose God's law, not
as a man or an external law.

Bellarmine. 1. *Concupiscence opposes the law of the mind as a contrary law
impelling toward evil, in the way that the devil and whatever incites to
transgression of the law also oppose the law.*

Protestant reply. Neither the devil nor anything else in and of itself incites
to transgression of the law unless it either sins in itself or contains sin.

15. Protestant. Romans 7:24. Concupiscence is called a body which makes man
miserable.

Bellarmine. *In Scripture unhappiness and misery are generally attributed to
punishment rather than guilt.*

Protestant reply. No one can doubt that unhappiness is a punishment. But it is
sufficiently apparent from verse 23 that by the “body of death” the cause of
unhappiness is meant, which is guilt alone. There the same thing is called “a
law making him captive to the law of sin in his members.”

16. Protestant. The movements of concupiscence are unlawful; therefore, they
are sins.

Bellarmine. *They are called unlawful because it is not lawful to give consent
to them.*

Protestant reply. There is no one who has disordered concupiscence in himself
who does not give it some consent. For through concupiscence the will itself
is infected “with marvelous perversity and conflicts with itself,” as
Bellarmine himself acknowledges in book 6, chapter 10. 2. It is unlawful not
only to consent to evil, but also to possess an inclination or propensity to
such consent, because all our powers ought to be borne toward the good.

17. Protestant. What in and of itself draws toward evil is prohibited by the
same law by which evil itself is prohibited. But concupiscence in and of
itself draws toward evil, James 1. Therefore—

Bellarmine. *What in and of itself draws toward evil is not considered
properly prohibited except insofar as it is in our*

[Printed p. 256 | Google Books PA256 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*power to assent to it or reject it.*

Protestant reply. This was once within man's choice and power. But the loss of
power contracted through our fault does not remove the character of guilt.

18. Protestant. God hates the concupiscence which remains in the baptized,
and we ought to hate it.

Bellarmine. *It is hated by God and ought to be hated by us because it incites
to sin.*

Protestant reply. God does not properly hate anything except what is worthy of
hatred. But no disposition of man can be worthy of hatred except sin. Evil of
punishment as such is not an object of divine hatred.

19. Protestant. What disagrees with the norm of the law is sin. But
concupiscence in the regenerate disagrees with the norm of the law. Therefore—

Bellarmine. 1. *The proposition is denied unless it is taken in a broad and
material manner. For the tempting devil, the sinning man, the will, and the
human action disagree with the norm of the law and are not sin.*

Protestant reply. Insofar as all these things have disagreement with the law
in themselves, they also have sin properly so called in themselves. Our
proposition is therefore confirmed by these instances.

Bellarmine. 2. *Not every lawlessness or departure from the law is sin.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine himself acknowledges that sin is accurately
defined by *anomia*. For he speaks as follows in *On the Loss of Grace*, book
2, chapter 18: “Sin could not be defined more correctly and briefly than it
was defined by St. John in these words: ‘sin is iniquity,’ or, as the Greek has
it more clearly, *hamartia estin anomia* [sin is lawlessness].” 1 John 3. But
an accurate definition is reciprocal. Therefore, every *anomia*, or departure
from the law, is sin.
