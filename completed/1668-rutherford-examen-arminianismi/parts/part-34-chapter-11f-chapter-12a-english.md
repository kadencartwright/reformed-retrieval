It is asked: Does the efficacy of grace depend upon a congruent calling? That
is: Are human beings converted to God because God applies a persuasion adapted
to such a person, so disposed, at such a place and time, which he foreknows
will move the will to obey God, even though that persuasion could in itself
and absolutely have been resisted? Bellarmine, Fonseca, Suárez, Valentia, and
Arminius, *Antiperk.*, page 665, and the Remonstrants, *Collatio Hagiensis*,
page 311, say yes, although after a more mature judgment and more thoroughly
reconsidering the matter they abandon that opinion. *Remonstrant Confession*,
chapter 13, thesis 10. We deny it.

1. For every persuasion, of whatever kind it may be, is Pelagian grace; and it
can no more heal the corrupted will than golden words can restore sight to a
blind person and life to a dead person.

2. There can be no adaptation to any disposition of the natural human being,
who regards the Gospel as sheer foolishness.

3. For this opinion rests upon the foolish and profane fiction of Middle
Knowledge.

4. For it resolves the efficacy of grace into Free Choice, which can spurn all
persuasions.

5. For it overturns the Dominion of divine providence over human wills and
supposes that God cannot convert either more or fewer people than God
foreknows before every act of his will.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 498 | PDF p. 549]

6. For it casts the cause of non-conversion upon God, who does not call
congruently, which they condemn in our Theologians.

7. According to the Scriptures, the reason why human beings are not converted
is not that they are not called congruently, but that they are not among
Christ's sheep, are not drawn by the Father, have not been given by God a heart
to understand, and have not heard and learned from the Father, etc.

CHAPTER TWELVE,

ON THE

Justification of the Sinner.

ROMANS 3:28.

“We therefore conclude that a human being is justified by faith, apart from
the works of the Law.”

It is asked: Are we justified by our works? Hence the state of the Question
must be fixed. The following points, however, are agreed. 1. A human being is
not properly justified by himself but by God. 2. God's free love in Christ
concurs in some manner. 3. Faith is prerequisite, although there is controversy
about the manner in which faith is present. 4. Good works cannot be absent from
one who is truly justified. 5. Full remission is required, because nothing
defiled enters the kingdom of heaven. 6. A human being cannot, from his own
resources,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 499 | PDF p. 550 | split word joined: conferre]

contribute as much as suffices for the satisfaction for sins required in
Justification. 7. A human being truly and really becomes just from being unjust
and ungodly, and is not just by imagination and bare opinion; although it is
asked where that Righteousness is—whether it is inherent in Christ and imputed
to us, or inherent and infused in us. 8. Satisfaction rendered to the Law is
included in the justification of the sinner.

First, therefore, it is asked: Are we justified by faith, that is, by a
trusting reliance upon Christ? Hence the question is not about the faith by
which we assent to all of God's Word as true, but about the faith by which we
rest trustingly upon Christ, or upon God through Christ the Mediator, as the
*object which*, and upon the Word of promise, as a good, as the *object by
which*.

2. Are we justified by faith alone? That is: Is it alone, though not in a
solitude of existence—as though existing faith were devoid of hope, love, and
a sincere endeavor by God's grace to please God in everything and to obey him—
and does it justify in this way? For such faith would be dead and empty and
would perform no action concerning Christ apprehended by trust. Rather, is it
by faith alone in a solitude of office, a solitude of the act of apprehending
Christ and his righteousness, and a solitude of organic causality, so that
hope, love, all operations that dispose us preparatorily for justification,
and all good

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 500 | PDF p. 551 | split word joined: opera]

works performed either by the powers of nature alone or even with the aid of
divine grace are excluded from the character of a condition, merit, or
instrument?

3. Are we justified by faith alone? That is, not by any inherent force, merit,
excellence, or worthiness of faith, but correlatively, because faith
apprehends “Jehovah our righteousness,” Jeremiah 23:6—namely, Christ, who is
our righteousness imputatively.

Are we, then, justified by works? 1. Not insofar as works are performed by the
powers of nature without the aid of grace. Thus the Papists think that these
must be called “works of the Law,” by which Paul denies that we are justified.

2. Are we justified by works, not insofar as they are considered in
themselves, nor insofar as they merit justification (thus Vorstius speaks in
the *Apology on Article 24*, pages 34–35), but by works insofar as works are a
certain part of faith, or as though they coalesce with faith itself into one
particular result? Or, as the Remonstrants put it in their *Confession*,
chapter 10: “Insofar as faith necessarily has good works joined to it and a
reformation of the whole life instituted according to the precepts of Jesus
Christ; indeed, insofar as by a natural property it includes the obedience of
faith, to which obedience faith neither can nor ought rightly to be opposed”?
This is what Socinus says in *On the Savior*, book 4, chapter 11. And thus
faith and works—that is, obedience to Christ's precepts—as the form of faith,
justify us before God.

3. Are we justified by faith as a good work and as our partial Righteousness,
graciously reckoned by God as full Righteousness? The Papists,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 501 | PDF p. 552 | split word joined: Remonstrantes]

Remonstrants, and Socinians hold that we are justified by works considered in
that manner and by faith as a good work. We deny it.

1. We are justified by faith apart from works. Romans 3:28: “Not from works of
righteousness which we have done.” Titus 3:8. And Galatians 2:16: “Through
faith.” Romans 5:1: “From faith.” “Only through faith,” Galatians 5:6. Romans
3:24: “Freely,” *gratis*. Luke 8:50: “Do not be afraid; only believe, and she
will be saved.”

2. We are justified in the same way and by the same rationale as Abraham, the
father of all believers, is justified. But Romans 4:3 says: “Abraham believed,
and it was imputed to him for righteousness”—not from debt, that is, not from
works even following faith, because then Abraham would have something in
which to glory. Therefore. The rationale for the major premise is that it was
written not only for Abraham's sake but also for ours, verses 23–24.

3. For Christ “was made sin for us, so that we might become the Righteousness
of God in him,” 2 Corinthians 5:21. “He was made a curse for us,” Galatians
3:13–14, “so that the blessing of Abraham might exist among the Gentiles in
Christ Jesus.” First Corinthians 1:30: “He was made for us by the Father
wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and redemption.”

4. For the promise of salvation is not made to the person who fulfills the Law
but to the one who believes. “And therefore the inheritance is from faith, so
that it might be according to grace, for the promise to all the offspring to
be established,” Romans 4:16.

5. For Romans 5:19 says: “Just as through the disobedience of one many were
condemned, so through Christ's

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 502 | PDF p. 553 | split word joined: obedientiam]

obedience many are constituted righteous.”

6. There is no peace of conscience, boldness, confidence, or hope of glory
except from the act of believing in Christ, who fulfilled the Law for us.

7. For glory is detracted from Christ's merits and death, and from the mercy
and grace shown to miserable sinners, if we are justified not by faith in
Christ alone but also by our works.

But it can be asked: Are we not justified by works performed with the aid of
the grace of regeneration, as the Papists hold, or by the obedience of works
insofar as it is the fruit, offspring, and natural property of faith, as the
Remonstrants teach? *Confession*, chapter 10, theses 2–3, and chapter 18,
thesis 3; and *Apology*, chapter 10. We deny it. 1. For this is Paul's entirely
valid inference, Romans 3:9: “Both Jews and Greeks are all under sin,” etc.
Verse 19: “Every mouth is stopped, and the whole world is liable to
condemnation because of sin.” Therefore, no one is justified by works. From
this it follows that the righteousness and obedience of works by which we are
justified before God must be free from sin and of such a kind that it absolves
a human being from condemnation and every liability for sin. Yet no mortal
lives who, even through the grace of regeneration, renders an obedience of
works free from every sin. For “cursed is everyone who does not remain in all
the things written in the book of the Law, to

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 503 | PDF p. 554]

do them.” Deuteronomy 27:26; Galatians 3:10; Proverbs 20:9; 1 John 1:8.

2. For if justification were from works performed with the aid of grace which
does not at all determine the free will to good works but leaves it to its own
absolute indifference, then a human being would have something in which to
glory before God. For the one furnished with grace that by no means separates
the believer from the nonbeliever and the one who works from the one who does
not work must glory not in grace but in Free Choice. For according to the
doctrine of the Jesuits and Arminians, Free Choice makes the separation, and
therefore also supplies just and suitable material for glorying before God in
nature, not in God's grace.

3. For according to the Jesuits and Arminians, these words of Romans 4:4–5
would have the following sense: “But to the one who works”—that is, who
fulfills the righteousness of the Law without any sin and without his own
violation of the Law—“the reward” of Justification, Remission, and eternal
life “is not imputed from grace but from debt,” that is, according to that
perfect rule of the Moral Law by which eternal life and remission of sins are
owed from merit and justice. “But to the one who does not work”—that is, who
does not fulfill the Law of legal righteousness exactly and by his own
powers—“but believes in him who justifies the ungodly,” that is, who relies
trustingly upon Christ to obtain absolution and remission of sins, “his faith
is imputed for righteousness,” that is, he is justified by

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 504 | PDF p. 555 | split word joined: gratiosâ]

gracious acceptance, not by his own merit. From this the following points
result. 1. If anyone brings forth good works by the powers of grace, then the
reward of remission is owed to him not from God's grace but from debt, and the
Adversaries' justification is not from God's grace but from legal debt.

2. It follows that God gives grace by which the Law is fulfilled perfectly and
according to exact strictness; and in this way justification would indeed be
from grace and at the same time from a secondary and qualified debt. But no
one receives such grace as makes him free from sin.

3. It follows that before the fall Adam could not have been justified by the
works of the Law and by legal debt, because the grace for fulfilling the Law
was supernatural grace, as the Arminians and Socinians teach with the Papists.
Hence all the regenerate are justified by grace under the Covenant of Grace
in the same way that Adam was justified by grace under the covenant of works.
And this certainly can neither be refuted nor evaded, because they teach that
God would have been unjust and cruel if he had given Adam a law impossible for
the powers of pure nature. Therefore, in order to be a just Lawgiver, he was
bound to give supernatural righteousness by which the Law and justification
through the law would be possible for Adam and would not exceed his powers.
They sing the same refrain about fallen humanity: namely, that God commands a
human being nothing impossible. Therefore, in order that the same Law under
the

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 505 | PDF p. 556 | split word joined: Fœdere]

Covenant of Grace be possible, God is bound to give grace sufficient in its
proper degrees, by which a human being, working through supernatural grace,
could obtain justification by works no less than Adam once did.

4. For if we are justified by works of the Law, though performed by the powers
of grace, the distinction between the Covenant of Works, or the Law, and the
Covenant of Grace, or the Gospel, is empty. For Galatians 3:17 says: “Now I say
this: the Law which arose four hundred and thirty years later did not render
useless, so as to abolish the promise, the covenant previously ratified by God
and having reference to Christ.” Verse 18: “For if the inheritance is from the
Law, it is no longer from the promise. But God graciously bestowed the
inheritance upon Abraham through the promise.” Hence a human being is in no
way justified by doing good works unless he were to fulfill the Law perfectly—
“Do this and you will live”—and “remain in all the things written in the Book
of the Law, to do them,” verse 10. And thus Christ died for him in vain and was
made a curse and a malediction. The Papists and Arminians remove this
distinction, full of the sweetest consolation, when they teach that the Gospel
is nothing other than a new Law or the Law made perfect—indeed, the very same
Law, but made easy and possible for us by the grace of the Spirit, so that we
can be justified through it. Bellarmine, *On Justification*, book 4, chapters
3–4. It is indeed true that the same moral doctrine is

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 506 | PDF p. 557]

in the Law and in the Gospel broadly understood. But in the manner of
justifying, the two Covenants—the Law and the Gospel—contend on opposite
fronts. For the Gospel justifies by believing, not by working through grace;
for in the latter way grace would establish and strengthen legal justification
and render it possible and easy. Rather, it justifies by believing the one who
promises, through which alone the inheritance of life is obtained, and not
through works of the Law performed with the aid of grace. For then the Gospel
promises would not be ratified except on the hypothesis that we perfectly
fulfill the Law through God's grace. That is not the Gospel and the Covenant
of Grace, whose one condition is faith in Jesus Christ.

5. For Christ's righteousness is imputed to us, precisely because Christ died
for us—that is, in our place and stead—as Sponsor and Surety, was made a curse
for us, and sustained in himself the malediction of the violated Law and the
punishment due to us. Hence a surety-righteousness necessarily flowed from
Christ to us, that is, the kind which a Sponsor furnishes for insolvent
debtors. This, then, is imputed righteousness:
