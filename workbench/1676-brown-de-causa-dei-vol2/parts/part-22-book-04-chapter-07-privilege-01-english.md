# CHAPTER VII (continued)

1. God himself promulgated these laws and uttered these ten words. Exodus
20:1: *Then God spoke all these words, saying, I am Jehovah your God, who
brought you out of the land of Egypt*, and so forth. The one who pronounced
these laws was therefore Jehovah, who brought them out of the land of Egypt.
He was the one of whom the people had said in Exodus 19:8, *Whatever Jehovah
says, we will do*; and *Jehovah, who was to descend on the third day before the
eyes of the whole people* (verses 11 and 20); and the one who had said to Moses
in verse 21, *Warn this people, lest they break through to Jehovah to see*, and
so forth. The people themselves, moved by that terrible sound, said to Moses in
Exodus 20:19, *You speak with us—and do not let God speak with us, lest we die.*
They therefore heard God himself speaking, and thereby testified that God
himself had promulgated this law. God himself likewise testifies to this in
verse 22: *Then Jehovah said to Moses, Thus shall you say to the children of
Israel: You have seen that I spoke with you from heaven.*

So too Deuteronomy 4:10–15: *On that day when you stood before Jehovah your God
in Horeb, when Jehovah said to me, Gather this people to me, that I may set my
words before them to be heard—then Jehovah spoke to you from the midst of that
fire; you heard the voice of words, but perceived no likeness, apart from the
voice; and he himself declared to you his covenant, which he commanded you to
observe, those ten words—while Jehovah commanded me at that time to teach you
statutes and judgments—you perceived no likeness on the day when Jehovah spoke
to you*, and so forth.

Likewise Deuteronomy 5:2–5, 22–25: *Jehovah our God made a covenant with us in
Horeb—Jehovah spoke to you face to face on that mountain—I standing between
Jehovah and you—saying, I am Jehovah your God—Jehovah spoke these words—and he
went no further—now when you had heard the voice—you said, Behold, Jehovah our
God has shown us his glory and his greatness, and we have heard his voice from
the midst of the fire. Today we have seen God speaking with a human being—the
great fire will consume us if we continue to hear the voice of Jehovah our
God—for what flesh is there that has heard the voice of the living God speaking
from the midst of the fire as we have?*, and so forth. And Deuteronomy 10:4:
*And he wrote upon those tablets—the same ten words that Jehovah had spoken to
you on the mountain from the midst of the fire on the day of the assembly.*

From these passages it is readily gathered, 1, that God—whether the Father or
the Son is immaterial to the present purpose, although we judge it far more
probable that it was the Son, as was said elsewhere, whose voice then shook the
earth (Hebrews 12:26)—

[Printed p. 88 | IA PDF 96 | IA leaf 96 | IA decausadei02brow]

promulgated this law in a special manner, and gave this law in a manner far
different from all the others he had given. For he enacted no law in a similar
manner. He himself pronounced this one and no other, since he did not go on to
pronounce anything more than those ten words, but commanded Moses to teach them
the judgments and statutes, that is, the judicial and ceremonial commands.

2. It follows that these ten words must be placed far above all other precepts
and that they contain a perpetual law. For to what purpose would Jehovah
himself have pronounced them, unless to indicate their excellence, necessity,
and perpetuity? Notwithstanding these considerations, the opponents raise
certain objections.

*Objection 1.* It is doubtful whether or not God himself promulgated this law.

*Answer.* 1. The sacred text puts this beyond all doubt, as we have already
seen. It is further evident from Deuteronomy 4:32–33, 36: *Now inquire of the
ancient times that were before you, from the day when God created man upon the
earth, from one end of heaven to the other, whether any event like this very
great one has happened, or whether anything like it has been heard. Has any
people heard the voice of God speaking from the midst of fire as you heard it,
and lived?—From heaven he caused you to hear his voice*, and so forth. This is
therefore recorded as something rare and unusual, indeed as something utterly
extraordinary to which no parallel can be given since the foundations of the
world were laid. We also see that the force and emphasis of Moses' argument
lies in the fact that God himself spoke these things to them with his living
voice.

In Deuteronomy 33:3, *when Jehovah came forth from Sinai surrounded by myriads
of holy ones, from his right hand came for them the fire of decree*, or *a
fiery law*, as Münster and the Vulgate version render the Hebrew אש דת, *fire
of decree.* Furthermore, Jeremiah 7:22–23 says: *I did not speak with your
forefathers, nor command them, on the day I brought them out of the land of
Egypt, concerning matters of burnt offering and sacrifices. But this thing I
commanded them, saying, Hearken to my voice, and I will be your God and you
will be my people.* Compare Exodus 19:5: *Now therefore, if you diligently
attend to my voice and keep my covenant, you will surely be my peculiar
possession above all other peoples.* God's voice is distinguished from the law
of sacrifices, and it can denote nothing but those ten words, which are often
called the covenant. God himself therefore pronounced this voice in a peculiar
manner. He did not give the law about burnt offerings and other sacrifices
immediately, but through Moses, whom he commanded to teach it to the people.

Let us hear Dr. Burmann himself in *Synopsis*, book 4, chapter 4, section 2:

> The legislator—or, rather, the commander—was Jehovah himself, who *spoke all
> those words* (Exodus 20:1). It is exceedingly clear that he was none other
> than God's Son, that Angel of the covenant who had long been accustomed to
> dwell in the church, converse with the patriarchs, and already then perform
> all the things that required God to be present. For in Exodus 3:1–2, 12 that
> Angel appears in the bush by the same mountain and declares himself to be
> that God whom the people would serve at that mountain. Stephen too testifies
> in Acts 7:38 that it was the same Angel from whom Moses received *living
> words on the mountain*, as do Psalm 69:9, 19 and likewise the apostle in
> Hebrews 12:16.

2. If it was not the Lord Jehovah who promulgated this law with his own voice,
we ask: who, then, was it?

*Objection 2.* They say that it was an angel: Acts 7:38, *He was with the Angel
speaking to him on Mount Sinai*; and verse 53, *You received the law through
the arrangement of angels*; Galatians 3:19, *the law was ordained through
angels by the hand of a mediator*; Hebrews 2:2, *If the word spoken through
angels was firm.*

[Printed p. 89 | IA PDF 97 | IA leaf 97 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Answer.* 1. We grant that it was an Angel, but an uncreated Angel: namely, the
Angel of the covenant (Malachi 3:1–2) and that Angel of the Lord who appeared
in the wilderness of Mount Sinai in the flaming fire of the bush (Acts 7:30).
He, however, was Jehovah, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and so forth,
and the Lord who said to Moses, *Take the sandals from your feet, for the place
where you stand is holy ground*; who descended to deliver his people out of
Egypt; and who sent Moses (Acts 7:31–35).

We grant that he was the Angel who now speaks from heaven, whose voice then
shook the earth, and who announced, *Yet once more I will shake not only the
earth but also heaven* (Hebrews 12:25–26; Haggai 2:7). He was the Angel in whom
God's name is and who will not bear iniquity (Exodus 23:20–21), that is, the
one who is true God. See the Dutch annotations. He is called Jehovah, whom they
ought to worship, and who would bless their bread and water (verse 25). He was
Christ, whom they tempted (1 Corinthians 10:9).

2. That he was not a created angel is evident from these considerations.
First, he is repeatedly and expressly called Jehovah, as we have seen. See also
Psalm 68:17–19: *God desired these mountains for his dwelling—the chariots of
God are twenty thousand—the Lord is among them, the Lord of Sinai dwelling in
the sanctuary*, and so forth.

Second, we never read that the law was given by angels, but only that it was
received through their arrangement, ordained through angels, and that the word
was spoken through angels. It is never called the angels' law, and nowhere are
the angels called legislators.

Third, this Angel speaks in his own name, saying, *I am Jehovah*, and so forth.
Created angels, who are only ministers and messengers, would not speak in their
own name in this way. Messengers always speak in the name of their lords, not
their own, as do gospel ministers (2 Corinthians 5:20). The prophets used this
formula, *Thus says Jehovah*, or one like it. Good angels always take care that
the honor owed to God is not given to themselves (Judges 13:16; Revelation
19:10; 22:9), knowing very well that God is Jehovah and a jealous God who will
not give his glory to another (Isaiah 42:8).

Fourth, a created angel does not send or commission another, since it is not
within his power to appoint another in his place. This legislator, however,
sends an angel (Exodus 32:34; 33:2), and this angel is distinct from the one
mentioned in Exodus 23:20–21. For although he says that he will send this
angel, he does not promise his own presence, but expressly says in verse 3 that
he will not ascend in their midst. When, however, he speaks of the other Angel
in Exodus 23, he says that his own name is in him.

3. We grant that created angels contributed their service here. The Lord used
them to produce those wonders in the air—namely, fire, thunder, lightning,
smoke, the sound of the trumpet, and other sounds—and to shake the mountain,
and perhaps also to form those voices which conveyed the words of the law to
the people's ears. Hence it is called the voice of angels in Hebrews 2:2.
Philo the Jew also intends this in his book *On the Decalogue.* This is why the
apostle says in Galatians 3:19 that the law was ordained through angels as
instruments. The word διά declares an instrument here, not a primary efficient
cause, as Beza testifies on the passage. Stephen says in Acts 7:53 that it was
received through their arrangement, that is, ministry (as Beza says on the
passage), for διαταγή is used here for what is called διακονία in Hebrews
1:14. The Arabic and Syriac read τοῦ Ἀγγέλου here, *of that Angel*, namely
Christ. Junius, in *Parallels*, book 1, parallel 92, says that *in arrangements*
means in the midst of the companies, or among the intervening

[Printed p. 90 | IA PDF 98 | IA leaf 98 | IA decausadei02brow]

ranks of angels who, for honor and duty, attended their Commander. This accords
quite well with Deuteronomy 28:2 and Psalm 68:17. See De Dieu on Acts 7.

There are also some who do not wish *that word* in Hebrews 2:2 to mean the law
of the Decalogue, but other precepts which God gave under the Old Testament by
the ministry of angels. It is more probable, however, that the law of the
Decalogue itself is meant here and that the angels formed words and voices in
the air. Dr. Burmann says in the passage cited above, section 4, that the words
of Hebrews 2 can suitably be taken of the presence and attendance of angels,
as διά πολλῶν μαρτύρων, *through many witnesses*, in 1 Timothy 2:2.

4. Even if we granted what we cannot concede, that created angels rather than
God himself promulgated this law, it would nevertheless follow that a very
great difference exists between this law and the others which the Lord did not
promulgate in this manner, and that God employed this different mode of
promulgation to declare this law's prerogative, including with respect to its
duration.

*Objection 3.* Christ denies that the Jews ever heard God's voice (John 5:37).

*Answer.* Christ speaks there only of the Jews whom he was then addressing,
whom he reproaches for their extreme unbelief. The commentators testify that
these words are spoken metaphorically, and the text itself confirms it, since
verse 38 follows: *And you do not have his word abiding in you.* Rollock says
on the passage:

> The expression is taken from people who are entirely unknown to one another.
> For we commonly speak in this way whenever we charge people with extreme
> ignorance: they know another person neither by voice nor by face. These are
> the two ways by which we recognize people and distinguish one from another,
> namely, voice and countenance. The sense is therefore this: although you
> boast of knowing God, you are ignorant of him, so that you know him neither
> by voice nor by countenance. But it may be asked whether they at least knew
> him by his voice. Were they so entirely ignorant of God? I answer that bare
> and simple ignorance is not meant here, but unbelief, by which they did not
> believe his word. Hence the next statement is added, *And you do not have his
> word abiding in you.* That is, you do not believe his word.

It is also certain that John the Baptist heard God's voice when he baptized
Christ. This statement is therefore not to be taken simply and absolutely, as
if no Jew had ever heard God's voice. See Calvin in Marlorat on the passage.

*Objection 4.* Although God himself promulgated this law, it does not follow
that it is entirely and simply moral. For what prevents God from giving some
positive law by his own immediate voice?

*Answer.* We grant that nothing would prevent God from promulgating even a
positive law by his own voice. We know that in this manner he gave Adam such a
law against eating the fruit of the forbidden tree, and another to Abraham.
But this is our point: when God had established the Jewish commonwealth and was
about to give them many precepts—both insofar as they were human beings,
insofar as they constituted a commonwealth, and insofar as they assumed the
form of a church—he selected some from the entire number of precepts to
promulgate himself, with his own living voice and with the greatest majesty,
glory, and splendor. It is certain that God willed these to be distinguished
from the others and indicated that there was something special in the commands
which he honored so greatly. And what else, I ask, could this be but that they
were immutable, always to endure, and to bind the church until the end

[Printed p. 91 | IA PDF 99 | IA leaf 99 | IA decausadei02brow]

of the world?

Moreover, that this promulgation also carries a certain weight will appear if
we properly consider the words of James 2:11: *For the one who said, Do not
commit adultery, also said, Do not kill*, and so forth. The apostle was here
exposing the hypocrisy of certain people who selected whatever they pleased
from the precepts of the law and imagined that in this way they were performing
the law admirably. He therefore said that anyone who had kept the whole law but
stumbled in one point became guilty of all, because the authority of the same
legislator is violated—the one who pronounced that whole law in a special
manner and said with his living voice not only *Do not commit adultery*, but
also *Do not kill.*

By these examples James places beyond all doubt that he is speaking of the
Decalogue, of this royal law, as it is called in verse 8—perhaps because it had
been promulgated by God, who is called King preeminently, in a singular manner;
see the Dutch annotations. When James therefore uses this reasoning, he clearly
indicates that all those laws which God pronounced in this manner possess equal
authority and perpetuity. For although the ceremonial law has now been
abrogated, he nevertheless asserts that this royal law perpetually binds the
Jews, because he earnestly exhorts them to observe it and declares those who
stumble in one point guilty of all. Indeed, he indicates plainly that they must
be regarded as perpetually binding because they were promulgated by God in
this manner, namely by his living voice.

For unless the words *For the one who said* are taken in this way, so that they
point to that singular mode of promulgation, the apostle's argument will be
ineffective. If *the one who said* is taken in a broader sense, the Jews could
have replied: this argument proves equally that we ought to be circumcised and
that we ought to love our neighbor, for the same legislator who said *Do not
kill* also said, *You shall circumcise the foreskin of the male's flesh.* Thus
the word *said* must be understood of that peculiar and preeminent manner. It
then follows that all these commands are equal in excellence and perpetuity,
because they were alike pronounced and promulgated by God in that singular
manner.
