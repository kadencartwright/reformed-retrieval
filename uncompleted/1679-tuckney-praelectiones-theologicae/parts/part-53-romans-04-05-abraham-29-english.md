The second opinion, held by the Roman theologians and Castellio, concerns
the perfection of righteousness in this life and therefore the complete
removal of sin here, at least in certain people. They hold that the remnants
of sin in most people must be purged in purgatory after this life, but that
Jeremiah, John the Baptist, and the Blessed Virgin were never stained with
any filth of sin. The same applies to others among their more perfect
figures, such as Bonaventure, in whom Adam, as they would have it, seemed
never to have sinned, since they were wholly purified from sin's dregs if
these had not always adhered to them. This second opinion pertains more to
the controversy we have treated—justification by either imputed or inherent
righteousness. But I do not intend to pursue it here.

Only the third opinion is to be treated today: true believers attain, for
the first time and completely, the total separation and abolition of sin
from the whole person at the very instant of death, when their souls are
separated from their bodies. The whole company of sounder theologians
supports this position and calls death the antidote. “What is death but the
burial of sins?” says Ambrose on Romans 6.

“Death is found not only not to be useless to believers, but even useful,
since it removes a person from the dangers of sinning and establishes him
in the security of not sinning.” Cyprian, cited by Augustine in book 1,
chapter 14 of *The Predestination of the Saints*. Thus, although death is
undoubtedly the punishment of one born from the perpetuated stock of the
first man, if it is weighed in relation to piety and righteousness, it
becomes the glory of the one reborn.

[1679 printed p. 272 | IA leaf 280]

“For whoever dies no longer sins, since death is the end of sin.”

“Final grace removes the stains of every sin in all who are to be saved.”
Thus Albert the Great, *Compendium*, 3.13.

“Sin cannot yet be cut out completely and by the roots. For how could this
occur before a person is subjected to death?” Thus Methodius, cited by
Epiphanius, *Heresy* 64.

He illustrates this with a beautiful comparison: “When a fig tree or any
other tree springs up within the structure of a beautiful temple and spreads
its roots through the joints of the stones, it cannot be uprooted until the
stones have been taken apart and the building constructed anew. So sin,
which has sprouted in man, God's temple, like a wild fig tree, is not
eradicated before the blow of death dissolves this temple; then it is
perfectly and completely abolished.” Augustine's parallel statement against
Julian, book 6, chapter 6, is: “All the evils of which they were afterward
to be stripped by dying or advancing.”

Gregory of Nyssa is clearer still: “Sin is in some manner interwoven with
those who come into being, being born and growing along with them and
ending together with the boundary of life,” *On the Beatitudes*, oration 6,
page 817. Tuckney renders it: “Sin exists together with those being born,
at once being born and growing, and ending together with the end of life.”

Gregory Nazianzen is equally clear: “He gains something even here: death
and the interruption of sin, lest evil be immortal; and thus punishment
becomes loving-kindness.” Tuckney renders it: “Here too he gains death and
the cutting off of sin, lest evil be immortal, and thus the penalty gives
way to mercy.” Nazianzen appears to have taken this from the earlier
Irenaeus. Irenaeus has the following in book 3, chapter 37: “God excluded
Adam from the tree of life not from envy but from pity, lest his evil be
immortal. He interposed death to make sin cease, bringing an end to it by
the dissolution of the flesh.”

Justin Martyr likewise says in *Exhortation to the Greeks*, chapter 2, that
when desire is removed the soul at once rejoices in tranquility; released
from the evils that continually beset its earthly condition, it departs to
the one who made it.

But enough of human testimonies, even though they are ecclesiastical and
come from Fathers most worthy of all confidence.
