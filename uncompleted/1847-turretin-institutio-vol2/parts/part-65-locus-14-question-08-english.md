QUESTION VIII.

CHRIST’S PRIESTLY OFFICE

*Was Christ a priest in the true sense of the name, who already began his
priesthood on earth? Or was he called this only figuratively, and did he
fulfill his office in heaven only after the ascension? The former is affirmed;
the latter denied; against the Socinians.*

I. The second part of Christ’s mediatorial office is the *priestly dignity*,
which is the foundation of all the benefits Christ acquired through death.
Because various controversies are raised about it by various parties, they
must be treated distinctly: first the priest, and then the priesthood with
respect to its two stages, namely satisfaction and intercession.

[Marginal heading: What is Christ’s priesthood?]

II. Christ’s priesthood is the function of the mediatorial office according to
which Christ accomplished before God the things which had to be accomplished
for sinners: both by offering himself once as a victim for them and by always
interceding for them before the Father. The great excellence of this function
is evident even from the fact that no mystery was formerly foretold by clearer
prophecies or shadowed forth by more frequent types.

[Marginal heading: On Christ as priest.]

III. Our first question is with the Socinians. Although they confess Christ as
priest with their lips, they nevertheless deny it in reality when they change
his priesthood from proper to figurative, so that they may more easily
overthrow the truth of his satisfaction. They explain their opinion in this
way in the Racovian Catechism on Christ’s priestly office,

[Printed p. 356 | Internet Archive PDF 384 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Smalcius’s *The Divinity of Christ*, chapter 23, and Volkelius, book 3, chapter
37. First, they deny that Christ was properly a priest, but say that he is
called this only figuratively and analogically. Nor, they say, does this office
differ from the kingdom otherwise than in that the latter is the ability and
the former the will to aid us: as king he can help us, and as priest he also
wills to do so. Second, they maintain that he was not a priest on earth, but
became one only in heaven, and that his death was not an offering or sacrifice
in the true sense of the name, but a certain preparation for that priesthood
to be administered only in heaven. Third, they maintain that he made no
expiation of sins through any sacrifice offered on earth, but that the
expiation performed in heaven is our liberation both from punishments, lest we
be condemned on account of sins, and from sins, lest we serve them.

IV. The orthodox, however, steadfastly affirm that Christ is a priest in the
true sense of the name and that his priesthood is a priesthood properly so
called. He discharged it partly on earth through the offering of his body and
a true and real sacrifice for the expiation of our sins, and partly discharges
it in heaven by interceding for us. Thus the priesthood is formally
distinguished from the kingdom: the former consists in the offering of the
sacrifice and intercession, while the latter consists in the government and
defense of the people acquired through the offering. This is proved
distinctly.

[Marginal heading: 1. Christ is proved to have been a true priest;]

V. First, it is clear that Christ was a priest in the true sense of the name
who offered a sacrifice properly so called: 1. He is called priest not just
once, Psalm 110:4 and Hebrews 5:5, and repeatedly in chapters 7–10, and he is
numbered with priests properly so called, Hebrews 5:1, who are appointed to
offer gifts and to accomplish the things which must be done before God. It is
no objection that he is distinguished from the Levitical priests in Hebrews
7–8, because a consequence does not follow from the negation of a species to
the negation of the genus. From this it is indeed gathered that this priesthood
differs from the Levitical and is far more excellent, but one would wrongly
conclude from it that the priesthood is improper and figurative. Indeed,
because it rises above the Levitical priesthood, it must be most proper and
perfect, just as Melchizedek’s priesthood does not cease to be a true
priesthood although it differs greatly from the Levitical. 2. All priestly
acts are attributed to him, offering and intercession, Hebrews 7:27, 9:24, and
10:7. 3. He was shadowed forth by various types, both the common types, such as
the Levitical priests who were deficient in various respects, and the singular
type in Melchizedek, who signified him in a peculiar manner, as will be seen
afterward. Therefore, just as such men were true priests, Christ too had to be
a true priest properly so called.

[Marginal heading: 2. And already to have been one on earth;]

VI. Second, it is evident that he was already a priest on earth and not only
in heaven: both because he was called to the priesthood on earth, Matthew 3:17
and Hebrews 5:5; and because he already exercised priestly acts on earth
through the offering of prayers, John 17 and Hebrews 5:7–8, and the sacrifice
of himself, Ephesians 5:2 and Hebrews 1:3 and 10:6–7, in which he gave himself
as a ransom (ἀντίλυτρον) for us and made expiation of sins. It is also evident
because the priest who was a type of Christ did not become a priest only when
he entered the sanctuary, but was already a priest before the victim was
offered. The apostle teaches that this obtained its truth in Christ, Hebrews
9:11–12. It is confirmed from the nature of the priesthood, which is
constituted to offer gifts and sacrifices for sins, Hebrews 5:1. But this had
to be done not in heaven, but on earth, because sacrifices are not offered
elsewhere. Further, where there is a true sacrifice, there must be a true
priest. But a true sacrifice was offered on the cross according to the
apostle, Hebrews 7:27 and 9:25, 28. This cannot be referred to the
intercessional offering which is made continually in heaven: 1. It concerns
an offering corresponding to the offering of victims which was made under the
Old Testament through their slaughter and the shedding of blood. 2. It
concerns

[Printed p. 357 | Internet Archive PDF 385 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

an offering made once for all (ἐφάπαξ), which consists in Christ’s suffering
and death, not one continually made in heaven. For what occurs until the
consummation of the age cannot be said to occur once. 3. It treats the offering
in which he appeared visibly, which must be understood of the days of his
flesh on earth, not of his stay in heaven, in which he is now hidden until he
returns for judgment. 4. It means the offering which had to precede his session
at God’s right hand, since Hebrews 10:12 says that after one victim was offered
for sins he sat at God’s right hand. There the one offering is opposed to the
session forever. But if that offering consisted in Christ’s perpetual
appearance for us, it would no longer be one sacrifice (μία θυσία), but a
sacrifice (θυσία) equally forever (εἰς τὸ διηνεκές) as his session is. Nor
could that session be said to follow the offering, since it would chiefly
consist in it.

[Marginal heading: 3. The priesthood is distinguished from the kingdom.]

VII. Third, it is clear that the priesthood is formally distinguished from the
kingdom and does not consist only in a general will to aid us, but in the
special will to offer as a sacrifice for our sins the body divinely prepared
for him. Although Christ’s kingly and priestly offices amicably conspire toward
the same common end, namely human salvation, they are nevertheless
distinguished by their peculiar functions, just as among human beings the
censer and scepter, the miter and diadem differ. Kings are appointed by God
among human beings to govern and protect them and keep them in duty. But
priests are appointed before God for human beings, to offer him gifts and
offerings for sins, Hebrews 5:1. Thus the formal acts of Christ’s priesthood
are offering and intercession, while those of the kingdom are the defense,
government, and liberation of the church. The king acts upon the people and
against enemies; the priest acts toward God in the people’s name. Power to
protect his own and conquer enemies is proper to the kingdom, but righteousness
to placate God and satisfy the law is proper to the priesthood. Hence the
types of priesthood and kingdom differed in the Levitical priesthood and the
Davidic kingdom. But the priesthood is wrongly placed only in the will to help
us, which belongs no less to the kingdom than to the priesthood, since
Scripture expressly places it in the offering of his body, Hebrews 10:5–7,
which is followed by expiation properly so called. This does not occur merely
through the forgiveness of sins by a certain absolute power, but through the
true and proper satisfaction made on earth through the blood and death of the
sponsor.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VIII. When Paul says in Hebrews 8:4 that “if Christ were on earth, he would
not even be a priest, since those priests remain who offer gifts according to
the law,” he does not mean to deny absolutely that Christ was a priest on
earth, which he asserts and confirms at length in chapters 7, 9, and 10.
Rather, he understands this in a qualified sense concerning the other act of
the priesthood, namely his appearance and intercession before the Father,
which could not occur anywhere but in heaven. Thus it pertains to the present
time in which the apostle was speaking, so that the meaning is that if Christ
were now on earth, he could not be a priest. For under the Old Testament the
high priest did not always remain in the court, but sometimes had to enter the
sanctuary with blood; for if he had not done this, he would not have fulfilled
all the parts of his office. But it does not follow that if Christ’s entrance
into heaven was required for the continuation or consummation of his
priesthood, it was required for its constitution. Indeed, on the contrary, he
would not have ascended into heaven unless he had already been a priest.

IX. Although the priesthood could not be completed on earth, it does not
follow that it could not be begun there. Nor do the things which pertain to
continuing the priesthood immediately pertain to its constitution. It is one
thing for his priesthood not to be perfected and consummated on earth, which
we concede, and another truly not to have been perfectly a priest already on
earth, which we deny. Indeed, even

to begin those acts he had already to be a priest in fact. Aaron was perfectly
a priest even when he remained in the court, although he had not fulfilled all
the parts of his office.

X. Christ’s suffering and death indeed had a preparatory and antecedent
relation to intercession, which was the other priestly act to be performed in
heaven. But they could not be called a preparation for a priesthood to be
administered only in heaven, since the priestly offering and surrender of
Christ for us were properly placed in that death.

XI. Hebrews 5:5 does not teach *when* Christ was constituted priest, but *by
whom*, namely by the Father, who acknowledged him as Son. Thus the divinity of
the calling is noted, not its time. Nor, if the declaration of Christ’s
priesthood pertains to the resurrection, must it immediately be denied that he
was a priest before it. For the thing must not be confused with its
manifestation. The other matters which pertain here can be sought from Part VI
of the disputation on satisfaction, from thesis 19 through 33.
