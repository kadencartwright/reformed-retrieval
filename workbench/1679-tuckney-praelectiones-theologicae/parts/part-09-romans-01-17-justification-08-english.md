Elsewhere Bellarmine replies that the apostle excludes the works of believers,
such as Abraham's in Romans 4, but not those works which flow from their
faith. Rather, he excludes works which they are sometimes able to do not from
faith, since even those who possess faith sometimes act not from faith—as when
they sin, or when they do purely moral works without reference to God. This
fiction is so rank and the evasion so wretched that a cardinal's purple might
blush at it. This piece of Jesuitical subtlety should be met with laughter
rather than an answer, except that we are indignant that Bellarmine wishes the
apostle's argument to labor under the same defect as his own reply to it,
namely, excessive absurdity. For what godly or prudent person would ever dare
believe that the apostle—elsewhere so prudent, and inspired, when he wrote
these things, by the Spirit of infallible wisdom; whom the Athenians themselves
in Acts 17 found to be so strong and ἐλεγκτικόν, skilled in refutation—when he
wrote to the Romans, who were not such dullards, and composed this epistle,
the most argumentative and forceful of all, would merely trifle? Would he,
with such great effort and so lengthy a labor, prove only what no godly,
prudent, sane person ever denied?

1. Namely, that Abraham was not justified by works not which he did, but which
he could have done, and which perhaps he therefore never did—for a potency
does not always posit an act. On the contrary, in that passage Paul excludes
from Abraham's justification the works not which he might sometimes have
done, but which he actually did, indeed which, already justified, he did from
faith. He even excludes all works universally, when he declares Abraham to
have been justified from faith, and does so in opposition to all works stated
indefinitely.

2. Still more inept is what Bellarmine wants the apostle to prove there,
namely, that men are not justified by those works in which they sin. What
madman ever dreamed this, so as to need the apostle to refute him? Certainly
the person who ever said that any man was to be justified by sin—whose wages
are death and which merits condemnation—has cast off not only all piety but
reason, and thus humanity itself.

3. Not much less frivolous is Bellarmine's third proposal: that those moral
works which a man, though in some sense faithful, sometimes does without
reference to God are excluded from justification. For:

1. Who has ever been reported to say that works of this kind justify? They can
be produced by unbelieving Gentiles just as much as by faithful Christians;
they lack an actual, though not at least a habitual, reference to God in the
person acting; they are also sinful and on that account do not justify, but
according to their desert condemn.

2. Besides, in the second place, the apostle wishes works from the law to be
excluded from the rationale of justification. But works of the kind Bellarmine
describes acknowledge God as lawgiver; indeed, they ought to proceed from the
obligation of the law and the love of God, and therefore cannot be done
without reference to God.

But these things only in passing. To return to the point, the foundation
already laid remains unshaken: in the Epistles to the Romans and Galatians the
apostle deals not so much with Gentiles and Pharisaic Jews, who opposed the
law to the gospel, as with seductive pseudo-apostles and faithful Christians
converted from Judaism or paganism, who combined the law with the gospel—in
precisely the same manner as Roman Catholics today, as will appear later.
From this also,

even if only in the smallest degree, we may infer what ought to be thought
concerning the second inquiry:

What, namely, was τὸ κεφάλαιον, the principal point? What was that chief thing
which the apostle intended specifically either to refute or to prove in the
Epistles to the Romans and Galatians?

1. If on this matter you ask what the opinion of the ancient fathers was,
Saint Augustine in particular is at hand. At the beginning of his book *An
Exposition of Certain Propositions from the Epistle to the Romans*, he has
these words: “These are the points in the Epistle of Paul the Apostle to the
Romans. First of all, one must understand that the question in this epistle
concerns works of the law and grace.” Anselm follows him, to whom Stapleton
gives first place above all the other expositors of this epistle. Cornelius a
Lapide seized quite eagerly upon this opinion of Augustine in order to
establish from it his own principle and that of the other Roman Catholics:
namely, that the apostle's purpose is to prove that no one is justified from
works of the law, but that one is justified from works of grace. Yet here the
following must be noted.

[Marginalia: In the *Antidotes*; In the argument of the epistle.]

1. Augustine's words, “the question concerns works of the law and grace,” can
be understood as including an opposition between works of the law and grace,
so that the question is whether a man is justified by those works or by this
grace. They need not be understood—as the Roman Catholics explain them—as
though they contained a division of works, namely, into either works of the
law or works of grace, so that on both sides it were conceded that
justification is from works, and Paul debated only whether it was from works
of the law or from works of grace. That was not Augustine's meaning there;
or, if it was, one must note:

2. Augustine wrote that book while he was still younger and a presbyter, and
perhaps did so extemporaneously and amid conversations with his friends, as
he himself acknowledges in the *Retractions*, book 1, chapter 23. Let us ask,
therefore, what he wrote about this subject once he had become more mature and
had treated it more deliberately. We have this at the beginning of his
unfinished exposition of the Epistle to the Romans, where he writes:

“So far as can be understood from its text, the epistle which Paul the apostle
wrote to the Romans contains the following question: whether the gospel of
our Lord Jesus Christ came to the Jews alone because of the merits of works
of the law, or whether, without any preceding merits of works, justification
by the faith which is in Christ Jesus came to all Gentiles, so that men did
not believe because they were righteous, but, having been justified by
believing, afterward began to live righteously. The apostle therefore intends
to teach that the grace of the gospel came to all, and shows that it is also
called grace for this reason: it was given freely, not as though it were a
debt of justice. For some who had believed from among the Jews had begun to
raise a tumult against the apostle Paul because he admitted into the grace of
the gospel those who were uncircumcised and free from the bonds of the old
law, preaching to them that they should believe in Christ, without imposing
the yoke of carnal circumcision.”

Augustine states these things there more explicitly. He has parallel words in
his second question to Simplicianus:

“Now this is the apostle's intention, which prevails throughout the epistle:
that no one should boast concerning the merits of works. The Israelites dared
to boast of these, because they had both served the law given to them and,
since they served the law, had received evangelical grace as though it were
owed to their merits. Hence they did not wish the same grace to be given to
the Gentiles as unworthy unless they received the Jewish sacraments. The
question which arose from this is resolved in the Acts of the Apostles. For
they did not understand that, by the very fact that it is evangelical grace,
it is not owed to works; otherwise grace is not grace.”

[Marginalia: Volume 4, part 1.]

[1679 printed p. 96 | IA leaf 104]

“And in many passages he often testifies to this: faith is placed before works,
not to extinguish works, but to show that works do not precede grace but
follow it. Thus no one should suppose that he received grace because he
worked well; rather, he cannot work well unless he has received grace through
faith.” We have quoted these passages from Augustine at greater length because
they explain τὸ ζητούμενον, the question of Paul's argument, more openly and
plainly than the other fathers do. For Ambrose, when he treats this subject,
has the following concerning the apostle's intention:

“The apostle teaches that the Jews wandered away from the law and the
prophets, and became like the Gentiles in the cause of Christ, so that both
have need of God's mercy, hoping for salvation not through the law but through
faith in Jesus Christ. Hence he strives with all diligence to remove us from
the law, because the law and the prophets were until John, and to establish
them in faith in Christ alone. As though against the law, he vindicates the
gospel—not destroying the law, but preferring Christianity. For he shows that
Christ was promised in such a way that at his advent the law would cease, not
the whole of it, because an abridgment of the law was made which would give
salvation in a compendious way.”

[Marginalia: In the preface to the Epistle to the Romans.]

Jerome likewise says that the subject matter of Paul's Epistle to the
Galatians is the same as that of the Epistle written to the Romans, and
asserts that both specifically contain the cessation of the old law and the
introduction of the new. As though this were the chief thing—and not only
something included—which the apostle wished to remove with such great effort
in both epistles: justification from the ceremonial law, which was to cease
now that the gospel had arisen! As though he taught only that the burdens of
the old law had been laid aside, and that all those things, to use Jerome's
words, “which preceded in types and images—that is, the leisure of the
Sabbath, the injury of circumcision, the recurring Kalends and the three
annual festivals, scrupulosity about foods, and washings every day only to be
defiled again—ceased when the grace of the gospel stole in.”

[Marginalia: In the proem to the Epistle to the Galatians, book 1.]

But although this point is not excluded, according to Augustine the apostle
also proves other things in this argument. So far as appears from Augustine's
words, they were three:

1. That justification and the grace of the gospel are not proper to the Jews
alone, as they sharply maintained, but common also to Gentiles who would
believe. The apostle pursues this at length in Romans 4 and 9–11. From this
also follows:

2. That not even the Jews, and least of all the Gentiles, are justified by
works of the law, namely the ceremonial law. The apostle treats this at
length in Romans 2 and 4 and throughout the Epistle to the Galatians, where he
asserts that the external and handmade circumcision, concerning which the
Jews contended and boasted so much, is useless and, now that the gospel has
arisen, even destructive. For he speaks with the following assurance: “Look,
I Paul say to you that if you are circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing.
I testify again to every man who is circumcised that he is a debtor to keep
the whole law. You who are justified by the law have been made empty,
separated from Christ, and have fallen from grace” (Galatians 5:2–4). With
these two points the apostle refutes the Jews' twofold error.

The first was that the Jews alone were heirs of grace, while the Gentiles, as
profane idolaters, were to be kept far from it. Even the faithful themselves
remained in this error for some time. But when it at last became known through
the vision given to Peter in Acts 10 that the Gentiles

[1679 printed p. 97 | IA leaf 105]

were no longer to be regarded as polluted, the Jews, cast down from the first
rank, wished to hold their ground at least in the second.

Their second heresy was that the yoke of the law had to be imposed upon
converted Gentiles and that “unless they were circumcised according to the
rite of Moses, they could not be saved,” as is evident from Acts 15:1. Roman
Catholics concede and affirm that these two matters are treated in the
epistles: namely, concerning Gentiles who were previously impious but at last
believed the gospel, (1) that they were not to be excluded from justification
and the grace of the gospel, and (2) that in order to partake of it they were
not to be circumcised or sent beneath the yoke of the ceremonial law, by
which, since it was now to cease after Christ's advent, not even the Jews were
to be justified. Our very learned recent paraphrast and commentator thinks
that these two points contain the sum of this whole apostolic argument.

[Marginalia: See Peter Martyr in the preface to the Epistle to the Romans;
Dr. Hammond in the preface to the Epistle to the Romans and in the annotation
on Romans 1.]

But Augustine rightly thought that a third point should be added to these. It
is indeed the chief point, and Augustine himself emphasizes it above the
others in the passages cited: namely, that a man is justified by no merits of
works—not only ceremonial works, but works of whatever kind they may be—but
by grace alone and by the faith which is in Christ Jesus. Thus it is from
grace that we first believe in Christ and, believing, are justified; afterward
(as he speaks in the closest agreement with the apostle's intention and the
Protestant doctrine), afterward, I say, we begin to live righteously.

This, to say it once, is both the aim and the sum of this Pauline argument:
namely, that it casts down all our merits and establishes gratuitous
justification by faith in Christ alone, not by works.

At the very entrance to the whole argument Paul lays this down as a
foundation in chapter 1, verse 17: “For the righteousness of God is revealed
in it from faith to faith, as it is written, The just shall live by faith.” As
Calvin rightly observes, the apostle descends to this point with great skill
from the opening of the epistle.

[Marginalia: In the argument of the Epistle to the Romans.]

He also pursues this through all that follows, if we consider the fabric of
the entire argument, proceeding in a continuous course and confirming it with
invincible arguments.

First, when Jews and Gentiles contended with one another about their merits
and prerogatives—namely, to whom first place should be given, which both our
writers and the Roman Catholics agree supplied the occasion for writing this
epistle—the former boasted more arrogantly in their law and works, and the
latter in their philosophy and virtues. The apostle cuts away all material
for boasting from both. In chapter 1 he makes the Gentiles, and in chapter 2
the Jews, all universally guilty of violating the law. He thus shows that
they are not to be justified by their works but rather condemned, and from
this concludes in chapter 3 that no one is justified by works but by faith.
There he also answers certain objections which manifestly demonstrate that
this alone is the meaning and state of this controversy.

[Marginalia: Pareus in the prolegomena to Romans; Estius in the argument.]

He proves this by a second argument in chapter 4 from the example of Abraham,
who was justified by faith, not by works. There he expressly asserts that “to
the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly,
faith is imputed for righteousness” (verse 5). He thus shows that
justification consists in the remission of sins and the gratuitous imputation
of righteousness (verses 6–8), and confirms this in chapter 5 by the
illustrious comparison between the second Adam and the first.

This is the careful sequence and fabric of Paul's argument. This is the
unanimous

[1679 printed p. 98 | IA leaf 106]

opinion of all Protestants, except certain people who savor somewhat of
popery.

“The opinion and aim of this epistle therefore remains: that we are justified
by faith alone without any works, and yet that good works are not therefore
condemned.” So Luther.

[Marginalia: In the preface to the Epistle to the Romans, volume 5, p. 99.]

“Let the thesis proposed in these chapters therefore be this: that men have
one righteousness, the mercy of God in Christ, when it is apprehended by
faith offered through the gospel.” Thus Calvin.

[Marginalia: In the argument on the Epistle to the Romans.]

“The state of this argument is that there is one way of obtaining salvation,
which is proposed in the gospel without any distinction of peoples: namely,
Jesus Christ apprehended through faith.” So Beza.

[Marginalia: In the marginal annotation on Romans 1:16.]

“The apostle therefore teaches that a man is justified before God—that is,
regarded and pronounced righteous by God—by faith alone in Christ, that is,
because of Christ alone apprehended by faith in the promise of the gospel;
and thus that a man is justified from the mere grace of God, not from the
merit of his works.” So Piscator.

[Marginalia: In the argument.]

There is no need for me to mention others, since this is the unanimous
opinion of all. If it is true, as indeed it is most true, it will be easy
enough to discern what should be determined concerning that twofold fiction
of the Roman Catholics. For if the apostle's whole purpose, in establishing
our justification upon the righteousness of Christ alone apprehended through
faith, is to deny it to all works universally, then he excludes from its
formal rationale not only ceremonial works, as they invent, but also moral
works. Nor does he exclude only moral works preceding faith, which are done
by the powers of nature or free choice, but also those following faith.
Although these necessarily flow from justification, they nevertheless do not
enter into or constitute its formal essence. Let this suffice concerning
both their opinions treated jointly. Now, if you please, let us treat each
separately and individually, beginning with the former.
