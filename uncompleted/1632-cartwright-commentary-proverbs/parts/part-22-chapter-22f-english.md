Verse 12. The eyes of Jehovah guard knowledge, but he overturns the words of the faithless man.

In עיני (*oculi*, eyes) there is a metaphor transferred from bodily eyes to the omniscience of God.

The purpose is to strengthen people in the true and sincere use of the tongue against the difficulties which the flesh suggests.

Someone might object against the truth of the preceding Proverb that it is not always true, since it is celebrated in the common saying, [*truth breeds hatred; compliance, friends*]. But I answer that this is a human proverb, which ought to yield to that divine one. Nor indeed would I deny that truth sometimes breeds hatred, as we read happened to the Prophet Micaiah. But it must be noted that the one who hated him on account of the truth was Ahab, who is said expressly to have devoted himself wholly to committing iniquity. Moreover, to establish the truth of the Proverb it is enough if it so happens often. Finally, it must not be passed over that flatterers not rarely become hateful to the very people to whom they have enslaved themselves through blandishments, 2 Sam. 1.4, 5, 6, etc.

But against this temptation the surest safeguard is what Solomon sets forth in this Proverb: namely, that God keeps watch lest the person who speaks the truth in the manner we have stated should suffer any harm. If people have given counsel to others, they will act with the utmost diligence so that it attains the desired outcome. For they think that if those to whom they gave counsel incur any loss from it, this will be turned to their own reproach. How much more, then, will God undertake the care of counsel of which he himself is the author, lest that in which we have shown ourselves obedient and compliant to him should prove harmful to us? Therefore, if his eyes are fixed upon causing the discourse of truth which we have brought forth from a pure heart to fare prosperously, then although the eyes of infinitely many people are intent upon either rendering it ineffectual or causing it to turn to our harm, they will nevertheless all accomplish nothing at all. For the single eye of God will more than suffice to frustrate whatever it may be with which they will oppose truth that has proceeded from a pure heart.

A great difference between the eyes of God and of people is also perceived from this. People cannot preserve someone by fixing their eyes upon him, but God preserves the person for whom he cares. Indeed, even in the very death of his people God will not cease both to preserve them and to confirm the truth to which they have borne witness. Zech. 1.5, 6.

Next, since in this verse he calls *knowledge* what he previously called truth from a pure heart, he appears to meet those who are accustomed to brand sincerity and simplicity of speech with the mark of ignorance. For the world regards as foolish almost all who do not know how to dissemble and transform themselves, like polyps, into every shape. The Holy Spirit, by contrast, pronounces these people knowledgeable, while convicting of ignorance and folly those who conform themselves through flatteries to the caprice of others.

In the latter hemistich he anticipates what might be objected concerning the apparently prosperous success of those who speak to gain favour, procure friends for themselves by their compliance, and obtain honours. For he affirms that God will overturn their words by causing them finally to be reduced to the greatest ignominy by those same flatteries through which they had hoped to become rich and great.

Verse 13. The lazy man says, a lion is outside; I shall be killed in the midst of the streets.

In אמר (*dicit*, says) there is a synecdoche of the part, with the past tense standing also for the present and future. In ארי (*leo*, lion) there is also a synecdoche of the part for every more serious danger.

The purpose is to set forth for greater detestation the vain and often absurd pretexts of lazy people for declining labour.

It is well known that God forbade idleness to man from the beginning of creation, but that with the entrance of sin into the world a harsher condition of labour, by God's command, fell upon him even when he was the sole ruler of the whole world, Gen. 2. It is no less true that God distributed labour in such a way that the things to be administered at home were assigned to women and those outside to men, Titus 2.5. Yet this must be understood of men ἐπὶ τὸ πολὺ (*for the most part*), since those who practise trades generally attend to their own business in private buildings and workshops. But because most people, as stated in Ps. 104.23, go out to their work and labour, this Proverb of Solomon arose. The lazy man therefore says that there is a lion in the field where the law and condition of his calling required him to be. So that we may more readily understand the folly of his vain and ridiculous excuse, we must recall what is set forth in the Psalm cited above: it was brought about by the singular providence of God that when the sun rises, the wild beasts immediately withdraw from the fields into their lairs, where they may hide from people's sight, Ps. 104.22, 23. For so we read that God promised Noah, Gen. 9.2. Yet although by the ordinary law of nature it comes about that lions and other harmful wild beasts betake themselves to their dens as soon as the sun has shone, it nevertheless sometimes happens by God's extraordinary direction that they encounter people in the fields by day and kill them. Thus we read that it happened to the Prophet who, after neglecting God's command, lodged with the old man, 1 Kings 13; and to the man who, though commanded by the word of God to inflict a wound upon the Prophet, refused under an appearance of piety to carry it out, 1 Kings 20.36. From this it is deduced that not only the person who declines labour wholly free from danger must be condemned for laziness, but also the person who alleges a danger to which he is exposed with no probability, or at least no great probability, and who demands greater immunity from harm than is commonly granted to people. Indeed, even amid evils and troubles which happen not infrequently, provided they are fortuitous—that is, those concerning which it is not clearly established whether they will occur—one ought not to flee from labour. For one who is deterred from sowing by fear of rain or wind, though these occur frequently, is condemned by Solomon at Eccles. 11.4. Therefore it is unjust to demand a private security against a common danger, and all the more unworthy and shameful to withdraw from one's duty because of a remote harm of which no trace appears.

This monstrous declining of labour by the lazy man is amplified by the words that follow, in which he alleges that destruction threatens him from an encounter with a lion if he should go out even into a street crowded with people. But since lions shrink so greatly from the sight of people that, when they have come out of the countryside, they hide in lurking places, how much more will they shrink from streets where assemblies are held and everything

resounds with the clamour of people! Yet this is not so unheard of that we do not read of its happening once. For bears tore apart in the town itself the boys who insolently and mockingly taunted the Prophet, 2 Kings 2. Those, therefore, who throw off from their shoulders the imposed burden of labour because perhaps some people have died under the weight of that burden may rightly be reckoned among such monstrous fugitives from labour, so to speak. Yet we see that nothing is more customary for most people than to reject labour—especially hard labour full of difficulty—because human memory records that some people who undertook it were crushed by grievous harm.

Here, however, the perversity of human nature must be considered. Men who languish in idleness shrink from the field where work was to be performed, while women, whom God appointed to domestic affairs, Titus 2.5, overflow with idleness and desire to wander outside through streets and fields. For women, running about outdoors is as much a fault as hiding at home in the shade is for men. To some extent the same may also be affirmed of men who delight in running here and there through the marketplace and fields when their calling required them to lead a sedentary life indoors.

When it is said that the lazy man *says* this, it is implicitly judged that he only says it and makes a show in words of the danger he alleges, but does not feel it so in his mind; and consequently that the person who is lazy is also a hypocrite. Thus, since he is convicted as αὐτοκατάκριτος (*self-condemned*) by the testimony of his own conscience, he is more worthy of being contended with by fists and blows than by reasons and arguments.

But if we wish to judge incorruptly and according to the matter as it is, we shall find it more agreeable to the truth that the lion lurks in the lazy man's bedroom and upon his bed. For by God's just judgment more danger threatens him because he has rejected the labour due from him than if he were to undertake in the field the work assigned to him. For God commands the angels to preserve us while we remain in our calling, and has promised that we will tread upon the old lion and the adder and trample the young lion and the dragon underfoot, Ps. 91.11, 13. By contrast, the lion lies in wait for those who, having abandoned their calling at home, roam outside. Therefore, those who stray from the right path expose themselves to dangers of every kind.
