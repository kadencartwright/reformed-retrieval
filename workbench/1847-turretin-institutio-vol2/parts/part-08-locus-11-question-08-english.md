## QUESTION VIII

### ON THE WORSHIP OF RELICS

Are the bodies and relics of the saints to be venerated with religious
worship?

It is denied, against the Roman Catholics.

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

I. *The worship of relics* is an appendage of the worship of the saints. For
it is believed to contribute considerably to the honor of the saints that
their bodies—or at least their bones, ashes, belts, clothing, and similar
relics—should be religiously worshiped. Although present-day sophists attempt
to conceal and varnish the shamefulness of this λειψανοθρησκεία, *relic
worship*, and deny that the adoration owed to God is rendered to them,
claiming that it is only veneration and honor, it is nevertheless certain that
this practice was sanctioned by the authority of the Second Council of
Nicaea. These are its words in Action 4: “Although we adore bones, ashes,
cloths, blood, and, finally, tombs, we do not sacrifice to them.” Baronius, at
A.D. 821, no. 14, says that Clement VIII sent him “to inspect and

[Printed p. 44 | Internet Archive PDF 72 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

adore the venerable body of Cecilia.” Vásquez, in book 3 of *On Adoration*,
Disputation 4, chapters 1–2, proves that “images and relics are to be adored in
the same manner.” The Tridentine Catechism on the third precept approves an
oath by relics, and no one can deny that this is an act of religion. But by
whatever name this worship is designated, whether adoration or veneration, it
is certain that for them it is religious.

II. We, however, judge that the bodies of the saints together with their
relics should not be cast aside or treated as objects of ridicule, but should
be honorably buried. This is not only because they are human remains, which by
a certain privilege of nature ought to receive the honor of burial, but also
because they are the remains of the faithful and saints, whose memory ought to
be blessed in the church. Nevertheless, we deny that they are to be worshiped
with religious worship, and we deservedly abhor the superstition by which,
through an extraordinary fraud intended to make sport of the people, the
relics of the apostles are displayed while their writings are hidden. For the
relics are mute, and there is no danger that they will say anything against
the pontiff, but the apostolic writings cut the papacy's throat and pierce
idolatry through.

[Marginal heading: Reasons against the worship of relics. 1. Because it is not
found in Scripture.]

III. Our reasons are as follows. First, Scripture nowhere sanctions such
worship by any command, promise, or example. Indeed, whenever examples of the
departed are mentioned in the Old or New Testament, their bodies are said to
have been committed to the earth without any display or religious veneration.
Thus, as Jacob and Joseph were dying, they ordered their bones to be carried
from Egypt into Canaan so that they might be laid with their fathers. But
nowhere are those bones read to have been adored or received with a kiss. Nor
were they brought into the tabernacle, carried about in a procession, or
placed under altars, all of which are commonly practiced in the Roman Church.
And how could the Israelites have been induced to kiss or carry relics when,
according to the law of Moses, anyone who touched a corpse even briefly was
regarded as defiled? Indeed, God himself is said to have buried and concealed
Moses' body (Deut. 34:6), lest the Israelites abuse the relics of so great a
man for idolatry. It is also highly fitting that the dispute between Michael
and Satan treated in Jude 9 had no other cause than that Satan wished Moses'
body to be brought forth, while Michael wished it to be kept buried and
unknown. The disciples laid the body of Stephen, the first martyr, to rest and
attended it with great lamentation (Acts 8), but no one is read to have
prostrated himself at Stephen's shrine or worshiped his bones with religious
worship.

[Marginal heading: 2. Matthew 23:29 is opposed to it.]

IV. Second, in Matthew 23:29 Christ rebukes “the hypocritical Pharisees and
scribes who built the tombs of the prophets and the righteous and adorned
them,” while in the meantime despising their teaching. Those who worship and
venerate the corpses of these men lying in their tombs are no less deserving
of rebuke. Paul declares in 2 Corinthians 5:16 that “he no longer knows anyone,
not even Christ, according to the flesh,” so that he may devote all his zeal
and effort to seeking and possessing Christ according to the spirit. But who
will deny that those who seek and worship the relics of Christ and the saints
still wish to know them according to the flesh? Thus “going to the dead on
behalf of the living” is expressly forbidden in Isaiah 8:19.

[Marginal heading: 3. It is useless and idolatrous.]

V. Third, such worship is not only useless, since it is shown to inanimate
things entirely devoid of perception, but also idolatrous and superstitious.
It cannot be rendered to them without sacrilege, since it is due to God alone.
Nor does the adversaries' varnish help when they deny that they render to
relics absolute religious worship, strictly and properly so called, or
*latreutic adoration*, which belongs to God alone, and claim that they render
only *relative and dulic* worship. Scripture recognizes no difference between
latreutic and

[Printed p. 45 | Internet Archive PDF 73 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

dulic, absolute and relative adoration, but claims religious adoration simply
for God alone. Nor will God judge according to those ingenious but empty
distinctions and intentions of transgressing men, which the common people do
not know, but according to the rule prescribed in the Word.

[Marginal heading: 4. It is exposed to impostures.]

VI. Fourth, this worship is exposed to a thousand frauds and impostures. A
thousand spurious and adulterated objects are thrust forward in place of the
true relics of Christ and the saints, and the bodies of many are worshiped on
earth whose souls are tormented in hell, as Augustine used to say. An example
is found in Socrates, book 7, chapter 14, concerning a certain Ammonius, a monk
and robber, who was long worshiped as a saint. Gregory of Tours, book 9 of his
*History*, chapter 6, testifies that “roots, moles' teeth, mice bones, and
foxes' claws were found stored in a certain saint's reliquary.” At Zurich,
when the most precious shrine in which the relics of the holy martyrs Felix
and Regula were believed to have been placed was opened at the Reformation,
there were found a few pieces of baked brick, a cord twelve cubits long, and
some ribs of a cat or similar animal, and nothing else. Our Geneva can attest
to a not dissimilar fraud. Before the Reformation it boasted especially of two
relics, Peter's brain and Anthony's arm, but afterward what was alleged to be
Peter's brain proved to be pumice, and Anthony's arm a deer's groin.
Cassander, in the chapter on the veneration of relics in his *Consultation*,
does not deny that “to entice the simple common people for the sake of greed,
false relics were substituted, fictitious miracles were proclaimed, and
superstition was nourished by those miracles,” and so forth. If we wished to
recount such frauds, countless examples of them would occur everywhere. From
these it would be perfectly clear with what license the clergy insult and
mock the wretched, bewitched common people. Indeed, it would seem utterly
incredible that the world had been deceived by so gross and so long-lasting
an imposture if the matter itself did not proclaim it. See Calvin's
*Admonition on Relics* and Hospinian's *On the Origin of Relics*. Therefore,
all who worship relics worship what they do not know. For after so many frauds
and impostures have been uncovered, what certainty can be given that the
relics belong to the saints whose relics they are boasted to be?

[Marginal heading: 5. It was unknown to the ancient church.]

VII. Fifth, λειψανοθρησκεία, *relic worship*, was unknown in the first
centuries of Christianity. This can be evident even from the following. The
Christians buried the bodies of the martyrs and saints with the greatest care,
which they would not have done if they had thought them worthy of adoration,
as Eusebius testifies that the church of Smyrna did with Polycarp's bones in
book 4, chapter 15. No mention of such worship rendered to relics occurs in
the books of the most ancient theologians where it would have been not only
appropriate but almost necessary to say something about it. Before three
hundred years had passed, the pagans never charged our people with such
worship, although they did so afterward. Reliquaries, which the Romans now
continually carry about like weapons and defenses, were then completely
unknown. From these and many other arguments of this kind, which may be seen
collected in the celebrated Dallaeus's book 4 of *On the Object of Religious
Worship*, it is clearer than the noonday light that the worship of relics was
completely unknown and unheard of in those first centuries and was finally
introduced in the following centuries, successively and by stages.

[Marginal heading: By what stages it was introduced.]

VIII. First, ecclesiastical assemblies began to be held at the tombs of the
martyrs, particularly on the day of their suffering. This was not done for
their worship and invocation, which the letter of the Smyrnaeans expressly
denies, but so that, it says, “the minds of those who follow may be stirred by
distinguished examples toward the path of their predecessors.” Basil confirms
this in the *Ascetical Works*, chapter 40, and Homily 19. Augustine writes in
*Against Faustus the Manichaean* that

[Printed p. 46 | Internet Archive PDF 74 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

“the Christian people celebrate the memorials of the martyrs with religious
solemnity to be stirred to imitation.” Up to this point, the practice was free
from superstition. But in the course of time the seeds of superstition were
gradually sown, when under Constantine the Great the relics began to be dug up
and transferred from more obscure tombs to more celebrated ones. This was
indeed established by the emperor with good zeal. For just as he endeavored to
transfer to the Christian religion various outward ornaments that had
obtained a certain appearance and authority for pagan religion, so that he might
make Christianity more commendable not only to Christians but also to the
Gentiles, as Eusebius says in the *Life of Constantine*, so, when he saw that
the Gentiles transferred the bodies of kings and other distinguished men from
more obscure burial places to more renowned monuments with great pomp and
solemnity and even deposited them in magnificent temples, he judged that this
honor was much more rightly shown to the bodies and relics of God's holy
apostles, martyrs, and others.

But as soon as the bodies and relics of the saints began to be dug up and
transferred, the persuasion immediately took possession of men's minds that
the martyrs and other saints were the pillars, towers, and defenses of the
region, city, or place where their relics or bodies were deposited, as Basil
says. This superstition was immediately followed by another: they no longer
buried the bodies of the saints and other relics under the altar, but placed
them upon altars and exposed them for veneration. Hence arose the carrying of
relics in procession so that they might be held in greater admiration. And
because various miracles occurred at the martyrs' tombs, people began not
only to invoke God there but also the martyrs themselves, and even to worship
their relics. Thus the error gradually prevailed and grew into the most
superstitious worship and the grossest idolatry that now prevails.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

IX. The miracle divinely performed at Elisha's bones (2 Kings 13:21)
confirmed confidence in his preceding prophecy about the coming incursion of
the Moabites, but it does not prove religious worship of his body. Hence,
neither before nor after that miracle is anyone read to have visited Elisha's
tomb or received his bones with a religious kiss of adoration. Moreover, that
miracle was true and free from every suspicion of falsehood, which no one
could say about the alleged miracles of relics.

X. The touching of the fringe of Christ's garment (Matt. 9:21) was an
extraordinary sign of a miraculous healing. But neither this nor any other
sign of divine miracles was religiously worshiped by any godly person. Nor is
the rationale the same for Christ's garment, which he willed to use while
wearing it to demonstrate his divine power, and papal relics, which are either
uncertain or separated from the living Christ and cannot possess the same
power and efficacy.

XI. *Peter's shadow* (Acts 5:15) and *Paul's handkerchiefs and aprons*, which
healed the sick (Acts 19:12), were likewise signs of an extraordinary cure.
But neither did the apostles command that they be adored on account of this
symbolic function, nor is anyone known to have done so. Did Peter, who refused
to be adored by Cornelius while alive, will to command the adoration of his
corpse? Or would he have suffered men to fall reverently before his shadow or
aprons, which did not even belong to his essence?

XII. The transfer of the patriarchs' bones served to confirm their faith in
God's promise concerning possession of the land and the Messiah who would be
born in it. But it contributed nothing to the religious worship of their
bones, which had to be laid away and buried again.

XIII. If *Vigilantius* did nothing more than oppose the growing superstition
in the worship of relics, he could not therefore justly be accused of heresy
by

[Printed p. 47 | Internet Archive PDF 75 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Jerome or associated with the Gnostics and Eunomians, from whose opinion he
was utterly remote. Erasmus himself cannot conceal that Jerome dealt unjustly
with him in this matter when he says that Jerome raged so violently against
him that one is compelled to desire somewhat more modesty in him. Jerome
spoke as though Vigilantius denied that martyrdom should be undergone, or
thought that the martyrs' blood should be trampled underfoot and was utterly
unworthy of any honor, although he denied only that the martyrs' relics should
be worshiped. In this matter we do not deny that we agree with Vigilantius.
But if he fostered certain errors in other heads of doctrine, as Jerome
reproaches him, though it is not sufficiently established how truly, we
profess that we have nothing in common with him.
