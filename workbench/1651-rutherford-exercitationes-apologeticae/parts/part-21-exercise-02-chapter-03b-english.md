8. The scholastics say that the justice of seemliness or congruity precedes
the act of divine good pleasure, because if God wills this, he cannot will it
in a way incongruous with his justice. But they maintain that the justice of
debt and obligation follows God's good pleasure. Yet one may freely ask what
this seemliness is. It is either an eternal attribute or a relation which
regards the natures of the things willed. If in the former sense, it appears
to be the same as God's wisdom, or at least

[1651 logical printed p. 306 | IA leaf 324 | PDF p. 324]

it is a general manner and operative power in God by which he acts suitably
to his infinite nature. Thus nothing is more true than that by necessity of
nature God cannot fail to act suitably to his wisdom. Nor could it be said
without grave absurdity that God freely wills and decrees that he himself
should be fitting to his wisdom in all his acts. Indeed, he cannot fail to be
fitting in his acts, for otherwise he would not be God. But if this seemliness
regards not a wise mode of acting in God but the order of things outside God
and the arrangement of means to the end, then this seemliness follows God's
good pleasure no less than the seemliness of justice does. For it is from
God's will that this thing is the means and that one the end. “And God,” says
Durandus, “in willing his own goodness, does not necessarily will the other
things which are ordered to him as their end.”

[Marginalia: Durandus, book 1, distinction 44, question 4.]

And it is from God's governing wisdom and commanding will that this ordering
of things rather than another obtains. For if the opposite order had
obtained, it too would have been congruous with divine wisdom. Perhaps it may
be said that God necessarily employs means suited to the end, and consequently
the external ordering of things necessitates the divine will to choose this
ordering and not another. Reply 1. It does not follow. That suitability is
only such a connection and a σχετικὴ, or relational, ordering of this thing to
that one. But it is from God that this thing is the end and that one the means.
What if God had appointed stone rather than bread as man's food? What if he
had willed prosperous circumstances rather than

[1651 logical printed p. 307 | IA leaf 325 | PDF p. 325]

hardships and adverse things to be the road to glory? That order, as well as the
one which now obtains, would have been congruous with divine wisdom. 2. Two
things must be distinguished here. 1. The terms of things ordered in relation
to one another. 2. The ordering. Although the ordering itself, considered in
itself, is good and wise prior to God's free choice, that ordering nevertheless
does not necessitate God's will, because he can reject it and choose its
opposite, and such a free choice of God would be most wise. For no order can
be given which so adequately measures divine wisdom that it limits it to this
order, as Aquinas and Scotus teach. 3. All possible orderings of things which
are congruous with God's wisdom prior to the act of divine good pleasure,
although congruous in that state, possess not actual but possible congruity.
By this things can be made—that is, there is no repugnancy to their being made
suitably—not that they are actually made. For formal and actual congruity is
from God's free will, which, out of many possible modes of operation proposed
by the intellect, freely chooses this and not that. Therefore this is good
because it is chosen by God.

[Marginalia: Aquinas, part 1, *On the Will of God*, question 35, article 5;
Scotus, book 1, distinction 43, sole question.]

9. That all moral justice related to creatures is regulated by God's good
pleasure is proved as follows. 1. It is from God's free constitution that this
thing and not that one is just obedience. Why, for example, should not eating
the fruit of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil be obedience, but not
eating from the tree of life not be obedience? Why should the sacrifice of
Isaac, the spoiling of the Egyptians, and the building of the temple by

[1651 logical printed p. 308 | IA leaf 326 | PDF p. 326]

Solomon but not by David be obedience? Why should men be commanded to confess
Christ while demons are forbidden to confess him? That all these things are
equitable and just comes from nothing other than God's will. 2. If anything
were just and good prior to God's will, then God would be bound by obligation
and duty to will and do it. But God is subject to no duty or moral obligation
except to his own infinite wisdom or to himself, an obligation which is
altogether καταναγκαστικὴ, or constraining. He owes nothing to the creature,
because everything God owes his creature is either from a promise or from a
free threat. But whatever God either promises or threatens is from his free
will. Therefore God owes it to no one to will or do anything as just prior to
his own free constitution. 3. If anything were intrinsically good and just
before God's good pleasure, his good pleasure would necessarily be regulated
to will it. But God's good pleasure is the first rule, regulated by no prior
rule. The connection is proved because God would not be infinitely just and
good if he could will or do something contrary to the just and the good. 4.
All moral justice revealed either by the law of nature, in writing, or in any
other way, is from God's free and wise will, because it is not from him by
necessity of nature. For if God imposed law by an impulse of nature, he could
not fail to command and forbid the very same things which he now commands and
forbids. The consequence is proved because there is no third alternative. 5.
If anything were just and good prior to God's good pleasure, there would be a
good and just thing which did not flow from

[1651 logical printed p. 309 | IA leaf 327 | PDF p. 327]

the highest good and first just one, which would be absurd. There would be an
effect which could not be resolved into the first cause. They have nothing to
say here except that goodness and justice flow from God as light flows from
the sun by a natural impulse. But who would say that by necessity of nature
God prohibited eating from the forbidden tree or prescribed the law to the
Jews? Indeed, by necessity of nature God would then have had to create
intellectual creatures upon whom he might impose law, and hence to create the
world. Thus the world would necessarily have flowed from God from eternity by
an eternal emanation. For there would be no reason why the world should begin
at some point, just as there is no reason why God should be infinitely good
and just today rather than a thousand years ago, and so forth. But reason
cries aloud, experience cries aloud, and the sacred oracles testify that God
freely created the world and every entity for his glory. Revelation 4:11;
Proverbs 16:4; Romans 11:36. Indeed, on this account God could not have created
creatures in any other manner—as to stature, species, number, weight, measure,
or perfection—than they are now created, which also conflicts with Scripture:
Romans 9:21–22; 1 Corinthians 15:38. 6. The Fathers and Scholastics teach that
God does not will things because they are good, but that they are good because
they are willed by God. “Thus, if he willed them either not to exist or to
exist otherwise,” says Gerson, “that too would then be good.”

[Marginalia: Gerson, *The Consolation of Theology*, book 2, prose 2, p. 28.]

10. Hence it is evident that the statement “nothing is shameful in relation
to the divine will” is partly true and partly false. It is true because it is
from God's free constitution that anything morally unjust exists.

[1651 logical printed p. 310 | IA leaf 328 | PDF p. 328]

For God could have prescribed no rule to men, just as he prescribed none to
natural agents, so that whether men and angels followed the guidance of reason
or not, they would not have been guilty of sin. He could, namely, have left it
free to them to follow or not follow that guidance. Since the law is God's
most free statute, if he had decreed no such thing there would have been
nothing shameful and no sin, for God did not make the law by an impulse of
nature. Yet it is also false that nothing is shameful in relation to the
divine will. For once it is posited that God freely established moral good and
laid down the law from eternity, whatever opposes such a law is supremely
displeasing to God as shameful and dishonorable. Thus God naturally opposes
sin because of the natural rectitude and constancy of the divine nature, by
which he cannot fail to hate what opposes his wisdom.

11. The things the adversaries assert against this are as easy to dissolve as
rain is when it falls. “If we speak,” says Jackson, “of goodness actually
existing in a creature, nothing can actually exist without a preceding act of
divine goodness. But objective goodness is prior to the act and exercise of
the divine will and is considered in certain things as logically possible.
This objective goodness is so joined to them that if it is his will to give
them actual existence, they must be actually good. He could have taken life
and existence away from everyone before the flood; but that he should preserve
Noah and his family as men and not as irrational creatures is not an object of
omnipotence.” Reply 1.

[Marginalia: Jackson, *On the Divine Essence*, chapter 13, section 2, p. 146.]

[1651 logical printed p. 311 | IA leaf 329 | PDF p. 329]

The disputant departs from the state of the question. No one, I suppose, will
deny that God cannot by his free decree cause man not to be a rational animal;
nor is this what is asked. For once it is posited that this thing is a man, it
must be an animal, and once this thing is sin, it must be evil. For God cannot
fail to will that the same thing should not be the same thing. But it is from
God's
good pleasure that this thing, taken materially and denominatively—that is,
insofar as it does not yet have existence, but in the order of reason prior to
God's decree will receive existence through God's good pleasure—has this or
that kind of existence. For it belongs to God to fashion the essences and
natures of things. 2. This objective goodness of a thing is not its
non-repugnancy to existence. For in that way sin, in its objective existence
before it exists, is good, because in that order there is no repugnancy to
sin's existing. 3. If God's will is the cause of every good that actually
exists, it will also be the cause of the non-existence of the reprobates'
obedience. Therefore the reprobates' failure to obey will be from God's will.
But the Arminians maintain that sin is from God's will neither according to
its essence nor according to its existence.

12. On p. 149 of the same work he adds: “First. As is the nature of the one
willing, so are the objects of the will; like rejoices in like. That God wills
only what is consonant with his own nature is as much more essential to God
than to us as God's nature is simpler than ours. Hence the first rule of all
goodness outside him is consonance with God's essential purity and justice.”
Reply 1.

[Marginalia: Jackson.]

[1651 logical printed p. 312 | IA leaf 330 | PDF p. 330]

It is blasphemous to imagine some likeness between Creator and creature. But I
ask what likeness the creation of the world has with God's nature rather than
the non-creation of the world. Yet it is certain that God wills the world's
creation. If, therefore, he had not willed it, he would have willed the world's
non-creation. But non-creation is just as consonant with the divine nature as
creation is. Since Jackson maintains that God's willing what is consonant with
his own nature is truly essential, it follows that God essentially wills the
creation of the world, and consequently everything which he wills and does.
Therefore God would not be God if he had not created the world, if he had
delivered Christ by twelve legions of angels, or if he had raised up children
for Abraham from stones. What absurd theology! Jacob Arminius himself grants
that whatever God does upon an external object, God does all of it freely.
Jackson will deny that God acts freely and will say that he wills and does
everything by necessity of nature. Thus God will not be an intellectual agent.

[Marginalia: Arminius, reply to article 6.]

2. If the first rule of all goodness outside God is consonance with God's
essential purity and so forth, it follows most absurdly that when God forbids
sacrifices, he forbids what agrees with his essential justice; and that when
God commands the sacrifice of Isaac and afterward forbids it, he forbids what
agrees with his essential justice. 3. Hence it follows that God freely wills
and loves his own purity and justice, because it cannot be denied that God
freely wills everything outside himself which he wills, since those things

[1651 logical printed p. 313 | IA leaf 331 | PDF p. 331]

have from him their consonance with God's essential purity.

“Objection 2. God himself loves truth and sincerity,” he says, “because he is
himself true and just. He cannot approve lust and intemperance or condemn
chastity in anyone, nor can he legitimize violence. Promiscuous lust and
violence are not only evil for us, to whom they are forbidden, but also in
themselves, so that the omnipotent One could not have failed to forbid and
condemn such things as manifestly hostile to his most sacred majesty.” Reply
1. No sane person has denied that God can will and command something which,
apart from his commanding will, would be violence and monstrous barbarity. He
commanded Abraham to sacrifice his son; the Israelites to despoil the
Egyptians; and Saul to devote the Amalekites—men, pregnant women, and
infants—to the sword and utter destruction. 2. The innovator departs from the
state of the question. Once it is posited that this thing is intemperance,
violence, impure lust, or barbarity, God cannot fail to prohibit it as
contrary to his approving will. Thus it was necessary for God to forbid
violence once it was posited to be violence. But that necessity is
hypothetical, arising from the position of God's free good pleasure, for he
can command this to the creature, forbid it, neither command it, nor forbid
it. 3. But if he means that prior to God's good pleasure these things are
hostilely opposed to God's most sacred majesty, he comes near to blasphemy.
What? Do eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, failing to

[Marginalia: Jackson, pp. 159, 151.]

[1651 logical printed p. 314 | IA leaf 332 | PDF p. 332]

circumcise the Jews, and the Jews' sparing the Canaanites whom God willed to
be utterly destroyed by them essentially conflict with God's nature? Then the
law against eating the forbidden fruit flows from God by necessity of nature,
than which nothing is more absurd.

“Objection 3. God,” he says, “cannot violate his own will or wrench it by force
from the strict observance of that rule of justice which he proposes for us to
imitate.” Reply 1. On the hypothesis that this is the statute of the divine
will, God cannot fail to follow his own will. But absolutely speaking, God
wills or does nothing outside himself whose contrary he cannot will or do. 2.
Nothing is more false than that God cannot fail to observe the rule of justice
proposed to us. He laid down a law for us, not for himself. What? Is God then
bound by his own holy nature to fulfill the law and gospel: namely, to fear
himself, worship himself, sanctify the Sabbath, and believe in his own Son?
For he prescribes this rule of justice to us. But what law obliges God to
permit sin, to institute sacrifices and sacraments, and to insist upon faith
rather than good works as the condition of the new covenant? God, presumably,
is obliged by the Mosaic rule of sacrifice to command sacrifices, yet no such
rule exists unless we return to God's good pleasure.

“Objection 4. Hence,” he says, “it is evident that those examples of holiness
and perfection which we ought to imitate in God are not to be taken from his
bare command or the revelation of his will, but from the objects of his
revealed will or from God's eternal actions, which he has exhibited to us as
so many express and

[Marginalia: p. 152.]

[1651 logical printed p. 315 | IA leaf 333 | PDF p. 333]

most fully attested arguments that his will is just and holy. God wills us to be conformed to this
will so that we may become conformed to his nature, for God commands us to be
holy and perfect as he himself is.” Reply 1. The holiness God requires from us
is conformity to God's bare, free, and revealed will, not simply to God's
nature. This is evident, for what else is God's law than God's free good
pleasure, by which he willed to be worshiped by us in this manner and not that
one, when, if it had seemed good to God's wise good pleasure, he could have
required from us a very different mode of worship, indeed an opposite worship?
Is it from necessity of the divine nature that he willed the gospel and not
the law to be the way to glory for sinners, or that he willed faith and not
the works of the law to be the condition of the new covenant? Yet all the
Arminians, on Romans 9, place God's absolute freedom in justifying either
workers or believers in precisely this. Is it from necessity of the divine
nature that God instituted water in baptism and bread and wine in the Lord's
Supper as the elemental signs, as if God would have sinned against his
essential justice if he had instituted other signs? Would God have been unjust
if he had instituted circumcision for the Gentiles and baptism for the Jews
as the seal of the covenant? 2. He maintains that the examples of divine
holiness which we ought to imitate must be taken from the objects of the
revealed will. Come then, Thomas: this is an object of the revealed will—that
the Canaanites are to be destroyed, that Isaac is to be sacrificed by his
father, and that water is to be the sign in baptism. If the example of divine

[1651 logical printed p. 316 | IA leaf 334 | PDF p. 334]

holiness is taken from these things, it will be true prior to God's will that
Isaac is to be sacrificed. Therefore the Jews who sacrificed tender infants
did not sin but in this imitated God's essential holiness. Again, these things
are holy—“the Canaanites are to be destroyed” and “infants are to be baptized
with water”—either because God freely willed them, in which case the examples
of divine holiness which we ought to imitate are to be taken from God's
revealed will, contrary to what he asserts; or they are holy and just in
themselves, essentially and objectively, as he says, and therefore God is holy
by the objective holiness of created things. What could be more absurd? 3. If
the formal reason of the holiness commanded to us is to be taken from God's
eternal acts and practice, proceed then: it was God's practice to command a
father to sacrifice his son; therefore we too may sacrifice our sons. It was
God's practice to permit sin; therefore Eli the priest did not sin by
permitting his sons to sin, but in this was holy as God is holy. It is God's
practice to kill kings; therefore we may kill the Lord's anointed. What absurd
theology! 4. It is most false that we ought to conform ourselves to the divine
nature, nor does this fall even upon angelic creatures. For men and angels are
conformed to God because they are conformed to his will which freely commands
this, not because they conform themselves to his nature. 5. And that holiness
of God which we ought to imitate is holiness in acting holily and blamelessly
toward his creatures, and it is not in God

[1651 logical printed p. 317 | IA leaf 335 | PDF p. 335]

essentially but from his absolute good pleasure, just as I said of God's mercy
and justice insofar as they are related to creatures. God's holiness shines
forth in this: that he sincerely loves and approves the things which he
commands rational creatures. If God had commanded both angels and men things
very different from, indeed opposite to, those which he now in fact commands,
Jehovah would have been no less holy than he now is. Christ commands us to be
merciful and willing lovers of our enemies, as the heavenly Father is. But the
heavenly Father would have been just as merciful and just as perfect as he now
is if he had provided his enemies neither sun nor an abundance of rain and had
shown them mercy in no way, for he has mercy upon whom he wills.

13. John Cameron thought that another path should be taken in resolving the
question. He says, “Nothing is loved by God because it is good, but each thing
is good because it is loved by God.” Cameron replies, “Therefore he will not
love even himself because he is good, but must be regarded as good because he
loves himself. If this is absurd to them, the same must hold in the image of
God. For if God inclines by a natural and immutable propensity to love
himself, he will embrace his own image with an entirely similar affection.”
Reply. Cameron's consequence is empty: therefore God is good because he loves
himself. For God is good essentially, not by participation. Created entities
are good by participation in divine goodness. But God freely bestows goodness
upon creatures because he wills to, and therefore

[Marginalia: John Cameron, volume 3, *Miscellanies*, p. 575; and volume 2,
*Lectures on Matthew*, chapter 16, pp. 140–143.]

[1651 logical printed p. 318 | IA leaf 336 | PDF p. 336]

they are good insofar as God wills them. 2. It by no means follows that God
loves his image by a natural propensity. For that a father cherishes his son
and provides for his life, and that a loan is repaid, are no small parts of
God's image in man. Yet God can will that Abraham slay his son and that the
Israelites not repay their loan to the Egyptians. Therefore he loves his image
freely, not by an impulse of nature. For God's image is nothing but legal
holiness in man, and legal holiness is conformity to God's free will, not to a
natural desire of God. Otherwise God cannot command things other than those
which he commands, or forbid others. Indeed, in that case sacred Scripture
will not be God's free counsel but God's natural desire, which can no more
fail to be in God than God can be not-God. “Some things,” he says, “are
ἀδιάφορα, indifferent things, which represent God's image only under the
aspect of being and not under the aspect of moral goodness—that is, insofar as
God is being, not insofar as he is just and holy. He can command or forbid
these as he pleases. But,” he says in volume 4, p. 572, “God can neither damn
an innocent person nor absolve the guilty, nor can he forbid love of himself,
commend hatred of himself, or command it. These things conflict with his
nature.” Reply 1. The distinction is altogether futile and empty. For all
created things and human actions, considered by themselves, are indifferent
in themselves. They are morally good insofar as God wills and commands them,
and evil insofar as he does not will and wills them. Such are loving,
worshiping, speaking, eating, drinking, and killing

[Marginalia: as cited at chapter 2.]

[1651 logical printed p. 319 | IA leaf 337 | PDF p. 337]

a man. For all these things, physically considered, are neither good nor evil.
Loving God is physically indifferent, and morally good because it is commanded
by God. 2. The distinction has no members. For there is no greater reason why
things are indifferent in themselves because they represent God as being than
because they represent God as just and holy. Things which represent God's
justice and holiness are not good because they represent God's nature, but
because they agree with God's just and holy yet free will. 3. God, he says,
cannot damn an innocent person. But this inability does not arise from God's
nature but from God's will. For he damned his own innocent Son and absolved us
who are guilty. And if God had not from eternity determined within himself
and most freely established that he would absolve the guilty, he absolutely
could have not absolved the guilty. 4. Forbidding the love of God does not
simply conflict with God's nature, but with his free will. Cameron says this
because he thinks loving God agrees with God's nature and therefore believes
that not loving God is opposed to God's nature. But I ask what kind of
opposition this is: relative, contradictory, contrary, or privative? Certainly
nothing is opposed either relatively or contrarily to God, the superessential
substance, for God is not an accident; nor is anything opposed to him
contradictorily except in the way in which all created entities are
contradictorily opposed to him. He must therefore say that τὸ non amare Deum,
“not to love God,” is privatively opposed to God. But in that way τὸ non-amare
Deum, “not-loving God,”

[1651 logical printed p. 320 | IA leaf 338 | PDF p. 338]

would privatively remove God and his nature. What could be more blasphemous?
For once the non-love of God were posited, God himself would be removed. But
just as τὸ amare Deum, “to love God,” agrees with God extrinsically, insofar
as it agrees with his revealed will—for nothing accrues to God when what he
wills is fulfilled, and he is no happier for it—so nothing adverse to the
divine nature is posited by the fact that men do not love God. Rather,
something is posited which God freely wills not to occur.

14. Of the same kind are the things which Arminius objected in the third place.
“There are many things,” he says, “which God wills because they are just. It
is just that God alone be acknowledged by the creature as the true God. God
wills this because it is just; it is not just because God wills it, and so
forth.” Reply. The whole is granted in a sound sense, for he continually
departs from the state of the question. After God has once expressed this in
his Word and implanted it in the human mind at creation as a certain law of
nature—“it is equitable that God be acknowledged by the rational creature as
the true God”—then God cannot fail to will it without the stain of inconstancy.
Thus God wills things which are just because by a prior act of his good
pleasure he had established those same things as just. But after God has
freely established that something should occur, it does not fall under
another free constitution of God so that it may freely be done or not done by
him. For now, by hypothetical necessity, God cannot will it otherwise. But
things in objective existence and taken materially—that is, in the order of
reason in which their existence is to be established by God's free decree and

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Antiperkins*, p. 693.]

[1651 logical printed p. 321 | IA leaf 339 | PDF p. 339]

has not yet been established—can fall under God's free constitution either as
just, or as not just by a negative decree. Therefore, if τὸ colere Deum, “to
worship God,” is included in the nature and quiddity of a rational creature—a
point which I do not wish to debate here—we will entirely depart from the state
of the question. For the question is not whether it is equitable from eternity
that man worship God, nor whether God wills it because it is equitable. Rather,
if τὸ colere Deum, “to worship God,” could be separated, with respect to the
equity of natural obligation, from the nature of a rational creature, then I
say that, if it had so seemed good to God, it could have been the case that it
was not equitable for the rational creature to worship the Creator. For if God
had established that the rational creature should not worship the Creator,
that would have been no less equitable and just than its opposite now is.
Here the question is implicated: it is from God's freedom that the rational
creature exists, and from that same freedom that the worship of God exists,
although God's existence is not from God's freedom. But there is no doubt
that God could have fashioned or not fashioned the rational creature.

15. A question akin to these is whether and to what extent we are bound to
conform our will to the divine will in its object. The answer is that we are
not bound to do so with respect to the material object. For God's intention
and will was that Abraham should not sacrifice Isaac. But in Abraham, who was
commanded to sacrifice him, there was an altogether contrary will and
intention that he should sacrifice him, and this will of Abraham was most just.

[1651 logical printed p. 322 | IA leaf 340 | PDF p. 340]

Hence it is evident that God's approving will by no means includes God's
intention that the object of the approving will should actually exist. God
wills that a father burdened with old age should die, yet a son does not sin
by willing that the old man should survive, as Augustine observes in
*Enchiridion*, chapter 10. But we are bound to conform our will to God's
approving will insofar as it is revealed to us. If we speak of the formal
object, Durandus and Valentia say that we ought to will every good in relation
to God's glory, which must be suitably understood. For all men are bound to
will the relation of things, even of sin, to God's glory. Although it is not
lawful for us even to will sin so that God's glory may be displayed, we
nevertheless ought to will that sin actually be directed to God's glory. For
this is God's revealed, equitable, and holy will. Therefore we can will that
God do something whose object or matter we ourselves can neither do nor will
without horrendous guilt. Nor can it be said that we ought, by our permissive
will, to will sin insofar as it conduces to the display of divine glory. God
can, with the best right and without violation of his duty, will this, and he
does will it. The sole cause of this difference is that God did not subject
himself to law, but to his own free constitution, whereas we are subject to
law. Hence it is evident that God did not bind his will precisely to the
particular rule to which he bound us. He wills the killing of a son by his
father to be homicide, yet in such a way that he himself could command a
father to kill his son; and he willed taking others' goods against their
owners' wishes

[Marginalia: Durandus, book 1, distinction 48, question 2; Valentia, volume 2,
disputation 2, question 14, p. 8.]

[1651 logical printed p. 323 | IA leaf 341 | PDF p. 341]

to be sin, yet in such a way that he himself could command the Israelites to
take the Egyptians' precious goods from them. But more on this in the
disputation on God's providence.
