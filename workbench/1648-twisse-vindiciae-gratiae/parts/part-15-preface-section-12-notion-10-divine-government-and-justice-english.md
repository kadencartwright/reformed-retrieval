SECTION TWELVE NOTION X

PERKINS

Not a part but the whole government of the world and execution of justice must be attributed to God as author.

ARMINIUS

It is certain that God presides over the whole world and all things created by him, and administers and governs all things collectively and individually. But he does so not only in justice, but also in mercy, insofar as he himself in his infinite wisdom knows what place must be assigned to each.

RESPONSE 1

Yet Perkins will not say that God administers all things collectively and individually only in justice. Rather, in my judgment he specifically designates the execution of justice because one might doubt more about it than about the execution of mercy—whether it is to be attributed to God—namely, whenever God executes his justice through the sins of human beings. For example, it sometimes happens that a sinful act, such as the sexual violation of David’s concubines perpetrated by Absalom, serves to punish a human being, just as that aforementioned act, according to Arminius, served to punish David, p. 162. “But punishments of sins are not permitted by God, but are inflicted by the just Judge, and have God himself as author,” according to the same Arminius, p. 127.

But what then? Is not the entire administration of things the execution of divine justice? Is not every administration of things directed toward the ends fixed by God? But once the ends fixed by God are posited, was it not just that God should administer things in the very manner in which he administers them? Certainly it was, since it cannot be denied that all things administered by God are administered fittingly and becomingly. But what else is this than justly? Indeed, Arminius himself does not hold that God exercised mercy in saving the human race unless justice governed it. Add to this the Schoolmen, who assert that whatever is done by God must be done justly by God, that is, with that justice which is the fittingness of goodness.

[Praefatio; printed p. 32; BSB canvas 40]

ARMINIUS 1

But indeed, my dear Perkins, do all those axioms seem to you to be natural and common notions? Perhaps they are notions of nature as it was instituted by the Creator, but certainly not of that nature which has been darkened through sin. For it is natural for few among human beings to know and understand them.

2. The whole cohort of Pelagians and Semipelagians in the Church itself was ignorant of them.

3. What the mind and opinion of many Greek and Latin philosophers was concerning most of these points is evident from a saying employed by more than one of them: “That we exist has been given to us by the gods; that we are good we have from ourselves.” Augustine vigorously opposes this notorious falsehood in more than one passage.

RESPONSE 1

But it is also most certain that, whether all these are common notions or not, provided that we agree among ourselves about them, and that our doctrine of predestination ought to accord with them and may, if it so happens, be founded upon them as well, the matter will be beyond controversy. Provided, I say, that they belong to the common understanding—if not of everyone, at least of Christians—although even among them not of all, yet at least of the learned. They are called principles of natural knowledge by Perkins either in this sense, that knowledge of them is naturally implanted, or rather because it is or can be naturally acquired, that is, demonstrated more or less evidently from natural principles. And certainly not even an Arminian would doubt that many of these can without question be established by natural reason. But as for those to which Arminius points his finger before the rest—namely, 6, 7, and 8, concerning the efficient causation of our good works ascribed to God—there is none among these notions which we judge cannot be evidently demonstrated, once this principle is posited: namely, that God concurs in every motion of a rational nature. But as for this principle: does not the Apostle quote from Aratus that “in God we live, move, and exist”? Yet from where did this become known to Aratus—from natural light or from supernatural? The Schoolmen also attempted to deduce this principle from natural principles and, unless I am mistaken, succeeded; perhaps there will be an occasion for us to examine this. For although the adversaries thus far agree with us concerning God’s concurrence required for the production of every act and entity, there is nevertheless a place where Arminius seems to have contemplated renouncing this principle. If at some time the fortunes of the Church call us to this point, it will have to be tried what can be established in this area. Likewise, Callimachus’s declaration on this point is well known: θεοὶ δωτῆρες ἐάων—“the gods are givers of good things.” Certainly Aristotle in the *Ethics* declares happiness, which he defines as an activity of the soul according to virtue, to be a gift of God. In the *Physics* he also expressly professes that, according to the ancient philosophers, θεῖόν τι—“something divine”—intervenes even in fortuitous and chance events. Then indeed, if we philosophize about natural reason insofar as it is illuminated by the light of Christian grace, who will not acknowledge how much more can be accomplished by it, provided that we ourselves have by no means failed either nature or grace? But however these matters may stand, provided we agree among ourselves about these principles, the matter will be safe.

[Marginalia: Acts 1; Aristotle, *Physics*, book 2.]

2. Allow me, I ask, to add weight to this argument and to sound in your ear that even the whole cohort of Arminians by no means embraces all these points, but rejects some of them; see also whether they reject the same ones as the Pelagians. I know that you will protest the contrary at the top of your voices; but why should I listen to words when I see deeds? Would that the matter itself said this. If it does not, we pay no heed to your words uttered with so little candor and modesty. But grant that the Pelagians reject them, and you as well; knowledge of them will not therefore cease to be natural because not everyone has embraced them. For that God exists is also a principle of natural knowledge, and yet there have not been lacking those who denied it. The same can be said about the philosophers, for not every natural truth became known to the philosophers. Finally, what do you mean when, after presenting their opinion, you lash it with censure as capable of being convicted of notorious falsehood? Must that be called notoriously false which is above all natural reason, so that it should be held to be false?
