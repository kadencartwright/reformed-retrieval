MATTHEW 5:13.

“You are the salt of the earth.”

Granted the truth of Hugo Grotius's judgment that these words should not be restricted to Christ's apostles, since they are common to all believers, nevertheless the opinion of more recent theologians suggests that they at least refer especially to ecclesiastical teachers; the authority of the ancient Church determines it; and finally, the sequence of the words and the manifest truth of the matter itself cry it aloud.

For these men, though the Socinians and the other enemies of the gospel ministry gnash their teeth, are—and until Christ's advent, we believe, will be—that city set on a hill, verse 14;

that lamp, now indeed placed for a little while under a bushel, but at last to be set again upon a lampstand, verse 15;

in short, to express everything in a few words, the light of the world and the salt of the earth: “You are the salt of the earth.”

“You”: namely, chiefly you apostles, but according to their measure the other ministers too, consecrated no less to Christ than to the Church.

“Are”: several in actual fact, all by virtue of office.

“The salt”: Nor, I think, should we fear that some Papist mass-monger, who knows how to transubstantiate bread into Christ's body from a similar phrase, might from this one finally transform himself into a pillar of salt, as we read happened to Lot's wife. For the expression is metaphorical, signifying not the same power but analogous offices and a similar virtue. What salt is to flesh, you are to the earth.

Finally, “the salt of the earth,” which in the following clause is called “the light of the world,” embraces the whole globe of the lands. The former was indeed peculiar to the apostles, as Saint Chrysostom notes, because of the universal episcopate entrusted to them in Mark 16:15, whereas the prophets of old were generally confined within the narrower borders of Judea, just as gospel pastors watch over their own flocks. Yet what belonged individually to those greater luminaries belongs collectively to the constellation of lesser stars—that is, to the whole company of ministers scattered throughout the world, as the Levites of old throughout all Israel.

[1679 new-series fol. 37 | IA leaf 357]

Nor does this belong so much to their persons as to their office. For, as Chemnitz rightly says, what properly belongs to the Word is transferred to the persons of the ministry. They enjoy this sort of commendation because the gospel has been entrusted to them; the gospel itself enjoys it because Christ is contained within it, who is both that light enlightening every person who comes into the world, John 1:9, and that salt which seasons for eternity all flesh that had corrupted itself upon the earth, Genesis 6:12.

As for the text, it sets before us three chief things to note: 1. the condition of the people; 2. the dignity of the ministry; and 3. finally, the ministers' duty and office.

1. First, as for the people's condition, it crawls on the ground and rises no higher than the earth.

This does not mean that we may look down upon them from the pulpit as from on high, as though they were children of the earth and, as Job says, viler than the earth itself; nor that we may tread them down more arrogantly under the foot of pride as though they were mud in the streets. This suits the pride of the Pharisees and of their Papist followers. The former used to call the crowd “an accursed rabble,” just as the earth had once been called accursed; the latter trample upon all the laity as though they were stones to be cast away, so that the thing may answer to the etymology of the word itself. They regard them as the dogs and swine before whom they refuse to cast their pearls, and finally they commonly call them their beasts of burden and asses, which they may ride with haughty insolence as they please. But our most gentle Savior, though he indeed rode upon an ass, did not act in this way; he did not regard as castaways the sheep for whom he shed his blood. Nor did the prince of the apostles, whose successors would rather bear his title than imitate him, wish Christ's lambs whom he was charged to feed to be treated badly. He sternly forbade even their overseers to lord it over the allotted flocks, and he distinguished by the most honorable title of “clergy” those whom they contemptuously call “laity,” despite Bellarmine's strenuous opposition.

[Marginalia: Chapter 30:8; Psalm 18:43; John 7:49; Genesis 3:17; Matthew 7:6; 1 Peter 5:3; *De Clericis*, book 1, chapter 1.]

For although the Christian people are here called earth, once enclosed within the Church's boundaries and diligently cultivated and seasoned by our careful labor, they are called the garden of Eden, the Bridegroom's paradise, God's cultivated field, and everywhere in Revelation, heaven. Far be it that they should be rejected like the earth that produces thorns and thistles and is near to a curse. They are indeed to be seasoned with this salt, not given over to salt like the muddy and marshy places in Ezekiel; to be irrigated by foot, Deuteronomy 11:10, not trampled by the Gentiles, Revelation 11:2.

[Marginalia: Hebrews 6:8; Ezekiel 47:11.]

Yet although this designation “earth” gives us no occasion to insult them, it supplies the people with a sufficiently powerful argument for the deepest humility.

1. First it indicates their place, and that the lowest. The earth settles at the bottom, and even the law of related words can sufficiently show that *humus*, the earth, is humble. If it were carried upward, it would act against nature. If it were to swell with a hill-like mass—especially against its cultivators—as commonly happens in an earthquake, and as the philosopher recalls once occurring on the “sacred island” (though by now this has plainly happened everywhere on our decidedly unsacred island), it relates portents and threatens ruin.

2. Second, it intimates their disposition and character: an anxious concern for earthly things together with disregard for heavenly things. For “the one from the earth is earthy,” 1 Corinthians 15:47; “he who is from the earth is earthly” and “speaks the things of the earth,” John 3:31; he “minds earthly things,” Philippians 3:19. He speaks, savors, and cares for earthly things; like a mole he has a snout

[1679 new-series fol. 38 | IA leaf 358]

with which to dig the earth, but no eyes with which to behold heaven, except when death alone is impending.

3. Finally, if this earth needs the seasoning of this salt, let it willingly acknowledge that it is altogether tasteless in itself and, insofar as it lacks this holier sprinkling, utterly putrid and rancid, very like that most wretched infant of Ezekiel 16 who was not salted with salt. Suppose, then—though may it never be—that we have no salt in ourselves: we have lost only that which they equally lack. A foolish pastor is salt that has lost its savor; but, if I may say it with their leave, a foolish people is no less insipid. Therefore let them pity us, who in this respect are their equals in sin; but if we are the salt of the earth, let them at least revere us as their superiors in office.

[Marginalia: Mark 9, final verse; Zechariah 11:15; Deuteronomy 32:6.]
