Second, the precept forbidding marriage to be contracted between blood
relations, or within the prohibited degrees, does not flow from God’s nature;
otherwise God would never have permitted or commanded this among Adam’s
children and among the Israelites. When God created Adam, he could have
created several distinct human beings and formed a wife for each of them, just
as he formed Eve, and in that way prevented this union of close relations.
Indeed, if such a union had been contrary to his nature, he would have guarded
against it in this way. Yet this precept is moral. The most distinguished
Essenius set out the argument as follows in his *Vindiciae*, page 33:
“Immediately there follows there”—namely in Leviticus 18—“verse 6: ‘No one
shall approach any close relation of his flesh to uncover nakedness: I am
Jehovah’; and, more particularly, verse 2: ‘You shall not uncover the nakedness
of your sister, the daughter of your father or the daughter of your mother,’
and so forth, ‘you shall not uncover their nakedness.’ These are certainly
moral precepts, and the Opponent, I think, will not deny it. Yet they do not
flow necessarily from

[1674 logical printed p. 220 | IA leaf 238 | PDF p. 238]

the nature of God, as though by his nature God could not have either ordained
or ethically permitted marriage, and consequently sexual union, between
persons closely related by blood, especially brother and sister, to be lawful.
Indeed, the children born from Adam and Eve contracted marriage with their
full sisters, and did so in accordance with God’s ordinance, ‘Be fruitful,
multiply, and fill the earth,’ as long, that is, as there were no more distant
persons with whom they could contract marriage. On the side of human beings,
or of human nature, I see in these circumstances a necessity why they should
contract marriage with such close relations. They were obliged to submit to
that necessity and to join themselves together while not yet prohibited by a
law of incest. But no necessity can be imposed upon the omnipotent and most
wise God to ordain or ethically permit anything contrary to his own nature.
Nor has anyone who disagrees with us yet demonstrated from God’s nature—or is
able to demonstrate—that, once sovereign power and a free command are set
aside, marriages of this sort are necessarily unlawful. We, however, hold and
know them to be unlawful and prohibited by the moral law, but on account of
the sovereign power and free command by which it pleased the supreme
legislator to bind human nature from the beginning, yet in such a way that
this law had to be observed as soon as persons not closely related by blood
existed with whom marriage could be contracted.”

The adversary replies in his *Apology*, pages 34–35: “That the law concerning
incest rests upon natural right is evident because it bound all nations also.
For God punished those nations for violating this law (Leviticus 18:24, 25,
28; 20:17; Deuteronomy 27:20). The Corinthian also was excommunicated for the
same cause, as for ‘sexual immorality that was not even named among the
nations’: having his father’s wife (1 Corinthians 5:1).” Reply: 1. The present
question is not whether this law rests upon natural right. It is whether it
flows from God’s free good pleasure or from his own nature and attributes. For
I am not afraid to affirm that a law may be said to rest upon natural right
and nevertheless flow from God’s entirely free good pleasure. 2. The reason
by which he proves that this law rests upon natural right is not consistent
with what preceded. For in his view the natural law and the moral law are the
same, and he wishes the precept of faith in Christ to be a moral or natural
law. Yet he will not say that the precept of faith in Christ also binds all
nations. Where, then, is the force of this reasoning? 3. We know that the
nations were punished for violating this law and that the Corinthian was
excommunicated, for we say that this precept is moral. Yet it is not proved
from this that the precept necessarily flows from the divine nature, either
immediately or mediately, once human nature and its creation in the image of
God are posited. Therefore this response is no response at all.

“And thus,” he continues, “all theologians who hold this law to be natural
also establish it from nature, and therefore from the nature and holiness of
God as well, once the order of things is posited, although some do so with
stronger and others with less firm reasons. Hence the natural right of this
law is not equally clear to everyone. Yet this always remains: those who wish
this law to be moral also confess it to be natural, while he who denies it to
be natural must at the same time deny it to be moral.” Reply: 1. Although all
theologians who hold this law to be natural establish it from nature, it does
not follow that for that very reason they establish it from the nature and
holiness of God, unless he supposes that God’s nature is the same as human
nature, or that everything which flows from the latter also flows from the
former and, conversely, that what flows from the former likewise flows from
the latter. That is absurd, even once the order of things is posited. 2. It
does not remain fixed and indubitable that those who wish this law to be moral
also confess it to be natural, if “natural” is said only of

[1674 logical printed p. 221 | IA leaf 239 | PDF p. 239]

that which necessarily flows from the divine nature or from human nature. 3.
It is no less false to say that those who deny it to be natural—that is, who
assert that it flows from sovereign power—must at the same time deny it to be
moral. For things can be moral which flow from God’s free good pleasure,
whatever he may say to the contrary.

“That incest is contrary to natural shame,” he says, “is sufficiently evident
from the nations, which knew this law apart from sovereign power and a free
command of God ever revealed to them, since they suffered punishments from God
for violating it.” Reply: 1. He has no need to prove that incest is contrary
to natural shame, for we do not deny it. We deny only that this precept flows
from God’s nature, and neither he nor any other theologian supplies us with a
proof of that. It has often been said that what is natural for us can be
positive for God, or can flow from his sovereign power and free good pleasure.
2. He supposes that sovereign power and God’s free command were never revealed
to the nations, as though it were impossible for anything which proceeds from
sovereign power and God’s free command to be implanted in the human mind and
expounded through the nature and order of things.

He sends us off to Hendrik Brouwer, whom I do not have at hand; to Heidegger,
who says nothing more than the adversary himself; and to Ames’s *On
Conscience*, book 5, chapter 35, section 14, who gives the following natural
reason for the matter: “Nature teaches that a special modesty and reverence is
owed to parents and close blood relations, to which the exercise of the
marital relation is opposed, something about which people are ashamed by
nature.” Reply: At most, this and the other reasons prove that the precept is a
matter of natural right for us. They do not prove that it is a matter of
natural right for God or that it flows necessarily from God’s nature and
attributes. For if it were such, God would never have commanded a Jew to marry
the widow of his brother who had died without offspring. He would never have
permitted the Patriarchs to be guilty of a thing bearing that name, and since
he could have forestalled it in many ways, he would by an act have prevented
this incest among Adam’s children.

To this last point he replies: “Nor is it an objection that things happened
differently among Adam’s sons and daughters. For by this very fact no
dispensation from natural shame was made. We said above in axiom 4 that
natural laws are indispensable and immutable once the order of things has
been established. Thus it is never lawful to do anything contrary to natural
shame. But through a change of certain circumstances it can happen that what
was formerly contrary to natural shame is now no longer contrary to it. In
this way it is not the moral precept, but the thing itself, which is changed.”
Reply: Above, in axiom 4, he had indeed said that certain things flow
mediately from the image of God through the intervention of the constitution
of things, and that these are not absolutely and immutably moral in themselves,
since they depend upon the will of God, who could have chosen not to create
these things. He added further that those things whose natures God willed to
be immutable must be called immutably moral, and that to that extent
dispensation has no place in them, since otherwise God would deny his own will
or decree. Hence it is evident that the established order of things, which he
mentions in the axiom, does nothing to remove this difficulty. For, 1. that
order is the order of creation, which was fully established when creation was
finished. Yet notwithstanding this established order, we see that at least a
dispensation was made from natural shame and that natural laws are not
indispensable and immutable for God. 2. We see likewise that, without the
natures of things being changed, but with only some change of circumstances,

[1674 logical printed p. 222 | IA leaf 240 | PDF p. 240]

a dispensation is made without God denying his own will and decree. But let
him show how the nature of things was changed when Adam’s sons married their
full sisters, so that what had previously been contrary to natural shame was
then no longer contrary to it. 3. What will he say about that divine
constitution prescribed to the Israelites? Was the order of things not then
established? Were the natures of things changed? Can any circumstance be named
whose alteration could have caused what was previously contrary to natural
shame to be no longer contrary to it? I know that God commanded nothing
without reason. But I ask whether there was any such change of circumstances
which, setting aside God’s command, required that practice or even made it
lawful. If no such change can be named, it is plain that this all depends upon
God’s free good pleasure and that, once God’s command is given, what would
otherwise be unlawful becomes lawful. Hence it is sufficiently clear that
something can be moral, and indeed natural, for us which is positive for God
and in which he can dispense as he pleases.

He continues: “Parents are never to be violated but always honored, and to
honor a parent can never be vice, nor to violate him virtue. Yet it can happen
that what was just now honoring a parent becomes, through changed
circumstances, violating him, and conversely—as when parents are killed at
God’s command (Deuteronomy 33:9). The honor of parents therefore always
remains, but the acts by which they are honored are not always the same. In the
same way, loving a son is always moral and immutable, but that love does not
always consist in nourishing and preserving the son; sometimes it consists in
the contrary act (Matthew 10:37; Genesis 22:2).” Reply: 1. I do not yet grasp
how killing parents is honoring them, or how sacrificing a son is nourishing
and preserving him. He should have spoken in this way, because love of one’s
neighbor is not commanded more in one precept of the second table than in
another. But, as it seems, he perceived sufficiently how absurd this would
appear and therefore wished to use another term, though less candidly. 2.
Precepts are given to regulate our particular actions; they are not proposed
as buskins to be fitted to any action whatever. Accordingly, when the fifth
precept commands us to honor our parents, it commands us to show them due
honor in everything and to do nothing which would violate their honor, whether
in thought, word, or deed. 3. If, when the circumstances changed, what had just
been honoring a parent became violating him, then as long as the circumstances
do not change, what was honoring a parent will always be honoring a parent.
But when the one circumstance here which caused what had previously been
violating a parent to become honoring him is God’s will commanding it, then,
as long as the divine will remains, killing parents will always be honoring
parents. Let us therefore suppose that God commands the same thing to others,
as he once did to the Levites. Will we say that this act is naturally good or
commanded in the law of nature, or that this command flows from God’s nature
and attributes? 4. Hence it is evident that the precept forbidding filicide or
parricide flows not from God’s nature but from his mere will. For when he
wills, an act which would otherwise be filicide or parricide will not be
filicide or parricide; and conversely, according to his good pleasure, an act
which would otherwise be the honor owed to parents, or the nourishment and
preservation owed to children, will be filicide and parricide. 5. He insinuates
that in killing their parents the Levites honored them, and that in killing
their children parents gave testimony of their love toward them. But the cited
texts prove nothing of the kind. For Deuteronomy 23:9 says, “Levi said of his
father and his mother, ‘I do not regard him’; he does not recognize his
brothers, and does not

[1674 logical printed p. 223 | IA leaf 241 | PDF p. 241]

acknowledge his children.” And Matthew 10:37 says, “He who loves father or
mother more than me is not worthy of me, and he who loves son or daughter more
than me is not worthy of me.” These words read as follows in the parallel
passage, Luke 14:26: “If anyone comes to me and does not hate his father and
mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters, and indeed even his own life,
he cannot be my disciple.” These words, however, intimate something else. 6.
These things abundantly confirm our position, because it depends not upon
God’s nature but upon his free good pleasure whether or not this act is a
violation of natural shame, a violation of the honor of parents, parricide, or
filicide, and so forth. What is said of this particular act can be said of all
others—at least all external acts. Therefore no precept concerning any
external act whatever will flow from God’s nature and attributes, and
consequently no external act will be morally good or evil but purely positive.
Yet since the generic precept, so to speak, is always moral, while the action
commanded by this precept is positive, we can say without any absurdity that
the precept concerning this particular action is moral-positive.

“Thus,” he continues, “stealing one’s neighbor’s goods is always evil. Yet
goods which belonged to one’s neighbor apart from an intervening command of
God are not always his goods, as is evident in the case of the Egyptians’
goods (Exodus 2:2). God therefore can no more permit, O man, that I steal
another’s goods while they are and remain another’s, but he can cause the
goods which I seize no longer to be another’s but to be mine.” Reply: Everyone
confesses that God has absolute dominion over us and our goods, and no one
denies that he can transfer title to those goods from one person to another.
But this does nothing to the point. For the same action which would have been
sin and theft apart from God’s command now becomes lawful when God’s command
intervenes. It is consequently clear that whether this action is morally good
or evil depends upon God’s free good pleasure; see what was said above in
chapter 3. 2. God does not command the Israelites to take the Egyptians’ goods
because he had transferred title to the goods to them. He simply commands
them to take the goods on loan, clearly denoting that the Egyptians had civil
title to those same goods even when the Israelites requested them. Here שאל
means “to request on loan,” as may be seen in the *Dutch Annotations*, and
elsewhere it means to request with entreaties (1 Samuel 20:28; Nehemiah 13:6;
Psalms 21:5; 27:4; see also 2 Chronicles 20:4). They accordingly requested them
on loan and received them on loan (Exodus 12:35–36). The text says in addition
that “they despoiled the Egyptians”; נצל means “he despoiled, seized, stole,
plundered.”

He continues: “Therefore, to philosophize correctly, in these precepts the
external act of beneficence, homicide, incest, or the seizure of goods
previously mine is not properly commanded or forbidden as moral, for these
acts are mutable. See axiom 8. In the fifth precept, however, the honor of
parents is commanded; in the sixth, hatred of one’s neighbor is forbidden; in
the seventh, the violation of natural shame; in the eighth, possession of
another’s goods. These are immutable, even though the acts themselves are
mutable.” Reply: 1. Then no external act, even clothed with all its
circumstances and prohibited by the fifth or sixth precept, will be morally
evil, nor any commanded act morally good. 2. Above, in axiom 8, he had said
that no external act at all is morally good or evil in itself. But here he says
that the violation of natural shame and possession of another’s goods, which
are external actions, are immutable and moral. I cannot reconcile these
statements. 3. It is remarkable indeed if the external act of beneficence,
homicide, or incest

[1674 logical printed p. 224 | IA leaf 242 | PDF p. 242]

is not something moral. 4. Above, in axiom 3, he wished, strictly speaking,
nothing to be called moral except love for God and one’s neighbor. Here,
however, he adds two other things, namely, “violation of natural shame” and
“possession of another’s goods.” I cannot make these statements agree either.
5. If only hatred of one’s neighbor is forbidden in the sixth precept, what is
prohibited there that is not prohibited in the other precepts of the second
table? 6. He says that the honor of parents is commanded in the fifth precept,
but in what does that honor consist? Is honor commanded without being
signified by any external sign? 7. I should like him to explain what he
understands by a violation of natural shame, if the act of incest is not such
a violation. 8. He will indeed be a remarkable expositor of the Decalogue’s
moral precepts. Nor is it any wonder that he denies the Sabbath precept to be
moral when he denies that a precept concerning an external act of beneficence,
homicide, incest, and the like is morally good or evil.
