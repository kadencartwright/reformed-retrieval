OBJECTIONS.

Objection 1. He to whom everyone appeals, and from whom it is not permissible
to appeal, is the head of the Church. But such is the Pope. Therefore.

Reply. The assumption is denied.

An instance by which the assumption is proved: the Council of Nicaea wishes
all bishops, if their ordinary judges are suspect, to appeal freely to the
Apostolic See. Athanasius cites this canon in his *Epistle to Pope Felix*, and
Pope Julius in his *Epistle against the Arian Bishops*. Therefore.

Reply. That canon is spurious, as is that epistle of Athanasius, which contains
many false and futile things. The decretal epistles of Julius are also suspect.
Thus the man who collected the councils says that this canon of Julius is
plainly not found in the Nicene Synod.

2. The Nicene canons are only twenty, and among them there is nothing about
appeals to Rome. Indeed, in canons 5 and 6 every appeal to Rome is plainly
removed, as is evident from the Sixth Council of Carthage. There, when
authentic copies of the Council of Nicaea sent from Alexandria and
Constantinople had been produced, the Pope was convicted of citing false
testimony in support of appeals to Rome.

Instance. The Council of Sardica, which was a general council, teaches in
canons 4 and 7 that it is permissible to appeal to Rome. Therefore.

Reply. It was not a general council, because although it was convened by
Emperor Constantius, the bishops of

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 81 | printed col. 163 | bsb10175568]

the East withdrew from the bishops of the West because Paul and Athanasius
were retained, and established another council at Philippi, according to
Socrates, book 2.

Next, the Sardican canons conflict with canons 5 and 6 of the Nicene Synod.

3. Not even the Sardican canons bestow upon the Pope a right of appeal or
supreme power.

Finally, these canons were not received even by the whole Western Church,
because when they were produced at the African council as though they were
Nicene, they were rejected.

Instance. There are various examples of appeals to the Pope. Thus in the year
142, Marcion, excommunicated by the bishop of Pontus, came to Rome to be
absolved by Pius. So too, in the year 252, Fortunatus and Felix, deposed by
Cyprian in Africa, came to Pope Cornelius. Basilides, deposed in Spain, came to
Stephen. Likewise, in the year 350, Athanasius, deposed by the Easterners, came
to Pope Julius. After the year 400, Chrysostom, deposed by Philip, appealed to
the Pope. And after the fiftieth year, Gregory deprived John, a Greek bishop,
of his communion because he had judged the Theban bishop who had appealed to
the Apostolic See. Therefore.

Reply. The first four were heretics. Therefore, their action proves nothing;
for, as Cyprian says in book 1, Epistle 55, *they ran about so that, although
they had been justly condemned by their own people, they might acquire
supporters for themselves.* Next, there is no appeal in the examples brought
forward. The final three fled to Rome only for the sake of assistance, because
the Roman Church had remained orthodox up to that time.

Objection 2. If the Pope’s primacy is not from Christ and the apostles, then it
began at another time and with another author. But no time and no author can be
assigned unless one goes back to the

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 81 | printed col. 164 | bsb10175568]

times of Christ. Therefore.

Reply. There are several termini, because it is one thing for something to have
begun at a definite time and to have had an author, but another for the time
and the author to be capable of being named. Similarly, no one knows at what
time and by what author a certain house, whose ruins we see, was destroyed, or
at what time and by what author a certain prostitute became pregnant.

2. It is easy to show from history by what stages the Pope reached the summit
of tyranny. For it is agreed that in the beginning all bishops were equal by
divine right, as Jerome testifies to Evagrius: *Whoever is a bishop*, he says,
*whether at Rome or elsewhere, is of the same worth and priesthood.* But
afterward a human episcopate was introduced, which degenerated into a satanic
one. For in the beginning everything was governed through greater and lesser
councils, until certain φιλοπρωτεύοντες—that is, men who loved primacy—arose.
And when contentions prevailed among the bishops, one man was continuously
elected who was prior to the others not in degree but in order, and was called
“bishop” κατ’ ἐξοχήν, by way of eminence. But when these men arrogated all
power to themselves, archbishops were created, and afterward patriarchs for
the same reason, until at last, around the year of Christ 600, they arrived at
a universal Pope.

Objection 3. He who possesses the right of convening councils is the head of
the Church. But such is the Pope. Therefore.

Reply. Both the proposition and the assumption are denied, because Bellarmine
himself, book 1, *On Councils*, chapter 12, concedes that provincial councils
can be convened by metropolitans, and even that general councils can be
convened by someone else, provided that the Pope consents to and approves the
convocation. Next, it is clear from history that many emperors convened
councils. Thus Constantine convened Nicaea, according to Eusebius, book 3,
chapter 3, and Socrates, book 1, chapter 5. Theodosius convened the Second
Universal Council, according to

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 82 | printed col. 165 | bsb10175568]

Sozomen, book 7, chapter 7.

Objection 4. He who possesses the right of confirming or invalidating councils
is the head of the Church. But such is the Pope. Therefore.

Reply. Confirmation occurs in two ways. 1. By decree; in this sense both the
proposition and the assumption are false, because an emperor confirmed
councils in this way and yet was not the head of the Church. Next, no decrees
of Popes can be produced by which they confirmed the first councils. 2. There
is confirmation through assent. If the proposition speaks of this, it is
false, because consent does not prove supreme authority.

Objection 5. He who has the right of presiding at all councils is the head of
the Church. But such is the Pope. Therefore.

Reply. The proposition is denied, because then all elected presidents would
be heads of the Church, since they have the right of presiding. 2. The
assumption is denied, because this is attributed to the Pope nowhere. Indeed,
with the legates of the bishop of Rome present, others presided, as was proved
above. Therefore.

Objection 6. According to certain fathers, those who agree with the bishop of
Rome are orthodox, and those who dissent from him are heretics. Therefore.

Reply. The consequence is denied, because not everyone with whom another
person agrees is the head of the Church. Next, someone can dissent from another
and yet not be a heretic, as Cyprian dissented from Pope Stephen. 2. The
examples Bellarmine brings forward concern Roman bishops who were still
orthodox. But it does not follow that because those who agreed with the Popes
at that time were orthodox, therefore those who agree with them now are
orthodox. Finally, the fathers testify that agreement with other churches as
well was a sign of orthodox faith. See Tertullian, *On Prescriptions*, chapter
86.

Objection 7. If the Roman faith was customarily consulted in all controversies
of faith, and if the determination of religious questions was committed to its
judgment, then it possessed supreme authority. But the former is true.
Therefore.

Reply. The assumption is denied, because it was not consulted in all
controversies or by everyone. Thus

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 82 | printed col. 166 | bsb10175568]

Alexander of Alexandria condemned Arius without consulting the Roman bishop.
Indeed, when others are consulted about controversies, nothing is said about
the Roman bishop. Thus, when Emperor Jovinian decreed that all priests should
agree with him that the Lord’s body had not been subject to death, they replied
that they were awaiting Athanasius’s judgment. 2. Polycarp disputed with
Anacletus at Rome concerning Easter, but did not acquiesce in Anacletus’s
judgment, according to Eusebius, *History*, book 5, chapter 24. Therefore.

Objection 8. The ancient pontiffs always exercised authority over the other
bishops, because they appointed bishops throughout the whole world. Therefore.
The antecedent is proved: Maximus of Antioch was confirmed by Leo.

Reply. The antecedent is denied, because the Council of Nicaea, canon 4,
assigns the appointment of a bishop to the provincials. Therefore, the Roman
bishop could do this no more than the other patriarchs. Next, it does not
follow that because a Roman bishop sometimes ventured beyond his own limits,
he could or ought to do so by right. 3. Many other bishops besides the Roman
appointed and confirmed other bishops even outside their own jurisdiction.
Thus Acacius of Caesarea placed Lucius at Alexandria, according to Socrates,
book 2, chapter 33. Meletius confirmed Nazianzen, according to Theodoret, book
5, chapter 8. Indeed, bishops were confirmed while the Pope dissented, as
Flavian of Antioch was confirmed by the emperor while Damasus dissented,
according to Theodoret, book 3, chapter 23.

Objection 9. The Roman bishops deposed other bishops throughout the whole
world. Therefore.

Reply. The consequence is denied.

Next, the examples brought forward by Bellarmine do not prove it, because he
falsely says Flavian was deposed by Damasus. 3. Marianus of Arles, of whom
Cyprian speaks in book 3, Epistle 13, was not deposed by Stephen. Cyprian only
asks Stephen to admonish the bishops of his province to depose him.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 83 | printed col. 167 | bsb10175568]

4. He also falsely says that the bishop of Jerusalem was deposed by Sixtus,
because he was deposed by a synod of seventy bishops.

5. It is clear from history that various bishops were deposed by other
bishops, and even patriarchs by patriarchs. Thus Acacius of Caesarea deposed
Macedonius of Constantinople, according to Socrates, book 2, chapter 23.
Theophilus of Alexandria deposed Chrysostom, in the same work, book 6, chapter
9. Likewise, Cyril deposed John of Antioch, in the same work, book 7, chapter
33. Likewise, the Eastern bishops deposed Pope Julius, according to Sozomen,
book 3, chapter 2.

Objection 10. The Roman bishops restored throughout the whole world bishops
who had been deposed by others. Therefore.

Reply. The consequence is denied, because that restoration was nothing other
than a testimony by which they testified that they acknowledged as bishop the
man who had been deposed by others. Thus Maximus of Jerusalem restored
Athanasius to the Alexandrian see, although he possessed no authority over
him. The same must be said of Cyril, John, and others.

2. Those restored by the Pope were not received by others. Thus Cyprian, in
Epistle 68, advised the Spaniards not to receive Basilides when he had been
restored by the Pope. Finally, many men other than the Roman bishop restored
other bishops, according to Socrates, book 2, chapter 18, and elsewhere.

Objection 11. The Pope exercised authority over the greatest and most noble
sons of the Church by making laws, granting dispensations, and punishing, as
is evident from the epistles of the Popes. Therefore.

Reply. The question is by what right the Pope did this, for it is clear that he
did many things tyrannically.

2. The deeds brought forward concerning laws and dispensations were performed
within the limits of the Roman patriarchate. Concerning censures, however, it
is clear that emperors could not be

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 83 | printed col. 168 | bsb10175568]

excommunicated by the Roman bishop alone. Ambrose excommunicated Theodosius,
according to Theodoret, book 5, chapter 17.
So too, Anastasius of Antioch pronounced an anathema against Justinian,
according to Evagrius, book 4, chapter 39. Likewise, John of Antioch
excommunicated Cyril, who was not under his jurisdiction, and the Westerners
were excommunicated by the Easterners.

Objection 12. Book 2, chapter 20, *On the Pope’s Vicars*. The Pope had his
vicars in various regions, either ordinary or temporary. Therefore.

The consequence is proved because the provinces where a king has viceroys and
where he assigns judgments to governors are subject to that king. Therefore.

Reply. There is an equivocation in the word *vicars*, because it is used in two
ways.

1. For those who were sent by another person to act in his place. In this
sense, Vitus and Vincentius were the vicars or legates of the Roman bishop at
the Synod of Nicaea. The consequence is denied in this sense, because all
metropolitans and patriarchs had the right to send their legates to councils.

2. For those who exercise jurisdiction somewhere in another person’s name,
the sense in which the title “legate” is used in the papal tyranny. Under this
meaning, the antecedent is denied, because in the first centuries the Pope had
no such vicar. Indeed, even after the papal tyranny had grown, he had no vicars
except in the West, that is, within his own patriarchate. Finally, the examples
Bellarmine brings forward either concern deeds performed in the Roman
patriarchate or are falsely alleged because he is not speaking of vicars of the
Pope. Indeed, the African council forbade the Pope even to send his legates
there.

Objection 13. He who is set over everyone in such a way that he is subject to
no one and can be judged by no one on earth is the head and

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 84 | printed col. 169 | bsb10175568]

prince of the Church. But such is the Pope, as certain councils, statements of
emperors, and testimonies of Popes and doctors prove. Therefore.

Reply. The proposition is denied, because according to Bellarmine, book 2,
chapter 26, all kings as such have this in common, that they can be judged by
no one; yet there is not one king of the whole world.

2. The assumption is denied, as is the consequence: he is an ecclesiastical
prince; therefore he is judged by no one. For the consequence is true only in
a monarchy. Next, it would follow only that the Pope has no superior in the
Church. But although the dignity of the priest holds first place in sacred
matters, nevertheless in political matters individual priests are subject to
their kings.

3. The councils he brings forward, the Sinuessan and Roman councils, are
particular, suspect, and fabricated. The Eighth Council and the Council of
Molenum only exclude the emperor from spiritual judgment. Finally, the Council
of Chalcedon only declares that Dioscorus wrongly passed sentence against Pope
Leo.

4. The testimonies of the Popes are suspect because they concern their own
case, and no testimonies of Popes who lived before the year of Christ 600 can
establish what is at issue. Gelasius, whom he cites, speaks of the privilege of
all priests.

5. The emperors Constantine and Basil speak about all bishops. We proved above
that Popes were very often judged by emperors. Finally, the testimonies of the
fathers prove nothing, because Ambrose speaks about the whole Church,
Nazianzen about all bishops, and Gregory does likewise. The remaining
testimonies are recent.

Objection 14. The Pope has fifteen titles, from each and all of which his
primacy is proved. They are:

1. Pope.

2. Father of the Fathers.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 84 | printed col. 170 | bsb10175568]

3. Pontiff of Christians.

4. Supreme Pontiff.

5. Prince of the Priests.

6. Vicar of Christ.

7. Head of the Church.

8. Foundation of the Church.

9. Shepherd of the Lord’s Fold.

10. Ruler of the House of God.

11. Keeper of the Vineyard.

12. Father and Teacher of All Christians.

13. Bridegroom of the Church.

14. Prelate of the Apostolic See.

15. Universal Bishop.

Reply. The consequence is denied, because some of these titles were recently
invented, some were tyrannically arrogated, and some are common to all bishops.
Finally, among the ancients no title exists that was attributed to the Roman
Pope alone.

Objection 15. Councils such as the First Council of Nicaea, canon 6, the
Council of Constantinople, Chalcedon, the Second Council of Nicaea, the
Lateran Council, and the Council of Florence bestow primacy upon the Pope.
Therefore.

Reply. The antecedent is denied, because the First Council of Nicaea removes
all primacy from the Pope and makes him equal to the other patriarchs. Next,
the Second Council of Nicaea was idolatrous and was condemned by the Synod of
Frankfurt. The councils of Florence and the Lateran, however, are recent and
antichristian. The others were discussed above.

Objection 16. The Roman pontiffs who lived from the beginning testify that
primacy belonged to the Popes. Therefore.

Reply. The consequence is denied, for these are testimonies in their own case.
Next, no pontiff testifies to this before he begins tyrannically to seek the
primacy. For the epistles of the Popes who lived during the first three hundred
years are for the most part spurious, and Bellarmine himself, book 2, chapter
14, concedes that errors crept into them and says that he does not dare affirm
that they are beyond doubt.

Objection 17. The Greek fathers testify to this same thing. Therefore.

Reply. All the testimonies he cites from the Greeks are irrelevant, because
they either speak

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 85 | printed col. 171 | bsb10175568]

of the Roman Church’s power within its own jurisdiction, or about the Catholic
Church in general; or they praise the Roman Church because of the preeminence
of the city and because it long remained uncorrupted while heresies raged in
the East; or the writings they cite are spurious or badly translated; or,
finally, they teach only that the Pope was summoned fraternally by others for
assistance because of his power against heretics.

Objection 18. The Latin fathers testify to this same thing. Therefore.

Reply. Many testimonies speak about the city of Rome, not the Roman Church;
others about the power of the Roman bishop within his own patriarchate; and
others are ineptly cited, because they do not prove the Pope to be the head of
the Church. Some attribute great power to the Roman Church, but not supreme
power above all other churches. Others exalt the distinction of the Roman
Church because it remained orthodox when other churches were corrupted.
Finally, some writers—but more recent ones—call it the head not with respect
to universal dominion, but with respect to its distinction above the other
churches; that is, comparatively, not absolutely.
