V. 26. A son who brings shame and affects his parents with reproaches lays waste his father and drives away his mother.

In אב there is a synecdoche of the part for both parents. Understand the same in the word אם (*matrem*, mother). The same is to be understood in the word בן, son, for children of either sex.

The purpose is, on the one side, to spur parents to the godly education of their children and, on the other, to spur children to piety toward their parents by a life lived holily and incorruptly.

Since what he predicates of either one applies to both parents, it may be asked why he attributes to the father that the son lays him waste, or overturns him in his goods, but to the mother that he drives her away and alienates her from himself. To the former difficulty I answer that the father is said to be laid waste rather than the mother because the right over goods and revenues properly belongs to the father, in such a way that the mother can give or disburse nothing except by the father’s direction or consent. For it is her part faithfully to preserve what has been brought in by her husband.

As concerns the other point, I answer that alienation of affection is attributed to the mother because a mother, having suffered the experience of many evils in conception, pregnancy, childbirth, and nursing, loves her children more ardently than the father. Hence the Prophet and the Apostle ennoble a mother’s love for her children in a certain special manner, Isa. 49.15 and 66.13; 1 Thess. 2.7. Thus it may easily be understood that a father’s mind will have been alienated from a dissolute son if the mother’s affection toward him has changed.

Now it must be seen what sort of son he is who inflicts this evil on his parents. He is, says the Prophet, “a son who brings shame and affects with reproach,” namely both himself and his parents. For the mark of infamy which he brands upon himself by his wickedness redounds upon his parents. By God’s command he ought to have procured honour for his parents. Therefore, not merely to fail to pursue them with honour but, further, to affect them with shame and insult is a most unworthy thing. Likewise, according to the prescription of the same command, he ought γηροτροφεῖν, to support his parents in old age, and to relieve them when destitute of the comforts of this life, 1 Tim. 5.4. Therefore, to overturn them in their goods and fortunes is plainly monstrous.

This is the fruit and effect of so ruined a son’s conduct: to render his parents’ minds alienated from and abhorrent to him to such a degree that they cannot bear even the sight of him. For just as the enmities of brothers who are at variance are said in Solomon’s Proverb to be irreconcilable, so the hatred of parents toward their children comes forth as altogether portentous after they have been provoked by their children’s repeated acts of disobedience or by more atrocious crimes. For the closer the bonds of nature by which people are bound to one another, so, when those bonds have been broken and shattered, hatreds

[1632 witness | PDF p. 423 | printed columns 820-821]

arise more hostile. This should admonish children not to presume upon their parents’ love—as though no stubbornness and wantonness could exhaust their love—and rashly expose themselves to any offence of their parents.

Next, how great an evil it is to experience the alienated minds and countenances of one’s parents is taught us by the example of Micah. Although he was an idolater and devoid of any sense of true religion, nevertheless, on hearing his mother’s dreadful curse, by which she cursed the person who had taken away her 1,100 silver pieces, he was so struck by this curse that he immediately restored the silver to his mother, Judg. 17.1. And if God hears the groaning of labourers who are defrauded of their wages, James 5, and the cry of those who are deprived of their garment, which they themselves voluntarily pledged, ascends into heaven, how much more will so extraordinary an injury against parents—who sustain the person of God himself toward their children—be avenged? This is perceived by contrast from the friendly countenance and unclouded brow of parents. For if Jacob, in his brother’s peaceful and fraternal aspect, seemed to himself to have beheld and contemplated the serene and joyful face of God himself, how much more is this perceived in the cheerful and eager gaze of parents?

Here we parents are admonished to nurture our children in the fear of God and in the discipline of the Lord, lest they turn out dissolute and gluttonous. But if parents should fall in with children who are spendthrifts and prodigals, they nevertheless must not, through foolish indulgence and disordered and perverse love, supply money for their monstrous expenses and extravagance, lest this afterward engender implacable hatred when they have been overturned in their own fortunes.

Indeed, it is fitting for children to undertake care for themselves, so that by mutual exhortations and admonitions they may advance toward piety, lest, if one of them who is uniquely dear to the parents should slip into a life of luxury, he consume his parents’ goods by his prodigality and defraud the rest of their proper right in the paternal goods.

Verse 27. Cease, my son (so that you may listen to instruction), to stray from the words of knowledge.

In בני there is a synecdoche for sons and daughters together.

The purpose is to dispose people’s minds to the pursuit of wisdom and divine fear.

In the affectionate address “son” and “daughter,” by which God in the person of the Prophet addresses all members of the household of faith, he declares God’s extraordinary love toward us. God, who in his only-begotten Christ has adopted us into the lot of sons, invites us to obedience to himself by this honour in which he holds us. And this love of God shines the more brightly because he counts as sons those who have not yet wholly renounced sin, a fact which is indicated not obscurely by the word *cease*. For if, reverencing the parents of our flesh, we show ourselves compliant to them, how much more ought we to show ourselves obedient to our heavenly Father?

Since he admonishes us to cease straying, let us remember what the Apostle puts us in mind of: it ought to be enough for us that, in the time already past, we conformed ourselves to the pursuits of the Gentiles when we walked in every kind of wantonness, lust, drunkenness, gluttony, drinking bouts, and unlawful idolatry, 1 Pet. 4.3. For it is an extraordinary act of kindness in a master if he is willing to count as acceptable a servant who for many years has been not merely useless but harmful, provided that the servant applies himself diligently during what remains of his life to serve his master faithfully. To this belongs that most equitable demand of the Apostle, by which, because of the weakness of the flesh, he requires of all the godly that, just as they presented their members as slaves to iniquity, so, and so forth, Rom. 6.19—especially since there arises no fruit from wickedness and impiety except death, while the fruit of obedience toward God is by far the greatest,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 424 | printed columns 822-823]

both in this life and in the future life, verses 21–22. To this should be referred what is written in Eph. 5 concerning redeeming the opportunity, especially since the days are evil.

Finally, it must be noted that listening to instruction is opposed to straying from the words of knowledge, so that they cannot stand together. Thus it is necessary that the person who applies his mind to instruction immediately bid farewell to error and the corruptions of life. See Matt. 6.24; 2 Cor. 6.14–16; and Rom. 6.20. This is said because there are very many who feign piety while in the meantime practising wickedness, like the prostitute of Prov. 7, who made a show of religious worship even at the time when she sought a partner in adultery. There are others who say that they are endowed with such great capacity for action that they are able both to worship God more than sufficiently and also to indulge their own inclination or to enslave their mind to the pursuit of riches. It is an arduous thing to practise with distinction two crafts or occupations which are merely different. Few have even been found with such felicity of talent and industry that they know how to join utility with pleasure. But no one has ever been found who has joined the worship of God with devotion to the world, and so forth, since these things are not merely different but diametrically opposed.

V. 28. A wicked witness makes a mockery of judgment, and the mouth of the wicked swallows iniquity.

In ובלע (*absorbet*, swallows) there is a metaphor drawn from monstrous beasts which gulp down other beasts in a single opening of the jaws, transferred to iniquity, which so wholly possesses the mouth of the wicked that it knows how to belch forth nothing but iniquity. There is also a metonymy of the adjunct. For iniquity is said to swallow the mouth in place of the fact that the mouth swallows iniquity, which it afterward regurgitates.

The purpose is to set the wicked witness before our eyes for the utmost detestation of him.

To speak falsely is among sins, even if it is said privately and even if it is done to the disadvantage of no one else. Therefore, to do this publicly before everyone—not without grave loss to not merely one but many—is a grave and criminal deed, especially if it is considered that God sits in the judgment and assembly of judges, by reverence for whom we ought to be led even if we had cast aside the restraint of human judgment. Next, even if someone were to do this through weakness, induced by fear or love, it would nevertheless be grave and bitter. But now, since this is play and sport to the man, it will certainly have to be judged an unspeakable crime. Finally, to have done this only once must be judged an atrocious deed. Since, therefore, it happens, as it very commonly does, that many other lies are accumulated and contrived for the defence of one lie, and this happens often and repeatedly, it will have to be attributed to ruined and hopeless malice.

Similar to this manner of speaking, by which iniquity is said to have swallowed the mouth of the wicked, are those expressions at Mark 1.23, “a man in an unclean spirit,” and Acts 8.23, “I see that you are in the gall and bond of iniquity.” By this manner of speaking extraordinary wickedness is indicated. For it is more than enough that sin should be in us and cling to us; but for us to be in sin and be possessed by it, so that at its own pleasure it may transfer us here and there, is a matter of some greater wickedness.

V. 29. Judgments are prepared for those scoffers, and beatings for the back of fools.

In גו (*tergo*, back) there is a synecdoche of the part for the whole person.

The purpose is to render us cautious against the foregoing sin of making a mockery of judgment.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 425 | printed columns 824-825]

By the law of retaliation he threatens judgments against those who have made a mockery of judgment, so that those who have not reverenced God’s judgment may be compelled, to their own great harm, to experience the dreadful judgment of God himself. Next, since in the preceding verse he had said that “a wicked witness makes a mockery of judgment” in the singular number, now in this verse, by the word “judgments” in the plural number, he announces punishments to the witness.

Now, since the word “judgment” is taken in various ways, he indicates sufficiently plainly by the word “beatings” in what sense and signification it is to be taken here. These judgments or beatings are sometimes inflicted by the ministry of human beings. In this place, however, he seems especially to embrace those judgments and beatings which are sent down from heaven, thereby teaching that it will not profit the wicked witness if he escapes human hands, for he will be crushed by God’s vengeance. Thus it would have been better to have undergone punishment inflicted by human beings than to experience God’s dreadful vengeance.

From time to time in Scripture God is said to be going to requite the wickedness of human beings upon their own face. Yet what is affirmed in this place—that beatings are to be laid upon their backs—does not diminish the severity of the punishment and vengeance. For they will be beaten not only upon their backs, but in their bellies and entrails and, finally, in the soul itself. But he especially made mention of backs because elsewhere he has testified that a whip is the due and just thing for the backs of fools, and that for their greater disgrace. For to be beaten on the back is more disgraceful than to be beaten from the front and in the face. Nor is this all: by this manner of speaking he also wished to indicate, not obscurely, the inescapable hand of God. For those who are beaten from behind cannot repel the blows inflicted upon them, as those who are attacked from the front and in the face are accustomed to turn aside the blow.

His statement that judgments are prepared has no small importance. For the hope and belief of escaping punishment, or at least delaying it, produces licence to sin. The reason people rush so rashly into false testimonies is that they set before themselves the hope of impunity. But when the Prophet affirms that judgments and beatings have long since been prepared, he utterly tears away all that vain and futile imagining of human beings. For how foolishly they dream that they will be exempted from punishments when the punishments, like rods or axes, have long since been gathered and fashioned. For just as parents in a household and Magistrates in the Commonwealth have their rods and axes, which they command to be carried before them before anyone offends, so that in this way they may strike fear into everyone that, when they have sinned, they will be punished, so God has his own rods and axes, as it were, laid up and stored away with him, which he will bring forth into the light at the proper time. Moreover, those punishments were prepared for wicked people before the foundations of the world were laid. For what Christ affirms in Matt. 25 concerning eternal exile appointed before the foundation of the world for the devil, his angels, and wicked people can by analogy be truly applied to punishments of every kind which are carried out in this age.
