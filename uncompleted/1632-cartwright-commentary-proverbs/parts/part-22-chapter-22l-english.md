Verse 26. Do not be among those who strike the palm, among those who stand surety for debts.

Verse 27. If you have nothing with which to pay, why should the creditor take your bed, which is beneath you?

In תקעי כף (*those who strike the palm*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct for those who stand surety, as is clear from the other half of the verse. In משכב (*bed*) there is a synecdoche for things of the greatest necessity, which you cannot do without except at serious inconvenience.

The purpose is to deter us from rash suretyship.

By the word *those who strike*, in the Hebrew idiom he designates those who frequently stand surety for other people's debts. From this it is clear that suretyship is not forbidden universally, but that it should not frequently be undertaken for just anyone. We have learned, however, that it should not be undertaken at all for a stranger, Proverbs 6:11 and 11:15. Such a person must be reckoned to include not only someone unknown to us, but also a reckless bankrupt, an idle man, and so forth, however well known he may be. That it is not forbidden generally can be gathered from Genesis 43:9, where Judah is praised for offering himself as surety for Benjamin's safe return.

Verse [27]. He strengthens the warning by the attendant loss. For since it may occur to the guarantor that the creditor will spare him because the debt is another's, not his own, he declares that the strictest legal right will be enforced against him. Nor will the creditor lack reasons of his own for maintaining that the guarantor should be treated more harshly than the debtor himself. He can urge that, had the other's guarantee not intervened, he would have been unwilling to entrust anything to the debtor, or would have been unwilling to demand the debt until the debtor was able to pay.

But his statement that the bed will be taken away raises a difficulty. For since the Mosaic law provided that no one should take in pledge things of daily and necessary use, Exodus 22:26–27; Deuteronomy 24:6, the same law seems to forbid anyone to take those things

[1632 witness | PDF p. 509 | printed columns 1002-1003]

in payment of a debt. The answer is that the obligation of a debt is stricter than that of a deposit. For God willed the payment of a debt to be so sacred that children should rather be sold, and the debtor himself reduced to servitude, than that the debt should remain unpaid, 2 Kings 4:1. Moreover, not everything forbidden by the law of charity is prohibited by the civil law of God's people. For although the law of charity forbids retaining a deposit that is especially necessary to one's neighbor, it does not follow from this alone that civil law forbids it. By the law of charity we are commanded rather to concede our cloak also to someone who takes away our tunic than to bring a lawsuit against our neighbor; but civil law, not even in a Mosaic court, would compel anyone to this.

When he says, *if you have nothing with which to pay*, he warns us that a guarantee must be proportioned to our own resources; and therefore, before we make another person's debt our own, we must consider how ample or how slender are the means at our disposal. Here we must also take care that payment of the debt is not deferred to a distant time. For since riches, as Solomon says, are winged, it may happen that someone who now has abundant means to discharge his pledged word may have nothing whatsoever left before the appointed time.
