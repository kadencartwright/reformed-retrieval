Verse 29. A wicked man hardens his face, but the upright man adapts his way.

In העז בפניו (*obfirmat faciem suam*, hardens his face) there is a metaphor from travellers who have determined in their mind to complete some journey, resolving to proceed in the journey they have undertaken after overcoming all the obstacles, whatever they may be, which can be placed in their way. In יכין דרכיו there is another metaphor from a traveller who, as there is need and as the circumstances require, enters upon a road, turns this way or that, goes back, and goes forward, as duty demands.

The purpose is, after condemning obstinacy, to commend flexibility in duty.

Satan’s deceit is to drive a person once led into error to persist in the course undertaken. For besides the fact that sin is enticing in itself, so that, just as after tasting delights we desire them more intensely, so, after sin has once been committed, the desire to sin is kindled more strongly, Satan also, by himself or through his satellites, suggests reasons to us by which we may be kept in the pursuit of sin: that it would be a mark of fickleness to abandon the course begun; that it would have been better never to have entered this road than to leave it after entering; likewise, that there are many who follow this kind of life, and these are people neither of the lowest rank nor inconsiderate in conducting affairs; on the contrary, there are very few who

[1632 witness | PDF p. 483 | printed columns 950-951]

abhor this road and are exposed on that account to everyone’s ridicule; nor are the wicked deterred from evil deeds by their dangers, as though they were in covenant with death and hell, Isa. 28. All these things are no sooner insinuated into our ears than they are seized by us greedily and thirstily. Nor indeed does the wickedness of a person of this kind stop here, but it proceeds further, because he holds in contempt the exhortations and chastisements which are applied to heal him. From this it follows that one who commits some evil, or indeed perpetrates some crime, is not immediately to be accounted wicked; but that person finally is, who persists in crime, especially when remedies are applied so that he may come to his senses and correct himself.

The cause of this shamelessness, however, is a heart of stone, to be compared with rock because of its hardness; hence the forehead must grow hard. For a stony heart—with which all the wicked are endowed—cannot fail to beget a bronze and adamantine forehead. For like prostitutes with rubbed foreheads, they are unable to blush and to be suffused with shame at sin, Jer. 3; Ezek. 5. A striking example of this hardening appears in Balaam, who, once held entangled by the enticement of the money and honour promised by the King of Moab, could be retarded neither by any admonitions from God nor by obstacles placed in his way from obtaining what he had come to love as the wages of unrighteousness, Num. 22. See the example of Pharaoh, Exod. 7, 8, 9, 10.

In the latter hemistich he illustrates the former point from its opposite, the endeavour of the godly and upright. They are not so swept crosswise by their passions that they fail to foresee in their mind the entire administration of the matter which they approach and take in hand, and to anticipate by what steps, as it were, they may arrive at the place they determine. Thus it comes about that they can safely hold the course upon which they have entered. But if some human mischance befalls them, so that they have been deceived by an error, they nevertheless do not persist in the error; rather, like skilled shipmasters who yield, as it were, to a storm, they bend the course of their voyage so that they may reach port and do not rashly struggle with the violent onset of the winds and waves. The wicked do the latter, for they wish to hold to the very end a road, however dangerous, because it has been undertaken. See Abraham’s foresight in carrying out God’s command concerning the offering of his son: how he conceals the matter, carries a bundle of wood with him, and orders the servants who might hinder him to remain at a distance. See also the example of David, who, although inflamed with anger he had determined upon Nabal’s death and bound himself by an oath to that slaughter, nevertheless turned his step back when admonished by a woman.

Yet it must be noted here that the godly ought to be no less constant in good than the wicked are stubborn in evil. Hence it is also counted to Daniel’s praise that he hardened his face to pray to and seek God, Dan. 9; likewise to Christ’s praise that he set his face toward Jerusalem in order to travel there for the sake of undergoing death, Luke 9.51.

Verse 30. There is no wisdom, nor is there understanding, nor is there counsel against Jehovah.

The purpose is to overthrow people’s vain confidence in their own cunning and craftiness.

Wisdom is the virtue by which, with a swift and ready movement of the mind and with reasoning, we grasp matters otherwise broad and great.

Understanding is the virtue by which, taught through long use and experience of affairs, we judge the outcome of future things.

Counsel is the mind’s determination, born from those two virtues.

And certainly the superiority of wisdom over folly is great, as is that of light over darkness,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 484 | printed columns 952-953]

provided that it does not overstep its bounds or is not applied to the contriving of evil things. But when it is directed against God, who gave it, its light is darkened and its edge is blunted. For just as the sight of the eyes, however firm and strong, is dulled by looking directly into the sun, so human wisdom and so forth, however well equipped, melts away when it is aimed against God. There is indeed, as Eliphaz says at Job 12, wisdom, and in length of days understanding; but, if we wish to speak truly, with God there is wisdom, and his strength is counsel and understanding. It is as though he should say that the wisdom which is in human beings, even the most judicious, is scarcely worthy of the name of wisdom in comparison with that which is God’s, Isa. 14.25: [For Jehovah of hosts has formed a counsel, and who will make it void?] Therefore, if among human beings the person who is inferior to another in wisdom and so forth is overcome by the one who excels him in wisdom, so that the latter circumvents him and handles him no differently than if he were an infant, who can doubt that God, by his infinite wisdom, will easily dissipate the plans of all people, however much wisdom and prudence have gone into their construction and arrangement, and—what is greater—will turn them to those people’s own destruction?

Yet someone may ask here whether there are throughout the whole world monsters of humankind of this sort, who oppose their own plans to God’s counsels. Most certainly there are, so that the use of this Proverb extends far and wide. See Pharaoh, who devised many ingenious plans to make void God’s counsel concerning the leading out of his people. See also Ahab, who, in order to elude God’s counsel concerning the destruction of his posterity, laboured so vigorously in begetting children that he left seventy sons after him, whom he entrusted to the principal men to be guarded in a very strongly fortified city, 2 Kings 10. See also the example of Herod, who, in order to kill Christ, caused all the two-year-old boys of the region of Bethlehem to be killed, not even sparing his own son—as history attests—besides the crooked plan which he formed concerning the scouts who were to report back to him when they had found Christ, so that under the guise of adoration he might slaughter him.

But why do we scrape up old things when recent ones are at hand? Or foreign ones, when domestic ones are present? For we see the synagogue of Satan, which forms plans day and night to remove Christ’s bride from the midst. Let us approach more closely those who profess the Gospel. Although they cannot be ignorant that those who do not honour God will be held in contempt by God’s counsel, they nevertheless suppose that by their own cunning plans they will manage to protect their reputation even if they persist in that depravity of theirs by which they treat God with contempt. And although they know perfectly well that riches obtained by violence and fraud are to be dissipated by God’s decree and are not to be transmitted to posterity, they nevertheless suppose that by their prudence and plans they will bring it about that their posterity enjoys them. But just as the plans of Iohar Achabi and of Herod perished, so too will those of these people be fruitless.

Verse 31. The horse is prepared for the day of battle, but salvation itself belongs to Jehovah.

In סוס (*equus*, horse) there is a synecdoche of the part for all military equipment: cavalry, infantry, arms, walls, and so forth.

The purpose is to overthrow people’s vain confidence in strength and arms.

Preparing horses, chariots, and military equipment is not only not condemned, but further is confirmed by this Proverb to be lawful. For it is not said that the wicked make this preparation, but it is said generally to be made by everyone. And certainly, unless God from heaven prescribes another method of waging war—as we read that he formerly did for the sake of displaying his power in Jonathan, 1 Sam. 13, and Gideon, Judg. 7—war ought not to be undertaken without this equipment. If the necessity of war

[1632 witness | PDF p. 485 | printed columns 954-955]

presses upon someone, and even against his will he must come to close combat, war in a case of this kind must be waged, although under unfavourable Mars, rather than that he should offer himself to be trampled by the enemy. Indeed, Solomon, to whom unbroken peace had been promised by God, nevertheless maintained 4,000 chariots and twelve thousand horsemen each year.

Therefore, what the Prophet wishes to teach by this Proverb is that the sum and chief point of victory is not to be placed in these things, nor ought people to rest in them, as commonly happens. See Isa. 22.11: “For the horses are flesh, not Spirit; the Egyptians are human beings, not God.” See also the woe denounced to them at Isa. 31.1, 2, 3, and Ps. 33.17. For it is just as if someone should lean upon the reed of Egypt, which not only does not support him but further befouls him; nor will horses equipped with their riders help more, when God does not stand by us propitiously, than bare horses forsaken by and bereft of their riders.

But the sum of victory and salvation comes from the victory of God alone, Ps. 39. Therefore, we ought to place our confidence in him; he must be invoked; we must turn to him with true repentance; war must be undertaken for lawful reasons and waged lawfully according to the rule of the divine word; drunkenness, fornications, adulteries, lawsuits, and quarrels must be banished far from the gates. For by these the walls are demolished; their hocks are broken, spears are cut apart, and lances are shattered. Therefore, counsel is taken, or a battle line drawn up, or vigorous fighting done in vain unless heaven is first rendered friendly and benevolent toward us. For from there insensible stones and arrows are launched, against which we are able to discharge none of our weapons.

From both these Proverbs it is easily gathered how unfit we are to attain eternal salvation by either our strength or our wisdom, since it has not been granted to us to preserve this present life by either our own strength or our own wisdom.
