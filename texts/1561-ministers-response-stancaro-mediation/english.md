# The Genevan Ministers' Response concerning the Controversy over the Mediator

March 1561

This is a fresh English translation of the complete corporate response printed
in *Ioannis Calvini opera quae supersunt omnia*, vol. IX (Corpus Reformatorum
XXXVII), pp. 349–358. The CO divisional title occupies pp. 345–346 and the next
opening is blank. The translation was made from the normalized Latin and
checked against the page images. No modern English translation supplied its
wording.

The CO title means *The Response of the Ministers of the Genevan Church to the
Polish Nobles and to Francesco Stancaro of Mantua concerning the Controversy
over the Mediator*. The response was issued corporately and its Bern witness
names twelve subscribers. Calvin was its drafter or principal author, but the
English therefore preserves its prevailing “we” rather than presenting the
document as an unqualified solo work by Calvin.

## Translation

### [CO IX, p. 349]

Previously, when the Polish brethren asked us in what manner Christ is
mediator—whether, namely, as he is God and man, or only with respect to his
human nature—we set out our judgment briefly yet clearly. In doing so, the
truth itself compelled us to condemn and refute Stancaro's invention. Later a
just necessity drove us to publish that response, not so much out of hatred
for Stancaro or a wish to burden him with ill repute as in order to escape
certain hostile rumors; for in disturbed affairs light is easily wrapped in
darkness.

Moreover, Stancaro has no cause for complaint if he considers within himself
how outrageously he assailed Philip Melanchthon, whose memory ought to be
valued and honored by all good people. He certainly forgot all moderation and
humanity when he tore at a man of such proven piety and such good service to
the church with atrocious abuse, repeatedly not hesitating to call him an
Arian and a heretic. If this intemperance did not gravely offend us, we would
be ungrateful both to God and to his faithful servant. If Stancaro persists in
being like himself, we must expect the same reward. Yet he will not harm us at
all by hurling insults; indeed, he will spatter more disgrace upon himself. We
nevertheless humbly ask God at last to bend him by the spirit of gentleness
and restrain him to godly teachableness. We also exhort him to begin pulling

### [CO IX, p. 350]

himself together and no longer to indulge his anger and violence in this way.
But if he is so greatly delighted by paradoxes that overthrow the church's
edification, let him at least learn to govern his disposition and restrain the
vehemence in which he is excessive.

As for the subject itself, we must first see what the name “mediator” means.
Certainly the eternal *Logos*, from the beginning before Adam's fall and
before the human race's estrangement and division from God, was mediator. It
is in this sense, unless we are mistaken, that Paul calls him the firstborn of
all creation. And when John declares that life was in him, he shows the mode
of communication by which God's grace flowed to human beings from a source
otherwise hidden. Therefore, insofar as Christ was the head of angels and
human beings while the created order was still whole, he is rightly reckoned
mediator—a mediator whom the elect angels still now experience and
acknowledge. Humanity's defection, however, made an expiation necessary to
reconcile us to God. Christ the mediator must therefore be considered
together with his sacrifice, by which he appeased God's wrath so as to restore
us to the hope of the blessed life from which we had fallen. We teach that he
is mediator not with respect to only one nature, but insofar as God was
manifested in the flesh. This belief rests on firm and valid reasons. Although
we explained them in part in our earlier response, it is useful here to gather
their substance again.

First, it is established that no one but God is to be believed in. Yet Christ
commands the disciples to believe in him (John 14:6). How, or on what basis?
Surely, as immediately follows, not only because he is the truth and the life,
but because he is the way to the Father. If Christ the mediator is to be
believed in, this office certainly embraces both natures together. Then,
since Christ possesses supreme dominion in heaven and on

### [CO IX, p. 351]

earth as mediator—something that would by no means suit the human nature
alone—we conclude that the name “mediator” is properly ascribed only to the
whole person (Romans 14:11). Certainly, that everyone is to stand before his
judgment seat—because it is written, “Every knee shall bend to me”—does not
belong to the human nature, and yet Paul applies it to the mediator's person
(Philippians 2:10). If someone objects that the kingdom was given to him by
the Father, we answer that, although nothing new can be conferred upon the
deity, there is nevertheless no absurdity in saying that Christ, with respect
to his whole person, is increased and adorned. Indeed, the same God who swears
that every knee will bend before him also denies that he will give his glory
to another. A man considered separately could not, therefore, receive this
honor of being judge of the world; and Paul's words plainly say so. To whom
does he say that supreme name was given if not to the Son of God, who, though
equal to the Father, emptied himself by taking the form of a servant? We see
that Paul places before our eyes the whole person, which consists of two
natures.

Third reason. It belongs to the mediator to join us to God. Since no one can
accomplish this unless he is one with God, no one is fit to discharge the
mediator's office unless he is man and at the same time God. The minor premise
is proved from Christ's words: “that they may be one with us, as you, Father,
and I are one” (John 17:21). The same point is made elsewhere when he says,
“As the Father has life in himself, so he also gave to the Son to have life in
himself” (John 5:26). Nor does the name “God” prevent this from being properly
applied to the whole person, because nothing is less fitting than for life,
which must be sought only in God, to reside in flesh.

Fourth reason. Just as the apostle reasons that Christ had to become a sharer
in our flesh and blood in order to be our brother (Hebrews 2:14, 17), so the
opposite inference is rightly drawn: to carry us all the way back to the
Father, he first had to be God. For what is the goal of the adoption that we
obtain through him, if not that we should at last become sharers in the divine
nature, as Peter testifies (2 Peter 1:4)? But how could he accomplish this if
he stood below the rank of angels? Nor do we become sons of God, grafted into
him, on any other basis than that he has been given to us as mediator. The
grace of adoption depends upon this: he transfers to us what he possesses

### [CO IX, p. 352]

by nature. He would therefore not be sufficient to fulfill the mediator's
functions unless he were God's only-begotten Son. Accordingly, to prove his
priesthood the apostle uses this testimony: “You are my Son; today I have
begotten you” (Hebrews 1:5). From this it is soundly concluded that, if the
dignity of the Son is inseparable from the priesthood, he is fit to be
mediator only because he was begotten from God. Nor can he be reckoned God's
Son without regard to his deity. The divine nature is therefore included in
the honor of the priesthood. Paul confirms this very point when he says that
he was declared Son of God in power (Romans 1:4). For when Paul defines the
mediator's person by that designation, he certainly embraces both natures at
once. Again, Christ's assertion elsewhere, “I am in the Father and the Father
in me” (John 14:11), cannot be predicated of the human nature alone. Yet the
image of the mediator is vividly expressed there. Indeed, John's entire
Gospel is filled with statements of this kind, in which the things Christ
claims for himself do not suit one of the natures but have regard to the whole
person. Whoever holds fast to Christ's meaning will free himself from every
knot, because he will see that it is wrong to draw predicates common to both
natures separately or distinctly to one of them.

In the meantime, however, we do not make everything attached to the
mediator's office, or every part of it, common to both natures. Since expiation
is not accomplished without death and the shedding of blood, the mediator had
to die. We acknowledge this to be peculiar to the humanity. But since death
is one thing and the effect of death another, the reconciliation accomplished
by death is wrongly ascribed to the human nature alone. This is why the
apostle says that Christ offered himself through the Spirit (Hebrews 9:14),
warning by this expression that he must not be considered simply as a man if
the efficacy and fruit of his death are to become evident to us. Similarly,
it was proper to the mediator to acquire righteousness for us by his
obedience and thus to become the Father's servant. This would be wrongly
ascribed to the divine nature; nevertheless, the acquisition of righteousness
itself borrows its power from the divine nature. The two are openly joined in
the composite person when Christ says, “For them I sanctify myself” (John
17:19). For while being sanctified belongs to the flesh and sanctifying to
God, both sides are found only in the whole person. Conversely, we also
acknowledge that some things are so proper to the deity in the mediator that
they ought not to be transferred to the human nature except by the
communication of properties. Christ says

### [CO IX, p. 353]

elsewhere, “I lay down my life of myself” (John 10:18). He speaks as mediator,
but not insofar as he is man, because no one has authority over life and death
from himself except the one God; and this is sufficiently plain. Stancaro is
also far astray concerning Paul's passage, “There is one God and one mediator
between God and men, the man Jesus Christ” (1 Timothy 2:5), because “man” was
put there for no other purpose than to give us familiar access to the Father.
Yet we do not deny that the whole deity and God of one essence are comprehended
under the Father. It is now apparent that the conjunction of both natures is
required for the mediator's office. Nevertheless, not everything that pertains
to the mediator's person is to be drawn indiscriminately to both natures. Calvin
pursues this argument more fully in book 2, chapter 14 of the *Institutes*.

The absurd consequence urged by the opposing side is no absurdity at all:
namely, that since a mediator stands between God and human beings, Christ will
be inferior to God with respect to deity if the name “mediator” is extended to
him. For
when Christ is placed in this way in the middle station, his essence is not at
issue, nor is there a subtle dispute over what he is. Instead we must attend
to God's plan, which the ancients called “economy” or “dispensation.” If the
question concerns essence, we know that in God there is neither lesser nor
greater. But because the eternal Son of God appeared, in accommodation to the
measure of our weakness, to stand between us and God, he is fittingly called
inferior to the Father in the very person and office of the mediator, in
accordance with the rule of piety. In this sense the prophets do not hesitate
to couple the name *Yahweh* with the title “angel,” as it is said in Hosea
12:5: “Jacob prevailed over the angel”; and immediately it is added, “Yahweh,
God of hosts; Yahweh is his memorial.” In Malachi 3:1 it is put a little
differently: “The Lord whom you seek will come into his temple, and the angel
of the covenant whom you desire.” Yet the meaning comes back to the same
point: supreme authority is not only accorded to God's envoy and mediator,
but divine majesty is also asserted when he is said to be coming into his own
temple.

The next objection—that if the mediator's office is common to the deity,
Christ must intercede before himself—is easily dispelled. Although Christ
prays in the mediator's person, this nevertheless regards the human nature in
a peculiar manner. Yet here again our understanding must be directed to God's
plan. We do not attack or flatly reject that statement of

### [CO IX, p. 354]

Augustine, that the mediator intercedes before the Son and the Holy Spirit so
that we may return to favor with the God from whom we have been estranged. For
the one and simple essence of God certainly subsists in three persons. But we
rightly value more highly the manner of speaking dictated by that heavenly
teacher. In many places Christ indeed exalts his deity, yet nowhere does he
teach that he was sent by the second or the third person. Still less does he
say that he intercedes before the Spirit, but rather before the Father.

Nothing, therefore, is safer than to dismiss profane clever arguments and
speculations and keep our minds fixed upon that wonderful dispensation in
which the Father is said to have sent his only-begotten Son so that in him he
might be favorable toward us and open to entreaty through the protection of
our advocate. Indeed, even when barbarism ran rampant throughout the whole
world, the common saying that the works of the Trinity are undivided was
corrected or softened by an inserted restriction, since by itself it was too
harsh: namely, the qualification “inwardly” was added. Conscious of our own
ignorance, however, we are content with the outward operation by which God
accommodates himself to our small measure of understanding. Thus Christ will
be placed between us and the Father without offense. Yet nothing is taken
away from his immeasurable glory, although beneath the veil of humanity he is
seen more obscurely, until, when the mediator's course has been completed,
Christ is subjected to the Father in his human nature and his divine essence
and majesty shine clearly in themselves, or without intermediary. Even if
curious people find no pleasure in such restraint, it will be more than
enough to settle modest and godly minds. No contentious wrangler, however
shameless, will be able to reproach us with anything except that, out of
reverence for the mystery—which should count as praise rather than blame—we
pursue simplicity.

We can now readily examine what Stancaro advances in confirmation of his
position. He says that a definition can be taken from Paul's passage: “A
mediator is not of one, but God is one” (Galatians 3:20). But if he weighed
the context prudently, he would see that the Gentiles are there brought
together with the Jews: Christ not only reconciled the Jews to God long ago,
but now also calls the Gentiles into fellowship in the same grace. The
interpretation Stancaro adduces is cold and tasteless, just as his added claim
that there is no need for a mediator except where there is division has no
force. We showed just above that the elect angels, who have never been at
variance with God, are united to him through Christ. But because this is not
the hinge on which the question turns, I acknowledge that Christ is set before
us and must be received by

### [CO IX, p. 355]

us as mediator insofar as he appeased God toward us by his sacrifice.

As for Stancaro's taking the term “God” to prove that the three
persons had been offended, I answer again that he wrongly and unskillfully
neglects the mystery of the dispensation, which Scripture commends everywhere.
It is astonishing that he does not attend to the plain testimonies he cites.
If, speaking properly and in Scripture's customary manner, Christ is mediator
between us and God the Father, that clever speculation does nothing but
entangle and obscure what would otherwise be clear. He is also far astray in
Paul's passage, “There is one God and one mediator between God and men, the man
Jesus Christ” (1 Timothy 2:5), because the name “man” was put there for no
other purpose than to give us familiar access to the Father. Yet we do not
deny that the whole deity is comprehended under the Father and that God, one
in essence in three persons, was appeased. Leaving aside that deep and
incomprehensible mystery, however, we consider only what we have learned from
Scripture and Christ's sacred mouth.

These two truths—that the *Logos* and eternal Son of God is equal to the
Father, and that the mediator is less than the Father—are no more in conflict
with each other than these other two: that the *Logos* by himself and
distinctly constitutes a person in the deity, and yet the one person of
Christ the mediator is constituted from two natures. Stancaro argues
unskillfully: “If Christ in two natures is mediator and less than the Father,
then he is so according to each nature.” We taught a little above that what
truly and fittingly belongs to the whole composite ought not to be torn apart
and assigned to the natures. In the same way, the middle station occupied by
Christ the redeemer takes absolutely nothing from his divine essence.

The reasons Stancaro draws from the mediator's office have no more weight,
because they reason from a part to the whole. Christ gave himself as the price
of redemption; he reconciled all things on the cross of his flesh; he is the
lamb who takes away the sins of the world; he was given as a propitiation
through faith in his blood. Although he accomplished all these things
according to the human nature, it does not follow that he is mediator only
with respect to it, because by expiation and sacrifice Christ did not exhaust
all the functions of his office. What is it to conquer death? What is it to
rise in the power of the Spirit and receive life from himself? What is it to
unite us to God and to be one with God? These things certainly will not be
found in Christ's human nature apart from the deity. And yet they enter into
the reckoning when the mediator's office is discussed. If only Stancaro would
pay attention to the words he recites, that God did not spare his only-begotten
Son (John 3:16). For if he is Son in each nature, he does not possess that
status from his

### [CO IX, p. 356]

humanity. Now if the name “God” signifies three persons and Stancaro presses
the point too obstinately, it follows that Christ is the Son both of himself
and of the Holy Spirit. This antithesis alone, if Stancaro will take the
trouble to weigh it reverently, will free him from many knots. When the
mediator compares himself with the Father, by whom he was sent, and likewise
when Paul calls him God manifested in flesh, Paul's purpose is to set the
mediator before us. He immediately adds that the mediator was justified in
the Spirit, appeared to angels, was preached among the Gentiles, and was
received into glory (1 Timothy 3:16). From here, rather, a simple and genuine
definition ought to have been sought. The same thing is plainly drawn from
chapter 3 of Colossians, where reconciliation through the flesh is described
in such a way that the deity retains its place in primacy and fullness.

Stancaro must therefore either prove that it does not belong to the
mediator's office for Christ to sit at the Father's right hand, defend and
protect us by the Spirit's power, entrust the guardianship of our salvation to
angels, and subdue the devil and the whole world; or he must acknowledge that
he wrongly infers from sacrifice and intercession that the mediator is not the
Son of God except insofar as he is man. We showed above why Paul, when
discussing the mediator, expressly ascribes to him the name “man”: so that,
with fear shaken off, he may gently invite us to him. Paul is not arguing
subtly about essence, but supplying material for confidence, lest we hesitate
to flee to one who is our brother.

We wonder under what pretext Stancaro twists to his own meaning what we showed
above to support us perfectly: that Christ, though in the form of God, emptied
himself. “This,” he says, “is the rule for resolving the question.” Let it
certainly be so, for we embrace it willingly. But if the only-begotten Son of
God, who was equal to the Father, emptied himself (Philippians 2:7), he is
certainly mediator in two natures. And that same one who became obedient unto
death was raised on high, receiving the name above every name, where every
tongue confesses that he is in the glory of God the Father. As for what
this glory is and how it should be understood, let Christ himself answer
rather than us. “Father,” he says, “glorify the Son with the glory that I had
with you from the beginning, before the creation of the world” (John 17:5).
Stancaro will surely admit that the prayer would be perverse if Christ asked
for the flesh alone to be adorned and illumined with God's eternal glory. But
if this is applicable only to the whole person, we may rightly conclude from
it that the passage he cited can be referred in no other way.

When he comes to the fathers, Stancaro indeed puts on impressive colors so as
to have them as patrons and supporters of his case. But if the question of
what Augustine thought is asked apart from contention,

### [CO IX, p. 357]

there is no disagreement between him and us. We acknowledge that in many
places this godly teacher makes Christ mediator insofar as he is man. We also
acknowledge his words from book 10 of *The City of God* as well as from other
works: “In the form of a servant he is priest and himself the sacrifice; in
this form he offered himself, in this form he was offered, and according to
this form he is mediator.” Meanwhile, however, Augustine does not conceal
that for the mediator's office he must necessarily also be God, as in the
penultimate chapter of book 1 of the *Confessions*: “In order to be mediator,
he had to have something in common with God and something with human beings,
lest, being like human beings in both respects, he should be far from God, or,
being like God in both respects, he should be far from human beings, and so
should not be mediator.”

Similarly, in book 9, chapter 10 of *The City of God*: “If all human beings
are wretched, a middle one must be sought who is not only man but also God, so
that his blessed mortality may carry human beings from mortal misery to
blessed immortality.” Likewise on Psalm 134: “Into this great separation the
mediator was sent. You, a man, could not reach God; God became man. If he were
man alone, by following what you are you would never arrive. If he were God
alone, by not comprehending what you are not you would never arrive.” And in
the book *On the Sheep*: “A man apart from deity is not mediator, nor is God
apart from humanity mediator. Behold the mediator: deity without humanity is
not mediatrix; humanity without deity is not mediatrix. But between deity
alone and humanity alone, Christ's divine humanity and Christ's human deity
are mediatrix.”

We also do not deny that Cyril often refers Christ's being mediator to the
human nature, lest he make the deity liable to subjection, which was what the
Arians pressed. But elsewhere he is a skillful and faithful interpreter of
his own statements, in dialogue 2 *On the Trinity*: “He is understood as
mediator for this reason: in himself he shows deity and humanity brought
together and united. For he is akin to God, since he is from him and in him;
he is also akin to us, because he is from us and in us.” You see how Cyril
constitutes the mediator from two natures and necessarily demands their union
for this office.

Chrysostom's reasoning is no different. Although he wishes the mediator to be
sought in the human nature, he nevertheless denies that the intercession will
be legitimate unless deity is also added. Thus on chapter 2 of the First
Epistle to Timothy: “The mediator must be joined to both parties whose
mediator

### [CO IX, p. 358]

he is. For this is the mediator's distinctive property: to become a sharer in
both parties between whom he interposes himself. Unless Christ therefore
shares in the Father's nature, he is certainly not mediator.” Hence, in the
*Homily on the Ascension*, Chrysostom teaches that it is not an angel or an
archangel, but the Son of God himself, who pleads. An explanation immediately
follows: Christ placed himself in the middle by sounding forth both natures.

Ambrose likewise takes the same position when expounding Paul's passage:
“Because the Father is one—not in person, but in undifferentiated nature—the
Son of God, in order to be arbiter between God and human beings, was born a man
after assuming flesh, so that the man Christ might be mediator between God and
human beings. This is not without deity, because the man was in God and God
was in the man, and so that he might be mediator from both and reconcile both.
He first accomplished these things in himself, for the mediator could not be
preserved while unity remained preserved.”

There is no need to pursue this at greater length, provided readers have once
been warned that whenever this form of speech occurs in the ancients—that
Christ is mediator in the flesh—their authentic meaning is distorted if it is
taken exclusively. We have judged that the proposed question should be
answered freely and clearly in this way. We would indeed have preferred to
leave Stancaro aside and explain the substance of the matter peaceably, but he
himself extorted a refutation from us against our will. Nevertheless, no
bitterness, contention, or desire to fight in our response will offend godly
and humane readers.

## Translator's notes

1. The Latin moves between corporate plurals and two first-person singulars,
   *fateor* (“I acknowledge,” p. 354) and *regero* (“I answer again,” p. 355).
   It also names “Calvin” in the third person at p. 353 when referring readers
   to *Institutes* 2.14. These features are retained rather than silently
   homogenized; they are consistent with a document drafted or assembled for
   subscription by the ministry, but do not by themselves settle the precise
   division of authorial labor.
2. The printed enumeration moves from “First” directly to “Third reason” and
   “Fourth reason.” No numbered “Second” is visible in the CO base text; the
   sequence has not been repaired conjecturally.
3. *Oeconomia* and *dispensatio* (p. 353) name God's ordered saving arrangement,
   not a difference of divine essence. “Communication of properties” (p. 352)
   translates *idiomatum communicatio*, the Christological rule by which what
   belongs properly to one nature can be predicated of the one person.
4. At p. 355 the printed parenthesis “John 3:16” accompanies “God did not spare
   his only-begotten Son.” CO's editors identify Romans 8:32 as the intended
   passage. At p. 356 the base text says “chapter 3 of Colossians,” while the
   reconciliation, primacy, and fullness language points to Colossians 1:20;
   CO's note makes the same correction. The translation preserves the body
   text and records the corrections here.
5. At p. 358 CO prints *utramque sonando naturam*, rendered literally “by
   sounding forth both natures.” Its note records *sociando* (“by joining”) in
   the Bern witness and judges that reading preferable. Because this edition
   follows CO's printed base text, the Latin and translation do not silently
   substitute the variant.
6. The Ambrose excerpt at p. 358 is textually difficult. CO's apparatus adds
   words after “Father” in other witnesses and records *vocari* (“be called”)
   for the base text's *servari* (“be preserved”) in the final sentence. The
   translation renders the printed base, whose syntax and final claim remain
   awkward; it should not be used to reconstruct Ambrose without consulting a
   critical edition of Ambrose.
7. Patristic titles and locators above reproduce the response's attributions.
   The translation does not claim that its embedded Latin excerpts match a
   modern critical text of Augustine, Cyril, Chrysostom, or Ambrose.
