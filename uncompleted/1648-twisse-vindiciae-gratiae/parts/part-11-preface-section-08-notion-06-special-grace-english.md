SECTION EIGHT NOTION VI

PERKINS

Nothing good can be done unless God absolutely wills and does it; we do good insofar as God does more or less in us.

ARMINIUS

God’s concurrence with secondary causes for producing some action or work has two forms: general concurrence and the special assistance of grace. It is most certain that without this special assistance of grace no good can be done by any rational creature.

2. But whether this assistance of grace belongs to the divine will willing to communicate it absolutely and, through it once communicated, to operate absolutely is a matter placed in controversy among theologians. Nor is this without reason, since the word *absolutely* is not found in the Scriptures, and it has not yet been proved that an equivalent to it is found in the Scriptures.

RESPONSE 1

Epicurus, as Cicero relates, posited gods in words but removed them in reality. Arminius posits grace in words—and special grace at that—as necessarily required not only for every good work but for every possibility of a good work; but see, reader, whether he does not remove it in reality. According to Arminius, this special grace operates only by way of persuasion. But, I say, persuasion to do good does not bestow the power to do what is urged, but rather presupposes it. It follows that the power to do what is good is already present beforehand, and that without special grace according to Arminius. Likewise, whatever someone can do as a result of persuasion, he can do even without persuasion, since no power of willing or acting is acquired through persuasion. I add also that this persuasion is nothing other than the preaching of the Gospel. I know, however, that the opponents stubbornly deny this. For they maintain that there is an internal persuasion proceeding from the Holy Spirit, distinct from that other, external persuasion. But I call God to witness that, although I have labored at this—so that from the comparison and consideration of various relevant passages in Corvinus I might discern what that internal persuasion distinct from the external one is, about which they philosophize—I have never, even after lengthy disputations were undertaken on this matter, been able to find anything distinct except that general concurrence of God by which he concurs with all orators, both ecclesiastical and secular, to cause the things proposed to be understood and the hearers to be moved by them according to the nature of the things proposed. That is, according to them, the grace of God necessarily operates so far as impressing the sense of what is said. This also happens in all the panegyric orations of the Gentiles, in precisely the same manner throughout, so that whatever occurs is done through the special grace of God. Indeed, by the same reasoning, in every incitement to perpetrate crimes and in every diabolical suggestion, whatever occurs is also done through the special grace of God. I am speaking of special grace which precedes the human act by way of a principle. But if he should advance those points about special grace concurrent with the act, or about grace by way of concurrence, as Corvinus, following Francisco Suárez, makes the distinction, it is also evident—to lay bare the mysteries of these theologians—that every evil, however criminal, is done through the special grace of God no less than every good. For Arminius, and all the Arminians to a man so far as I know, concede God’s concurrence in every act, however impure. In these matters Arminius also appears to present himself as agreeing with Perkins, but meanwhile he states Perkins’s position incompletely. Perkins said that nothing good is done unless God does it. Arminius says that nothing good is done without the special grace of God, but this means only that grace is the cause without which there is no good work, not its truly efficient cause. Indeed, persuasion—in which Arminius would locate all the force and efficacy of special grace operating by way of a principle—does not truly effect the act. For one who persuades acts only by way of proposing the object, while the object acts in the manner of a final cause, not an efficient cause.

2. God works in us both to will and to accomplish. The question is whether God works this absolutely or under a condition. If under a condition, tell me, what was this condition? Certainly no other easily falls under consideration than this: provided that we will. But how absurd it would be to say that God works willing in us provided that we will! For in this way the same thing would be prior to itself. In commanded acts, I grant, such a condition has some appearance and color—namely, that God works in us acts of praying, acts of bestowing alms, doing good to our neighbors, sustaining the weak, comforting the afflicted, assisting the poor, and so forth, provided that we will. But in elicited acts the same thing cannot be said without gross absurdity. And yet the Holy Spirit testifies that God works the elicited acts of the will themselves in us when he says that God works in us both to will and to accomplish according to his good pleasure. Arminius adds that the word *absolutely* is not found in the Scriptures. But where, I ask, in the sacred Scriptures, whenever our renewal, sanctification, and recall to repentance are attributed to the Holy Spirit, is even the slightest mention made of a condition? Jeremiah 31:38: “This is the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel after these days, says Jehovah: I will put my law in their mind and inscribe it on their heart; and I will be their God, and they will be my people.” Isaiah 32:39: “For I will put in them one heart and one way to revere me all their days, for their own good and the good of their children after them. And I will make an everlasting covenant with them, that I will not turn away from attending to them, and I will put reverence for me into their mind so that they do not depart from me.” Likewise Ezekiel 36:26–27: “And I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit in your midst; and, removing the stony heart from your flesh, I will put in you a heart of flesh. And I will put my Spirit in your midst, by whom I will cause you to walk in my statutes and to keep my judgments by doing them.” Thomas Bradwardine too demonstrated by various arguments that every will of God is absolute and none conditional, in *On the Cause of God against Pelagius*, book 2.
