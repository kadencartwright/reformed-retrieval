You will say: with greater necessitation, a predetermining decree, fixed
beforehand concerning one determinate act, establishes its good pleasure as
something settled, than the will itself settles anything concerning that act.
I answer that a greater degree of necessitation does not follow from this,
because only one act was going to be future if, by an impossibility, we suppose
every decree of GOD removed. For a disjunctive power for several contrary
acts—namely, either to will or not to will—is, with respect to futurition, a
power for one determinately, not for several conjointly. Therefore, from the
fact that the decree settles something fixed concerning one determinately
future act, it does not follow that the necessitation arising from the decree
is greater than the necessitation flowing from the elective power itself,
unless

[Marginal summary: How necessitation follows neither from the decree nor from
predetermination.]

[Chapter XXVI, Part C; printed page 410; IA PDF page 468; BSB canvas 462]

GOD's decree or predetermination were to impel the will more strongly in the
manner of breaking forth into a determinate act than the will freely impels
itself. That must not be said. Peter promises that his daughter Valeria will be
John's wife, and therefore settles determinately and immutably concerning the
marriage—let us suppose this for the sake of instruction—that it will occur on
a specified day. Meanwhile, nothing has been revealed to the girl concerning
the matter. Peter nevertheless knows that he can entice the virgin's mind into
a most free consent by the promise of a very great dowry and by reasons drawn
from John's nobility, strength, power, wealth, beauty, and extraordinary and
proven integrity of character. He counsels and persuades Valeria, and on the
specified day the marriage is freely celebrated on both sides. GOD, however,
can by a determining decree settle absolutely concerning a free act, because
he knows much more exactly every hidden recess of the free will, its elective
powers and inward inclinations. He can move, move thoroughly, and infallibly
bend the will to that act to which, in his decree, he knows perfectly well the
will itself will in fact bend itself. I would rather philosophize about these
matters while admitting ignorance than deny a providence that determines
positive acts altogether.

*If GOD, determined in himself, can efficaciously will and determine the
material acts of sin, it follows that GOD could have spoken with human beings
in such a way as to cause the material entity of a lie by predetermining the
will and the intellect to the whole reality of assent to a lie and an error,
provided that GOD did not intend the formal character of the lie and
falsehood, but instead gathered greater glory for himself from it. That GOD
could do this is proved because he could bring forth visions and sensible
objects and form voices in the air that signify something false at his
pleasure; or he could perform miracles by his power while asserting that they
were done in confirmation of the truth. The certainty of faith would thus be
overthrown no less than if GOD caused a lie as a lie.*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, in the same work, disputation 30, section 1.]

I answer: this argument harms neither our adversaries' cause nor ours. For by
universal concurrence, which is just as causative as predetermination is, GOD
could cause the material entity of hatred of GOD and of a lie. This is no more
absurd than GOD's causing, in the office of the first cause, every entitative
act of sin—something I know no philosopher or theologian, with the exception of
Durandus, to have denied. For in this manner GOD would not be the cause of the
lie. 2. Visions, voices, and sensible signs that are indifferent

[Marginal summary: GOD's determination does not compel anyone to sin.]

[Chapter XXVI, Part C; printed page 411; IA PDF page 469; BSB canvas 463]

to truth or falsehood, and that are symbols having by bare potency—but not
formally—the capacity to signify truth or falsehood by GOD's institution,
could be formed by GOD in the air. But if they signified something false and
falsely signified GOD's will—that is, if they signified that GOD willed what he
did not will—then such signs could not be ascribed to GOD, even though he did
not intend a lie. For GOD would speak falsely through those signs and would
signify that he willed what he does not will. That would conflict with GOD's
truthfulness and candor, for he would not play the hypocrite. The Jesuit should
have proved this point, not merely repeated it. 3. Miracles confirm no truth
unless the authority of the Divine Word is joined to those miracles. Therefore
GOD could not confirm a falsehood through miracles unless he spoke falsely,
which would be for GOD to lie—an indignity beyond words. Nor would the faith of
those who believed such false signs be imperiled in this way, for GOD could not
form such signs in the air per se and immediately.

13. Ruiz. *GOD tempts no one; therefore he physically predetermines no one to
the material act of sin.* The consequence is proved: *Temptation is an action
that impels choice to sin. Therefore, the more efficaciously and the more per
se something impels choice to sin, the more closely and perfectly will it
possess the essence of temptation. But physical causality—the causality of
predetermination—impels to sin more efficaciously and more per se than moral
causality does. For the efficacy of physical causality really exercises an
influence and necessarily brings about its effect, whereas moral causality
neither really exercises an influence nor necessarily brings about its effect.
It also exercises influence more per se, for disobedience so inheres in the
free and real volition of trampling the divine precepts that it cannot be
separated from it by any power. But temptations can exist without the
disobedience of the person tempted existing.* And Suarez says: *The intention
of malice is not required for the nature of temptation, nor does the Demon
induce someone to the formal element of sin as such—that is, to love the
forbidden object insofar as it is forbidden—but only to the act.* And Fasolus
says: *That GOD does not necessitate is not enough to vindicate GOD, because a
human being and the Devil do not tempt by necessitating. Moreover, to tempt it
is enough to intend evil indirectly, namely, by inducing a good to which malice
is attached, even if the inducement is moral and infallible.* Then, Ruiz and
Fasolus say, *concupiscence knows nothing about malice, for it is not capable of
intending the formal character of malice. Rather, it tempts to the act as a
positive entity from sensory cognition, which is ignorant of the
characteristics of moral good and evil.* Indeed, says Ruiz, *if GOD persuaded
and counseled the material action of sin,*

*he would tempt someone to sin even though he contrived to abstract his
intention from the formal character of malice. Likewise, even if the intention
of malice is abstracted from it, the Demon's outward suggestion of the material
act directly and immediately induces nothing other than a positive and real
consent, delight, and honor contrary to the divine law. But GOD does nothing
else by predetermining someone to the act.*

[Marginal summary: GOD's determination neither tempts anyone to sin nor
exercises efficacious influence upon sin—whether by persuading, necessitating,
or any other means.]

I answer, 1. This statement must be distinguished: *GOD exercises influence
upon the act more efficaciously and really than the Devil does.* If we speak of
the efficacy, strength, and reality of exercising influence physically, I
concede it. For GOD exercises influence upon the act in the physical line more
efficaciously than the Devil does, since the Devil does not exercise influence
in that manner at all, with physical efficacy. But if we speak of the efficacy
and reality of exercising influence and causing morally, then the Devil
exercises influence much more efficaciously than GOD, because GOD exercises no
moral influence whatsoever. The comparison in either direction is as
senseless as if I were to say that the raven is much whiter than the swan and
that the swan is blacker than the raven. It therefore follows that GOD is the
first cause of the act in the physical line, but is not in any manner a cause
of malice, because the Holy One of Israel is no cause of malice at all. 2.
Although predetermination necessarily entails the effect, because that
necessity harmonizes gently with liberty, it does not follow that GOD causes
malice with as much necessity or reality of causing as the Devil causes it.
For GOD is caused to cause malice by no necessity whatsoever, because he is no
cause of it at all; nor does the inseparability of malice from the material act
make GOD be caused to cause malice with any “per-se-ness,” so to speak. This was
the point that had
to be proved, but our vigorous assertor is a sickly prover. 3. It is false that
the Devil neither induces nor intends the malice of the act but only the act
itself. For the Demon tempted Eve, not to the act of eating insofar as it was
an act, but to the act insofar as it was forbidden. The tempting Devil's aim
was not simply that they should eat, but that they should eat the forbidden
fruit, so that our first parents, cast down from their original dignity, would
be rendered hateful to GOD and profoundly miserable. Nor was the Devil so
foolish that he would solicit them to the bare material act of eating other
fruits that had not been forbidden. In that way he had no hope of attaining
his intention, which was to drive the human race headlong into destruction and
eternal damnation. 4. It is falsely and deceptively said that, in tempting, the
Devil does not intend the malice of sin as such. For that is a fallacy from

[Chapter XXVI, Part C; printed page 413; IA PDF page 471; BSB canvas 465]

*kata ti*—what is true in a certain respect—to what is said *haplōs*, without
qualification. When tempting, the Demon does not intend evil insofar as it is
evil to the Demon himself. This is true, because the more dishonorable the
thing is to which the Devil solicits a person, the better and more delightful
it appears to the Devil's depraved will, and therefore it is good to him. But
because the Demon burns with a blazing hatred of GOD and of the divine image,
in every temptation he wills and intends what displeases GOD. What displeases
GOD, however, is sheer malice, not the act that is entitatively good. 5. This
argument cleverly proves that GOD contributes a more efficacious and more real
influence to sin than the Devil contributes. But happily, according to the
Jesuits, GOD is a tempter to sin in virtue of his office as the first cause,
and is himself determined by the sinful will. 6. It is also false that
concupiscence tempts only to the act and not to malice. Because a natural
faculty is subject to the law, by the very fact that it inclines to an act when
inclining is forbidden, it inclines to malice, although it is ignorant of the
characteristics of moral good and evil. 7. If GOD were to persuade someone to
the material action of sin while abstracting from malice—that is, if the action
were forbidden by no law of GOD—he would not persuade someone to evil or tempt
that person to sin. Yet a merely intellectual abstraction from malice would
not free GOD from culpable temptation, because it would involve repugnancy and
contradiction for GOD to persuade someone to a material act that is forbidden.
For GOD to forbid an act is to dissuade someone from that act; he would thus at
once forbid and not forbid, persuade and not persuade, the act. An argument
from a hypothesis involving contradiction is empty. 8. I answer similarly to
the point about the Demon's outward suggestion of a material act contrary to
the Divine Law, even if the intention of malice is abstracted from it. Because
the Demon is prohibited by GOD's Law from suggesting or persuading anyone to
an act forbidden by the law, he would do what conflicts with GOD's Law and
would formally tempt, even if he did not actually intend the malice. A virtual
intention, which suffices for the formal nature of temptation, would
nevertheless be present. But if he solicited someone to a material act
forbidden by no law of GOD, he would not tempt that person to evil. Indeed, the
Jesuits' hypothesis is also contradictory. Even if you abstract the formal
intention of malice a thousand times over, if the Devil by tempting were to
induce a positive and real consent to a delight and honor really contrary to
the Divine Law—as the Jesuit says in express words in his hypothesis—then that
consent would not really be abstracted from malice. Indeed, it would really be
forbidden by GOD's Law and, by that very fact, would formally include malice by
a real inclusion.

[Chapter XXVI, Part C; printed page 414; IA PDF page 472; BSB canvas 466]

To induce someone to it would be formally to tempt, even if someone mentally
abstracted the act from malice. For a mental abstraction of the act from malice
does not free a person committing a crime when he performs a real act contrary
to GOD's Law. In that way David would have been absolved of adultery, Absalom
of incest, and the Chaldeans of robbery, even though they committed such acts
of adultery and robbery, provided that in their minds they intended the
material acts and not the malice. But in such a serious matter is this not to
cheat GOD's Law with specious and badly constructed petty distinctions? The
Jesuit could in this way commit adultery and acquit himself by this answer: “I
do not intend the malice in the adultery, but merely the act.” Here the
adversaries fail to understand that sin does not formally consist of an act
and malice, but formally is an entirely moral entity and the very disorder of
the act. The act, however, is included in the definition of sin only “by
addition,” as the logicians say.

Ruiz says: *It follows from this that neither the one who plants—namely, the
Demon—is anything, nor is the human being who is the Demon's servant anything,
because the voice of temptations sounds outwardly in vain unless GOD gives the
increase and predetermines the will to positive consent to the temptation.*

[Marginal reference: In the same place, figure 23.]

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, in the same place, references 18 and 19.]

I answer: the objection is impious. Because predetermination is given only to
someone willing to have it and is tempered to liberty, it follows only that
temptations are futile unless a depraved will freely consents. 2. I acknowledge
that the person is tempted in vain unless GOD predetermines him to the act, but
this is not to consent to the temptation. Indeed, GOD's predetermination is
good, but the consent is impious because it is forbidden by the Law. 3. On the
Jesuit's view, the thanks for sin avoided will here be owed to the Devil and to
free choice, not to GOD, who acts in such a manner that sin can or cannot
follow.

Ruiz. *If predetermination determines the will to material consent independently
of human liberty, then it is no benefit that the Devil is bound with a chain,
contrary to GOD's word in Revelation 20:2. There will be no greater danger of
consenting to the gravest temptation than to the slightest. Indeed, even if no
temptation at all presses upon someone, there will be precisely the same danger
as there is in the gravest temptation, because once predetermination is
admitted, consent follows from it inevitably, and by no power can consent
follow if predetermination is absent, whether the gravest temptation, the
slightest, or none is posited. All these things are absurd. Therefore it follows
that CHRIST did not come to destroy the works of the Devil if he predetermines
people to them, yet he cannot*

*destroy malice in any other way than by removing the material works and
fulfilling the office of the first and most universal cause. But to fulfill
this office, a predetermining concurrence of the greatest efficacy is not
required. A concurrence that of itself inclines to good actions and leaves a
free power of determining it is sufficient. Therefore—* The major premise is
proved: *It is unworthy of the divine goodness to display a special care and
providence so that the same action is produced by the created will. But GOD
would do this if he furnished a more efficacious concurrence than sufficed for
the office of the first cause. This is confirmed because both the Scholastics
and the predeterminers*—he means our theologians—*free GOD, as concurring with
the material action of sin, from blame especially for the reason that, by his
office as the universal cause, GOD is bound to furnish concurrence.* The minor
premise is proved: *GOD abundantly fulfills the office of the first cause when
he governs and moves those human beings who never elicit the material action of
hatred of GOD. This is confirmed: the office of the first cause does not
necessarily demand that GOD, determined spontaneously and intrinsically, will
to exercise that office in shameful acts, and indeed in acts far exceeding
honest actions in number, rather than, conversely, predetermine more honest
actions. Human demerits are not the cause, because the office of the first
cause did not demand that GOD predetermine Lucifer and our first parents to
conceive the material action of pride in the second instant rather than the
action of perseverance in the love of GOD, since no demerit of blame preceded
that instant.*

I answer, 1. It is false that predetermining concurrence possesses the greatest
efficacy and so removes free power, for purely physical efficacy—such as that
of GOD concurring with fire to heat—would be greater. 2. It is also false that
predetermination possesses greater efficacy than suffices for the office of
the first and universal cause. The office of the first cause requires that GOD
retain full dominion over free acts and over absolutely all acts with respect
to their determinate number. Without predetermination this is in no way
possible, for without such dominion providence would be deficient. Indeed, as
demonstrated above, our faith, hope, consolation, patience, fervor in prayer,
gratitude, and fear would all be empty and vain. 3. Nor is it more unworthy of
GOD's goodness to display care that the material action of sin be produced by
the created will through predetermining concurrence than to take care that it
be produced through universal and simultaneous

[Marginal summary: Predetermination alone, not indifferent concurrence,
fulfills the office and duty of the first cause.]

[Chapter XXVI, Part C; printed page 416; IA PDF page 474; BSB canvas 468]

concurrence, as the adversaries teach. Those who say that GOD produces the
material act of sin without care and special providence deserve to be hissed
out of CHRIST's school. 4. If it were unworthy of GOD's goodness to produce the
material act of sin, the reason would be that sin is an abominable evil. But on
that reasoning, for GOD to permit sin would be unworthy of GOD's goodness,
since both acts are good. 5. Nor do our Scholastics free GOD, as concurring
with the action of sin, from blame because he is bound or obligated to furnish
his concurrence. Rather, because GOD freely concurs as the first cause, he
furnishes a holy and good concurrence, free from every moral causality that
would brand GOD with the mark of sin. 6. GOD would abundantly fulfill the
office of the first cause by moving and governing human beings who never elicit
the material action of sin, on the hypothesis that GOD had not decreed to
declare in human beings the glory of punitive justice and sparing mercy, as he
did with the good Angels. But while the hypothesis of such a decree stands,
the office of the first cause would never have been fulfilled if GOD had moved
all human beings to no acts other than those of complete obedience. In that
case GOD could never have actually declared his justice and mercy. 7. The
Jesuit's conception of GOD is entirely carnal when he imagines it unworthy of
the office of the first cause that GOD, intrinsically determined, should
determine people to shameful material acts, and indeed to far more shameful
than honest acts. For if we wish to imagine GOD performing the office of the
first cause solely from goodness and love for human salvation, then in that
office GOD would permit no human beings whatsoever to commit any shameful acts.
No earthly father who acted with his children in everything from paternal love
alone would permit them to fall into acts that are inevitable means to eternal
destruction, if he could prevent them consistently with paternal right and
duty. And if GOD acted from goodness and love alone for the eternal salvation
of rational creatures, he would not permit them to sin. But because he deals
with rational creatures from his free decree to declare the glory of punitive
justice and sparing mercy—for he freely makes everything for himself, that is,
for his own glory—the Jesuit savors the things of the flesh when he wishes to
make it unworthy of GOD's goodness and love for the salvation of creatures
that, determined by his own free choice, GOD should in the office of the first
cause predetermine human beings to shameful actions

and indeed to more shameful than honest ones. By equal reasoning, that audacious
little man can accuse GOD because he concurs with any human beings in shameful
acts, for GOD concurs with such acts from his free good pleasure and under no
necessity. With the same madness he accuses GOD because there are human beings

For GOD, freely and without compulsion, contributes universal, determinable,

we maintain that human demerits are the cause of divine concurrence with

*The whole preceding discourse is confirmed. If the universal concurrence of
the first cause, insofar as it is from itself, preserves uniformity and
equality in comparison with all human beings, all times, and all actions, and
receives from the created choice intrinsically its disparity and dissimilarity,
by which it is bent to diverse and contrary material actions of sins, then it
is rightly understood how that concurrence is owed always and everywhere to
all human beings and in every action, whether honest or shameful. But, on the
contrary, if the concurrence of the first and most universal cause has from
itself its being manifold and applied to the material actions of sins, no
reason can now be given why GOD would fail to fulfill the office of the first
cause if he always, or almost always, employed predetermining concurrence for
honest actions and never, or very rarely, employed predetermining concurrence
for material actions that are shameful, since the office of the first cause
would still be exercised in other actions.* Here we see that the Jesuits
maintain that GOD, insofar as it is from himself, never declares the glory of
punitive justice; insofar as it is from himself, reprobates no one; and insofar
as it is from himself, does not decree the incarnation of his Son, the grace of
repentance, and the gracious justification of sinners. For, insofar as it is
from himself, he neither permits sins nor contributes concurrence to the
material acts of sins, but does so under compulsion and determination by
created choice. 2. It follows that the fact that there are so many determinate
acts of sin—no more and no fewer—and so many determinate honest and
supernatural acts, such as giving alms, praying, repenting, believing, loving
GOD and one's neighbor, and so forth—no more and no fewer—comes from created
choice, not from GOD. Hence, 3. it necessarily follows that the determinate
number of the elect inscribed in the book of life is not ultimately traced
back to the free determination

of GOD's gracious good pleasure as to its first and highest cause, but to sheer
created free choice. From this, as the determining cause, it comes about that
there are as many divine concurrences with shameful actions as choice pleases,
and as many divine concurrences with honest and supernatural actions as that
same petty human choice desires. 4. Hence it follows that the declaration of
the glory of punitive justice and sparing mercy lies absolutely within the
power of created free choice, not GOD, except insofar as he is bent by created
choice to that declaration. 5. We teach that it is false that, given our
physical predetermination, the office of the first cause would not be fulfilled
if GOD predetermines more shameful than honest acts. For the office of the
first cause is to effect willing or not willing in all actions, whether of
nature or grace, according to his good pleasure, and according to his freedom
to concur or not concur with every secondary cause. Indeed, even the office of
the first cause does not compel GOD to concur.

16. Ruiz continues: *Whatever GOD permits by mere permission strictly so
called, GOD does not do, nor does he predetermine that it be done by us. But
GOD permits the material action of sin by mere permission strictly so called.
Therefore GOD does not do the material action of sin or predetermine that it
be done by us.* The major premise is proved: *In the sacred writings,
permission strictly so called means not preventing an action of sin by
withdrawing protection and governance. This is confirmed because, conversely,
not permitting means preventing not only the malice but also the material
action of sin by bestowing protection and favor toward a good work. And when
human beings permit, they are said not to prevent sinful actions. This is
further confirmed because in permission GOD bears himself as one who removes
something, who is called an accidental mover in the Physics, as Thomas says.*
The minor is proved: *The formal character of malice cannot be prevented by GOD
unless he prevents the free material action to which the malice is attached.
Thus: to permit is not to prevent; but not preventing necessarily refers to
the material action of sin, not only to the malice. And so the argument runs:
the power to prevent what is permitted belongs to the essential nature of
permission. But GOD has no power to prevent the formal character of malice
alone from following upon a freely conceived material action of hatred of GOD.
Therefore, the formal character of malice alone is not what GOD permits. Again,
GOD's power to prevent malice exists insofar as he can prevent the material
action from which*

*it inseparably follows. Therefore the material action of hatred of GOD is what
permission regards more immediately. If the foregoing solution were
satisfactory, the consequence would also be that it could rightly be affirmed
of human beings that they merely permit and do not commit or cause sin when
they elicit some material action of sin without intending its malice, but
rather grieving that such an action has malice attached to it. Finally, if
only the malice and not the act itself is permitted, then permission does not
regard a power indifferent to both opposites, but a power for one thing—namely,
evil—which is determined in fact. But the latter is absurd.*

I answer, 1. Although the major premise can be conceded without harm to our
cause, the Jesuit proves it badly. For the permission of which Scripture speaks
is not the bare non-prevention of an evil action, but is posited by theologians
and Scholastics as a sign of the divine will. It therefore connotes a positive
act of the divine will. Nor is permitting in the Scriptures formally a failure
to prevent the bare entitative act. I do not deny that the act is permitted
insofar as, in sin, GOD withdraws the gracious influence by which the act would
be done; nevertheless, malice itself is rather that which GOD formally and per
se permits through the negation of efficacious grace. The act is said to be
permitted connotatively and *kat' allo*—in another respect—on account of the
malice. For the statement *GOD permitted the nations to walk in their own ways*
is the same as saying that GOD permitted them to walk in their own evil and
criminal ways. This was a punishment divinely inflicted upon the nations,
either materially, as in the first permission, or formally, when GOD permitted
the nations to sin with reference to the demerits of their previous dissolute
life. But simply to walk in the ways of pure nature and to perform physical
acts is in no way a punishment, nor is being permitted to act in this way a
punishment. Rather, it is a benefit of the first cause commonly granted to
secondary causes. Nor does Scripture ever say that GOD permits sin except on
account of the malice attached to the act. I add that what GOD effects by
positive efficiency as the first cause, and therefore wills by the will of
complacency because he wills the entitative being and good effected by himself,
GOD does not permit. But GOD effects by positive efficiency as the first
cause—and therefore wills by the will of complacency with which he wills
entitative good—the bare material act of sin. Therefore GOD does not properly
permit the bare material act of sin. The major premise is certain, because GOD
is not

both the permitter—and thus the hater and avenger—and the effector of the same
thing. The minor premise is evident, because the mere and bare material act of
sin is an entitatively good act and a positive entity. Therefore it is
effected and willed by GOD. 2. The Jesuit argues in vain from human permission
to GOD's permission, for human beings do not permit sin by denying efficacious
grace through which an act of obedience would be done, as GOD permits sin. 3.
It is false that in permission GOD bears himself as one who removes a
hindrance. For this idle permission is a fiction of the sophists refuted above.
And if GOD here were only the one who removes a hindrance and an accidental
cause, he would be an accidental cause of the material acts of sin, and
universal concurrence would be an accidental cause. But in that case he would
be an accidental cause of supernatural acts as well, for GOD concurs with
supernatural acts by permitting—that is, by not violating free choice and not
preventing it from eliciting this act or the contrary one. This is the
adversaries' permission. 4. It is also false that GOD has no power to prevent
the formal character of malice alone from following upon a freely conceived
material action. GOD could have added grace to the material act, by which the
act would be qualified and elevated to a supernatural act, and in this way
could have prevented the formal character of malice alone. He could have made
Adam's act of eating a good act. But it is no wonder that GOD could not prevent
formal malice alone from following the material action of hatred of GOD,
because to prevent malice from following an act of hatred of GOD involves a
contradiction: an act of hatred of GOD essentially includes malice. Given that
the material act is forbidden, it is contradictory that GOD could make it not
forbidden. It is contradictory that GOD prevent malice from following the act
of eating the forbidden fruit, because the act of eating forbidden fruit
essentially includes malice. The Jesuit therefore says the same here as if he
said that GOD lacked the power to prevent a human being from being a rational
animal or twice three from being six. But if GOD were to qualify by grace the
material act that was going to underlie malice, giving a human being the grace
by which he would produce a gracious act—as GOD is able—he could in this way
prevent formal malice alone. For no sane person would deny that GOD could give
all human beings grace so abundant that all their acts would be made gracious
and pleasing to GOD. 5. It is also false that GOD

has power to prevent malice only insofar as he can prevent the material act
from which malice inseparably follows, because GOD can prevent malice in
another way than by preventing the material act. The bestowal of efficacious
grace is the formal mode of preventing malice. Yet it is not the formal mode of
preventing physical acts, but rather of advancing and elevating them to the
state of supernatural obedience. Nor is this consequence valid: GOD cannot
prevent the malice inseparably attached to the act except insofar as he
prevents the act; therefore the material act is what permission regards more
especially. For the antecedent is true of a forbidden material act, and the
consequence resembles this one: GOD cannot prevent the existence of a rational
animal except insofar as he prevents the existence of a human being. Yet it
does not follow from this that GOD properly and per se prevents the existence
of a human being, and only secondarily, improperly, and immediately prevents
the existence of a rational animal. 6. What he deduces from our doctrine is
also false and plainly does not follow. He says that it follows that a human
being does not commit but only permits sin when he elicits the material act of
sin without intending its malice, but rather grieving that such an action has
malice attached to it. But by the very fact that he elicits a forbidden act,
even if he does not intend the malice, he commits sin with a true causality,
not by bare permission. Because he is subject to the law, he performs an act
contrary to the law and efficaciously elicits and chooses an evil and forbidden
act. And the bare wish and ineffectual volition by which, with mere
wishfulness, he wishes that malice not be attached to the act no more excuses
the perpetrator and makes him a bare permitter than a woman who prostitutes
her body to someone other than her husband would be excused if, meanwhile, she
wished that adultery were not prohibited by GOD's Law and that her husband not
be indignant that she committed such an act. Would mere wishes really free
criminals from a crime in this way? 7. We deny that permission in act regards
a power indifferent to opposites. For in that case, when the will in fact and
actually determines itself to an impious action, it would not actually
determine itself to one permitted by GOD. Nor does actual determination to
evil exclude permission of evil; rather, it wholly includes it. Thus, when
permission is actual, determination to evil is actual, and the positing and
actual existence of permission posits—not causally, but consequently—the
removal of every indifference to opposites and posits determination to

evil. Nothing, therefore, is weaker or more false than this argument.

17. Ruiz. *The material works of sins, even when the formal character of malice
is abstracted from them, are not the good works that GOD foreknew he would do
in the children of grace. Therefore GOD did not predestine them.*

[Marginal reference: In the same work, section 4, figure 7.]

I answer, 1. The whole statement is conceded, for the text in Ephesians 2
concerns only morally and supernaturally good works, which GOD ordained as
means of election. 2. Material works of sins, with the formal character of
malice abstracted from them, are not evil. Otherwise, on the Jesuits' view,
GOD would be the author of evil actions through a truly causative universal
concurrence, as the adversaries teach. They are entitatively good. Therefore
they are effects of the divine decree; but the things GOD decreed to be done,
he decreed to be done determinately. 3. According to Suarez, many free acts,
such as the generation of Mary's parents and their consent to marriage, are
absolutely decreed by GOD. Yet they are not means of predestination according
to this text.

Ruiz. *The Cainite heretics in Epiphanius, when they were about to undertake
wicked actions, used to say, “O you Angel, I use your work; O you power, I
perform your action.” Imagine someone about to undertake the material action
of blasphemy, perjury, or murder speaking thus: “O GOD, I use your work; O
supreme power, I perform your action.” Is this prayer consistent with piety
because the first cause concurs?*

[Marginal reference: Disputation 32, section 4, reference 10; Epiphanius, book
1, heresy 38.]

I answer: let a Jew about to undertake the killing of CHRIST, by which the
redemption of the world was accomplished, say: *“O GOD, I perform the material
act of killing your Son, and I do not need your work. I do it against your will
and without your willing it, while you look on idly. In you I neither move nor
live; nothing in this action is yours, and nothing is to be attributed to your
providence when immaculate innocence suffers things so cruel and atrocious.”*
It is no more absurd for someone performing an act abstracted from malice to
use GOD's work than it is for us to use GOD's work and need his aid in walking,
speaking, writing, eating, drinking, going into the city, and trading, as James
says. 2. The Cainites will call their crimes the works of Angels. The Jesuit
knows that we name crimes the works of the Devil and the sinner, not of GOD.
Even a sinner who follows the adversaries says: *“O GOD, I use your work, which
truly needs the causative influence of your indifferent and universal
concurrence.”*

Ruiz. *What can by no reasoning be made honorable, no will, even an uncreated
will, can honorably will for any end, however good. But the material and real
volition of destroying GOD, if one could, and*

*of blaspheming him as far as one can, can by no reasoning be made honorable,

therefore he cannot will what is contrary to love, however much it is abstracted
from the formal character of malice. This is confirmed: whatever is contrary
to the nature of willing is repugnant to the divine will. But it is contrary
to the nature of willing that someone should will a human being knowingly to
perform works contrary to justice and continence, and yet not will him to
contract malice and a depraved habit of injustice and intemperance.* Thus
Thomas says: *“For if someone wills a cause from which he knows such an effect
will follow, it is consequent that he wills such an effect,”* and so forth.
Likewise: *What is per se and most immediately directed to an end no one can
will unless he efficaciously wills that same end. But to predetermine the
material action of hatred of GOD is something per se and most immediately

action of heating without efficaciously willing heat to be produced. To will a
means per se is by that very fact virtually to will the end.*

[Marginal reference: Section 3, references 3, 4, 6, and 6.]

I answer, 1. The whole first argument involves a contradiction, because the
volition of destroying and blaspheming GOD is a volition prohibited by GOD's
law and not a bare material act. Therefore it is contradictory to abstract the
formal character of malice from an act that formally includes malice. This
would be a strange and contradictory abstraction. 2. The physical act present
in blasphemy, insofar as it abstracts from its object—namely GOD, for the
object specifies the act and specifically contracts it to evil—differs greatly
from the act of blaspheming GOD. The former act can be made honorable, because
no entitative act is essentially evil, and malice and goodness are accidents
of physical or entitative acts. Thus there is no physical act that cannot be
made honorable. But it is no wonder that a formally dishonorable act, such as
the volition of blaspheming GOD, cannot be honorably desired, however much it
is abstracted from malice. For it involves a contradiction to consider an act
that includes malice as an act that does not include malice. 2. We acknowledge
that it is contrary to reason for GOD to will a human being to perform the
material works of injustice and not contract malice or the habit of injustice.
If the discussion concerns GOD's will of good pleasure, and also the physical
volition that belongs to GOD as the first cause and Governor of the world, we
also concede

[Chapter XXVI, Part C; printed page 424; IA PDF page 482; BSB canvas 476]

that GOD wills with such a will that the human being contract malice. But if
the discussion concerns GOD's will of sign and approbation, we teach that GOD
does not will the material acts of sin with such a will; and it does not follow
from this that GOD wills malice with such a will. 3. I say that GOD can desire
the act without desiring the malice, because the malice follows the act not per
se but accidentally, insofar as the sinner abuses the material act contrary to
reason and GOD's Law, qualifying it with malice. Nor does Thomas prove anything
to the contrary. 4. The Jesuit labors in vain to prove that GOD wills the
material act of sin because he efficaciously predetermines someone to it. We
teach precisely that, and it does not follow that GOD wills the malice.
