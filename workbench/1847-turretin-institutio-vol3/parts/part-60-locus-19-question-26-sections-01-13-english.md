QUESTION XXVI.

ON THE MEANING OF THE SACRAMENTAL WORDS.

Are the words of the Supper to be understood properly and literally, *kata to
rhēton* (κατὰ τὸ ῥητὸν), or tropically and sacramentally? We deny the former
and affirm the latter against the papists and Lutherans.

I. Since this question is laid down as the foundation of the other
controversies of this locus that depend on its interpretation—such as the
controversies concerning Christ’s bodily presence and oral eating in the
Supper, transubstantiation, the sacrifice of the Mass, the carrying about and
adoration of this sacrament, and others of that kind—it requires all the
greater examination and must be treated all the more attentively.

[Marginalia: State of the question.]

II. To establish the state of the question, it must be observed, 1. that the
words of the Supper are of two kinds: some are words of command and others of
promise. The words of command prescribe the observance and end of this
sacrament: “Take, eat, drink; do this in remembrance of me.” The words of
promise are added to the element to declare its office and use so that it
becomes a sacrament: “This is my body, which is delivered or broken for you.”

[1847 vol. III printed p. 397 | IA leaf 431 | PDF p. 431]

“This is my blood of the New Testament, or this cup is the New Testament in my
blood, which is poured out for you and for many for the remission of sins.”
The question here is not properly about the words of command, but about the
words of promise and their meaning: how are they to be understood according
to Christ’s intention?

III. 2. The meaning of Scripture is twofold: proper or literal, and tropical
or figurative. The proper meaning is that which words yield when taken in
their proper and native signification and insofar as they are naturally
predicated of one another. The tropical meaning occurs when words are changed
from their native signification into another and, joined by some trope, are
predicated of one another. Some also call this meaning literal, not
immediately but mediately, insofar as it is referred back to proper words and
resolved into them according to the speaker’s intention and the nature of the
subject itself. The former is commonly called *to rhēton* (τὸ ῥητὸν), “what is
said,” and the latter *hē dianoia* (ἡ διάνοια), “the intended meaning.” It is
certain that one or the other applies to and must be assigned to the words of
promise in the Supper; which one it is, however, is disputed. The papists and,
with them, the Lutherans contend that *to rhēton* (τὸ ῥητὸν), or the proper
and literal meaning strictly so called, must be retained, but they do not
agree among themselves about what that literal meaning is. The orthodox
affirm that *tēn dianoian* (την διάνοιαν), or the tropical and figurative
meaning, must be followed. Again, since a proposition may be proper or
figurative either in all its parts or only in some of them, the papists, who
insist that the literal meaning must be tenaciously retained here, are bound
to prove that all the parts of the proposition “This is my body” are to be
expounded in a proper and literal sense. But because we maintain that this
proposition is only partly figurative, it is enough for us to show that even
one of its parts is tropical. Thus the question is reduced to these terms:
“Are the sacramental words of promise, ‘This is my body; this is my blood,’
and so forth, to be understood properly and *kata to rhēton* (κατα τὸ ῥητὸν),
or tropically and *kata tēn dianoian* (κατα την διάνοιαν)?” The papists affirm
the former; we affirm the latter, supported by various reasons.

[Marginalia: The tropical meaning is proved. 1. From the words themselves.]

IV. The first reason is drawn from the very words of promise, because these
words cannot bear a proper meaning but necessarily require a tropical and
figurative one. To make this plain, each part of the proposition—the subject,
predicate, and copula—and what each signifies must first of all be weighed.
Once this has been established, it will easily be perceived what judgment
should be made about the whole proposition, whether it is tropical or proper.
First comes the subject, expressed by the demonstrative pronoun “this.” We
maintain that it can refer to nothing other than the bread, and therefore the
proposition “this is my body” must be resolved as “this bread is my body.”
There are various reasons. (1) It is a demonstrative pronoun, which points out
a present and therefore actually existing thing, as Bellarmine himself
testifies in book 1, chapter 27, *On the Mass*. But nothing was present as yet
except the substance of bread, nor could Christ point out anything else. (2)
The sequence of the discourse proves that it can designate nothing other than
what Christ had taken, broken after giving thanks, and visibly offered broken
to the disciples to eat. This was nothing other than bread: “The Lord took
bread, and after giving thanks broke it and gave it to the disciples, and
said, ‘Take, eat; this is my body.’” Who does not see that the pronoun is here
put in place of the noun that preceded it and designates nothing other than
the bread? The meaning is: “The bread that I took and broke and offered you to
eat—this bread is my body.” Although Christ gave blessed and not ordinary
bread, it does not follow that it was anything other than true bread, because
the change produced by the blessing is not a change of substance but of use.
(3) The *to hoc* (τὸ “Hoc”), or “this,” in the first symbol must be expounded
in the same way as in the second, because the account of each is the same; but
in the second it refers

[1847 vol. III printed p. 398 | IA leaf 432 | PDF p. 432]

manifestly to the cup, as Luke and Paul explain. What Matthew and Mark have as
*touto esti to haima mou* (τοῦτο ἐστὶ τὸ αἷμά μου), “this is my blood,” they
interpret of the cup: *touto to potērion hē kainē diathēkē en tō haimati mou*
(τοῦτο τὸ ποτήριον ἡ καινὴ διαθήκη ἐν τῷ αἵματί μου), “This cup is the New
Testament in my blood.” (4) Paul interprets *to hoc* (τὸ hoc), or “this,” of
the bread in 1 Corinthians 10:16: “The bread that we break.” Here he manifestly looks back to
the history of the institution. What the evangelists had said—that the Lord
took bread, gave thanks, broke it, gave it, and said, “Take, eat”—he compresses
into these words: “The bread that we break.” Likewise, he expounds the words
that follow, “It is my body,” by this circumlocution: “It is the communion of
the body of Christ.” This is confirmed by the preceding words concerning the
chalice: “The chalice that we bless.” He means a chalice not of blood but of
wine, because we do not bless and consecrate the Lord’s blood but wine.

Nor should it be said here either that “bread” is put for the accidents of
bread, because, besides its being assumed without proof that the accidents of
bread can be separated from the substance of bread—a claim that will be
proved utterly false in its place—this would not be a genuine but an abusive
use of the word, since the accidents of bread are not bread, nor will it ever
be proved that Scripture speaks in this way; or that “bread” is taken for the
body of Christ, both because that would make the expression tropical, which
they refuse to admit, and because the body of Christ is not broken, and also
because the proposition would become not only indicative rather than
operative, but absurd as well: “The body that we break is the communion of the
body of Christ.” (5) By *to hoc* (τὸ “hoc”), or “this,” Christ meant either
his body or something else
distinct from his body. If the former, it follows that transubstantiation had
already taken place when he said “this,” and consequently that it is not
effected through the words “This is my body,” as they maintain. It will also
follow that, according to their position, the words are not operative and
practical, but speculative. If, however, he means not his body but something
distinct from it, the meaning of Christ’s proposition would be, “This, which
is not my body, is my body.” That cannot be true properly and literally, but
only sacramentally and figuratively, in this sense: “This bread, which is not
my body, is my body,” namely insofar as it is the sacrament of his body. (6)
On the adversaries’ hypothesis, this proposition, which converts and
consecrates, must necessarily contain both terms of the conversion and their
order, for in every conversion there are two terms, that from which and that
to which. But the term to which is contained in the predicate of the
proposition through *to Corpus meum* (τὸ “Corpus meum”), or “my body.”
Therefore, the term from which must
be contained in the subject, which is the pronoun “this.” The term from which
is bread; therefore “this” points out the bread. (7) If the pronoun “this”
does not point out the bread, the bread is not that whose substance is
converted into Christ’s body by the power of these words, and consequently
transubstantiation is false. But if it is the bread whose substance is
converted, then our exposition, in which we say that bread is pointed out by
“this,” is true. (8) Not a few papists acknowledge with us that *to hoc* (τὸ
“hoc”), or “this,” designates bread. Gerson says in book 4, *Against the Florentines*: “The
pronoun *this* points out the substance of bread.” So do Bonaventure, on book
4 of the *Sentences*, distinction 8, question 1, and Bellarmine, book 3,
chapter 19, *On the Mass*: “The Lord took bread in his hands, blessed it, and
said of it, ‘This is my body.’” Arnauld himself, in volume 2, page 162 and
following of *Perpetuity*, acknowledges more than once that “this” signifies
bread, though bread conceived confusedly. How perplexed the adversaries are
here when they define what is to be understood by the pronoun “this”—so that
there are almost as many opinions as heads—will be seen in what follows, and
the emptiness of the fictions they have devised here will be shown.

V. Second, the predicate of the proposition is the body of Christ—not his
mystical body, which is the church (Colossians 1:24; 1 Corinthians 12:27), but
the natural and proper body he assumed from the blessed Virgin. Nor is it
considered simply

[1847 vol. III printed p. 399 | IA leaf 433 | PDF p. 433]

as such, or as living and glorious in heaven, but as dead or shortly to die,
for here the past is put for the near future, as often elsewhere. Under this
aspect Scripture commonly speaks of Christ’s body. Nor is it ever mentioned
under another *schesei* (σχέσει), or relation, without something being added
to signify this, as in Philippians 3:21, where Paul speaks of “Christ’s
glorious body.” It would not be difficult to prove this from innumerable
passages of Scripture in which Christ’s body is discussed under this relation
(Matthew 27:58; Mark 15:43; Luke 23:52; John 19:40; Romans 7:4; Colossians
1:22; Hebrews 10:5, 10). Many points prove that the body
of Christ must be understood this way in the present passage. (1) Christ’s
body and blood are treated separately, as divided and separated, which can
refer to nothing other than the state of death. (2) The distinct institution
and distribution of the symbols teaches the same thing, namely that the two
parts they represent must be regarded here not as united in a living man, but
as separated and torn from each other by death. Otherwise the symbols that
represent them to us as separated would deceive us—as would Christ himself,
who willed to institute them in this way, which it is impious even to think.
(3) The following words confirm this when the body is described as one that
was given, that is, exposed or shortly to be exposed to death for us in
Scripture’s ordinary style, and, as Christ himself explains, “broken,” namely
on the cross; and when the blood is considered not as still in the veins, but
as poured out and scattered outside the body. (4) The body and blood are
presented here as the efficacious means both of redemption and the remission
of sins and of spiritual nourishment. This can be said only of the dead
Christ, since nowhere but in death, which was the price of our redemption, did
he acquire for us the remission of sins and salvation (Ephesians 1:7; Romans
5:10; Colossians 1:20) and become for us the bread and food of life (John
6:51).

VI. Third, the copula “is” connects the subject with the predicate, but in the
way in which they can be connected: properly, if they can be predicated of one
another, but tropically, if proper attribution cannot occur, as with disparate
things. Thus this substantive verb is taken either *hyparktikōs* (ὑπαρκτικῶς)
and *ousiōdōs* (οὐσιωδῶς), existentially and essentially, in its proper and
native signification, when there is identity of supposit or subject—as when a
genus is predicated of a species, a species of an individual, or a property of
a subject—or *sēmantikōs* (σημαντικῶς), significatively and figuratively, when
things cannot properly be predicated of one another but only by analogy and
similarity. A trope is therefore rightly placed in the copula, not insofar as
it is simply a copula, but insofar as it is a word that has its own particular
signification, from which its power of connecting results.

For just as a sign has two kinds of being—one primitive and absolute, which
is nothing other than the substance of the thing itself, and the other
relative and significative, by which that substance becomes a sign and in a
manner assumes a relative nature, as a stone made a boundary has absolute
being insofar as it is a stone and significative and relative being insofar
as it is a boundary, and water in baptism has absolute being insofar as it is
water and relative being insofar as it is a sacrament—so the substantive verb
that connects the sign with the thing signified must connect it not in respect
of absolute being but in respect of relative and sacramental being. So far is
this relative being from destroying the natural and absolute being of the
thing that, on the contrary, it necessarily presupposes it. For if the thing
did not have its natural being, it could not have relative being, since it
would not exist at all. Thus the water of baptism could not be the sacrament
of Christ’s blood unless it had the substance of water and the power to wash
away filth.

VII. From this arises the very common manner of speaking in every language by
which the thing signified is predicated of the sign, and the name of the former
is given to the latter. Augustine observes this in book 3, question 57 of his
*Questions on Leviticus*: “The thing which

[1847 vol. III printed p. 400 | IA leaf 434 | PDF p. 434]

signifies is customarily called by the name of the thing it signifies, as it
is written, ‘The seven heads of grain are seven years,’ and as it is said, ‘The
rock was Christ.’ For it did not say, ‘The rock signifies Christ,’ but spoke as
though it were what it certainly was not in substance, but was by
signification.” Nothing is more common in ordinary speech; nothing occurs more
frequently in the Scripture of both the Old and New Testaments. Hence “seven
cows” are called “seven years” (Genesis 41:26); the “bones” are called “the
house of Israel” (Ezekiel 37:11); “four beasts are four kings,” and “ten horns
are ten kings” (Daniel 7:17, 23, 24; 8:20, 21); and so everywhere else.
Although the substantive verb is not supplied in these and similar passages
because the Hebrews lack it, interpreters acknowledge that it must
nevertheless be supplied, and so it is supplied almost everywhere in the
Septuagint and the Vulgate. The same occurs very frequently in the New
Testament. Christ uses no other manner of speaking in the parables (Matthew
13:38–39): “The field is the world; the good seed are the children of the
kingdom; the enemy is the devil; the harvest is the end of the world; the
reapers are the angels,” and so forth, to indicate that they were signs of the
things or persons they represented and were predicated of one another because
of that analogy. Thus Revelation 1:20 says, “The seven stars are seven angels;
the seven lampstands are seven churches,” and chapter 17 says, “The seven
heads are seven mountains” (verse 9), “The ten horns are ten kings” (verse
12), and “The woman whom you saw is the great city that has dominion over the
kings of the earth” (verse 18), and so in innumerable passages.

But this applies particularly and far more strongly where sacraments are
concerned, since they are not simple signs but seals that on God’s part seal to
believers the truth of the things they represent and, indeed, in their own way
most certainly exhibit those things in lawful use, as will be seen below.

VIII. From what has thus been established about each part of this proposition,
it necessarily follows that the proposition itself is not proper but
figurative. (1) One disparate thing cannot be predicated properly of another,
but only figuratively, as the philosophers consistently teach and Bellarmine
himself does not deny in book 3, chapter 19, *On the Eucharist*, because one
thing cannot be said to be another except by a trope. But it is certain that
the bread pointed out by “this,” as stated, and Christ’s body are disparate,
since the substance of bread is not and cannot be called the substance of
Christ’s body, nor the substance of the cup the substance of Christ’s blood;
they truly differ from each other in number, definition, essence, and
attributes. Not a few papists have acknowledged this. Salmerón, volume 9,
treatise 20, says, “If Christ had said, ‘This bread is my body,’ we would be
compelled to resort to a figure.” Bellarmine, book 1, chapter 7, *On the
Eucharist*, says: “This proposition, ‘Bread is my body,’ is utterly absurd and
impossible unless it is taken figuratively, that is, as meaning that the bread
signifies the body.”

Some object here in vain that the predications concerning Christ the
*Theanthrōpos* (Θεανθρώπῳ), “God is man” and “man is God,” have without
question been regarded as proper and natural, although deity and humanity are
disparate substances. For although deity and humanity in the abstract and
outside the hypostatic union are disparate natures and cannot be predicated of
one another, so that it cannot be said that deity is humanity or, conversely,
humanity is deity, nevertheless God and man in the concrete are not disparate,
because they do not denote two supposits or two persons but one and the same
person. Just as that person subsists in two natures, so he is denominated in
different ways from them. Thus when it is said, “God is man,” nothing else is
indicated than that the person who is called God from the divine nature also
is and is rightly called man from the human nature, which is hypostatically
united with the divine. But the account here is entirely different, because
bread and Christ’s body are wholly different substances and therefore cannot
be predicated of one another except figuratively. (2) This is even more
strongly confirmed by the fact that the body, as stated, is understood here
not as living and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 401 | IA leaf 435 | PDF p. 435]

glorious, but as dead and crucified. Yet it is utterly impossible and plainly
contradictory for bread or any other substance to be properly and without a
figure Christ’s dead body at a time when Christ is not dead and cannot die,
because it would follow that Christ’s body is simultaneously dead and alive,
that is, that it both exists and does not exist. Everyone sees that this is
*asystaton* (ἀσύστατον), incoherent. Again, Christ’s body is presented not only
as dead but also as the mystical nourishment of our soul. It cannot be such by
a real conversion of bread into body or wine into blood, but by its
efficacious representation, insofar as it is presented to us as the object of
our faith and takes on the idea of the bodily food and nourishment that we
receive in the sacrament because of the likeness it bears to it. For this
reason he calls the bread his body. (3) Every proper predication occurs when a
genus, species, property, or accident is predicated of a subject, for nothing
can properly be affirmed of another that does not belong to it in one of these
ways. But Christ’s body is neither the genus, species, property, nor accident
of bread. (4) If bread is properly called Christ’s body, it will follow:

1. That all the attributes of Christ’s body properly belong to bread, such as
being born, dying, rising again, and so forth, and all the attributes of bread
belong to the body, such as being sown, reaped, threshed, baked, and so forth.

2. That when Christ took and broke bread from the table, he took and broke his
own body and poured out his blood before he was crucified; and that we now
break Christ’s body and pour out his blood today after Christ has risen and is
in the state of glory, where he can no longer die.

3. That the bread was broken and the wine poured out for us for the remission
of sins.

4. No predication of the thing signified concerning the sign can be proper; it
must be tropical, because it is made not according to the thing’s absolute and
proper being but according to its relational and significative being. Such is
the predication in the Supper, because bread and wine, as all agree, are signs,
while body and blood are the things signified. Indeed, unless this proposition
is expounded in this way, it will not be sacramental and the nature of the
sacraments will be overthrown, because signs will no longer be signs.

[Marginalia: 2. Class of arguments from Christ’s purpose and the circumstances
of the institution.]

IX. The second class of arguments is drawn from Christ’s purpose, the
disciples’ condition, and the circumstances of the whole institution. These
lead us to the tropical and sacramental meaning in such a way that they are
incompatible with the proper and literal meaning. Christ’s purpose was
twofold: the general purpose was to institute the sacrament of the New
Testament, as is said, “This is the New Testament in my blood”; the particular
purpose was to substitute a new rite, the Holy Supper, for the old rite, the
paschal lamb. From this arises a twofold argument. (1) Words are to be taken
according to the speaker’s intention and purpose; but Christ’s purpose in the
institution was to ordain the sacrament of the New Testament. Therefore the
words must also be understood sacramentally and tropically. (2) If the Supper
was to be substituted for the Passover, the same tropical manner of speaking
that had a place in the latter, as is evident, also had to be retained here.

The circumstances, both antecedent and consequent, prove the same. (1)
Christ’s body reclined at the table in the first Supper; therefore it could
not be offered to the disciples, but the bread placed on the table could be.
(2) Christ separated bread and wine in the Supper, but no separation of body
and blood had then taken place. (3) He took bread from the table, blessed it,
broke it, and gave it to the disciples. He did or said none of these things
concerning his body. (4) Christ himself ate from that bread and drank from the
cup: “I will not drink again from this fruit of the vine.” But who would say
that he ate and drank his own body and blood? The subsequent circumstances
point the same way. (1) The Supper was instituted in remembrance of the absent
Christ; therefore bread and wine must be symbols and memorials of his body and
blood, and thus can only tropically be called Christ’s body and blood. (2) For

[1847 vol. III printed p. 402 | IA leaf 436 | PDF p. 436]

Christ wills that his death be proclaimed by the celebration of the Holy
Supper until he comes; but his death cannot be proclaimed in the Supper except
symbolically, nor can the one who is to come only on the last day properly be
said to be present in the symbols, but only sacramentally.

X. The consideration of the disciples whom Christ addressed also belongs
here. They could give these words no meaning other than a tropical one. (1) At
the same time they saw the Lord’s body reclining at the table and the bread he
had taken as things separate from each other in both nature and place, so that
they could not but conceive very different ideas of them. (2) They had often
heard the Lord teach figuratively; by his frequent parables he had accustomed
them to this manner of speaking, which could only be understood tropically and
by analogy, as in Matthew 13 and elsewhere. And if they sometimes took such
expressions properly, they knew that Christ had corrected them, as when he
spoke of the leaven of the Pharisees (Matthew 16:11) and of the food he had to
eat by doing his Father’s will (John 4:34). (3) The time in which they found
themselves and the business they were conducting were mystical and full of
various figures. For in the sacrament of the Passover that Christ was
celebrating, this tropical expression occurred everywhere, as already stated,
and its idea presented itself to their minds in such a way that they could
easily understand that Christ’s words in the sacrament he wished to substitute
for the Passover were not to be understood otherwise.

This was so not only because the lamb was everywhere called the Passover and
the very body of the roasted lamb was called *gufo shel pesach* (גופו של פסח),
“the body of the Passover,” as the celebrated Buxtorf observes in section 250
of his *Exercise on the Holy Supper*, to which it is entirely likely that
Christ alluded, but also because the unleavened bread presented by the head of
the household was described as the bread of misery that had been eaten in
Egypt: “This is the bread of misery that our fathers ate in Egypt.” This
certainly could only be understood figuratively, because it was the symbol and
memorial of the unleavened bread they had eaten in Egypt. From all these
things it is clear that the apostles could give these words no meaning other
than the figurative one. Indeed, unless it had occurred to them that the bread
was figuratively called the body because it was the symbol of the body, how
could they have failed to be troubled by so astonishing a matter? In the
smallest difficulties they remain perplexed; they debate among themselves how
Christ was to go to the Father and question how he was to depart from the
world; they understand nothing of what is said about the heavenly Father. How
then could they have been so ready to believe what all reason rejects—that
Christ was reclining at the table in their sight and invisibly enclosed under
the bread? Therefore, since by eating the bread without hesitation they
testify to their assent, it is evident that they understood Christ’s words in
the same sense as we do, because what occurs in them should not seem unusual
in mysteries: the name of the thing signified is transferred to the sign.

[Marginalia: 3. Class of arguments from the nature and analogy of the
sacraments.]

XI. The third class is drawn from the nature and analogy of all the sacraments
of the Old and New Testaments. Both the nature of a sacrament requires a
tropical meaning and the analogy of all the sacraments consistently retains
it. First, as to the nature of a sacrament: since its formal account is placed
in signifying, and all signification is *ek tōn pros ti* (ἐκ τῶν πρός τί),
“of things related to something,” and denotes relative being, by that very
fact it involves figurative and not proper speech, as stated above. This is
confirmed by an induction from all the sacraments, on which its universality
depends. In the sacraments of the Old Testament, whether ordinary or
extraordinary, this kind of tropical predication is found in all of them.
Circumcision is called “God’s covenant” (Genesis 17:10), that is, the sign of
the covenant, as God himself explains (verse 11), and Paul (Romans 4:11). The
paschal lamb is called “the passover of Jehovah” (Exodus 12:11), that is, a
memorial of the passing over both

[1847 vol. III printed p. 403 | IA leaf 437 | PDF p. 437]

of the angel and of the people, according to God’s own declaration (chapter
13:9). The rock in the wilderness is called “Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4)
because it spiritually signified Christ, according to the apostle, who for
that reason calls it spiritual. The legal sacrifices and purifications are
called washings and expiations of sin (Leviticus 4:31; 14:7–8), that is,
types, shadows, and signs of that true expiation and washing to come through
Christ, as the Holy Spirit explains (Hebrews 8:5; 10:1). The Sabbath is called
“the covenant of Jehovah,” that is, the sign of the covenant, as God himself
explains (Exodus 31:13, 17). Baptism is called “the washing of regeneration”
(Titus 3:5) and the washing away of sins because it is the antitype of these
benefits of Christ, according to Peter’s declaration (1 Peter 3:21). From
these things we infer by parity that, since the account of predication in the
other sacrament cannot and ought not to be different, it too must be given a
tropical and not a proper meaning.

XII. With respect to circumcision, it should not be said either that *berith*
(ברית) does not properly denote “covenant” here, but a seal and visible mark
confirming the covenant that was made by the cutting of flesh, as covenants
were once confirmed by cutting animals. The proper signification of the word,
which is everywhere used for a covenant and nowhere for such a cutting,
opposes this, as do the very words of verse 11, where it is called “the sign of
the covenant.” Nor should it be said that it is called a covenant because it
was the condition of the covenant that God required from Abraham and his
descendants. Although God required circumcision as Abraham’s duty when he had
entered into covenant with him, he did not require it as the proper condition
of the covenant, which was faith and repentance (Genesis 12:4; 17:1), but as a
sign confirming the covenant both on God’s part and on Abraham’s, as God
expressly says in verse 11: “You shall circumcise the flesh of your foreskin,
and this shall be to you a sign of the covenant between me and you.”

Nor, finally, should it be said that it is properly called a covenant or
testament insofar as the word *testament* denotes the instrument of a
testament and the sign of a testament, while *testament* denotes the
testator’s will. It is clear that circumcision is called the same covenant of
which it is afterward called the sign; but it could not be called the sign of
the instrument of the testament, but of the testament or covenant itself.
Furthermore, it is assumed without proof that what is the instrument of the
testament is properly and without a trope called the testament. Who does not
see that the instrument of a covenant is metonymically called the covenant
because it contains the testator’s will, which consists of written words by
which the will is customarily signified publicly? Circumcision is a covenant
in the same sense, not because it contains the covenant, but because it
signifies it.

XIII. With regard to the rock, it is no better to say that it was Christ
himself and did not signify him sacramentally, so that the sense is obtained by
inverting the expression: “Christ was the rock.” It is argued both that it is
called “the spiritual rock” and that it is said to have followed the
Israelites, which cannot be said of the material rock but can very well be
said of Christ. Yet the words themselves prove the contrary, namely that the
material rock in the wilderness is meant, from which waters flowed for the
Israelites to drink. 1. The relative phrase *hē de petra* (ἡ δὲ πέτρα), “and
the rock,” indicates no rock other than that which he had just mentioned from
Moses (Exodus 17; Numbers 20), which was certainly the rock in the wilderness.
2. All are said to have drunk from that rock (verse 4), even those with whom
God was not pleased and whom he struck down. This cannot be said of Christ,
from whom whoever eats and drinks cannot perish. 3. The account of the manna
here is the same as the account of the rock. But the manna is understood not
spiritually and properly of Christ, but sacramentally and figuratively of the
material food on which they fed in the wilderness. Nor does it matter that
this rock is called spiritual, because this is said not according to the truth
of the thing but because of the mystery it signifies and “on account of the
intelligible signification of each,” as Augustine says everywhere, insofar as
it prefigured the pouring out of Christ’s blood on the cross. In this way
Lyra, Carthusianus, and Gagnée expound “The rock was Christ” as meaning that
it

signified him. As for the statement that the rock “followed” the Israelites,
this is not understood properly and in itself, as if the rock itself actually
followed them, but improperly and by its effect, namely by the water gushing
from the rock that was at hand for the Israelites, as Emanuel Sa and Menochius
observe. Or it is because it complied with them, so that the word
*akoloutheisēs* (ἀκολουθείσης) should be rendered “obeying,” and the sense is
that they drank from the rock as it complied with the desire of the Israelites
through the pouring out of waters, as Salmerón and Justinianus maintain after
Photius. The proposition cannot be inverted here so that Christ becomes the
subject and the rock the predicate. The apostle’s purpose is not to teach what
Christ was, but what that rock struck in the wilderness was, in order to show
that Christians have no prerogative over the Israelites that can exempt them
from God’s judgments, since the Israelites also had sacraments similar and
corresponding to ours by which Christ and his benefits were signified. This is
the apostle’s purpose.
