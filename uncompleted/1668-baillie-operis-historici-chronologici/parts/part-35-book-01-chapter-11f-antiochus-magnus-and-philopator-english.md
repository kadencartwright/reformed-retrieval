The young Seleucus was vigorous but imprudent. From his great impetuosity he
received the surname Ceraunus, “Thunderbolt.” He moved against Attalus of
Pergamum, who possessed a great part of Asia after Eumenes, but while crossing
the Taurus he was killed by his own commanders Apaturius and Nicanor. Achaeus,
a kinsman of Ceraunus, immediately avenged him upon these men and continued
his march against Attalus. So great were his prudence and courage that he soon
confined Attalus within Pergamum’s walls. When the army of Asia offered him
the diadem, he rejected it and summoned the young Antiochus—Ceraunus’s brother,
later surnamed the Great—from the upper provinces to his paternal throne.

In Egypt Euergetes was poisoned by his son Ptolemy Philopator, together with
his wife and another son. Philopator ascended the throne through the
abominable murder of his nearest relations and governed the kingdom most
wickedly for seventeen years. Immersing himself in pleasures, he devolved the
whole burden of the republic upon Sosibius and Agathocles. If Antiochus had
permitted it, they would have shrunk far from every war, but Antiochus’s
ambition disturbed their peace.

> Marginalia, printed p. 216: *Ptolemy Philopator kills his father*.

Antiochus was raised to the kingdom at age fifteen through the efforts of
Achaeus, and continued in it for forty-five years. When Seleucus Ceraunus
moved against Attalus, he left a wicked man named Hermias as administrator of
the rest of the kingdom. During Antiochus’s youth Hermias managed everything
according to his own pleasure. Achaeus afterward accepted, at Ptolemy’s
urging, the kingdom of western Asia which he had rejected at first. Theodotus
had made himself king of the Bactrians, just as Arsaces had of the Parthians.
But it was Molo’s rebellion that most greatly disturbed the court.

Molo, satrap of Media, observed the king’s youth and his counselors’
depravity. Joining his brother, the prefect of Persia, to himself,

he assumed the diadem and added Babylonia and various neighboring provinces
to his dominion. Hermias, the arbiter of Antiochus’s counsels, was a
small-minded man. Fearing for his own skin in a difficult war with Molo, he
devoted all his strength to a different and safer war against the effeminate
Ptolemy. Epigenes, however, a man as prudent as he was brave, recognized that
the prince’s presence was of the greatest importance against rebellious
subjects and strove to have Antiochus make his first campaign against Molo.
But Hermias, who directed the boy-king’s plans according to his own judgment,
arranged for Epigenes to be stabbed by suborned accusers because of his frank
admonitions. He then showed the king fabricated letters about treason from the
dead man to Molo. Once Epigenes had been removed, Hermias led the king against
Ptolemy. When they reached the narrows on the approach into Syria between
Lebanon and Anti-Lebanon, Theodotus the Aetolian, who held a fortress on each
side of the pass, easily shut out the king and his army.

> Marginalia, printed p. 217: *Hermias removes Epigenes*.

While Antiochus remained there for several months, Zinaetas, whom he had sent
against Molo at Hermias’s urging, was routed and killed in Mesopotamia.
Zinaetas and Molo had encamped with large forces on opposite banks of the
Tigris. By night Zinaetas led a great part of his men across the river. Molo
pretended to be afraid, left his camp full of wine and provisions, and made a
show of hastening toward Media in flight. Zinaetas ordered his men to feast
for one day in Molo’s camp and pursue the fugitives on the next. But that
night Molo returned to his camp and massacred Zinaetas together with his
troops, who were half buried in drunkenness. Many threw themselves into the
Tigris and were swallowed by its waters, and thus the whole army perished.

> Marginalia, printed p. 217: *Molo defeats Zinaetas*.

When news of the disaster flew to Antiochus, who was stationed before the
passes of Syria, even Hermias consented—for he could no longer resist the
plain evidence—and the camp was immediately moved into Mesopotamia. Antiochus
and Molo each hastened to be the first to reach Media. Their armies met on the
way, and most of Molo’s soldiers deserted to the king. Molo and his brother
killed themselves to escape torture. Molo’s flayed body was fixed to a cross
on the highest mountain in Media. From there the victorious army proceeded
against Ariobazanes, king of the Atropatians, who submitted to Antiochus. They
then advanced into Parthia. The Parthians declined battle, but Antiochus made
an alliance with their king, as also with the king of the Bactrians, after
receiving money and elephants. He did the same with Sophagasenus the Indian,
successor of Sandracottus and lord of many kingdoms, though not, as I think,
six hundred of them.

On the journey the physician Apollonius informed Antiochus of Hermias’s
depravity, arrogance, and hatred toward everyone. To the great joy of all,
Antiochus handed Hermias over to be stabbed by his guards. As he returned
home, letters arrived from Theodotus the Aetolian offering him the Syria which
Theodotus governed under Ptolemy. Offended by Philopator’s effeminate ways and
frightened by the courtiers’ plots against every man’s life, Theodotus had
decided to defect from him. Antiochus came at once upon being invited and
received most of Syria from Theodotus, who surrendered it. He found such ease
in this campaign that he raised his aspirations to occupying all Egypt.

Ptolemy sent envoys to Antiochus about peace. Meanwhile, however, Sosibius and
Agathocles secretly assembled large bands of mercenaries from the Greeks,
Gauls, and Thracians. Antiochus also prepared everything for a spring campaign,
and each side passed the winter in pretended peace negotiations. In his hopes
Antiochus had already devoured the effeminate Egyptians. But behold, at the
beginning of spring Ptolemy was dragged out of the brothels and came seeking
Antiochus in Syria with an army of seventy-four thousand brave men. Antiochus
did not refuse battle. Before the fight, while both kings had their camps at
Raphia, Theodotus, commander of Antiochus’s phalanx, attempted a deed of the
greatest daring and very nearly accomplished it. He knew Ptolemy’s court and
entire manner of life. Before dawn he entered the Egyptian camp with only two
companions, reached the king’s tent unrecognized, killed the king’s chief
physician, and wounded two others sleeping in the tent. Because the king was
sleeping outside the tent that night, Theodotus returned safely to his own
men.

> Marginalia, printed p. 217: *Theodotus’s daring deed*.

When the armies came to blows, both sides fought with great spirit and
strength. Arsinoe, Ptolemy’s sister and wife, rode about the squadrons and
added courage to the Egyptians by her speech and entreaties. Defeated,
Antiochus fled to Antioch and sought peace from Ptolemy. He easily obtained it
from a man delighted by his unexpected victory and turning all his thoughts
back to his former brothels and taverns.

> Marginalia, printed p. 217: *Ptolemy defeats Antiochus*.

Ptolemy did indeed recover all Syria and even Judaea by this victory. But
Christian Matthias’s claim that Ptolemy came to Jerusalem, was prevented by
the high priest when he tried to enter the Holy of Holies, led many thousands
of Jews in chains to Alexandria, and exposed them to be trampled by five
hundred elephants made drunk with wine mixed with incense is a poorly
fabricated tale of Matthias. As elsewhere, his carelessness often corrupts
much in history. Josephus does relate that Ptolemy Philometor, not Philopator,
exposed several thousand Alexandrian Jews to elephants. When, by a great
miracle, the beasts could not be provoked against them, the astonished king
spared the Jews.

> Marginalia, printed p. 217: *The fable of Matthias and Ussher*.

[Printed p. 218 | IA leaf 248 | access PDF 248 | OCR object 247]

But Matthias’s story about Philopator is an invention. It is true that Ussher
also stuffed the tale, greatly expanded, into his Chronicle, but he admits
that it was taken from the Third Book of Maccabees, regarded as apocryphal
even by the papists themselves. He adds that Philostorgius called that book
monstrous, since it was filled with monstrous fables.

Philopator, free from wars, poured himself wholly into luxury. He murdered his
incestuous wife Arsinoe and entrusted the government of the kingdom to the
prostitute Agathoclea, her brother Agathocles, and their mother Euanthe. At
last, his strength exhausted by pleasures, he died in the seventeenth year of
his reign, leaving Ptolemy Epiphanes, his five-year-old son by Arsinoe. No
sooner had Philopator died than the people of Alexandria rose against his
courtiers, dragged Agathocles, his sister, and his mother into the streets,
exposed them to every mockery, tortured them in various ways, and killed them.

> Philopator’s flatterers to death*.

Antiochus took up arms against his cousin Achaeus, who refused to lay down the
diadem. Joining his forces with Attalus, Antiochus stripped Achaeus of his
provinces and cities with little effort and besieged him after he was shut
within the citadel of Sardis. Achaeus treated the besiegers with contempt
because of the place’s strength, but his friends betrayed him. Ptolemy and
Achaeus’s other friends reached an agreement with Bolis the Cretan, one of the
besieging commanders who knew the citadel’s paths: for a large payment in hand
and a promise of more, he would bring Achaeus out of the citadel by night and
past Antiochus’s camp. Bolis pledged his solemn word, but—as one might expect
of a Cretan—revealed the secret to Antiochus. For a greater price and with
Cretan fidelity, he led Achaeus out of the citadel according to their
agreement and delivered him into Antiochus’s hands. Antiochus cruelly and
shamefully tortured Achaeus to death.

> Marginalia, printed p. 218: *Antiochus kills Achaeus after a Cretan betrays
> him*.

When Antiochus heard of Philopator’s death, he again aspired to the kingdom of
Egypt, filled with great hope of occupying it because Ptolemy Epiphanes was a
child. To obtain his desires more easily, he entered a treaty with Philip of
Macedonia to divide Ptolemy’s kingdoms between them.

As soon as the Roman name began to become known to the world, after their arms
had driven Pyrrhus out of Italy, Ptolemy Philadelphus sent a splendid embassy
to Rome and made a treaty of friendship with the Roman people. Euergetes
maintained that friendship, and the dying Philopator, fearing Antiochus,
appointed the Roman people guardians of his son and kingdom. The Romans most
readily accepted that guardianship, not so much from concern for Ptolemy and
the Egyptians as because they foresaw that it would furnish them a very great
opportunity to extend their own empire.

Antiochus first moved into Syria, where Scopas the Aetolian met him from Egypt
with a great army. Antiochus defeated Scopas and regained possession of Syria.
As he advanced farther into Egypt, Roman envoys met him and ordered him to
keep away from Egypt, which was under their guardianship. Not daring to
resist, Antiochus withdrew into Syria. Scopas had left a garrison in the
citadel of Jerusalem; the Jews expelled it to gain Antiochus’s favor. For this
service Antiochus became exceedingly friendly to the Jews and adorned the
temple with many gifts.
