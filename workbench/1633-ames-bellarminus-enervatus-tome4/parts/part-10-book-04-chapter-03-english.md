### Chapter III

#### On the Powers of Free Choice

Concerning natural powers in natural and civil matters, there is no
controversy between us and the Papists; nor in truth has Bellarmine rightly
framed any controversy about moral matters. For we grant “that a man can know
some moral truth by his own powers”: this is Bellarmine's first question. We
also grant “that a man without faith, with special assistance, and even without
it, can perform some moral good” (this is his second question), if “perform” is
taken for the production of the work itself considered in itself, without
respect to the principle from which it flows and the end to which it ought to
be referred. “Finally, we grant that man in the state of fallen nature
possesses free choice in choosing moral good and avoiding evil” (this is
Bellarmine's third question), if *some* good and *some* evil are understood in
the same sense in which the preceding question was explained. In truth, this
question does not differ at all from the former.

Concerning supernatural things which properly pertain to salvation, the
question is weighty, but it is treated with extraordinary calumny by
Bellarmine.

#### On the State of the Controversy

1. Bellarmine. *The question is whether, after the fall of the first man, man
truly possesses free choice in those things which pertain to salvation, so
that, although he can do nothing without the assistance of grace, yet, when
exci-*

[Printed p. 286 | Google Books PA286 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*ted by prevenient grace and helped by God, he is able so to operate that he
can also not operate.*

Protestant reply. These slippery phrases entangle the question more than they
explain it. For I. Under the phrase “truly possesses free choice,” the
questions and errors about the true nature of free choice which were noted
earlier in Bellarmine are mixed in here. II. The object of this question is not
designated distinctly enough: “in those things which pertain to salvation.”
For hearing or reading pertains to salvation, and yet this does not pertain to
the question. III. It has not yet been established what prevenient grace is or
of what kind. IV. That final phrase, “can operate and not operate,” can be
understood in two ways, as Álvarez rightly observes in disputation 119, number
2. First, the meaning may be that the power not to operate stands at the same
time in the same subject with the grace and assistance required for operating;
and this meaning is most true. Second, it may be understood to mean that the
grace and assistance given for such a particular operation, together with the
absence of such an act or the contrary act, can exist—and sometimes do exist—
simultaneously in the same subject. This is Bellarmine's meaning, and it is
false.

2. Bellarmine. *Calvin and Chemnitz rightly teach that our will is able to
effect absolutely no spiritual and saving good without the assistance of
God's grace; but they do not rightly attribute the contrary position to the
Catholics.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine joins himself to our men with altogether too much
familiarity in order to hide his own position. For I. Our men teach that a man
can effect no spiritual good before he has been regenerated; but Bellarmine
requires only the assistance of grace which persuades and stands by him. II.
Our men require that assistance for the spiritual good proper to those who
have been effectually called; but Bellarmine understands a general assistance
which is lacking to no one at the proper place and time. III. The contrary
position is most rightly attributed to Catholics such as Bellarmine: namely,
that they do not wish the good use of free choice to depend upon grace, but
upon the will itself.

3. Bellarmine. *Calvin and Chemnitz teach that in good works of piety our will
is acted upon by God in such a way that it cannot fail to act; and although it
also concurs actively, it nevertheless concurs as a mere natural instrument
of God, not as a free cause and mistress of its own act.*

Protestant reply. I. It was explained a little earlier how the will, when acted
upon by God, is rightly said to be able, and not to be able, not to act. II. It
is false that our men teach that the will is a *mere* instrument of the actions
which it produces, or even a *natural* instrument. III. We say that liberty is
not taken away by gra-*

[Printed p. 287 | Google Books PA287 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*ce, but perfected. IV. We do not deny that the will has dominion over its own
act; but we say that this dominion is subject and subordinated to the first and
absolute dominion of God. Bellarmine denies this.

The question is whether in supernatural things we are so acted upon by God's
grace that the actual efficacy of that grace is left to the choice of the
human will, to be determined by it. We deny this. Throughout this whole
disputation Bellarmine has been content merely to hint at the affirmative. For
in setting out the state of the controversy he states absolutely nothing,
carefully concealing a matter which he could not profess either chastely or
safely enough.

#### Bellarmine's Arguments

4. Bellarmine. *If in works of piety men properly did nothing through free
choice, but were merely led and moved as bare instruments, they ought not to be
called God's fellow workers and helpers. But they are so called. Therefore.*

Protestant reply. I. Here Bellarmine plays with a phantom fashioned by himself,
not with us, who acknowledge that his conclusion is true. II. It should
be observed that we admit the phrase “to act through free choice” if it means
that free choice acts as the *principle which* acts. But if it means that
natural liberty is the *principle by which* the spiritual action is performed,
and does not receive its ability and its willing from grace, we reject it in
that sense as Pelagian. III. A mere instrument is something so moved by another
that it does not have in itself the principle of such motion. Since we contend
that spiritual life must be communicated to men so that they may act
spiritually, Bellarmine was hardly of sound mind when he fastened upon us the
position that men are moved in works of piety as bare instruments. Conversely,
since he wishes men to be moved to supernatural operations without the
engendering of a supernatural principle, it necessarily follows from his
position that natural free choice is either a bare instrument of such
operations or—as comes nearer to his meaning—a cause that is principal without
qualification. IV. The phrases “to cooperate” and “helpers” are entirely
foreign to the present question. For men are nowhere so called with respect to
the supernatural operation or action to be elicited in them through saving
grace, but with respect to completing in others the work of ministry through
the grace which Bellarmine calls grace freely given, as in 1 Corinthians 3:9.
Yet the whole of this is attributed to grace in 1 Corinthians 15:10 and 2
Corinthians 3:5–6.

[Printed p. 288 | Google Books PA288 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

5. Bellarmine. *It would be foolish to command or urge works of piety upon
someone who did not possess freedom of choice. But God commands and urges
works of piety upon all men, and even conversion itself to God, about which
the adversaries contend most of all. Therefore.*

Protestant reply. I. We grant the whole: namely, that man possesses a natural
freedom of choice. But we deny that without the grace of regeneration man has
powers fit to perform spiritual works of piety. II. It should be observed that
Bellarmine understood well enough that this controversy is concerned precisely
with conversion to God, about which he says that we “contend most of all.” He
therefore sophistically acknowledges what he has done in this controversy,
since in framing the state of the question he made no mention of this
conversion, much less explained his position about it. III. On the matter
itself, we say that God commands these works of men: 1. To show the duty
incumbent upon them. 2. That they may be humbled by a sense of their own
deficiency. 3. That those among them who are elect may perform by grace what is
commanded of nature. 4. That the excuse of the rest may be removed. These
commands indicate that men are not moved as natural things but as causes
acting from deliberation—nothing more.

6. Bellarmine. *When Scripture says, “God works the willing and the
accomplishing,” and when we say to God, “Convert us,” the adversaries contend
that testimonies of this kind signify that God truly and properly performs and
works our good actions. Why, then, when Scripture says, “Work out your
salvation,” “Make yourselves a new heart,” and “Turn to me,” do they not allow
us to understand these same words in the same way and to say that we truly and
properly perform our good actions?*

Protestant reply. I. We most readily allow it to be said that we truly and
properly perform our good actions—but through the grace of God, not by the
powers of our own choice. II. Nevertheless, the same words cannot be
understood in the same manner when used by God and of men. For God does these
things as the cause principal without qualification; man does them as a
subordinate cause subject to the power and grace of God. God acts insofar as
he causes man to elicit his own action, but man is the supposit which elicits
its own action. God converts, while the other is converted; man is converted
by converting.

7. Bellarmine. *Scripture teaches that we are helped by God in works of piety.
But one who does nothing cannot properly be said to be helped; rather, one who
labors in acting can.*

Protestant reply. I. We grant the conclusion. II. This pagan argument tends to
substitute Jove as our God in place of Jehovah. It was therefore rightly
refuted long ago by Lactantius, *On False Religion*, book 1, chapter 11: “The
impiety of this name expresses not divine but human power. Cicero interprets
Jove and*

[Printed p. 289 | Google Books PA289 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*Juno as names derived from helping, and Jupiter as though he were a
helping father. This name is by no means suitable to God, because helping
belongs to a man who contributes some aid to another who is a stranger, and it
is a slight benefit. No one prays to God in this way, that he would help him,
but that he would preserve him and grant him life and salvation, which is far
greater and more than helping. And since we speak about a father, no father is
said to help when he begets or educates his children. For that is too slight a
word to express the greatness of a father's benefit. How much more unfitting
it is for God, who is the true Father through whom we exist, and whose
possession we are; by whom we are fashioned, animated, and illuminated; who
imparts life to us, grants salvation, and supplies manifold sustenance. A man
who thinks that he is merely helped by God does not understand the divine
benefits. Thus he is not merely unskilled but also impious, since by the name
Jove he diminishes the power of supreme authority.” III. There are four terms
in this syllogism unless Bellarmine can show from Scripture that “we are
properly helped by God” in the sense in which this word is customarily used by
the common people. In Scripture even one bereft of strength is said to be
helped. Job 26.

Bellarmine. 1. Romans 8:26: *The Spirit helps our infirmity. The word Paul
uses, συναντιλαμβάνεται, is properly said of those who, as though opposite one
another, take up a burden with their hands in order to aid one who labors.*

Protestant reply. I. How will Bellarmine prove that this word is used in that
proper sense in this passage? II. If its proper meaning is to be pressed
rigidly here, it would follow that our infirmities sustain one part of the
burden and the Spirit the other, which is utterly absurd. III. The manner in
which the Spirit helps is explained by the words which follow, “he intercedes
for us.” If the proper meaning has a place there, the whole is attributed to
the Spirit and absolutely nothing to us.

Bellarmine. 2. *In 1 Corinthians 15:10, the grace of God is said to have
labored with Paul.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine himself refutes himself here. For he adds, ἀλλὰ
ἡ χάρις τοῦ θεοῦ ἡ σὺν ἐμοί, which means literally, “but the grace of God
itself, which is with me.” II. What Bellarmine affirms—that God's grace labors
with us, κοπιάζειν—cannot properly be said unless we also wish to attribute
fatigue and weariness to God's grace; that this is the force of the word is
apparent from John 4:6.

8. Bellarmine. *If a man called by God can come and not come, he is without
doubt proved to possess free choice. But the former is true; Proverbs 1:24;
Matthew 23:37; John 6:67; Acts 7:51; Revelation 3:30. Therefore.*

Protestant reply. I. We grant the conclusion in the sense given many times
already. II. If any kind of calling is understood, the assumption is denied
with respect to one part: “he can come”—that is, by his own internal power. If
an effectually drawing call is understood, it is denied with respect to

[Printed p. 290 | Google Books PA290 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

the other part: “he can not come,” in the composite sense—just as though such
a call could be present without the act itself of coming following upon it. If
“being able to come and not come” is taken in another sense, the proposition
is denied as Bellarmine understands freedom of choice, so that it includes
indifference toward either alternative. Enough was said earlier in answer to
the passages of Scripture cited here.

9. Bellarmine. *If man did not possess free choice in works of piety, it would
follow that those who refuse to believe in Christ, refuse to turn from their
sins, or refuse to do good works for God's sake do not sin.*

Protestant reply. I. The conclusion is granted as above. II. The consequent
cannot appear absurd to Bellarmine, who in book 1, chapter 6 teaches that “the
assistance necessary for conversion is not present to sinners at every
moment,” and yet in chapter 8 contends that this same assistance is present to
all, “since without conversion they cannot avoid sin.” For these two claims
cannot be reconciled in any other way than by denying that those who refuse to
turn from their sins always sin. But if one who refuses to turn sometimes does
not sin, then that refusal in itself is not a sin. II. According to the
Pontificians, refusing in itself to do good works for God's sake is a sin only
on the rarest occasion. For the Jesuit Filincius, in treatise 22, number 186,
defines the time at which the command of charity toward God is binding and
concludes that between the first use of reason and the point of death “it is
binding at least every fifth year, because this time is left to the judgment
of the wise, and learned theologians—Soto, Ledesma, and Suárez—have so judged.”
From these words it also follows that whether it binds only once or twice from
the first use of reason to the very point of death is left to the judgment of
the wise—Soto, Ledesma, and Suárez—even if a man reaches one or two hundred
years. III. We nevertheless deny the consequence if free choice is understood
in the Bellarminian sense.

10. Bellarmine. *It would follow, next, that those who will to believe, hope,
love, be converted, and so forth are not praiseworthy or worthy of reward.*

Protestant reply. The consequent may be granted concerning the meritorious
worthiness of reward which the Pontificians claim for themselves. But
concerning the relation of praise and reward by grace, we say from logic that
the manner in which such a cause acts from deliberation is sufficient to
sustain that relation, although another causes him to act in that manner.

11. Bellarmine. *Third, in matters of this kind there would be no place for
precepts, counsels, exhortations, admonitions, and so forth.*

Protestant reply. I. This would indeed follow if God moved the will only by
real efficacy. But because at the same time he gently joins moral persuasion
to it, there is therefore not even an apparent semblance of this consequence.
II. Bel-*

[Printed p. 291 | Google Books PA291 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*larmine himself answers in book 2, chapter 15 that “predestination does not
hinder but confirms corrections, exhortations, and so forth, because God so
predestined men that they should attain the end through these means.” In the
same way, God's efficacious grace confirms exhortations, admonitions, and so
forth, because through these means it bends the will effectually and
certainly.

12. Bellarmine. *Fourth, all diligence and industry in increasing and
cultivating faith and piety would be removed from the scene.*

Protestant reply. I. This objection is the same as the preceding one and
admits the same answer. II. Here we truly apply to Bellarmine what, without any
reason, he asserts about his pope in *On the Roman Pontiff*, book 4, chapters 2
and 3. There he affirms that the pontiff cannot err in general decrees and yet
cannot define something rashly—that is, proceed without maturity and without
consulting learned men—or, as Bañez speaks in 2.2, question 1, article 10,
“cannot neglect due diligence.” We similarly affirm that it cannot happen that
those who are effectually moved by grace to increase faith and piety neglect
such diligence. For
Bellarmine's very defense of the infallibility of the pope's judgment is
sufficient to silence those outcries by which he strives to defame the
infallibility of God's grace. He will not say that a pope would not sin if he
defined something false in matters of faith or morals; or that popes who will
to define the truth are unworthy of praise or reward; or that precepts,
counsels, exhortations, admonitions, reproofs, examples, and other such things
have no place in a pope about to frame decrees of this kind; or that all the
pope's diligence and industry in defining matters of faith and morals are
removed from the scene; or finally, that the pope does not possess free choice
in conceiving them, although he cannot err but always defines the truth with
infallibility. Who, then, bewitched this man along with the other Pontificians,
so that they do not cease to burden the infallibility of divine grace with
calumnies of this kind?

13. Bellarmine. *Since the adversaries maintain that we act from necessity no
less in those things which pertain to conversion, faith, and piety than we are
determined by necessity to pursue good in general and flee evil; and since
they make us no freer in those things which pertain to conversion, faith, and
piety than in things pertaining to gifts of prophecy, tongues, and miracles,
let them explain, if they can, why they agree that sins and good works,
precepts and counsels, diligence and industry—which therefore have no place in
the latter things because they are not within the power of free choice—do have
a place in conversion, faith, piety, and the rest.*

Protestant reply. I. Let anyone who can explain among the Pontificians what was
said about the pope in the preceding thesis, and then our answer will be clear
enough even with Bellarmine himself as judge. II. Mean-*

[Printed p. 292 | Google Books PA292 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*while, we say that what Bellarmine assumes as true is a false calumny: that we
hold that men act in those things which pertain to conversion and so forth
from the same necessity by which they are determined to will good in general,
or that we make men no freer in those things which pertain to conversion and
so forth than in gifts of prophecy and the like. III. We agree that counsel,
industry, and diligence have a place in conversion, because these are means
instituted by God through which, and together with which, he effectually
produces those effects. This cannot be affirmed of those other things which
Bellarmine introduces for comparison.

#### Some Arguments of Ours

For Bellarmine does not treat all of them, and of those which he does treat,
some are irrelevant and some have been discussed already.

14. Protestant argument. Ezekiel 36: “I will take the stony heart from you and
give you a heart of flesh,” and so forth. From these words we argue as follows:
A stony heart does not have it within its power to become a heart of flesh.
But the will of a sinful man is a stony heart. Therefore it does not have it
within its power to become fleshly—that is, spiritually good through
regeneration.

Bellarmine. 1. *Free choice is neither the stony heart nor the heart of flesh,
but a certain intermediate and natural power.*

Protestant reply. If Bellarmine means physical essence, then not only free
choice or the will, but also the whole man and even the devil, is an
intermediate and natural thing. But if he denies that the will of a sinner, in
the state and condition which it has by reason of reigning sin, is a stony
heart, he manifestly and directly contradicts the Holy Spirit.

Bellarmine. 2. *When this intermediate and natural power has been made such by
sin that it is called a stony heart, it has from itself no power to turn to
God. But it receives this through the prevenient grace of God, through which
it receives strength from God to cooperate in its own conversion.*

Protestant reply. This is an honorable statement as it sounds, and it embraces
our position, but it proceeds from deceitful lips. For I. By prevenient and
exciting grace Bellarmine understands nothing but moral persuasion, as he
explained his thought earlier. Persuasion, however, does not give strength and
power to do what is urged, but either presupposes or requires them. Therefore,
through the prevenient grace Bellarmine understands, the will receives no new
strength. II. If the will receives new spiritual strength from prevenient
grace, it is then necessarily changed and converted with respect to its
internal disposition antecedently to its own operation. For prevenient grace
precedes the operation of the will, and the communication of new habitual pow-*

[Printed p. 293 | Google Books PA293 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*er is a change or conversion of disposition. If the Pontificians sincerely
confessed this, the present controversy, insofar as it concerns conversion,
would be at an end. Bellarmine therefore grants in words what he strives with
all his strength to oppose.

3. In chapter 15 Bellarmine teaches two things which make the sense of this
sophism manifest. I. “Before grace, free choice stands in relation to works of
piety as the power of sight stands to eliciting an act when the sensible
species is absent.” Here it is easy to assume that the sensible species does
not communicate any new internal strength to the eye, but adds a
representation of the object to the power already present in the eye, from
which the act follows. Bellarmine therefore does not hold that the cause's
power or internal capacity proceeds from grace, but only the possibility of
the effect. II. “Exciting grace includes two things: the beginning of a good
thought and the beginning of a good desire; things of the same kind as impure
desires and sordid thoughts which the devil sometimes casts into us even
against our will.” Here too anyone can see that this assumption can be made:
the devil by his suggestions gives us no new internal and active power;
therefore neither does God confer any such power upon us through the
prevenient grace which Bellarmine teaches.

15. Protestant argument. Man does not cooperate toward that which is created
in him by God. But a clean heart is created in man by God, Psalm 51:12.
Therefore.

Bellarmine. 1. *The substance of the heart is not created in justification;
rather, the cleanness itself is effected.*

Protestant reply. I. The question does not concern justification, but
regeneration or conversion through effectual calling. II. Bellarmine most
shamelessly corrects the Holy Spirit in order to weaken the force of the
argument, changing “is created” into “is effected.” III. It is enough for us
if the cleanness itself is created by God in the heart, and therefore the
heart itself insofar as it is clean. For if man contributes nothing to the
creation of cleanness in his own heart, then God alone is the cause of the
conversion or habitual change in man's conversion.

Bellarmine. 2. *The substance of the spiritual heart cooperates toward the
fruit of its own conversion.*

Protestant reply. As though this had any bearing on the question.

16. Protestant argument. Sinners are said in the Scriptures to be dead in
sins.

Bellarmine. 1. *A dead sinner does not cooperate with God in order that he may
die; rather, he possesses vital power from God.*

Protestant reply. This is the perpetual entanglement of Bellarmine's de-*

[Printed p. 294 | Google Books PA294 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*fense: a dead man has vital power and life within himself.*

Bellarmine. 2. *A man dead with respect to the life of grace lives with the
life of nature, and this cooperates toward the life of grace after it has
received from God the power to cooperate.*

Protestant reply. This is the same contradiction clothed in other words. For
cooperating with grace is an action of the life of grace, not of nature—unless
the Pelagians were condemned without deserving it.
