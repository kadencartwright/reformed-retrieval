XXIII. Having stated his position, he proceeds to explain it, and first sets
forth to us the force and efficacy of the Spirit. Here, therefore, close
attention must be paid to how he explains himself. 1. *His force and efficacy*,
he says on page 58, *can be twofold: the one unbound and free, moving the minds
of men by itself and bound to no things placed outside itself; the other
restricted to things and at last exerting itself through them. The former
affects minds by immediate power, the latter only by mediated power. The
former insinuates itself into minds in the manner of a new testimony or
argument; the latter causes a testimony or argument already existing to
possess authority with us. The Spirit's unbound power is effectual even
without the Scriptures, and this is extraordinary, granted neither to all
believers nor at every time. The Spirit's other, bound power, attached to
things, acts effectually through the Scriptures; and it belongs to our times
and to all believers.* Answer: We acknowledge that this distinction is true
in itself, and that the Spirit exercises his power in this twofold manner.
But we affirm that it has been wrongly applied. For in this way *Wolzogen*
holds that God now by no means moves the minds of men immediately, or by his
unbound and free force and efficacy, and thus denies all motions, inspirations,
inward operations, impulses, and immediate revelations. And in this
he contradicts Holy Scripture, the experience of the saints, and the doctrine
of the Reformed. Scripture says that the faithful *have an anointing from that
Holy One, by which they know all things, and that the anointing which they
received from him abides in them; nor do they need anyone to teach them,
because the same anointing teaches them concerning all things*, 1 John 2:20,
27. *They received the Spirit who is from God, that they might know the things
God has graciously given them*, 1 Corinthians 2:12. *The Spirit leads them
into all truth* and *announces to them the things he has received from Christ*,
John 16:13, 14. *The Spirit bears witness together with our spirits*, Romans
8:16. *God sent forth the Spirit of his Son into the hearts of his people,
crying, Abba, Father*, Galatians 4:6. *By the Spirit they are le-*

*-d*, Galatians 5:18; they are *filled with the Spirit*, Ephesians 5:18. Hence
they can sometimes *quench the Spirit*, 1 Thessalonians 5:19. *We ought not to
believe every spirit*, 1 John 4:1. *The Spirit testifies that the Spirit is
truth, and they testify on earth*, 1 John 5:6, 8. *Do not grieve
that Holy Spirit of God, by whom you have been sealed unto the day of
redemption*, says Paul, Ephesians 4:30. These and similar passages can prove
clearly enough that the Spirit sometimes operates in the souls of his people
by his immediate breathings and inspirations—not that he teaches them
anything which does not agree perfectly with the Scriptures, or that he
observes this method and manner ordinarily. Yet *Wolzogen* denies every such
inward and immediate operation, motion, and force. And in denying this he then
contradicts the experience of many truly godly people. O how often do they
feel themselves moved by the Spirit to perform this or that godly work? How
often does the Spirit recall to their minds many things consigned to oblivion?
How often does he confirm truth, inscribe and engrave it on their hearts, and
persuade them of it when perhaps they are thinking little or nothing about
Scripture? Do they not feel many motions injected by God when they pray? Does
it not sometimes happen that, after pouring out prayers to God, they have the
true meaning of Scripture revealed by God—a meaning which they could not
search out or attain by any study or labor, even with all the Rules of
interpretation employed? What shall I say about that spiritual joy with which
God sometimes floods their souls, or about the immediate manifestation of
himself, and many other things which abundantly confirm that even today God
moves the minds of men by an unbound, free, and immediate force and efficacy
whenever it has seemed good to him? The Orthodox subscribe to this, as will be
clear later.

Let us hear how *Wolzogen* makes this plain to us by an example. *Let us
consider the Apostles*, he says on page 59, *to whom their divine Mas-*

[Page 107 | ONB canvas 161 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000161]

*-ter had promised the Holy Spirit. I indeed admit that he opened their eyes
by this Spirit, so that they might observe the things contained in the
Scriptures of the Old Testament. Hence they are said to have taught nothing
beyond Moses and the Prophets. Here it is plain that the Spirit exercised his
power only in the latter mode, for he did no more than cause them to discern
what Moses and the Prophets possessed more keenly than before. But because the
economy of the
New Testament had many peculiar things which indeed did not argue a different
Gospel but a different dispensation of the same Gospel, did the Spirit teach
the Apostles those peculiar things without scriptures—things which were not
yet contained in any scriptures?* Answer: 1. Our divine Master Christ promised
the Holy Spirit not only to the Apostles but also to all believers. 2. Then,
as regards the things which *Moses and the Prophets* possessed, the Holy Spirit
moved the minds of the Apostles by no unbound, free, and immediate force or
efficacy. But if the Apostles had those things revealed to them in the same
manner in which the writings are revealed today to all believers, they were
not taught by God in any special or extraordinary manner, or immediately
inspired by God. Yet the Reformed say and teach that they were. Next, if the
matter stood thus, how does the Apostles' interpretation differ from the
interpretation of any Pastor? Who does not see where this tends? If, as
regards those things previously revealed, they were led by the Spirit into all
truth only in the same way that all the godly are led today, they could have
been no more infallible than the godly are today. Nor will their interpretation
be of greater authority than that of these men. All these things are absurd
and utterly false.

But he adds more on pages 61–62: *The question is what force of the Spirit it
is by which one is moved to believe that this and no other is the meaning of
Scripture. I reply that it can be twofold. Namely, if by a certain inward
impulse the Spirit either discloses the mind of God as a divine witness,*

[Page 108 | ONB canvas 162 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000162]

*or brings a new argument different from the one which can be drawn from the
things Scripture itself supplies, it is evident that the Spirit acts directly
here. How he could have acted in this way upon those who interpreted the older
Scriptures—the amanuenses of the more recent Scriptures, incapable of error,
the Evangelists and Apostles—is unclear to me. Although we do not know whether
the Holy Spirit used this way so that the holy men might be infallible in
interpreting, as he did use it so that they might be rendered infallible in
writing. For the reason of necessity cannot appear equal. They could not
believe many truths as certain unless resting upon the extraordinary testimony
of the Spirit, because those truths could rest upon no other support, not even
that of reason itself, since very many are above reason. But a sure conviction
concerning interpretation both has another source by which it is confirmed
and therefore will be able to be content with the ordinary grace of the
Spirit, which causes it to perceive the things by which it is confirmed. And
this second way of acting, proceeding from the Spirit, is beneficial to us. For
that first is nothing now, even if it ever existed formerly.*
