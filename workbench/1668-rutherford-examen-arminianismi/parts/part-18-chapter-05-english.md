CHAPTER FIVE

ON THE STATE OF THE FIRST HUMAN BEING

EPHESIANS 4:24

“And put on that new human being, who according to God was created unto
righteousness and true holiness.”

In these words we have a description of the state of the first human being
[the Latin prints the anomalous *primi homines*] before the fall, namely: 1.
That he was created upright and holy. 2. That this
uprightness and righteousness was concreated with him and natural. 3. That in
that state he was able to stand and persevere, or to fall away from it. Hence
these questions arise.

It is asked whether original righteousness was natural or supernatural to the
human being before the fall; that is, whether it was a gift concreated with
nature or grace superadded to a created human being. The Remonstrants affirm
that it was a supernatural gift. We say that it was natural: 1. Because it was
implanted and concreated in the thing from its very creation. 2. Because it
does not transcend the powers of nature and has a cause in nature. And in both
ways original righteousness is natural.

1. For Ecclesiastes 7:29 says, “God created the human being”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 298 | PDF p. 349]

“upright.” Hebrew יָשָׁר, “upright, just”; the Septuagint εὐθὺς, “upright,”
Ecclesiastes 7:30; δίκαιος, “righteous,” Numbers 23:10; καλὸς, “good,” Numbers
12..24; καθαρός, “pure,” Job 33:3. The Chaldean paraphrase says, “God made the
human being upright before himself and innocent.” Damascene, *On the Orthodox
Faith*, book 2, chapter 14, says that he made the human being ἄκακον,
“innocent”; Paul says δίκαιον καὶ ἅγιον, “righteous and holy.” And “upright” is
there opposed to the one who is crooked and has devised many thoughts, or
ways. Therefore, if God did not fashion the human being upright by nature, he
certainly fashioned him crooked, which is absurd.

2. For in Genesis 1:27, “God made the human being in his image and likeness,”
that is, endowed with true righteousness and holiness, Ephesians 4:24;
Colossians 3:10.

3. For this righteousness is naturally due to the end for which the human
being was made by God through nature, namely, to serve God. For God could not
have demanded obedience from the human being out of the powers of nature
unless he had given him a natural capacity to obey.

4. For concupiscence and an inclination toward evil would have been natural to
the human being through nature if this righteousness was not natural, because
either a form or a privation inheres in a subject capable of it. But to say,
as the adversaries do, that concupiscence was natural to the human being before
the fall, that the human being was naturally inclined to sin, and that the act
of sinning was merely restrained by the bridle of original righteousness
makes God the author of sin and the author of concupiscence, which nevertheless
is not from God, according to the testimony of the apostle John.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 299 | PDF p. 350]

5. For it conflicts with reason that the human being before the fall obeyed
God contrary to nature. Indeed, we judge blasphemous that statement of the
Remonstrants in their *Confession*, chapter 15, thesis 4: that Adam abstained
from eating the fruit of the knowledge of good and evil only with the greatest
distress. For that distress in obeying could not have failed to be culpable
and contrary to the natural love of God. Thus a fault would have preceded the
first fault, and the human being would have been created evil and crooked.

Objection: This image is now supernatural to the fallen human being.
Therefore, it was supernatural to the human being not yet fallen.

Response: It does not follow. For the image restored to us in Christ is
analogically the same as the image that was lost, but different in species and
of an entirely different nature.

6. And what they say is worthless: that inclinations toward contrary things
flowed from the condition of matter—namely, toward what pleases the flesh and
the sensitive faculty and toward what pleases reason—but that these
inclinations did not flow from God's intention. Thus they teach that rust in a
sword is not from the craftsman's intention but from the condition of its
matter. For the objects of the sensitive faculty and of reason are indeed
different, but not contrary; and a natural harmony was concreated in the
upright human being, by which the sensitive appetite would obey reason and
reason would obey God. But the human being would not be upright by nature and
fashioned in the image and likeness of God if by the force of matter and

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 300 | PDF p. 351]

a concreated propensity of nature he inclined toward what is opposed to God's
law.

7. In this way there would be a struggle in the human being between the
flesh—or the sensitive appetite tending toward what is forbidden in God's law
and by right reason—and right reason. But such a natural struggle could not
have failed to make the human being miserable by nature, for Paul laments
miserably over an inclination toward contrary things, Romans 7:24.

8. If that concupiscence which was in Adam, as they teach, is a bare capacity
to act or not act, then they say nothing. For in that case concupiscence would
be in the good angels, in the glorified, and in Christ, which is absurd. But if
it is a quality superadded to the capacity to act or not act by which the
human being naturally inclines toward sin, then: 1. It is natural to the human
being and implanted by reason to sin, and obeying God will be contrary to the
creature's concreated inclination. 2. The human being could not tend toward an
object naturally proportioned and suitable to his capacity when he sinned,
and purely natural acts would be culpable, which is absurd.

9. It is Manichaean that anything concreated should be morally evil.

It is asked whether God is not established as the author of the first sin if
the human being sinned because God, apart from the human being's fault, denied
him the grace necessary for avoiding the first sin. The Arminians affirm it
with their *Anti-Perkins*, pages 704, 505. We deny it.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 301 | PDF p. 352]

1. For God withdrew nothing of his image from the human being. Actual grace,
or God's efficacious influence required for an act of obedience, is not part
of the divine image; for Adam had both the ability to sin and the ability not
to sin.

2. For by no law or debt was that influence owed to the human being. But God
cannot be the author of sin because he withdraws from Adam something not owed,
even though it is necessary for avoiding sin.

3. For God denied efficacious influence to Adam when Adam was unwilling to
have it. Therefore, there was here a virtual and interpretive demerit on
Adam's part, though not a formal one. For in the same instant of time in which
God denied this actual influence, Adam freely willed to lack it; and by the
very fact that he committed the first sin, he interpretively willed to lack
that predetermining grace by which, if he had possessed it, he would have
devoutly avoided the first sin. Nor do we deem it absurd that the lack of
predetermining grace and the culpable demerit of that lack should coexist at
the same time, even though in the order of nature the lack is prior to the
demerit. I acknowledge that this is a mystery of our theology which must not
be searched out.

It is asked whether the first sin dissolves and releases Adam from every
covenant because, namely, the covenant of grace had not yet been entered into
with him before the promised blessed seed; and because, once the first
covenant is broken, says Corvinus against

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 302 | PDF p. 353]

Molina, chapter 8, section 7, following his Arminius, the obligation to render
obedience to that covenant ceases, since the law, as the Remonstrants teach,
does not simultaneously bind one to active and passive obedience. We maintain
the contrary.

All are bound to obey the law, or covenant of works, entered into with Adam
while he was not yet fallen, even after that covenant was broken.

1. “The wages of sin is death,” Romans 6:23. And death would have existed even
on the supposition that God had never entered into the covenant of grace with
the human race in Christ the Mediator. Otherwise, God could not have actually
inflicted the threatened penalty for a second transgression of the law unless
he had promised Christ the Mediator, which is absurd.

2. God rebuked Adam while he was fleeing and hiding among the trees, even
before the blessed seed was promised. Therefore, during that short interval
he sinned after the first covenant was broken. But he did not sin against the
covenant of grace, which did not then exist. Therefore, he sinned against the
first covenant of works. But he could not sin against the covenant of works
unless he was bound to render active obedience to that covenant.

3. For the first sin makes no one lawless.

4. For in that case perjurers, idolaters, fornicators, murderers, and
parricides would not sin against the law as law. But pagans who commit such
things cannot sin against the covenant of grace, which was never

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 303 | PDF p. 354]

revealed to them, because a law that has not been promulgated in any way does
not bind, and therefore that law cannot be sinned against. Hence the most
wicked sinners do not sin at all, because “where there is no law, there is no
transgression.”

5. For if all are under the covenant of grace and released from the first
covenant of works, then no mortal would be subject to the law's curse as it is
law, contrary to Scripture, Deuteronomy 27:26; Galatians 3:10. For those who
are not bound to render active obedience to the law are not cursed with the
law's penal curse, because a law that does not bind to obedience does not bind
to punishment.

6. For in this way some would not be under the law, while others would be
under grace and Christ, the new husband, as Paul says in Romans 7.

7. This doctrine of the adversaries rests on false hypotheses. The first is
that all mortals are covenanted in the covenant of grace, even those who have
never heard, even by report, either of the covenant or of the name of Christ,
the covenant's surety, although Scripture encompasses only the elect, the
house of Israel, and the children of promise in that covenant, Jeremiah 31:31;
Hebrews 8:10–11; Romans 8:9; Galatians 5:26–27. The second is that no one is
simultaneously bound to punishment and obedience. This is indeed true in this
sense: no one is bound to obedience in those acts for whose neglect he is
paying the penalties; nevertheless, he is bound to other subsequent acts.
Otherwise, the first sin would make all mortals lawless and subject to no

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 304 | PDF p. 355]

law unless God had made the covenant of grace. And in that case God would
have made the covenant of grace from necessity, lest human beings perhaps
remain lawless.

8. For the reprobate are always children of the bondwoman and subject to the
legal covenant and its curse, since they are never in Christ.

It is asked whether sinful concupiscence is a privative being or a positive
quality. The adversaries maintain that it is a privative being.

We distinguish. A positive quality is taken in three ways: 1. Physically. 2.
Ethically. 3. Logically. Concupiscence is not a physically positive quality,
because every such quality is from God as some good of nature. Nor in this
sense do our theologians teach that concupiscence is a positive quality. But
concupiscence is also considered in two ways. 1. Formally and in the abstract;
and so, logically speaking, it is not a positive quality but logically
privative, and is a crookedness of a capacity rather than a capacity. But 2.
concupiscence is considered in the concrete, insofar as it connotes a subject
capable of a contrary habit; and so our theologians teach that concupiscence is
not a wholly privative being but an ethically positive being.

1. For it does not signify only that the bare absence of rectitude owed to the
faculties inheres in them; it also connotes a subject capable either of the
form or of the privation.

2. For it not only deprives the soul of original

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 305 | PDF p. 356]

righteousness, as blindness deprives the eyes of sight; it also affects the
soul with an evil quality and inclination, as rot corrupts an apple and fever
makes the body diseased.

3. For Scripture ascribes positive actions to it, namely, that it opposes
God's Spirit, leads human beings captive, performs the works of the flesh, and
so forth.

It is asked whether there was in Adam no capacity to believe in Christ, and
therefore whether he did not lose that capacity by the force of the first sin
until it is restored in Christ. The Remonstrants deny that there was such a
capacity in him. So Arminius, *Response to Article 31*; Episcopius,
*Disputation* 5, thesis 6; and likewise Socinus, *Theological Lectures*,
chapter 3, folios 8–9.

We say that there was such a capacity in Adam. But 1. We do not teach that
justifying faith was actually in Adam, for that presupposes sin. 2. It is not
asked whether there was in him a passive and remote capacity to believe in
Christ; the adversaries do not deny this. Nor 3. is it asked whether there was
legal faith in Adam, for all concede this.

We nevertheless assert that in Adam, while he was not yet fallen, there was a
proximate capacity to believe in Christ.

1. For the image of God was in Adam, perfect in the manner of a habit.
Therefore, there was a capacity for every act which the Creator could command
him, either absolutely or on a supposition. For the image of God includes the
capacity to believe everything that the first truth could reveal.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 306 | PDF p. 357]

2. I suppose that Adam, while not yet fallen, had had his misery and a
restoring Savior revealed to him. In that case, either he was bound to believe
in the Savior or he was not. If the first, then he had a capacity to believe;
for moral obligation in a human being not yet fallen presupposes physical
capacity, because God cannot bind an unfallen human being to the impossible.
If the second, then he was bound not to believe, and thus to despair of the
Savior even when revealed, which is absurd.

3. For Adam was bound to rest upon God in every possible danger—for example,
if he were tempted by the Devil to sin. Therefore, he was also so bound under
sin, the greatest danger, if the remedy had been revealed. Therefore, he had
the capacity to believe in Christ.

4. Adam had the capacity to believe in God as Creator. Therefore, he also had
the capacity to believe in God as Redeemer. The reason for the consequence is
that if these two kinds of faith differed in species, there would not be “one
baptism, one faith,” contrary to the apostle in Ephesians 4:5. Thus in Hebrews
11:3 and 13, by one faith we believe creation and the gospel promises.

5. Adam had the capacity to believe everything commanded in the moral law,
either explicitly or implicitly. Therefore, he had the capacity to believe
every truth either revealed or to be revealed by God. For the law commands
the intellect and affections to give assent to every truth.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 307 | PDF p. 358]

6. Abraham had the habitual charity to exercise acts of love for God both in
acts commanded and acts to be commanded by God—for example, in sacrificing
his son, if God were to command it. Therefore, in like manner, Adam had the
capacity to believe both what had been revealed and what was to be revealed.

7. If Adam did not have the capacity to believe in Christ, neither did he have
a devout inclination of mind to take the intellect captive to the obedience
of God. This cannot stand with the true holiness and righteousness which were
in Adam.

It is asked whether the image of God in Adam was original righteousness and
habitual holiness, or merely a bare free will to obey God without any infused
or concreated habit of holiness. The Remonstrants affirm in the *Apology*,
chapter 7, section 6, that it makes no difference whether someone says or
denies that Adam was endowed with such righteousness. Episcopius,
*Disputation* 5, thesis 3; Corvinus, *Against the Wallachians*, folio 55;
Socinus, *Theological Lectures*, chapter 3, folios 7–8; Smalcius, *Refutation
of Frantzius*, disputation 2, folios 44–45. They deny that any virtues were
infused into Adam's will. We maintain the contrary.

1. Adam was fashioned in the image of God, and this image consists in true
righteousness and holiness, Ephesians 4:24; Colossians 3:10.

2. For God fashioned the human being upright and inclined to love God.
Otherwise, Adam while sleeping would not have been good and holy by habit.

3. For a bare capacity and free will to obey God without any infused virtue
in the will

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 308 | PDF p. 359]

remains in the human being after the fall as something natural, concreated,
and essential to the will, as they teach. Hence it follows that through the
fall Adam lost nothing of the divine image and nothing in the will of a holy
inclination toward the good agreeable to God's law. Consequently, the human
being was as sound and holy after the fall as he was before the fall.

It is asked whether Adam was created mortal. The Remonstrants and Socinians,
as formerly the Pelagians, teach that mortality belongs to the human being by
nature. We respond to them with these assertions.

Assertion 1. If “natural” is taken for what is implanted in a thing at its
creation, then mortality is not natural to the human being.

*[Correction from the printed errata, PDF 52: at p. 308, line 12, the body has
`S. naturale`; the errata directs `read Si naturale`, which is adopted here.]*

1. For God fashioned the human being such that he was able not to die; and if
he had persevered in obedience toward God, he would not have died.

2. For not only death but also mortality is a punishment of sin, because
mortality is an evil and a disposition toward destruction. Thus runs God's
threat: “On the day you eat, by dying you shall die,” that is, “you shall be
mortal and shall finally die,” Genesis 2:17.

3. For death and dissolution are a disturbance of the harmony of the elements
and of the soul's union with the body. If it had been concreated with the human
being, this would appear to turn to the Creator's dishonor.

Assertion 2. If “natural” is taken for what has a cause in nature in any
manner, that is, either in matter or in form, then mortality is natural to
Adam's body of dust.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 309 | PDF p. 360]

1. For everything generable is corruptible.

2. For what is joined together from contraries can be dissolved.

3. For the human being, as to his body, was dust.

4. For every contrary can be acted upon by its contrary.

Assertion 3. The possibility of not dying did not belong to the human being
simply by reason of the soul, but by the Creator's beneficence.

1. For the rational soul cannot prevent the action and counteraction of
contraries.

2. For the soul cannot prevent external force, compression, and suffocations
to which the body could be physically subjected if it had seemed good to God
to inflict external force upon the body of Adam while not yet fallen. And God
could have inflicted that force through an external agent according to his
absolute good pleasure, apart from the demerit of sin. But what belongs to the
human being as something concreated by the beneficence of the Creator, as
Creator, must be deemed simply natural and a certain connatural due of nature,
not supernatural; for God as Creator does not confer supernatural things.

Assertion 4. By moral obligation, Adam's body was not subject to death,
mortality, old age, diseases, or dissolution before sin.

1. For according to the apostle in Romans 6:23, “The wages of sin is death.”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 310 | PDF p. 361 | stop before CAPUT SEXTUM]

2. For that threat, “You are dust,” and so forth, would never have taken
effect unless the human being had sinned.

3. For the Arminians and Socinians, together with the papists, impiously
subject the human being to mortality by nature. For in that way he could die
naturally without the demerit of sin. The reason for this is that, because
they teach that original sin is not properly called sin, it appears unjust to
them and foreign to God's justice that infants should die.
