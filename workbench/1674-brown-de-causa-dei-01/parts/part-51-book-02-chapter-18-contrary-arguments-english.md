CHAPTER XVIII

The Contrary Arguments of the Adversaries Are Examined

Although later, when the Adversaries' arguments by which they endeavor to
attack the morality of the Sabbath or the Lord's Day are called up for
examination, a very suitable occasion will be given to respond fully even to
those things which contend against what has now been said in the preceding
chapter, nevertheless it will not be amiss here at least briefly to touch upon
certain things which are especially urged against the foregoing.

*It is therefore objected, 1. The distinction of days is something legal which
has no place under the Gospel, which on the contrary side contends with the
Law.* *Response.* Something can be called *Legal* either with respect to the
*Moral* Law or with respect to the *Ceremonial* Law. What is called legal with
respect to the Moral Law can have a place under the Gospel, because the Moral
Law itself has force even in the time of the Gospel. What is called Legal with
respect to the Ceremonial Law certainly has no place under the Gospel, because
the whole Ceremonial Law has now been abrogated and made obsolete. But it will
never be proved that every distinction of days is something ceremonially legal,
any more than it can be proved that every distinction of foods is something
purely and simply Ceremonial or Ceremonially Legal. This cannot be proved,
because Christ, by instituting the Sacraments, established a distinction of
foods. For bread and wine in the celebration of the Lord's Supper are
distinguished from other, common bread and wine; so also the water in baptism
is distinguished from other water applied to common uses. Moreover, in the
following book we shall demonstrate that the distinction of days was instituted
from the beginning, long before the Ceremonial Law was established. And from
what has been said in this same book it is evident how necessary the distinction
of Times is; and in the preceding chapter we showed that corrupt nature itself
judges the distinction of times to be necessary and determines that all times
are not equal. Accordingly, some distinction of Times and Days will be
Moral-Natural. *Finally,* we shall later make clear how the institution of the
Sabbath pertains to the Moral Law, in *book III.*

*It is objected, 2. No distinction of Years exists under the Gospel; why, then,
should a distinction of Days exist any more?* *Response.* We grant that the
Sabbatical Year was ceremonial. It does not follow that the weekly Sabbath was
therefore likewise ceremonial, because these two differ greatly. Nature teaches
nothing about such a distinction of years, but it does teach something about
the distinction of days. No Sabbatical Year had been instituted from the
beginning; nevertheless, the distinction of days had been instituted from the
beginning, as will appear in its proper place. The Sabbatical Year had a
particular relation to the land of Canaan, but not every distinction of days had
such a particular relation. We read nothing in the New Testament about an
approved observance of the Sabbatical year; nevertheless, we do read about the
approved observance of a certain day. No year was called *the Lord's,* yet a
certain Day was so named. *Revelation 1:10.*

*It is objected, 3. No distinction of Places exists under the Gospel. Therefore
the distinction of days is no more consistent with the Gospel state.*
*Response.* Although what was said above about the determination of *Time,* both
for God's glory and for advancing our salvation,

[1674 logical printed p. 359 | IA leaf 377 | PDF p. 377]

sufficiently demonstrates how necessary it is that *Time* be determined, and
although it can be evident to anyone that nothing of the kind can be said about
*Place,* so that the whole force of this objection vanishes of its own accord,
we shall nevertheless show clearly later how immense the difference is between
*Time* and *Place.*

*It is objected, 4. There is that statement of the Apostle in Romans, chapter
14, verse 17: “For the kingdom of God is not food and drink, but Righteousness
and peace,” and so forth.* *Response.* It is true that the use of those external
intermediate things does not contribute much to advancing the kingdom of grace
in us or to bringing us to the kingdom of glory. For *food does not commend us
to God; neither are we better off if we eat, nor are we worse off if we do not
eat,* 1 Corinthians 8:8. Nevertheless, the kingdom of God consists in the use
of those intermediate things to this extent: care must be taken that this
liberty of ours does not in any way become a cause of stumbling to the weak, 1
Corinthians 8:9, and that by our knowledge a weak brother for whom Christ died
does not perish, verse 12; Romans 14:15; and that we do not destroy God's work
on account of food, *Romans 14:20.* 2. What will follow from this? Does it follow
that the Kingdom of God does not consist in commanded actions or in the
observance of any Solemn Times? How will this consequence be proved? It does not
follow from indifferent things, about which the Apostle is disputing, to things
expressly commanded. 3. Because the Kingdom of God consists in righteousness
and so forth, does it follow that all external observances are to be rejected?
Then every external means, every external act of worship, and every external
duty will have to be rejected; and by this reasoning not only Sabbaths but also
all preaching of the Gospel, administration of the Sacraments, public prayers,
profession, and confession of Christ's name will be cast out, and thus all
external worship. And why not every relationship too? Thus, together with the
indiscriminate use of days, the indiscriminate use of wives also will be
introduced—and what will not be? Indeed, the principles which some lay down to
abolish every distinction of days tend in this direction.

*It is objected, 5. The Christian Sabbath must be perpetual and constant,
according to the prediction of Isaiah, chapter 66, verse 23: “from New Moon to
New Moon, from Sabbath to Sabbath”; that is, constantly and perpetually without
interruption.* *Response.* 1. The constant and perpetual usage of the expression
employed here and of similar expressions proves the contrary—namely, that this
is not the sense of the passage. Rather, the sense is as if he were saying,
every New Moon, every Sabbath. See 1 Samuel 7:16; 2 Chronicles 24:5; Zechariah
14:16; Nehemiah 10:35, 39; and Esther 3:7. See the *English Annotations* on the
passage. 2. Even if this were the sense of the passage, it would do nothing
against the distinction of days. For there is an internal Sabbath which
consists in unceasing abstinence from sin. Yet notwithstanding this, an external
and Solemn Sabbath can be admitted under the New Testament no less than under
the Old Testament, because even then, when very many Solemn Times had been
instituted, everyone was bound to observe this internal Sabbath, *Hebrews 4:1,
2.* And experience has proved that observing the external Sabbath does not
hinder but rather advances the observance of this internal Sabbath. 3. From
this foundation it will equally well follow that no day occasionally instituted
and to be observed exists now under the New Testament. And if so, we may bid
farewell to all Christ's institutions, because there will be no appointed Time
in which to administer them.

*It is objected, 6. The worship of God ought to be spiritual, and therefore
internal, flowing from the spirit, Not external. And whatever the believer does
ought to be a part of worship, because it is the fruit of love and faith.*

[1674 logical printed p. 360 | IA leaf 378 | PDF p. 378]

*And throughout the whole course of his life he is bound to obey God and to
worship him spiritually, with his whole mind and his whole spirit.* *Response.*
By these and similar Familistic burrows John de Labadie endeavors to undermine
the whole Sabbath, as I saw in a certain manuscript written by him. But who does
not see where these things tend? He does not distinguish between Mediate and
Immediate worship, but supposes that we worship God equally when we seek food
and clothing and perform other duties prescribed in the second table of the
Decalogue as when we solemnly pray to God or worship him immediately. And thus
he destroys all solemn worship and bids farewell to solemn prayer, the
administration of the word and sacraments, and the rest of Christ's
institutions. That is, he is an Antinomian and a Familist. What? Was God's
people not formerly bound to worship God spiritually, to do all things from
faith and love, and to obey God in all things? Yet were they therefore not bound
to observe Solemn Times? Or did the prescription of solemnities oppose this
internal worship in any way, and did it not rather advance it? Are we not bound
also to serve God with our body? This reasoning has the appearance of piety,
but in reality it opens the gate to all impiety. For it no more follows from
this that Sabbaths or any distinction of days are not now to be observed than
that the Church is not bound at any time to worship God solemnly, to pray and
praise, or to hold assemblies for administering the word and sacraments; nor
that domestic worship ought to be instituted. Indeed, from this it will follow
that Christians are not bound to worship God even in their chamber, because
this will be done with the body, while the worship of God ought to be spiritual,
in the spirit and in the soul. And from this it will follow that God is worshiped
no less through daily works than through prayers poured out immediately into
the bosom of God through our Lord Jesus Christ. But the palpable contradiction
must also be observed, since he would have all the external actions of
Christians be part of worship and yet asserts that all worship ought to be
spiritual and internal, befitting the nature of God, who is spirit, and not
external and bodily.

*It is objected, 7. God always demands that in all our actions we worship him as
purely and spiritually as possible. And there is greater religion where there
is a greater measure of the Spirit, purer faith, and more ardent love. And when
he pours a greater measure of the Spirit into us while we are occupied with
worldly matters than when we are engaged in other exercises which appear more
solemn and sacred, the former are to be preferred to the latter. And God is free
to give his gifts when and where he wills, because he is bound to no place or
time. And sometimes he draws our hearts closer to himself through worldly
things than through other things which are outwardly spiritual, so as to teach
us to depend wholly upon him and not upon those means.* *Response.* Thus Dr. de
Labadie continues in that manuscript. But these are mere trappings which cannot
deceive one who savors spiritual things. For although a good and spiritual
habitual disposition is required, so that we intend God's glory in all things,
God does not nevertheless demand that we have our mind as actually intent upon
him, or our thoughts as fixed, when engaged in earthly affairs that concern our
body—in which God also requires diligence, as we showed above—as when we
immediately worship, revere, adore, pray to, or address God. 2. Who denies that
internal Religion, Reverence, and Devotion are greater where faith is purer and
love more ardent? But what does this do against external means of worship
instituted by God himself? 3. Although it has sometimes seemed good to God to
pour out a greater measure of his Spirit upon his people when they are in
private exercises than when in public ones, and when they are engaged in
earthly affairs

[1674 logical printed p. 361 | IA leaf 379 | PDF p. 379]

than when they immediately worship God, nevertheless it is not fitting for us
to treat the external means instituted by him with contempt or to place them
after earthly affairs. Rather, we ought all the more to await God there because
of his promises annexed to them; and we must remember that saying of *Solomon,
Proverbs 28:9: “He who turns his ear away from hearing the Law, even his prayer
is an abomination.”* 4. This argument no more overthrows the Sabbath than all
the other institutions of Christ. For to what end would we pray, read the
Scriptures, hear the word preached, and so forth, since God could pour out a
greater measure of the Holy Spirit upon us when we seek earthly things than
when we immediately seek God himself? These things truly pave the way to
profanity and to neglect of all divine worship. 5. Nothing is said here against
Christian solemnities which does not also attack Jewish solemnities. For the
Law of God formerly demanded that in all their actions they worship God as
purely and spiritually as possible; and there was greater religion where there
was a greater measure of the Spirit, purer faith, and so forth. And it had
sometimes seemed good to God to bless private exercises more than public ones,
as the experience of the Bride in *Song of Songs,* chapter 3:4, teaches. 6.
Daily experience teaches that those who find the Lord in private pursue the
public exercises of worship with greater love; and those who find him in the
works of their private calling esteem his public institutions more highly,
whatever some may dream.

*It is objected, 8. It is required that believers be equally and in equal
measure holy in all their works, both worldly and religious, and that they
intend God's glory with the highest zeal in all things; and therefore God does
not require that they be holier on one day than another or in one work than in
another.* So Labadie above. *Response.* These things no more contend against the
Sabbath or the distinction of days than against the distinction of actions and
that distinction of worship into immediate and mediate. And by this reasoning
Dr. de Labadie will destroy all immediate worship and all solemn worship,
public or private, no less than the Sabbath. For in the same manner the
*Familist,* or anyone who opposes Christ's institutions, will be able to argue
and say: Why should I pray publicly in an ecclesiastical assembly, or privately
at home and in my chamber, since I am always equally and in equal measure holy,
and I seek and intend God's glory when I discharge my civil office no less than
when I pray, and since God requires this of me? To what end, then, are all those
external things? To what end the administration of the Word and Sacraments?

*Objection 9. Since Christians are bound in this manner to intend God's glory
in all things, God is equally glorified through their works of whatever kind,
and they cannot profane any day by them.* *Response.* Everything tends to this:
that it may be proved that his followers glorify God equally by knitting
stockings and doing things of that kind as by praying and worshiping God on the
Lord's Day. But who would not abhor this carnal spirituality—or spiritual
profanity, so to speak? What? Does he think that our good intention can make an
evil action good? Could not the pious of old also have made this pretense and
therefore have neglected all God's institutions, never gone to *Jerusalem,*
offered no sacrifices, and observed no solemnity? But the Spirit taught them
otherwise of old, and also teaches his people otherwise today—namely, to love
all God's precepts. But leaving these things, let us proceed to resolve the
other objections.

*Objection 10. From Paul's Epistle to the Romans, chapter 14, verse 5, the
adversaries argue that all days are now equal under the Gospel, because the
Apostle would have those to be stronger who*

[1674 logical printed p. 362 | IA leaf 380 | PDF p. 380]

*recognized no distinction of days but esteemed them all equally, and did not
esteem the Lord's Day more than any other day, just as they made no distinction
of foods, as the weaker did, who had not yet been perfectly taught that every
such distinction had been abrogated.* *Response.* Because we will speak more
fully elsewhere about this and similar passages, it will be enough to answer a
few things here. 1. It is evident that the Lord's Day was observed before this
time, as *Paul* testifies in his former Epistle to the *Corinthians,* as does
*Luke in Acts 20,* and as does the practice of the Apostles before Christ's
ascension, with Christ himself present and approving. Therefore, if what is
objected is true, Paul must have regarded as weak those who observed this day
and distinguished it from the others, and thus tacitly condemned what elsewhere
he not only approves but also commands. 2. The Adversaries assume what they will
never be able to prove—namely, that a dispute arose among the Christians *at
Rome* about the observance of the Lord's Day, as if there were some who observed
certain days and, among others, the Lord's Day, but others who rejected not only
the feasts and Sabbaths of the Jews but also the Lord's Day. But it is
appropriate to ask the Adversaries whether they think that the Lord's Day had
been observed before this time or not. If *not,* then the Apostle says nothing
about the observance of this day, because he speaks only about the observance
of days which then obtained; and, as is admitted, he speaks with reference to
the controversy then agitated in the Roman Church. If *the former* is said,
then this was done either lawfully and with the Apostles' approval or not. If
*that* is said, should we believe that the Apostle wished to regard as weak
those who observed a day instituted at least by the Apostles? If *this* is said,
is it probable that the Apostle wished to say that the weak observe the day to
the Lord and ought not to be condemned in what was less lawful? 3. Here he no
more condemns every distinction of days than every distinction of foods. But no
one will say that he condemns every distinction of foods—for example, the
distinction between bread and wine in the Eucharist and common bread and wine.
4. If *Paul* and all the other Apostles—for I believe that they did not dictate
diverse and conflicting things—condemned every distinction of days, how did it
happen that the primitive and earliest primitive Church made any distinction
of days and esteemed the Lord's Day before all the others? It must then
necessarily be said that it erred; that all the fathers who spoke magnificently
about this day also erred; that the whole Church from that time erred; and that
all the Magistrates or Emperors who commanded the observance of this day under
civil penalties erred, as did the Provincial Councils which commanded the same
observance. 5. It will follow from this that the Socinians, Anabaptists,
Antinomians, Tremblers, Familists, and Fanatics of that kind are to be approved,
and indeed all profane people who make no distinction of days, and that all
these are to be regarded as stronger; but that all pious and upright Christians
who, from conscience of the divine command, esteem this day before others
are to be numbered among the weak. 6. Since it is certain that he speaks about
the ceremonial distinction of *foods,* it is just as clearly evident that he
speaks about the ceremonial distinction of *days.* For by far the greatest
contention had arisen in the primitive Church about the observance of days
instituted under the Mosaic pedagogy, as also about the entire Ceremonial Law,
as Scripture abundantly testifies. And this is clearly evident from other
passages of Scripture now to be examined. But more on this subject later.

*Objection 11. From Paul's Epistle to the Galatians 4:10, where the Apostle
speaks thus, “You observe Days, and months, and appointed times, and years,” the
adversaries argue against every*

[1674 logical printed p. 363 | IA leaf 381 | PDF p. 381]

*distinction of days under the Gospel.* *Response.* 1. Paul himself testifies
in *1 Corinthians 16:1* that he ordained for the Churches in Galatia that they
make a collection on the Lord's Day, or the first day of the week, and therefore
that they hold assemblies on that day and perform certain duties appropriate to
that day. It is therefore not probable that he wished to condemn them here for
observing this day, or at least to do so without any defense or explanation of
the earlier injunction given. 2. He speaks about those days whose observance
was urged by those False Teachers who moved them to observe the Mosaic Law. But
who will say that those False Teachers wished to urge so keenly the observance
of the Lord's Day, instituted by no Mosaic Law, or to contend for it as
vigorously as for the other ceremonies? 3. If the Apostles instituted the
Lord's Day, is it probable that those False Teachers wished to urge its
observance by arguments, together with the other ceremonies instituted by God
himself, and that *Paul,* on the contrary, condemned it? If observance of the
Lord's Day had been instituted by those False Teachers, is it not probable that
the Apostle would have wished to condemn it under a very different name from
that under which he condemned the observance of ceremonial days? Would he have
condemned those inventions of men ill-disposed toward the Gospel by the same
arguments with which he condemned and attacked the *Mosaic* ceremonies that God
himself had instituted? It is much more probable that he would have wished to
brand them with another mark. 4. It is evident from the text itself that he
condemns such days as were ceremonial, of which there had been a great number;
he therefore names *days* in the plural and names them together with *months and
years,* whose observance was undoubtedly ceremonial. He means those days which
were *weak and beggarly elements, verse 9,* and whose observance rendered the
whole Christian religion vain, *verse 11.* But who will say this about the
Lord's Day? 5. If he condemned all *καιρούς,* or opportunities, it would not now
be lawful to preach the Gospel or to do anything opportunely; the Apostle
himself teaches Timothy the contrary in *2 Timothy 4:2,* and teaches the
*Colossian* Church the contrary when he commands them to *redeem the
opportunity, Colossians 4:5.* Therefore this word must be limited to the
appointed Jewish times. 6. Will this statement of the Apostle condemn our
appointed times for supplication or thanksgiving? If not, the Apostle does not
here condemn every distinction of days.

*Objection 12. They argue similarly from Paul's Epistle to the Colossians,
chapter 2, verse 16: “Therefore let no one condemn you for food or drink, or
with respect to a feast, or a new moon, or Sabbaths.”* *Response.* 1. Here he no
more condemns the distinction of days than the distinction of food and drink.
But here he condemns only the ceremonial distinction of food and drink. 2. He
condemns those days which were elements of the world, verse 8; which formed
part of the handwritten record of rites, verse 14; and which were a shadow of
things to come, verse 17. 3. Did the Jews not have other days which were called
Sabbaths besides the weekly Sabbath? How, then, will it be evident that the
Apostle condemns every such Sabbath? 4. And if it is granted that the Jewish
weekly Sabbath is included here, how will it be proved that our Lord's Day is
also included here? 5. Is it so clearly evident that the False Teachers urged
the observance of this day, as it is evident that they contended for the
observance of all ceremonial days? But more will be said later in its proper
place about this testimony of Scripture and the others. What has been said here
will suffice to prove that the Apostle does not condemn every distinction of
days under the

[1674 logical printed p. 364 | IA leaf 382 | PDF p. 382 | ends before CAP. XIX.]

Gospel. We will add only this here: that the most distinguished *Hoornbeeck,
disputing against the Socinians* in *book 2, chapter 2, p. 477,* responds thus
to this passage alleged by the Racovian Catechism: *By Sabbaths ceremonial
feasts are meant there, because these indeed were a shadow of the future and
were referred to Christ as their body; not so the weekly Sabbath of the
Decalogue, instituted from the beginning of the world—or insofar as it simply
says that after six days of labor one day is to be sanctified to God and
therefore kept as a day of rest. Hence Sabbaths are joined with New Moons and
with the choice of foods, which were* τὰ ἀσθενῆ καὶ πτωχὰ στοιχεῖα, *the weak
and beggarly elements, from which we have been liberated through Christ,
Galatians 4:9. But what is said about the distinction of a day can no more be
extended to the Lord's Day than what is said about food and drink can be
extended to the prejudice of the bread and wine instituted by Christ in the
Holy Supper, and for that reason to be observed by us and, with respect both to
that precept and to that use, more sacred than ordinary bread and wine.*
