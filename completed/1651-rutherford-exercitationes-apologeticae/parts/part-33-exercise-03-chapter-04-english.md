Chapter IV.

*The new fable of Suárez, Fonseca, Bellarmine, and others concerning congruous
calling is summoned to account.*

The Jesuits, new monsters of the church,

[1651 logical printed p. 503 | IA leaf 521 | PDF p. 521]

resolve the efficacy of grace into the congruity of calling. Alvarez, an
opponent of this congruity, located it in three things: 1. in
persuasion; 2. in such an adjustment of the persuasion to such a person, so
disposed, at such a place and time; 3. in the middle knowledge by which God
foreknows how the will, placed at such a time and amid such occasions, will be
inclined. The matter is still under judgment whether the efficacy of grace is
to be referred to the congruity of the persuasion itself, as certain Jesuits
think. Suárez is more cautious, teaching with a sufficiently valid argument
that congruity does not suffice for the efficacy of grace. *For this calling,*
he says, *is a means suited to God’s efficacious intention; but it implies a
contradiction for God’s efficacious will not to be fulfilled. Therefore that
calling cannot be a proportionate means unless it is so infallible that its
failure to attain its effect is utterly repugnant and implies a contradiction.
But that whole congruity of itself produces only a certain moral certainty,
to which it is not absolutely repugnant that free choice should resist a
congruous calling in this way.* Believe me, the Jesuit who rejects congruity
and takes refuge in middle knowledge shows some sense. Bellarmine, Fonseca,
Valentia, and Suárez follow that path. “What of the Arminians?” you will ask,
reader. Their own great doctor Arminius wavers. *Perhaps,* he says, *it can be
maintained from the sense of Scripture.* The Remonstrants say at the Hague
Conference: *There would be nothing absurd if God’s operation were called
efficacious because, through God’s infinite knowledge, it is administered to
people of this kind, in such a state, at about that time.* But then, brotherly
gentlemen, there is nothing absurd in maintaining Calvin’s absolute decree.
For Corvinus rejects congruous calling on this very ground.

Its defenders must therefore maintain an absolute decree. Yet the Arminians
cry out that this is fate. Such, however, is the wavering of heretics and of
those who stray from the faith that the Remonstrants directly rejected this
calling in their Confession.

2. They accordingly teach that, if the substance of the calling is regarded,
the grace in the converted and the unconverted can be equal and the same.
*Indeed,* says Fonseca in the same place, *perhaps it is greater in the
unconverted. Yet solely because such a calling is applied to the person who
is converted in the circumstance in which he is foreknown to obey, whereas it
is applied to the person who is not converted in the circumstance in which he
is foreknown to resist—even though he could be called in another circumstance
in which he would be foreknown to obey—the former’s calling is efficacious,
but the latter’s is not.* If we ask why some are called congruously and others
incongruously, they answer: the elect are called congruously according to the
absolute decree of predestination, by which efficacious grace is administered;
the reprobate are called incongruously according to the decree of common
providence, by which only sufficient grace is conferred upon them. It is
indeed sufficient, because God wills all to be saved and does not fail to
supply necessary things—otherwise men would not be without excuse—but it is
nevertheless not efficacious. For God foreknows that sufficient grace will
never be efficacious, yet he gives it to the reprobate according to the decree
of common providence. So they say.

I attack it first in this way. This efficacious and congruous grace is either
necessary for every supernatural act, and thus for conversion, or it is not.

[Marginalia: Argument 1.]

[1651 logical printed p. 505 | IA leaf 523 | PDF p. 523]

If the former, those not congruously called lack sufficient grace, which
Jesuit theology does not allow. For they lack the congruity necessary for
conversion. Fonseca, however, maintains that it is contrary to God’s
providence for anyone to lack the aids necessary for salvation. If congruity
is not necessary, those not congruously called can be converted no less than
those congruously called. But if congruity is necessary, then, since it is not
in man’s power whether God calls him congruously or incongruously, culpable
nonconversion will not be imputed to the person who is not converted, since
it results from a lack of congruity that lies in God’s power alone, not man’s.
Suárez, disputing against physical determination, says quite rightly that
this follows. Reply. Suárez says that nothing is lacking to the person not
congruously called on man’s side, although something is lacking to him on
God’s side. *For,* he says, *foreknowledge of the effect is lacking in the one
case and not in the other; yet this very difference is not from God alone but
also from free choice. For it lies in the power of the man concerning whom God
foreknew that he would not consent to the caller to cause God to have
foreknown that he would consent to the calling. For it truly lies in his power
to consent, and thus it lies in his power to have an efficacious calling.*
Reply 1. The Jesuit is condemned by Fonseca’s words: *For Pelagius, attributing
too much to the will of our choice, ascribed the efficacy of divine calling
to the will’s free consent.* Bellarmine says, *This opinion is foreign to the
teaching of the divine Scriptures.* But does Suárez believe that the reader
has the head of

objection 1.]

[Marginalia: Fonseca, in the same place.]

Medusa and understands nothing? For if it lies in the unconverted man’s power
to cause God to have foreknown
that he would consent to the calling, actual conversion, and thus the efficacy
of grace, precedes God’s foreknowledge. What causes God to foreknow this
rather than that is prior to God’s foreknowledge. Why, then, does he twist
himself about in reducing the efficacy of grace to God’s foreknowledge, by
which God foreknows what will infallibly move freedom, when he admits that the
efficacy of grace is prior to God’s foreknowledge? Can what is prior be
reduced, as to its cause, to what is posterior? What? Are you not laughing,
Jesuit? And what else did Pelagius say? On this answer, congruous calling, at
least when employed, lies in the power of the person congruously called. They
have therefore invented this congruity in vain to avoid the error of Pelagius.
2. Because it lies in everyone’s power to hear preachers and to read and
meditate upon God’s word, by which all are called, all can be called
congruously. They have therefore invented two decrees in vain, one of common
providence and the other of predestination, according to which the reprobate
are called incongruously and the elect congruously. For if all can be called
congruously, the decree of providence can be erased; and by the same reasoning
all can be called incongruously, so the other decree is empty as well. 3.
Above, the Jesuit said that the efficacy of grace is not to be reduced to
incongruity itself; here he denies that it can be reduced to God’s
foreknowledge.

[1651 logical printed p. 507 | IA leaf 525 | PDF p. 525]

What remains? If not grace itself, only free choice remains.

3. Argument 2. The incongruous calling by which God calls many in the
circumstance in which he knows they will not obey conflicts with God’s
antecedent will, by which he wills each and every person to be saved. It also
conflicts with all the vows, desires, entreaties, appeals, and wishes by which
the Jesuits maintain that God earnestly testifies how truly he desires the
reprobate themselves to be saved. Indeed, he would thus desire it naturally,
and yet call them in a way by which he foreknows that they will incur a far
graver punishment and torment than if God did not call them at all. Truly
contradictory wills would exist in God, and God would not be free of deceit
and hypocrisy. Suárez answers many things here. 1. *As regards the permitting
will,* he says, *there is nothing absurd in God’s choosing to permit what he
certainly knows and foresees will happen—namely, that if such an order of
providence is maintained, so many men will be condemned. It merely has that
cause for wonder of which Paul speaks: O the depth, and so forth. But as
regards the ordering will, God does not choose this order of providence, nor
did he observe the times in which he calls men who will resist in order that
he might not save them efficaciously. For that is both unworthy of God’s
goodness and foreign to that will by which he wills all men to be saved.*
Reply 1. We agree with the Jesuit that it is not absurd for God to call those
who will resist. But given the Jesuit’s teaching that God wills each and every
person to be saved, it is utterly absurd.

[1651 logical printed p. 508 | IA leaf 526 | PDF p. 526]

For what earthly father, loving his son with a natural love and the most
intense passion, would knowingly and willingly permit him to fall into
torments that will never end? Although one must therefore say, “O the depth,”
must one not also say, “O monstrous thing! O permitting will unworthy of God
and foreign to that most overflowing natural love of God by which he
strenuously desires the reprobate to be saved”? According to that permitting
will he would allow them to fall into eternal destruction, and, though he
could call them otherwise with all his attributes intact, he would call them
in a way by which their condemnation—which he does not will with his natural
love—becomes far more grievous. 2. The Jesuit is not ashamed openly to
acknowledge that the permitting will is unworthy of God’s goodness and foreign
to the will by which he naturally wills all to be saved. There must plainly be
contrary wills in God here. Indeed, I have never been able to see how the
Jesuits and Arminians can dissolve the contrariety between God’s antecedent
will and the will by which he permits the greatest part of mankind to sin and
perish eternally. 3. God’s permitting will has a place only in sins. But to
call some incongruously is not a sin; it is an act both of justice and of
goodness. Yet it is absurd for God to perform acts of justice and goodness
permissively rather than by an effecting will. Scripture never ascribes God’s
good acts to a permitting will, nor does right reason allow it. *According to
the ordering will,* says Suárez, *God does not choose this order of
providence.*

[1651 logical printed p. 509 | IA leaf 527 | PDF p. 527]

But if this ordering will is absolute and does not depend upon creatures,
then God absolutely willed the destruction of the reprobate antecedently to
their merits. Now, Jesuit, preserve God’s natural will to save all if you can.
If it was not absolute but dependent upon creatures, and that by God’s
natural justice, then all perish and are saved by fate.

2. In every opinion it must necessarily be admitted that many who will resist
are called. Reply. It must be admitted in the Arminian opinion and in the
Jesuit opinion—but what then? The argument does not remove the difficulty. In
our view, however, in which we hold that God freely and by an absolute decree
antecedes all the creature’s acts, assigning eternal life to these for the
display of the glory of mercy and eternal death to those for the display of
the glory of justice, it is not absurd to call those who will resist. Indeed,
it is most worthy of God’s freedom: he has mercy on whom he wills and can do
with his own as it pleases him, including calling many who will resist. But
for God to will eternal life for each and every person and meanwhile arrange
such an order of providence in which he foreknows that they will infallibly
incur eternal death posits contrary wills in God. 3. Suárez replies: *Therefore,
in this whole order of providence God first and of himself intends the
salvation of men. Although he could have procured it in infinitely many ways
stored in the treasury of his wisdom, from all of them he chooses this one by
his will and choice and perhaps by the same knowledge by which he foresaw that,
if this order of providence were maintained, so many men would be condemned.*

[Marginalia: Reply 2, Suárez.]

[1651 logical printed p. 510 | IA leaf 528 | PDF p. 528]

*He foresaw that the same thing would have had to happen either by creating
other men or by providing for them otherwise, unless he conferred the most
special gifts of grace upon each and every one—something God was not bound to
do and that perhaps was not expedient, so that divine grace and justice might
be displayed together more fully.* Reply. It is false, on Jesuit doctrine,
that in this whole order God first and of himself intends the salvation of
men. For then he would first and of himself intend men’s obedience, which is
false. On the Jesuit’s teaching, every man’s free obedience and disobedience
had futurition antecedently to God’s intention, decree, and conditional
knowledge. But for God to intend that something be which already is
antecedently to his intention is truly absurd. If God first and of himself
intends men’s salvation, and does so by infinitely many possible ways of
infinite wisdom, why did he not accomplish it? Do you therefore claim that
God’s wisdom judges it congruous that there should be no reprobate? Then the
existence of reprobates and the perdition of as many as perish are opposed to
God’s wisdom, and the decree to reprobate and condemn men will be foolish. O
blasphemy! Indeed, you claim that the free choice of perishing men compelled
God and drove him away from every way of saving each and every person, whom by
a natural impulse he wills to save as a mother loves her infants. Contrary to
his primary intention and the dictate of infinite wisdom, he is forced by
fate to save no more, and no fewer, than are saved. Man’s free choice thus

[1651 logical printed p. 511 | IA leaf 529 | PDF p. 529]

drives God away from his original intended end and from all those infinite
ways, stored in wisdom’s treasury, of saving each
and every man. O infinite providence lulled to sleep! You will say that God
chose this order of providence by his own will and choice. I ask whether that
choice was free from every condition in men. If so, God will absolutely
convert and not convert all, and congruous calling is a useless fiction. If
the choice depends upon indifferent human freedom, he chooses this order by
necessity, not by will. 2. The claim that God first and of himself intends the
salvation of all conceals beneath it the claim that the condemnation of the
reprobate is not intended by God first and of himself but happens beyond his
intention, and therefore by chance. 3. To say that God could have procured the
salvation of each and every person, which he first and of himself intends, in
infinitely many ways, and yet follows an order of wisdom by which he fails of
that best intention and by which the greatest part of mankind, failing of the
intended salvation, falls into inescapable torments that will never end,
although God had willed the contrary by natural desire, displays not wisdom
but the Jesuit’s deceitful ingenuity. 4. He maintains that by the same
knowledge God foreknew that as many men as are now condemned would have been
condemned if he had established another order of providence. Yet in this way
he prostrates all God’s wisdom and providence, because human freedom, released
from every predetermining

[1651 logical printed p. 512 | IA leaf 530 | PDF p. 530]

providence of God, could always have committed the same sins it now commits.
In that case the
same men now condemned would have been condemned, since they sin antecedently
to God’s decree and knowledge; and the same men could have continued to sin
finally just as they do now, and so the same men now condemned could be
condemned. For they sinned antecedently to God’s knowledge and will, and thus
antecedently to all God’s providence. The Jesuit is therefore playing games
when he mentions the infinite ways stored in the treasury of divine wisdom by
which God could procure the salvation of all. If men are free agents worthy
of reward and punishment, no order of providence lies open to the all-wise
God except this one and only order. See, reader, I will reduce the infinite
number for you to a non-number, one miserable unit: all who now sin finally
could, antecedently to God’s providence, have sinned finally and been
condemned, just as they now do. For if God had chosen another order, he would
undoubtedly have had to exercise a different dominion over free acts than
allowing all of them futurition antecedently to his providence; Jesuit freedom
would thus have been overthrown. Otherwise the same men—neither more nor
fewer—could have been condemned as are now condemned. 5. Suárez says that it
was perhaps not expedient for the display of grace and justice to confer the
most special gifts of grace upon all, because then all would have been saved

[1651 logical printed p. 513 | IA leaf 531 | PDF p. 531]

and avenging justice would have lain hidden. Does the Jesuit not suggest that
God denied the reprobate efficacious grace by which they would be saved
because he had previously resolved absolutely to display avenging justice in
vessels of wrath—which is our position? Wine, women, and kings possess great
power, but truth conquers all, and here it has conquered the Jesuit. For when
asked why God, intending the salvation of each and every person, does not give
efficacious grace to each and every one, the Jesuit answers that perhaps—as
Calvin teaches—God had previously intended absolutely to display avenging
justice in many, upon whom, he says, he was not bound to confer the most
special gifts of grace. Calvin would surely have owed you the profoundest
thanks, Jesuit, if you had only erased the word “perhaps.” It is therefore
perhaps you, not Calvin, who errs; and the Jesuit judges that Calvin is
probably orthodox here.

4. Argument 3. From free consent or dissent given to a congruous or
incongruous calling antecedently to God’s conditional foreknowledge, it
follows that God cannot convert by any means the man whom he foreknows will
finally dissent from the caller, and cannot fail to convert the man whom he
foreknows will yield and obey the congruous calling even to the end. But this
is contrary to God’s omnipotence and dominion over the creature’s free acts.
Therefore that from which it follows must not be admitted. Proof of the
connection in the major: the object of conditional foreknowledge is

[1651 logical printed p. 514 | IA leaf 532 | PDF p. 532]

contingent and can either be or not be. Therefore the reprobate, since they
obey freely, can finally not obey, just as the elect can also finally not
obey. Consequently, God cannot by omnipotence convert the former or fail to
convert the latter. Suárez replies: *A man can be so disposed that he would
not be converted by any ordinary calling. It is therefore not improper that
God could, by that conditional knowledge, foresee concerning someone that he
would not be converted by all the ordinary aids of grace. Yet because, beyond
these aids, God can assist and call a man in infinitely many ways, it simply
cannot happen that God should not foreknow more graces or aids by which he
would know that the man would in fact be converted if he willed to furnish
them. Given the hypothesis of a good man who would always resist God’s
calling, it follows that God cannot efficaciously and freely convert such a
will, because he is presupposed to have known that it would always resist if
its free exercise were allowed. Yet he could convert that will, if he wished,
by preventing the exercise of freedom, and in this the divine power is
sufficiently displayed; on that hypothesis, however, as I said, it is simply
impossible.* Reply. O Jesuit conscience! If one man will obey God when he
calls and another will never obey, antecedently to conditional knowledge and
with a necessity antecedent to God’s decree, God cannot cause the future not
to be future. Neither Jesuit nor Arminian will ever remove this most fatal
fate from God by any machinery of evasions. For if God can arrange these acts

[1651 logical printed p. 515 | IA leaf 533 | PDF p. 533]

of the will otherwise, he would do it by a free decree. But, for the Jesuits,
all the evil acts of
the reprobate and all the obedient acts of the elect precede God’s free decree
and middle knowledge. For God freely to cause what is future antecedently to
his freedom not to be future implies an everlasting contradiction. 2. If God
knows extraordinary callings by which the reprobate would in fact be
converted, they would be converted either freely or necessarily. If freely,
the same argument returns with equal force; if necessarily, the dispute has
gone beyond its proper bounds. For, on the Jesuits’ position, conversion that
is praiseworthy and obedient will be neither obedient nor necessary. 3. He
reduces all God’s omnipotence to preventing the exercise of freedom. God’s
dominion and omnipotence therefore cannot cause more to be freely converted
than are actually converted, nor more to remain unconverted and be condemned
than are actually converted. But if God’s dominion extends to necessary acts,
by equal reason it extends to free acts. Yet listen, Jupiter: the Jesuits have
bound our Jehovah together with you by adamantine chains of fate. 4. The
Jesuit declared this hypothesis impossible when he said: *It is impossible
that he should resist by the will alone, but it will be infallible that he
falls to temptation in some way.* He sufficiently implies that the heart can
stand against two blows of temptation when it stands against one; and by the
same reasoning, because no temptation determines the will but leaves it in
its indifference—otherwise, according to the Jesuits, if one sins by
necessity, one does not sin at all—

[1651 logical printed p. 516 | IA leaf 534 | PDF p. 534]

it can happen that he yields to no temptation whatsoever. But how do you
prove it? *It is evident,* you say in the same place, *from the stronger case:
if God allows a man to be tempted by a demon in all the ways by which the
demon knows and can tempt him, it is impossible for him to resist by freedom
alone; rather, it will be infallible that he falls to temptation in some way.
But divine grace is far more powerful, and God knows more ways of inclining
and drawing the will to consent to good things. Therefore it is not contingent
that, from so many aids and callings of grace, some one should not be
foreknown to which the man will consent.* Reply 1. He says it is impossible
for an unskilled man to resist Satan in all possible ways by freedom alone.
Indeed, if by freedom alone he could avoid yielding to any temptation, the
prayer “Lead us not into temptation” would be pointless. 2. If it is
impossible for him to resist all temptations by freedom alone, he does not sin
by failing to resist, for the Jesuits hold that there is no sin in
impossibilities. 3. If such a man is equipped with sufficient grace, he can
resist all things as much as succumb to all. Otherwise grace is not
sufficient, and it is not contingent for a man equipped with sufficient grace
to sin or not to sin. Say, then, that it is necessary—you dare not. For then I
would infer that neither sinning nor not sinning occurs there, if the Jesuit
dogmas are not to collapse. 4. The fact that God’s grace is far more powerful
than diabolical temptation does not establish what the Jesuit denied unless
grace bends the will undeviatingly, in which case freedom does not remain.
Therefore it always remains contingent whether man is moved to obey by that
more powerful grace or not.

[1651 logical printed p. 517 | IA leaf 535 | PDF p. 535]

5. And what if God knows more ways of drawing the will to consent? Let there
be three thousand times a million ways. If they always act persuasively and
resistibly, there can be a man who resists God’s calling to the end of his
life; and then I ask whether God could convert such a man while preserving
Jesuit freedom. Indeed, that would be impossible. 6. The Jesuit contradicts
Jupiter himself. For above, figure 8, p. 148, he gave a possible hypothesis:
*For that whole congruity*—and thus the moral drawing of the will by any number
of ways—*of itself produces only a certain moral certainty, to which it is not
absolutely repugnant that free choice should resist a calling congruous in
this way.* 7. Finally, he says: Therefore it cannot happen that among so many
aids and callings of grace—namely, those moving freedom morally and
resistibly; unless you say this, you say nothing—some one should not be
foreknown to which the man will consent. Do you dare say, Jesuit: Therefore it
is necessary that God know some calling that acts resistibly and which the
man cannot resist? But then the calling will assuredly be both resistible and
irresistible, which involves a contradiction, not to mention that God is
wrongly said to know a future thing contingently. For if it is future, God
cannot fail to know it. Yet because it is future contingently, and therefore
can happen never to be, knowledge of it could have been absent from God and
thus does not

[1651 logical printed p. 518 | IA leaf 536 | PDF p. 536]

exist in him necessarily.

5. Argument 4. No other efficacious cause can be given here than the human
will. For I assume from our opponents’ doctrine that the same prevenient
grace, equal in degree and measure, is present in the person called
incongruously and the person called congruously, as Fonseca and Suárez grant.
The cause why grace is efficacious in the latter but not the former is not
congruous calling. For God’s foreknowledge presupposes the efficacy of grace,
as all knowledge presupposes its object, and therefore does not produce it;
hence foreknowledge is not the cause. 2. Time and place are not causes. They
are external adjuncts destitute of all causality, unless they say that nature
prescribes a time for human conversion to God just as roses bloom in June—a
claim that will make the reader laugh. Nor can place contribute anything of
causality: Matthew is converted at the city gate, Peter at sea, and Zacchaeus
in a fig tree. It is impossible even to conceive what weight these things
bring to free assent. 3. The will’s natural disposition cannot be the cause.
If it is supernatural, it nevertheless leaves the will to its own
indifference. It does not overthrow freedom; and a natural disposition cannot
of itself exercise an effective influence upon a supernatural effect. 4. Nor
is the bare application and approximation of the agent to what receives its
action a cause. It is a manner and condition of causing, not a cause. For
approximation to fire is not what heats; it is only a condition of heating.

[1651 logical printed p. 519 | IA leaf 537 | PDF p. 537]

Fire alone heats. If bare approximation or such application added any causal
power to
prevenient grace, grace would not be equal in the incongruously called person,
who lacks such application, and the congruously called person, contrary to the
hypothesis. Calling, therefore, precisely as congruous, is destitute of all
causal force and cannot be a cause. Let it therefore be granted, in the fifth
place, that free choice alone is the cause. For God foreknows the man’s
obedience to the calling prior by nature to calling him congruously, because
no one says that the man obeys the caller because God foreknows it, but the
reverse. What does Suárez say here, he who has learned to turn and twist his
talent into every form, so that you might call him Proteus rather than a
theologian? *In execution,* he says, *that efficacy is never exercised without
the cooperation of free choice, although it is not reduced to it simply, but
to calling and assisting grace. In the intention or preparation of such a
calling and aid, however—an aid that is not only sufficient but will
infallibly be efficacious—the reduction is made to the divine will alone.*
Vásquez speaks more candidly: *It is not in our power that a congruous calling
be given, but it is indeed in our power that it be efficacious.* And Fonseca
reduces it to an absolute decree. Reply. Suárez thinks he is not patronizing
Pelagius if he says that the efficacy of grace is to be reduced not to free
choice alone, and yet to free choice. 2. He wants it to be reduced to calling
grace, but falsely. For the efficacy of grace is ultimately and causally
reduced

[Marginalia: Same work, folio 8, p. 151.]

[Marginalia: Vásquez, vol. 1, folio 549.]

[1651 logical printed p. 520 | IA leaf 538 | PDF p. 538]

to that upon which conversion depends—why it occurs rather than not and in
this man rather than another. But it is not from the calling or from assisting
grace that conversion occurs rather than not, and Peter is converted rather
than Judas. For the calling is equal in substance and thus in causal force,
and assisting grace is equal in the converted and the unconverted. Indeed,
according to the Jesuits it comes from Peter’s will rather than Judas’s that
he is converted. Conversion and nonconversion cannot in any way come from a
common cause as the efficacy of grace—that is, as the actual efficiency of
grace. 3. God’s will and intention are not that to which the efficacy of grace
is reduced. For this intention is either conditional or absolute, and on
Jesuit principles the efficacy of grace can be reduced to neither. If the
intention and will are conditional, God willed from eternity, on a hypothesis,
that grace would be efficacious in Peter and not Judas. I now grant the
hypothesis in all candor, namely, that Peter would obey calling grace; no other
condition can be imagined. Suppose, then, God resolved as follows: From the
window of middle knowledge I foresee that grace will be efficacious in Peter,
not Judas; therefore I will and intend that it be efficacious in Peter rather
than Judas. Would any sober man say that the efficacy which has being
antecedently to God’s will comes from God’s will as an effect from a cause?
Wake up at last, Jesuit—or sleep off that Pelagian intoxication.

If the will is absolute, then by an absolute decree God determined all the
free conversions of all the elect, and by the same reasoning all the free
nonconversions of the reprobate as well. The Jesuit or Arminian stomach will
digest iron before it digests this. “O, now freedom is removed,” they say;
“all religion is overthrown; fate, Stoicism, Manichaeism, and what not are
introduced!” Grevinchovius tries to aid his allies here, as does the noble
talent of Peter Fonseca. They want all things reduced to an absolute decree as
the highest cause. Their argument runs: If Peter is placed in these and not
other circumstances and in this order of things, and if he is called while so
disposed, he will obey the divine calling. But I, says God, resolve absolutely
to place Peter in this order of things and to call him while so disposed.
Therefore my absolute will is the first cause why Peter obeys my calling.
Reply 2. This is not the first and highest syllogism into which the whole
efficacy of calling is to be reduced. 1. The major premise is not necessary.
Indeed, given the Jesuit and Arminian indifference in Peter’s will, the
connected major proposition is no more true than false, nor false than true.
For in whatever order of things Peter is placed, will that order determine his
will to assent to God’s calling? I ask, will merely a banquet, a cheerful
mind, some fear of hell, or

[Marginalia: Pages 216–217.]

[1651 logical printed p. 522 | IA leaf 540 | PDF p. 540]

circumstances of this kind determine Peter to obey God’s calling? Then the
efficacy of grace would be reduced to the banquet, not God’s will; and because
Peter’s will is freely moved by a sumptuous banquet or by a mind made somewhat
merrier after the cups, this free motion must antecede God’s middle knowledge
and decree. How, then, will it be reduced to the decree? Behold where the
absolute decree has now vanished and withdrawn into thin air. 2. Nor is the
minor premise of that syllogism any more true than false, given the
indifference of Peter’s will. God does not absolutely place Peter in such an
order of things: Peter freely drinks, attends the banquet, and goes to the
sacred sermon at the hour and place where he is converted. He freely slept,
and so is better disposed by this. Indeed, he freely hears the preacher
propose a congruous calling in God’s name, since he could fail to hear; and he
freely directs his mind to the sense, wording, aim, equity, and rational force
of the calling. For the free will and affections, like the Devil’s rhetoricians,
can at that time and place draw the intellect away to ponder earthly and
emptier things. Unless, therefore, they say that free acts are absolutely
determined by God—which to them is the most fatal fate—the truth of the minor
premise in no way depends upon God’s decree. Indeed, it is true or false
antecedently to God’s will and middle knowledge. Hence the plainly contrary
conclusion follows: Peter’s will, not God’s

[1651 logical printed p. 523 | IA leaf 541 | PDF p. 541]

absolute decree, is the cause why Peter, congruously called, answers God’s
calling. 3. Grevinchovius admits that God foreknew Peter, placed in such
circumstances, would obey God’s calling. Fonseca, Suárez, and Bellarmine say
the same. Who, then, does not see that the absolute human will is prior here
to God’s will? For middle knowledge does not precede the free acts of either
the divine or the human will. 4. Their syllogism thus flows: [If Peter is
called in such circumstances, he will obey the calling; but God absolutely
decreed to place Peter in such circumstances; therefore Peter will obey]. The
conclusion is not absolute, as it ought to be if Peter’s act of believing is
ultimately resolved into God’s absolute decree. For the conclusion follows
the inferior part; but the major premise has contingent, not necessary,
truth. Therefore the conclusion will be of that kind, as in this example: If
Peter runs, he must move; but Peter runs; therefore he must move. The
conclusion cannot have necessary truth, because Peter does not have to run;
he runs contingently. 5. I will frame for them a syllogism prior to the one
they have framed, indeed, one whose truth will cause their syllogism to stand:
If Peter is placed in such circumstances, he will be free to believe or not
believe. But it is Peter’s will to place or not place himself in such
circumstances. Therefore it will be Peter’s will to believe or

[Marginalia: Page 217.]

[1651 logical printed p. 524 | IA leaf 542 | PDF p. 542]

not believe. This syllogism precedes God’s absolute decree. For it is the
object of
conditional knowledge, and therefore their absolute decree is resolved into
the created will.

Argument 5. If sufficient grace is given to those not congruously called, by
which they can be converted if they will, then despite all incongruity of the
calling it can happen that they are converted. For incongruity does not bend
the will infallibly or undeviatingly but leaves it to its indifference. Yet
this is absurd: 1. because Fonseca wishes the actual obedience of the
congruously called person to be included in the essential concept of
congruous calling, and actual disobedience also in the essence of congruous
calling; 2. because here too the efficacy of grace would be reduced to free
choice; and 3. because it follows that congruity is not necessarily required
for anyone to be converted, since those not congruously called would also be
converted. Nor will they satisfy us if they say that incongruous calling
presupposes a will that will resist the calling. I then say that the will’s
future resistance is prior by nature to the incongruity’s being, which is
absurd. For an effect is not prior to its cause, whether as a cause or as the
manner or rationale of acting in a cause; therefore the will’s resistance
cannot be presupposed by congruity. Perhaps they will say that sufficient
grace can of itself produce obedience with respect to the human will as a
species, but in fact does not produce it with respect to such an individual
will—namely, Judas’s will as it is

[1651 logical printed p. 525 | IA leaf 543 | PDF p. 543]

placed and disposed in such circumstances. I dissolve this as follows:
Grace sufficient to convert the will as a species is also sufficient to
convert this individual will. Otherwise, if the corruption in Judas is
stronger than sufficient grace can remove, the grace is not sufficient for
this will—Judas’s—to be converted; and thus Judas does not sin because he
does not will to be converted. 2. Fonseca says that congruous calling in fact
removes rebellion. But it removes it either as a calling or as congruous. If
as a calling, then, since the calling is still the same in substance, as
Fonseca admits, when applied to the congruously and the incongruously called,
the incongruously called man can still be converted no less than the
congruously called man, which is absurd. But if the calling removes rebellion
as congruous, then bare congruity and such application, precisely considered,
will possess causal power to subdue rebellion. But that a thing which is not
a being but a mode of being should possess causal power will give me cause to
ask Diogenes to return the mina paid to the man who taught me metaphysics.
Bellarmine, sharp-nosed enough to evade the blow of this argument, says: *To
grace belong not only an internal motion or arousal but also the circumstance
of place and time, and that in itself.* Reply. The great Jesuit surely could
not remain silent; he preferred to say that Peter’s being a fisherman and
hearing Christ preach in a boat were grace to Peter. Thus, for you, the sea,
the boat, and perhaps the third hour of the day are graces. A grace

[1651 logical printed p. 526 | IA leaf 544 | PDF p. 544]

certainly worthy of the Jesuit school, the fiercest enemy of God’s grace among
the papal prelates! But let these be graces: the incongruously called man does
not lack as many circumstances as the congruously called man. Hear Fonseca’s
escape: *If the calling is congruous for those called, that is, if it is going
to attain its effect in them, it too, like the substance of the calling, will
belong to prevenient grace. For it is a great grace to the person who is
called in the circumstance in which he is foreknown to obey. But if it is not
congruous, since it is not going to attain the effect, the substance of the
calling itself will indeed pertain to prevenient grace, but this manner of
application will not; it must be referred to common providence, because God
does not distribute equal things to all but deems it enough if he confers upon
each things sufficient for salvation unless the man himself wills them to be
lacking to him.* Reply 1. Prevenient grace will not be equal in the
congruously and incongruously called man; indeed, it cannot be, if congruous
calling itself is a part of prevenient grace. 2. Applying the calling at such
a time and in such a manner cannot be grace unless it heals nature. For,
according to Valentia and the great scholastics, healing nature is the formal
effect of grace; but such application does not heal nature and formally
possesses no causality. It is not a cause or a being, but a mode and condition
of a cause or being. The Jesuit therefore raves against the best-known first
principles. 3. If being called in such a manner is grace, it is gracious and
not owed. Yet antecedently to God’s middle knowledge, and thus to the decree,
it was true that God would call in such a manner and that the man would obey
at such a time.

Therefore, if contingent fate constraining God is grace, or rather a fated
debt, let them judge. 4. What is the source of this inequality of callings,
that according to the decree of predestination God calls some congruously and
according to the decree of common providence calls others incongruously? Does
it come from an absolute decree? But I proved above that no such decree
concerning men can exist in God without a notable injury to human freedom. On
their view all obedience would therefore be removed and religion trampled
down. Consequently, the inequality of these callings and decrees will come
from men’s merits. Thus merits and the free acts of obedience and disobedience
will precede the first absolute decree. This decree is therefore not the
highest cause of the efficacy of grace, contrary to the Jesuits’ teaching.

Argument 6. If the grace of calling infallibly produces conversion because

falsely urge conversion by an argument drawn from the opportunity of the

voice, do not harden your hearts. Isaiah 55:6: Seek the Lord while he may be
found. 2 Corinthians 6:2: Behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the
day of salvation. Joel 2:12: Now, therefore, says Jehovah, return to me with

be true: Today is not the day of salvation; the present is not the time to

when it is
the time at which we are to be called congruously—is the accepted time. God
would indeed know that the present time was not the time best suited to their

Argument 7. The reason men are not converted by God is that they are not among
Christ’s sheep, that they are not drawn by the Father, that God did not give
them a heart to understand, that they did not hear and learn from the Father,
and that Jehovah did not circumcise their heart—not that God does not draw
them in such a manner and at such a time.

Argument 8. Congruous grace produces its effect either necessarily or
contingently. If the former, the converted cannot fail to be converted and
therefore are not converted freely. If

[1651 logical printed p. 529 | IA leaf 547 | PDF p. 547]

contingently, they can fail to be converted, which is false for our opponents;
the same reasoning will press upon incongruous conversion. They say that
congruity includes middle foreknowledge, by which God infallibly foreknows a
free act as future, though not necessarily. Reply. By middle foreknowledge God
foreknows a possible free act that will never be future no less infallibly
than he foreknows a future free act. It will therefore be repugnant for there
to be more free acts than there are, and acts that do not exist will be unable
not to exist. Because they are future antecedently to this congruity, even if
God had called these men incongruously, they cannot fail to be converted
unless God’s middle knowledge is mistaken, which is absurd; and even if God
had called the unconverted in the most congruous manner, they would not have
been converted. They will say that congruous conversion essentially includes
conversion. Reply. Everyone now sees that they beg the question. Indeed, for
a free effect to be essentially included in a contingently operating cause is
the most absurd of absurdities.

the unconverted. For God, as a cause exercising real influence, does nothing

he owes thanks to pure nature’s choice that made the distinction. 3. But

Argument 14. Given this Jesuit principle—[God gives efficacious concurrence

absurd. But if God gives or denies congruous calling by absolute freedom, the
Jesuits will return to Calvin and must bid farewell to God’s conditional

6. The Jesuits do not prove this calling from the Scriptures but from

rightly. It must therefore be known that a motion is called congruous in

1. When God moves the will through the word’s persuasive arguments, so that
he moves it most congruously in the class of persuasion, since intellect,
will, and affection would accord with the character and disposition of the
word. In this way God calls everyone in the visible church congruously. But
the Jesuits do not wish the human affections to be adjusted to the word; they
wish the word and God’s volition to be accommodated to the character and
individual dispositions of the human affections. I have never been able to
understand this adjustment. For the adjustment of the word to the character
of the affections can be understood in only three ways. 1. To a purely natural
character. But then there would be no reason why the congruously called man
would obey. For what influence would pure nature exercise unless it were
elevated by grace? Pure nature is not strong enough to influence supernatural
acts. 2. If it is adjusted to the character of corrupt nature, conversion
would never follow. For the natural man regards the things of God as sheer
foolishness, and nothing is more distasteful to him than the simplicity of the
gospel. 3. To the character of a nature elevated by prevenient grace. But
then, since according to the Jesuits equal grace can be in the converted and
the unconverted, the calling would not be more congruous for the converted
than the unconverted. Perhaps they grant a fourth way, namely, the adjustment
of the calling to the temperament and individual dispositions superadded to
pure nature. Yet

[1651 logical printed p. 534 | IA leaf 552 | PDF p. 552]

this cannot be said consistently with sound reason. For what agreement is
there between invisible things, God’s eternal glory, and the lot of God’s
children, which is oppressed by every calamity? They must be ready to expose
themselves throughout life to the cross, hunger, starvation, cold,
persecutions, disgrace, exile, and the denial of desires, and finally to
endure the most bitter and untimely death, if God so wills. What agreement, I
ask, is there between these things and individual dispositions? Indeed, there
seems to me no small contrariety. It is remarkable that Scripture never
mentions any such disposition or circumstance, if conversion or nonconversion
depends upon it. God especially ought to have given in Scripture rules and
precepts by which men would place themselves in such a location, time,
sadness, convivial or musical cheerfulness, or the like, so as to be disposed
congruously for God’s calling—not to mention that, by the Jesuits’ own
admission, these dispositions have no activity for bending the will this way
or that. 2. God moves congruously insofar as he determines the will of the
elect in the way he knows accords with the character of natural freedom. For
he knows the efficacy of grace and how to drive the will while leaving its
freedom unharmed. In this sense we too grant that efficacious calling is
congruous. 3. It might mean that he foreknows acts by a mixed knowledge
antecedently to his motion and then adjusts his motion to the character of the
will. This we utterly deny.

7. They cite this from Augustine: *From this it appears that some possess in

gift of understanding in their very disposition, as Twisse observes. But the
Jesuits maintain that all can naturally be moved and induced if a congruous

nothing about the manner of calling. 3. This Augustinian congruity concerns
the substance of the calling, as appears from the preceding words: *But where
are the rest left except in the mass of perdition by God’s just judgment?

Augustine cannot speak there of the manner of applying the calling, but of the

divine absolute predestination, as is evident from his words. But for the
Jesuits such a predestination of free acts is a chimerical fable.

8. They cite Augustine to Simplicianus: *It is true that many are called, but
few chosen. Those are chosen who are congruously called; those who did not
accord or were not adjusted to the calling were not chosen, because they did
not follow, although they were called.* In the same place: *It is therefore
not of him who wills, and so forth, but of God who has mercy, who called in the
manner that he knows accords with the man so that he does not reject the one
who calls.* Reply. I would not deny that Augustine—as is in fact the case—
wrote these things before the Pelagian heresy arose. Further, Augustine uses
the word “congruity” very frequently because he always wished to anticipate
that claim of Pelagius and human reason according to which the omnipotent
bending of the free will seems to imply that freedom is compelled and forced
by fate—a charge the Pelagians and Semi-Pelagians later often made against
Augustine. Augustine therefore expounded that most omnipotent bending through
such a moral congruity, by which God bent the will where he wills while the
nature of freedom remained unharmed. Likewise, against the Jesuits and
Arminians we are compelled to teach such a physical predetermination as
nevertheless leaves room for judgment and vital choice and preserves, in a
wonderful manner, the natural indifference of freedom. Augustine therefore
establishes our congruity, not the Jesuit one, since he openly taught the most
omnipotent inspirations and drawings. *He is truly called omnipotent for no
other reason than that he can do whatever he wills, and the effect of the
omnipotent will is not hindered by the will of any creature.* He likewise
says, *No human choice resists God when he wills to save someone.*

the absolute indifference not to obey God when he calls remains. 4. Augustine

shrink from a scholastic disputation (διατριβὴν) with the Jesuits and
