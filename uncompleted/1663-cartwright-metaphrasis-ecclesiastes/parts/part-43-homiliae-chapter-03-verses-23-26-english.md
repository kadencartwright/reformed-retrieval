VERSE 23. But when I turned again and saw all the oppressed who suffer beneath
the sun, behold, there were the tears of those who are oppressed, but there
was no comforter for them, nor was there strength to escape from the hand of
their oppressors; in short, there was no comforter for them.

VERSE 24. At first I praised the dead themselves, who are already dead, above
the living who still live.

VERSE 25. But afterward the man who has not yet existed and has not seen this
most evil deed which is done beneath the sun appeared better than either.

VERSE 26. For I saw that every labor and all the rightness of a man's deeds
became a cause of envy toward him from his neighbor. This too is vanity and
affliction of spirit.

After setting forth the flesh's perception and inquiry concerning those who
are oppressed, the Holy Spirit closes this treatment with his judgment upon
the entire condition both of the oppressors and of the oppressed.

The parts concern:

1. Those afflicted by more powerful men.
2. Those afflicted by others who are their equals or inferiors.
3. The judgment of the flesh.
4. The judgment of the Holy Spirit.

First, in verse 23, the efficient cause of this carnal reasoning is set forth;
then the judgment itself in verses 24 and 25. The cause is described: men see
the less powerful oppressed by the more powerful just as little fish in the
sea are swallowed by larger ones. The calamity of this oppression is
magnified first by its effects, because tears are pressed from the oppressed.
This is intensified by a circumstance, namely, their deprivation and lack of
a comforter; then by other circumstances, the oppressed man's inability to
escape from the hand and violence of the oppressor. This is further magnified
by comparison with a greater case, because not even then does anyone hasten
to console him. His turning himself to look and contemplate with the mind's
eyes shows the wise man wholly attentive to the matter he treats, whereas the
fool's eyes wander here and there. Proverbs 27:14. This must be done above all
in the study and meditation of the Word. Because it is not done, it is no
wonder if everything is full of fools.

Here we must first observe the bitterness and sharpness of violence and
oppression. When goods are plundered, reputation violated, or the body beaten,
the injury is not confined to these things: the spirit is tortured and made
bloody. Once it is afflicted, the body's radical moisture is consumed and
life itself is often assailed. Hence the prophets say that those who oppress
others have bloody hands. Isaiah 10. For when the blood is exhausted by grief,
men turn pale; and grief also dries and corrupts the bones, which are like the
foundations of the flesh. Many men whose minds recoil from shedding blood
nevertheless permit themselves to despoil others of their goods. While they
wish to appear to abhor blood, they commit both crimes, theft and murder.

He magnifies the misery of the oppressed from the accompanying lack of
comforters. There are men who wound, but none who bind the wound; assassins or
cutters to inflict the blow,

but no surgeons to wrap it. Finally, there are men who press out tears, but
none who apply a little cloth to wipe them away. Jeremiah complains of this
in Lamentations 1:2. Therefore, let us learn to console the afflicted. James
1:26; Romans 12:15; Hebrews 10:32–34; Job 4:3–4.

He magnifies the misery of the oppressed by comparison with greater things:
men do not send aid or bring consolation even when the oppressed have no
strength or ability by which to rescue themselves from their oppressors'
claws or jaws. They ought to console the afflicted man because of the injury
which occurs first and by which he cannot help but be affected. But when they
see that he is to be shaken and crushed beneath this burden because he lacks
the strength to free himself, it is the height of cruelty not to aid one who
labors so grievously. It is the mark of a freeborn man and of the greatest and
most generous spirit to extend a helping hand to the weaker man. Thus, if
someone sees two men fighting—one robust and strong, the other weak and sick;
one tall, the other a dwarf—he should put his hand beneath the weak man who
has been crushed or thrown to the ground by the robust and tall man, and aid
him. Why, then, should men not do from conscience toward God what they do
willingly enough to catch the breeze of popular favor, especially since by
this act they conform themselves to the image of God, who is the God of the
wretched, the father of orphans, and the patron of widows? See 2 Corinthians
1:3 and 7:6.

Above all, this duty belongs to those who rule and hold power, who ought to be
an eye to the blind, a foot to the lame, a mouth to the mute, and an arm to the
weak. This is Solomon's judgment with regard to what is beneath the sun in
verse 24. Now the flesh's judgment arising from the condition of the oppressed
follows: that the condition of the dead is preferable to that of the living.
Thus the flesh's perception is that it is better to wish not to live than not
to live according to the desire of one's mind, as though life were not God's
gift and to be numbered among God's benefits. Many testimonies of Scripture
make clear that it is, for life is set before those who walk in God's
commandments as a reward. To be admitted to the spectacle of the world's
fabric, constructed with such art and skill; to enjoy the light of the sun,
moon, and stars; God's bounty in food, drink, and clothing for the body; and
the society and fellowship of men ought to affect us with a taste of God's
singular kindness. Above all, the fact that we are permitted here to advance
in the knowledge of God and sing his praises ought wonderfully to console and
refresh us against the anguish caused by violence. We count it a pleasure to
see one creature of God, such as a lion we have never seen before; how much
more the adornment of the entire world? If the oppression of one mortal man
obliterates and destroys our sense of all these things, and if we despise and
trample God's bounty underfoot because men bear us ill will, that cannot be
free from the mark of the deepest ingratitude.

If he had concluded that the condition of those who have died in the Lord is
better than that of those who are alive, the conclusion would have been just.
Otherwise it is utterly false, since all who judge man's good or evil by the
perception of the flesh pass from this life's momentary and very light
troubles to dreadful torments of both body and soul which will endure forever.
We experience even in God's servants that the judgment of the flesh is to
prefer death to life whenever one is pressed by this life's troubles, because
the remnants of the flesh remain fastened to them. Job, Jonah, and Elijah in
particular all desired, when grievously afflicted, to exchange life for death.
Here, then, we must consider the perversity of our nature. When affairs are
prosperous and flow according to our mind, we value our life beyond measure
and wish to preserve it at the expense of our neighbors and even of divine
glory; but when the winds blow against us,

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA175 | printed pp. 175–176 | begins with complete illico after catchword on p. 174; ends before complete lætetur from catchword at foot of p. 176]

we immediately become disgusted with life and are ready to cast it away as a
thing of no worth. Therefore, when God's chastening hand presses us, let us set
before our eyes the good things which remain to us, so that by looking upon
them we may bear adversity more equably. Let us also remember that celebrated
saying from Job 1: it is absurd and improper for us eagerly and willingly to
receive good things from God but not likewise to bear the evils sent by him.

VERSE 25. But afterward the man who has not yet existed and has not seen this
most evil deed which is done beneath the sun appeared better than either.

This is an intensification of the flesh's preceding opinion, but here it
withdraws still farther from the truth than before. For it affirms that never
to have existed is better than to be either among the dead, because they were
afflicted by evils while alive, or among those who live uncomfortably and
calamitously. The blemish of a mind ungrateful to God adheres to the former
opinion because it values this present life lightly; but this opinion also
takes away every hope of the future life.

Here a difficulty arises. We read that men who were illuminated only by the
light of reason and had acquired some belief in the soul's immortality were
raised to some hope of a better good by a life lived honorably and
incorruptly. This strongly conflicts with the present opinion. I answer that
this occurred to men who lived according to the conviction of their minds:
the belief that the soul would be transferred to a better condition settled
within them. But because it was only an opinion, some grievous affliction
easily shook it out of them and caused them to lose all hope of a better life.
If, however, this opinion of the flesh is understood only of the wicked, who
do not rest by faith upon Christ, it is most true, and it is to some extent
confirmed by Christ the Savior in the person of Judas alone. For although it
is not absolutely good that the wicked did not exist, it would nevertheless
have been good for them if they had never been born. Matthew 26. It belongs to
the godly, however, to be led by these evils to acknowledge their sins, to be
led by evils to patient endurance of evils, to aspire to another and better
life, and to settle most certainly that judgment will occur at the end of the
world. 2 Thessalonians 2.

Here it is evident how dangerous it is to follow reason's counsel in sacred
affairs and to hand it the rudder and reins. It rushes headlong, observes
neither measure nor reason, and slides from bad to worse. What, then, will
happen to those who disdain reason as their guide and are carried away by
mere sense and desire toward things which brute animals also seek?

VERSE 26. For I saw that every labor and all the rightness of a man's deeds
became a cause of envy toward him from his neighbor. This too is vanity and
affliction of spirit.

He describes another efficient cause, arising not from more powerful men but
from equals or even inferiors: because men, through envy of those who live
industriously and uprightly, leap forth to injure others either by violence or
fraud. If men were treated contemptuously because they lived idly and gave
themselves to committing crimes, they would receive the reward due their
wickedness and have no ground to complain of oppression. But when they are
treated with contempt and burdened with injuries for things well done, it
reveals the greatest possible confusion of all affairs. For envy is pain
conceived from another man's prosperity. This is the chief cause why men are
miserably anguished and tormented: they are often treated wickedly, with such
indignity, beyond what they deserve, and even contrary to their deserts. Yet
the fact that others violate them undeservedly ought instead to console them
above all. For no one, Peter says, should suffer as an evildoer; but if he
suffers as a Christian, let him not be ashamed, but let him glorify God in
this respect and in this respect

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA177 | printed pp. 177–178 | begins with complete lætetur after catchword on p. 176; ends on p. 177 before the dividing rule and CAPUT IV]

rejoice.

Examples of this envy appear in Cain against Abel, the sons of Jacob against
Joseph, Daniel's colleagues against Daniel himself, and the Jews against
Christ. Although a brother is very closely related to his brother, he does
not by this singular closeness escape the envy of his own proper brother.
Solomon expressly notes this when he says that envy proceeds from one who is
joined to the other by fellowship. Next, let us take care that through envy of
others' virtue or prosperity we do not drive men into this dreadful error, so
that they settle in their minds that it would have been better never to have
been born than to suffer such things. Let us instead do what belongs to love:
rejoice in things well done and grieve over their opposites. Let us do this
not only concerning those joined to us by kinship, but also concerning those
who pursue us with hatred, in agreement with Christ's command that we bless
those who curse us.

So much for the judgment of the flesh. Now follows the Holy Spirit's opinion,
by which he concludes what he has set before himself throughout this entire
book: both that these men oppress and that those men are oppressed are the
very height of vanity and affliction of spirit.
