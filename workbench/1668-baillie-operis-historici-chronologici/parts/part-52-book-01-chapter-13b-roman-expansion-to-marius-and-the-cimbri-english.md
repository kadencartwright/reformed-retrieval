This place requires us to sample briefly some matters from Roman history.
After the Romans had exterminated the kings of Macedonia and so broken the
Seleucids that there was no further fear from them, they cast aside every veil
of shame and revealed to the world their monstrous pride, greed, and cruelty,
long kept hidden. They reduced not only Macedonia but also all the rest of
Greece, which they had long courted, into provinces. They dissolved the
Achaean commonwealth, though it had done no wrong, and forbade its cities to
maintain their league any longer. They reduced Corinth, which clung more
tenaciously to its ancient liberty, to ashes, killed most of its citizens by
the sword, and sold the survivors with their wives and children at auction.
Thus the rest of Greece, terrified by this dreadful example of severity and
pressed beneath the Roman yoke, however harsh, no longer dared open its mouth.

In the same year they produced an equally unjust example of cruelty against
Carthage. They had enriched Masinissa, king of Numidia, with many spoils from
the Carthaginians because of the service he rendered Scipio in the Second
Punic War. Not content to have enlarged the broad lands of his kingdom by the
addition of a great territory of the Carthaginians given to him by the Romans,
he further wished to snatch from them day by day what remained to the
Carthaginians under their treaty with Rome. Since there was no end to
Masinissa’s injuries, the Carthaginians, after bringing many complaints to the
Romans without result, at last secretly took up arms against Masinissa. He was
then more than ninety years old, yet in the Numidian manner he daily bore the
toils of military service on horseback without a saddle. Although the
Carthaginians had not recovered their former power, they had by then returned
to considerable strength. The Romans, distracted at that time by various wars
in Spain, Gaul, and Italy, sent envoys to Africa. If they found Masinissa
defeated, they were to reconcile him with the Carthaginians; if victorious,
they were to take up arms against Carthage as a treaty-breaker, because the
Carthaginians had begun a war against others without consulting them. The
envoys found Masinissa far superior to the Carthaginians, for he had destroyed
more than half of their army of 120,000. Accordingly, as their instructions
required, the envoys declared war on Carthage. Masinissa complained bitterly,
as though they were tearing a morsel from his jaws and taking away the entire
reward of his victories.

Through fear of the Romans the Carthaginians offered everything to the Senate.
The Romans first demanded three hundred hostages from the noblest families,
and these were sent to Rome. Second, they demanded the fleet; they received it
too and burned it in sight of Carthage. Third, they demanded the arms of the
city. Even these, though with the greatest reluctance, were nevertheless
handed over, and in such quantity that they would have been more than enough
to arm all Africa. Yet the Romans were still unsatisfied, being determined to
avenge by Carthage’s destruction the calamities they had formerly suffered
from Hannibal.

The fate of Carthage was debated long and fiercely in the Roman Senate.
Scipio Nasica, chief man of the Senate, strove with all his power to preserve
the ancient city, so that the Carthaginians might be a means of courage and
every kind of virtue for the youth of Rome. Cato, however, now more than
eighty years old, finally persuaded the Senate to destroy it, lest it someday
destroy Rome.

In accordance with this secret decision of the Senate, the envoys finally
made a fourth demand: that Carthage be abandoned and its people choose for
themselves whatever new settlement they wished, provided it was sixty stadia
from the sea. On receiving this demand the people truly went mad. They stoned
the senators who had handed over the hostages, fleet, and arms, and fashioned
new weapons, a fleet, and instruments of war day and night, until with
incredible industry they had assembled such forces by land and sea that they
inflicted many defeats upon the Romans and Masinissa together, routed several
consuls and various armies, and would have destroyed them completely had the
valor of Scipio alone not stood in the way.

This Scipio, a young man of thirty and grandson of the elder Africanus, left
the army for Rome intending to seek the aedileship. Yet while thinking
nothing of it, he obtained the consulship from the people long before the
legal age, and the province of Africa and the destruction of Carthage were
entrusted to him. When he returned to the army as consul, he immediately
trained the soldiers in the ancient discipline so well that he soon shut the
Carthaginians within the city walls under a close siege and finally took it
by force. There was an immense slaughter of the Carthaginian people, and the
city was consumed by a continuous fire of seventeen days, while Scipio wept,
in accordance with Cato’s cruel judgment.

Hasdrubal, the Carthaginians’ commander, surrendered, but his wife, a woman of
greater courage, stabbed their two sons in the burning citadel while Scipio
looked on and threw herself into the flames. The survivors were sold, many of
the armed men were thrown to wild beasts, and the city was leveled, with
curses pronounced upon those who might rebuild it.

The divine power soon avenged this cruelty of the Romans against nearly all
their neighbors. Although no nation at that time was able to blunt Roman
power, God the avenger turned the Romans’ own hands against themselves.

Among the peoples of Spain, the Numantines fought most bravely for the liberty
of their homeland. After they had inflicted many defeats upon the Romans, the
consul Mancinus at last surrounded Numantia with thirty thousand men in a
close siege. The besieged broke out, drove the consul into flight, and trapped
the fugitives in such narrow terrain that the consul willingly made a peace
treaty to preserve himself and his army from immediate destruction. The
Senate refused to stand by this dishonorable treaty, but handed the consul
Mancinus naked and with his hands bound behind his back to the Numantines. The
Numantines refused to receive him inside the gates and demanded either that
the thirty thousand soldiers saved by the treaty be surrendered to them or
that the treaty be preserved in full. The Romans rejected these most
equitable demands. The Numantines defended themselves to their last breath,
and at the cost of the lives of many Roman soldiers. At last the younger
Scipio Africanus was again sent as consul to subdue them. By his courage he
broke the Numantines, but did not master them: when they could not break out
through Scipio’s heavily fortified camp, most of them burned their city and
cast themselves, their wives, and their children into the fire so that they
would not fall into the most cruel hands of the Romans.

> Marginalia, printed p. 290: *The dreadful fate of Numantia*.

Tiberius Gracchus, the elder Africanus’s grandson through his sister, was
Mancinus’s quaestor when he made that infamous treaty. Whether from fear of
punishment by the Senate or moved by ambition, Gracchus sought to bind the
people to himself and introduced agrarian laws. Among other provisions most
pleasing to the people, they enacted that whatever anyone possessed from the
public land beyond five hundred *jugera* should be divided among the poor.
They also provided that the treasures of King Attalus of Pergamum, who had
bequeathed everything he owned to the Roman people, should be distributed to
buy farming implements with which poor settlers could cultivate their land.
While Gracchus pressed these laws tumultuously against the will of the Senate,
Scipio Nasica, the chief pontiff, led the Senate and others with him in an
attack upon Gracchus. In the resulting tumult Gracchus fell, and three hundred
with him. Terrified by their slaughter, the people remained quiet for a time.
But after a year or two they recovered and assailed Nasica with insults
wherever he appeared, so that the Senate had to send him away to Asia under
the honorable guise of an embassy. He died near Pergamum, virtually an exile.

> Marginalia, printed p. 290: *The seditions of the Gracchi*.

Gaius, the brother of Tiberius Gracchus, gained the tribunate through popular
favor and wished to follow in his brother’s footsteps. While he promulgated
the same agrarian laws, his cousin and brother-in-law, the younger Scipio
Aemilianus Africanus, opposed him. Scipio was strangled in his bed, either by
poison or a noose, by his wife Sempronia, the sister of Gracchus. Yet no one
dared institute an inquiry into his death. Soon afterward, however, the Senate
took greater courage under the leadership of the consul Opimius and killed
Gaius Gracchus, tribune of the plebs, the consul Fulvius, and three thousand of
their followers in the streets, then sold their possessions at auction. These
were the beginnings of the civil wars that soon filled the city with blood
and plunder, as divine vengeance and some consolation for the rest of the
world, in which the Romans had then poured out copious streams of innocent
blood and from which they had scraped together such great heaps of spoil.

> Marginalia, printed p. 290: *Origin of the Jugurthine War*.

When Masinissa died at nearly one hundred years of age, he left his kingdom
to Scipio, who was then besieging Carthage, to divide among his three
legitimate sons. Scipio decreed that the eldest, Micipsa, should enjoy the
royal title and authority, while sufficiently large territories should be
assigned to his brothers Gulussa and Manastabal. Gulussa died without
children. Manastabal had only one illegitimate son, Jugurtha; Micipsa had

[Printed p. 291 | IA leaf 321 | access PDF 321 | OCR object 320]

two legitimate sons, Hiempsal and Adherbal. Jugurtha attained such courage
and fortitude that Micipsa feared him for the sake of his own children.
Therefore, so that Jugurtha might be removed by violence, he sent him with
troops to Scipio at Numantia. There Jugurtha conducted himself so admirably
that when the war ended Scipio sent him back to Micipsa heaped with praises.
Micipsa then changed his plan and resolved to bind Jugurtha’s affection to
himself and his children by kindnesses. With this end in view, as he was
dying he made Jugurtha coheir of the kingdom with his sons. Not content with
a third of the kingdom, Jugurtha aspired to the whole of it by every art. He
sent assassins who stabbed Hiempsal in his bed, and occupied his portion of
the kingdom. He also attacked Adherbal and compelled him to flee to the
Romans. They sent envoys to examine affairs in Numidia, but Jugurtha corrupted
the consul Opimius, Scaurus the chief man of the Senate, and all the other
envoys with his gifts. Consequently they divided the kingdom between him and
Adherbal without inflicting any punishment upon that most wicked man Jugurtha.
Made bolder by impunity, he soon drove Adherbal from the whole kingdom,
besieged him in the royal city, captured him, and killed him.

So foul at that time was the Romans’ greed that most of the leading men,
corrupted by gold, excused Jugurtha’s crimes, and the importunity of the
tribunes of the plebs scarcely obtained from the Senate a declaration of war
against the criminal. Jugurtha, however, possessed such a vast and resourceful
intelligence that for several years he made fools of consuls and their armies,
defeated some in battle, and corrupted more with gold, until Caecilius
Metellus came to Numidia. All Jugurtha’s arts were powerless against his
integrity, courage, and industry. Metellus captured most of Numidia’s cities
and often defeated and routed Jugurtha. Nothing delayed him from finishing
the war except the envy and ambition of his legate Gaius Marius.

> Marginalia, printed p. 291: *Metellus breaks Jugurtha*.

The seditious spirit of the brothers Gracchus was not extinguished with them.
Although the urban people, terrified by their slaughter, remained quiet for a
while, they soon rallied to kindle a new flame from the ashes of the Gracchi.
They erected statues of both men in the same places where they had fallen and
offered sacrifices and incense to them as new gods. This open madness inflamed
the mind of every seditious person to rise boldly against the Senate for the
favor of the people. Gaius Marius, an entirely new and obscure man, obtained
the tribunate and immediately proposed a law transferring judicial decisions
from the Senate to the people’s votes. When the consuls opposed a senatorial
decree to this law, Gaius Marius entered the senate house and demanded of the
consul Cotta that the decree be rescinded. Cotta asked the opinion of his
colleague Metellus Dalmaticus, under whose patronage Marius and his ancestors
had lived. When both consuls stood by the decree, Marius summoned a lictor
into the senate house and ordered him to bind both consuls and take them in
chains to prison. This done, he promulgated his law without further
opposition. Some years later Marius served under Metellus in Numidia and
showed himself sufficiently brave and energetic. But when he asked Metellus
for leave to go to Rome and appear as a candidate for the consulship, and
obtained his discharge only with difficulty and after a delay, he stirred up
as much of the army as he could against Metellus and arranged for many people
to write various slanders to Rome—as though Metellus, corrupted by Jugurtha
like the others, were prolonging the war. When Marius came to Rome he not only
obtained the consulship but was immediately sent to the army in Numidia so
that he might take the glory for a war all but finished by Metellus. Metellus
relinquished the army with the greatest reluctance, but nevertheless received
a triumph and the title “Numidicus” from the people, however foolish and
ungrateful.

Marius had little work left with Jugurtha already broken, but Jugurtha had
brought his father-in-law Bocchus, king of Mauritania, into alliance in the
war. Marius sent his legate Sulla to draw Bocchus into friendship with the
Roman people and, if he could, to persuade him to hand over his father-in-law
Jugurtha. Sulla handled the fickle mind of Bocchus so skillfully that he
finally obtained everything from him, even Jugurtha in chains—to Marius’s
great pain, for the glory of ending the war was thereby transferred to Sulla.
In any case, Marius led Jugurtha in his triumph and killed him in a Roman
prison, either by starvation or by strangling.

> Marginalia, printed p. 291: *Bocchus hands Jugurtha over to Sulla*.

At that time several hundreds of thousands of the bravest men of the Cimbri
and Teutones were advancing into Italy to extort lands from the Romans. They
utterly destroyed the consul Scipio, who confronted them in Transalpine Gaul,
together with eighty thousand Romans, and routed other armies as well. Marius
was the people’s sole hope against them. He conducted the war with such energy
and courage that he killed more than two hundred thousand Teutones near
Marseille and more than one hundred thousand Cimbri near the Po, while all
the remaining rabble of barbarians were sold at auction. As a reward for this
labor Marius obtained from the most willing people as many consulships as he
pleased. Yet although no fear of an enemy then pressed them, he could scarcely
obtain the sixth consulship, which he desired most eagerly and purchased with
great largesse.
