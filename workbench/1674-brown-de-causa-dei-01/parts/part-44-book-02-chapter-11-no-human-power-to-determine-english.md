CHAPTER XI

No Human Being Is Permitted to Determine This Principal and Solemn Time

In the preceding chapter we explained fully the nature of this principal and
solemn, or sufficient, time. We also showed how necessary it is that this time
be determined. It follows now that we inquire by whom this determination ought
to be made.

The adversaries maintain that this right and power belongs to human beings or
to the church, and they wish to rest the observance of the Lord's Day on this
foundation alone. We will see later, in Book V, what must be said about the
Lord's Day. Here we inquire only in general whether this solemn and sufficient
time can be determined by human beings. If we establish that no human beings
at all possess the power to institute and determine this time, they themselves
must see by what right they observe such a day, can observe it in any manner,
and assert that it is determined by the church.

By *determine* we do not here mean discovering or explaining which time God
has determined by his Word or the law of nature and obliged everyone to
observe. We mean devising this time with respect to *how long, how often,* and
also *when*; constituting that no other time with respect to how long, how
often, or when is principal and solemn time; and finally obliging everyone
else duly to observe this time as defined by these limits.

The question, therefore, is whether any human beings possess the power to
make such a determination of principal time. Whatever the adversaries say, we
decisively deny it. Although the adversaries are bound to establish this by
firm reasons (for the burden of proof always rests upon the one who affirms),
and it is our part to await and examine their arguments, we will nevertheless
propose a few questions to show how improbable their assertion is.

First, we may ask to whom, in distinction from everyone else, God has granted
this power of determining principal and solemn time that is necessary and
ordinarily sufficient. This power must have been granted either to certain
pagans or to certain members of the church, or partly to both: that is, to
certain pagans as regards the obligation of their subjects, and to certain
members of the church as regards the obligation of other church members.

The first cannot be said. 1. It cannot be said who or what sort of person this
pagan was. 2. What power could any pagan possess over members of the church
who do not belong to his dominion and are not members of his kingdom? 3. The
determination of this time is a matter of the greatest importance. It is
therefore improbable that

[1674 logical printed p. 282 | IA leaf 300 | PDF p. 300]

God gave them the power to determine it. 4. They lack the knowledge of God,
divine worship, and ourselves which is required to make this determination.
5. We have never heard of any such thing being done. 6. Nor can it be proved
that they thought this power belonged to them. They may perhaps sometimes have
instituted extraordinary days when warned by oracles, but they never
instituted any day as *necessary and principal solemn time*. Much less did
they decree that every other inhabitant of the world, whether under their
power or not, was obliged precisely to observe and holily to spend that
determinate time. 7. By what reason, whether sought from nature or deduced from
God's Word, can this be proved? Certainly neither Scripture nor nature teaches
any such thing.

The second cannot be said. 1. It cannot be proved by any reason that a member
of the church has power to command every other inhabitant of the world. The
pope himself, although he calls himself the head of the universal church and
therefore asserts that he possesses the power to make laws for all members of
the church, has not yet claimed for himself authority to command the whole
world or prescribe laws to it. 2. That member of the church is either a
political or an ecclesiastical person. If ecclesiastical, he can exercise power
only over the church and its members, who are distinguished from those outside
(1 Corinthians 5:12), even when he is not alone but constitutes a council with
others. If the person is political—that is, a civil magistrate—he can exercise
no power over strangers who do not belong to his dominion. Nor can it be
proved by any testimony of Scripture or solid reason that he possesses any
such power. 3. We never read of any such thing being done. We know, indeed,
that both civil magistrates and the church have declared that a certain
definite time ought to be given to the solemn worship of God, or that a
certain day ought to be spent holily as principal and solemn time, and have
bound their subjects devoutly to observe that day. But they did not devise this
day from their own brain; rather, they commended it to others instrumentally,
according to their authority, as instituted by God. Moreover, they never
prescribed such a day for every other pagan to observe, but only for their own
subjects.

The third cannot be said, because this solemn and sufficient time is necessary
for everyone and must therefore necessarily be observed by everyone. But if
pagans are permitted to determine such a day for their subjects, and certain
members of the church to do the same for others subject to them, it is
improbable that everyone will determine one and the same time with respect to
how long, how often, and when. It is consequently improbable that one and the
same solemn time will be determined.

I next answer by considering the parties separately. First, this power does
not belong to just any member of the church, for it has devolved neither upon
a private member, a civil magistrate, nor the rulers of the church. It has not
devolved upon the rulers of the church, as we will make plain in the following
chapter. Nor has it devolved upon private members. For 1. they possess no power
of government over either other members or rulers. Yet the power to determine
and prescribe such a time is undoubtedly a power of government. 2. Either
this power belongs to all private members, to only some, or to one. If to all,
then individuals must prescribe this time to themselves—which we will
immediately show to be false—or each must prescribe it to others. But what
confusion, contradiction, discord, and so forth must then arise!

[1674 logical printed p. 283 | IA leaf 301 | PDF p. 301]

Second, it does not belong to Christian magistrates. For 1. many of them are
not equipped with knowledge suitable for making this determination rightly.
2. This power would then belong to every supreme magistrate in relation to his
subjects. But since each would be free to prescribe whatever time he pleased,
it would follow that this magistrate could vary the continuation to one hour;
that one to two or four hours; another could prescribe a duration of ten or
twelve hours; another the span of a whole day; and still another the span of
two days, and so forth. There would consequently be the greatest discrepancy
in time with respect to *how long*. There could be no less disagreement with
respect to *how often*, even when we suppose that everyone wished to determine
a whole day. For one could prescribe a day in the weekly cycle; another one
out of every four or three; another one out of twenty, thirty, one hundred, or
one thousand; and another only one in the whole course of life. Again,
supposing that all wished to determine one day in the weekly cycle, this one
could nevertheless designate the second day, that one the third, another the
fourth, and another the fifth or sixth. Who will deny that this is indeed
probable? And once this is conceded, who will not see how much confusion,
distraction of mind, contention, doubt, contradiction, and scandal will arise
from it? Who will say that all these distinct determinations are good and just?

If someone should reply and say that everyone ought to determine one and the
same time in all its aspects, I answer: 1. it would then not be in their power
to institute whatever time they pleased for this use. 2. There would then be
some determination already made which they were bound to follow and merely to
set forth ministerially and declaratively. This is what we maintain: that this
sufficient and principal solemn time is determined by God, and that this
determination binds absolutely everyone, whether magistrates or subjects. If
they press again and say that certain general rules are given according to
which the determination ought to be made, while no particular and special
determination is given, I answer: 1. What, I ask, are those general rules? 2.
Those who make a determination according to those rules are either bound to
designate one and the same time with respect to how long, how often, and when,
or some latitude is given. If the former is said, then it appears that those
rules, however general they may be called, present us with the very divine
determination itself. But if the latter is said, then one and the same time
will not be observed everywhere, yet each determination will be good and
equitable. One will prescribe a day, another only two or three hours; one a day
in the weekly cycle, another only one day each year—and each will do well.
Nothing can be said here except that those rules are not so general. When the
adversaries explain them to us, we will judge better.

Furthermore, that this power does not belong to magistrates is clear from the
following. First, we do not read in Scripture that God granted them such a
power. And what we do not read, we do not believe. Next, whatever belongs to a
magistrate belongs to him either insofar as he is a magistrate or insofar as
he is a Christian. But neither can be said. Not the first, for then this power
would belong to all magistrates, including pagans, because the inference from
“insofar as” to every instance is valid. We will immediately see why this
cannot be said. Not the second, for it would follow that this power belongs to
all Christians, which is likewise false. Furthermore, if magistrates can
determine solemn and sufficient time at pleasure, they can also

[1674 logical printed p. 284 | IA leaf 302 | PDF p. 302]

change that determination. Thus in one year they could prescribe the first
day of the week; in the next, the seventh day; and so forth. In one year they
could determine a whole day in the weekly cycle as solemn time; in the next,
only one hour each week. Similarly, in one year they could determine some day
each week; in the next, only one day each year. Finally, we will later prove
that this privilege belongs to God and to God alone, who is the supreme Lord
of our time.

Third, this power does not belong to a pagan magistrate, both for the reasons
given above and because it would follow that a Christian who happened to be
subject to a pagan magistrate or master ought to observe no other time than
that which the pagan magistrate wished to be observed, whether for celebrating
domestic or solitary worship, much less public worship, even though their
condition and state required something very different. They might thus have
no solemn time for worshiping God, either in their private homes or in the
chamber of a person who lives with a pagan master and is strictly driven to
labor every day. Nor would that person be permitted to determine any time for
himself without first obtaining his master's permission or consent, because
this power is given by God only to masters and magistrates, even pagans. But
who will say this?

Perhaps they will reply that such a Christian is bound to spend as holily as
he can in private the day determined in the Christian church. I answer: 1.
This power is then not given to pagan magistrates with respect to all their
subjects, for we suppose that this Christian is his subject and even his
servant. 2. Why must he do this? Is it because he is bound by some divine law
to observe the time so determined and even to suffer persecution for it? Then
what we maintain is conceded. Is it because that time had been determined by
the church of which he was once a member? But what if he had never been a
member of any particular church? Furthermore, we may ask whether human
determinations so bind the conscience that for observing them a person can or
ought to expose his life and all his possessions to the most imminent dangers.
And who will say that a magistrate, even a pagan one, does not have as much
power in matters belonging to his jurisdiction as the church has? Can the
magistrate make laws for his subjects even though they are Christians? And if
he can, is the subject bound to comply with them notwithstanding that they
conflict with the laws of the Christian commonwealth of which he was formerly
a member, provided they do not also conflict with divine laws? If someone says
that in this case the Christian is bound to dedicate at least some part of
that day to sacred exercises, I answer: 1. What part, or how great a part, is
he bound to spend in this way? 2. By what law will he be bound to consecrate
any part of that day to God? 3. What if the pagan magistrate or master will not
permit even this? Must he therefore suffer persecution patiently? Or can he
with a good conscience suffer affliction for that cause? Is an ecclesiastical
determination to be valued so highly? Ought a merely human institution to have
such force that it binds the conscience even when that man is no longer a
subject but is subject to others? All these things will be sufficiently
difficult to prove.

Furthermore, if this power belonged to a pagan, a Christian living under the
Turkish emperor would be bound to observe Friday holily, because a subject is
bound to

[1674 logical printed p. 285 | IA leaf 303 | PDF p. 303]

obey the magistrate in matters committed by God to his power which do not
conflict with the law of God. And Muhammad instituted that day. Perhaps the
adversaries will not concede this, because the Christian church has already
instituted the Lord's Day. But then they must concede that the Christian is
bound, because of the church's determination, to refuse obedience to his
masters and expose himself to dangers and the harshest persecutions, even
though the matter itself, as they maintain, is not of great importance but is
a mere circumstance of worship. If they perhaps say that he is bound to
observe Friday no less religiously than he formerly observed the Lord's Day,
then everything we read in the New Testament about the Lord's Day and its
observance is entirely useless. The day which the Christian church instituted,
as they maintain, and about which alone we read in the New Testament, must be
abandoned—the day which, as will be evident in its proper place, the apostles
and primitive church more than probably observed. Instead, a day devised by a
most wicked impostor must be observed, and that to Christ's dishonor.

Perhaps they will say that everyone ought to detest that impostor and the day
he devised and enjoined, and observe some other day. I answer: in any event,
if the adversaries' opinion is true, they will suffer persecution for a mere
circumstance. For it is improbable that those pagans will grant permission to
consecrate some other day, lest by this means they be deprived of their
Christian servants' labor for two whole days in the weekly cycle. Let us
suppose, as is indeed not improbable, that the Turks are unwilling to permit
this. Could Christians with a good conscience suffer for disobedience in so
trivial a matter? Let the adversaries now see what they will answer.

Second, since, as is now clear and will immediately be still clearer, no one
is permitted to determine and prescribe for another this *sufficient,
necessary, principal, and solemn time*, we may ask whether they think this
power of determining it for oneself has been granted to every individual.
Primerose seems to insinuate something of this sort in the place cited above,
Part 2, chapter 7, section 4, when he speaks as follows: “The equity of the
reason contained in the Fourth Commandment is this: since a person has several
days to perform his work, reason requires him—note well—to consecrate one of
the many to God.” Elsewhere he says that “the Fourth Commandment always binds
us to appoint an ordinary day for divine worship.” In this sense he
undoubtedly intends the commandment to be moral for everyone, and consequently
to oblige everyone to determine a time for himself. Thomas Aquinas suggests
the same thing quite plainly in the words we recently examined in Chapter V.

Those who say that this power belongs to every individual when they deny that
God reserved it to himself speak far more consistently. For if this power is
granted to humanity generally, every reason necessarily requires us to
acknowledge that it has been granted to individuals. Everyone needs such a
time, at least for solitary worship when God cannot be worshiped publicly or
in families. But time appointed for this solitary worship cannot be observed
unless each person has the power to determine it for himself when others are
unwilling. This will undoubtedly happen when someone lives among pagans who
neither wish nor know how to determine this time. Yet no one will say that a
person can with a good conscience abstain from the solitary worship of God in
that situation. Each person must therefore possess the power to determine
such a time for himself. If someone says that a person can worship God even
though he has no predetermined time, he will say nothing to the point, for we
now suppose (as

[1674 logical printed p. 286 | IA leaf 304 | PDF p. 304]

we proved above) that time must necessarily be determined or predetermined.
Since solemn time must necessarily be determined, as we established above, it
must be determined by each person as far as he himself is concerned if it is
to be determined by a human being.

But that it is absurd to say this power of determining time was given to
individuals for themselves is clear, first, because many unwillingly observe
even a time determined by God, as they themselves do not deny, who would
undoubtedly never determine any time willingly—especially when no one could
demonstrate from Scripture that this was their duty. It is also clear because
many—servants, children, wives, and similar people—are not under their own
authority, while others are so entangled in business and exposed to
impediments that they would readily judge themselves released from this
burden. This is especially so when it could not be demonstrated that God's
Word expressly required it from them, and when they would expose themselves
to dangers by even observing the time, much less determining it. Finally, it
is clear because many lack the wisdom especially necessary to make this
determination and have no rule according to which they ought to proceed.

Hence many could offer many excuses for making no such determination: namely,
that they lack a rule according to which they ought to determine; that if the
matter were so important there would certainly be some clear precept or rule
about it; and that they do not know how much time is to be spent upon this or
that business, or how often the same labor is to be repeated, and so forth.
How, I ask, could the adversaries convict this refractory person of sin? Or
how could they resolve these doubts? From all these things considered together
it is abundantly clear that God did not grant this power to individual human
beings. For no one will say that God wished to leave a matter of such
importance to the determination of people furnished with no judgment or
conscience. They would either determine no time at all or at least determine a
wholly unsuitable time.

Indeed, this is clear not only from those ill-disposed toward religion and the
worship of God, but also from those who have religion at heart but face many
impediments, especially if they are servants and under the power of others,
and have no means of answering those impediments. For if it were either plainly
established that God enjoined this upon them, or an express rule were presented
by which they could judge this solemn time, they would have an answer and the
means of satisfying their consciences in this matter. But since nothing of the
kind appears, it is evident that they lack the spur by which they might be
urged to the devout observance of determinate time, and lack the argument by
which they might satisfy their consciences when they doubt whether they made
the right determination with respect to how long, how often, or when. It is
therefore incredible that God wished to leave this for human beings to
determine, since their determination would not honor God himself or benefit
them, advance their salvation, or provide spiritual consolation as much as a
divine determination would.

Moreover, it is incredible—because it is scarcely possible—that everyone
would wish to determine one and the same continuation of time, recurring so
often and beginning precisely at the same time, since everyone scarcely agrees
about anything which in any way opposes corrupt nature or people's carnal
affections. As regards determining solemn time, one person would be moved by
this cause and another by that as to why

[1674 logical printed p. 287 | IA leaf 305 | PDF p. 305]

he would refuse to determine the time another thought best. One, being
profane, would desire either no determinate time or at least one that was very
short and recurred very rarely. Another, being worldly, would not consent to
an equitable and devout determination, or at least not to a strict one. Yet
God's honor and the communion of saints require everyone, at one and the same
time so far as possible, to extol God with praises and worship him.

Third and finally, we may ask upon what foundation this right belonging to
human beings rests. It must rest upon some divine right. But divine right, or
God's will, is revealed to us either through nature or through Scripture. I
desire to know which of these laws proves and confirms that this right belongs
to human beings. As for the law of nature, it will readily teach us that God
is absolute Lord of all our time and therefore that every right and all power
to determine time sufficient for worshiping him belong to him alone. As for
Scripture, which constitutes our compendium of law, it says nothing of the
kind. Nowhere do we read that God devolved this entire power upon any creature
or stripped himself of his privilege. Sacred Scripture nowhere even suggests
such a thing. The sacred writings abundantly testify that God formerly
determined solemn time; but where do we read that he has now delivered to
anyone all the power of determining this principal time?

Since Scripture is silent here, upon what foundation, I ask, will our
conscience rest in a matter of such importance? What! Has anyone dared even to
attempt this without the express and clear testimony of Scripture, when it
seems at least to be an invasion of God's own throne? When Scripture does not
speak, how can we be certain that we ought to do this? Is Scripture the one
rule of our conduct? How, except through Scripture, will we know when this time
has been rightly determined with respect to duration, frequency, and the
season itself; when we must submit to the determination made; when a
determination must be corrected; or how it will be certainly established that
a determination made by any human being ought to be holily observed? Hence it
is clear that our adversaries are bound on many grounds to demonstrate by
clear and undoubted testimonies of Scripture that human beings possess this
right of determining principal and solemn time which is sufficient and
ordinarily necessary.
