2. Precedence above all the dukes of the realm.

“The bishops also,” says Mocket, “have precedence over the other barons of the
realm, not only in private gatherings but also in Parliament, the supreme
council of the realm. Because they are lord barons, they have their seats there
in this order. After the most reverend archbishops of Canterbury and York, the
bishop of London sits first, then Durham, next Winchester, and the remaining
bishops according to priority of consecration, except that those who have been
members of the king’s privy council hold the first place after the bishop of
Durham.” When John Stratford, archbishop of Canterbury under Edward III,
approached the Parliament chamber and was kept from the doors by the king’s
ministers, he said to those standing by: “The king summoned me to this
Parliament by his writ; and since I, as the chief peer of the realm after the
king, ought to have the first vote in Parliament, I claim the rights of my
church and seek entrance into Parliament.” Godwin reports this from
Birchington in his commentary on the prelates.

[Marginalia: *On Polity*, chapter 4, p. 313; p. 153.]

[1623 printed p. 78 | IA leaf 90 | PDF p. 90]

From the register of Archbishop William Courtenay, the same author transcribed
this protest: “Since by the law and custom of the realm of England it belongs
to the archbishop of Canterbury, as well as his other suffragans, fellow
bishops, abbots, and others holding from the king by barony, to be personally
present in all the king’s Parliaments as the aforesaid peers of the realm with
the other peers of the realm, to advise, deliberate, decide, and so forth, they
protest concerning their right to be present, and so forth, with the other
peers. But because certain matters are being handled in this Parliament in
which the institutions of the sacred canons do not permit them to take part,
they further protest that they will withdraw, with the right of each one’s
peerage nevertheless always preserved.” The mother of Zebedee’s sons asked
Christ that her two sons might sit, one at his right and the other at his left,
in his kingdom, Matthew 20:21, which they dreamed would be worldly. To cure
their delirium, Christ raised them to contemplate the heavenly kingdom. Yet our
prelates, patrician men, claim for themselves an honorary seat and precedence
among the peers of the realm. They aspire to sit at princes’ right hands and
refuse to be relieved of this disease. Canterbury, as first peer of the realm,
has precedence over all officers of the realm; York over all except the lord
chancellor; and the bishops over the other barons of the realm. At the Scottish
Parliament of 1606, when the bishops were restored to the third estate, they
disputed in that very Parliament with the nobles over prerogative of place and
vote. The Eighth General Council of Constantinople in 871 decreed that bishops
should uphold their dignity: “If, after this enactment, any bishop dishonors
his dignity, falling from the honor owed him by the canons, and lives basely
according to an old and rustic custom, let him be suspended for a year, and let
the prince who was the cause be excommunicated for two years.” This canon
forbids “bishops to proceed a very long way from their churches to meet princes
or men invested with military dignity; to dismount from mules or horses at
their approach; or timidly and abjectly fall before their knees as if to adore
them.” The Council of Trent, “hearing that certain bishops, forgetful of their
estate, gravely dishonor the pontifical dignity—men who conduct themselves with
the ministers of kings, petty kings, and barons, both inside and outside the
church, with an unbecoming abasement, and who, as if they were inferior
ministers of the altar, not only yield place to them with too little dignity
but even personally serve them”—commanded “that, keeping their rank and order
before their eyes both inside and outside the church, they remember everywhere
that they are fathers and pastors.” What does Gentillet say to this? “We do not
deny that a pastor ought to be solicitous to preserve and increase his
reputation and to take care that his ministry is not rendered contemptible;
yet this is not done by contending over a seat, claiming the first place among
the nobles, or showing himself too little courteous to them, but by rightly
teaching God’s word and reproving even all the nobles for their faults. The
ancient bishops did not present themselves as venerable and distinguished by
that swollen gravity, but by the sincere discharge of their duties and the
exercises of true piety.”

[Marginalia: p. 173; Acts of the Eighth Council, chapter 14; Session 25,
Decree 17; *Examination*, fol. 184.]

[1623 printed p. 79 | IA leaf 91 | PDF p. 91]

Our Reverend and Most Reverend Fathers learned from the Fathers of Trent and
the Roman Curia to seek the honorary prerogative of precedence in public
gatherings and place in the assemblies of the nobles. If we praise this
arrangement, why do we not also praise the order in which kings and cardinals
sit at public assemblies according to the rules of the papal curia? Or why do
we not at least equate the more celebrated patriarchs with princes and approve
the order observed at the emperor’s court in the greater assemblies of the
German Empire, where the bishop of Trier possesses the right to sit opposite
the king of the Romans—or, in the Golden Bull’s words, “over against him, in a
straight line toward the emperor’s face”—as Choppin reports? To the extent that
the Roman bishop, patriarch of the West, is more worthy than an Englishman who
is one of the patriarchs of lesser nations, he can claim for himself a more
worthy place. Why should I recall disputes among prelates over precedence, when
even among their inferiors disputes have often arisen in processions, at the
burial of the dead, in carrying the canopy, and similar matters, as the
Tridentine fathers themselves attest in decree 13 of session 25?

It must be observed that the ancient order has been changed in the English
arrangement. The councils of Milevis, Africa, Greek Carthage, First Braga, and
Fourth Toledo decreed that the order of consecration was to be observed without
prejudice to the metropolitan’s dignity. From the sequence in which bishops
subscribed at councils, it is clear that prerogative was granted not to sees
but to seniority, so that the one who had spent more years in the episcopate
claimed the prior place. Thus at the Council of Aquileia the bishop of Brescia
is placed before Genoa, while at Milan the bishop of Genoa is put first. See
Baronius. This order was so scrupulously observed that presbyters sent to
councils by bishops in their place retained, in subscribing, the rank of their
own bishops ahead of other bishops ordained later than they, as at the councils
of Chalon, Second Orléans, Epaone, and Florence. Here the bishops of London,
Durham, and Winchester rely on the prerogative of their sees, not the date of
their consecration.

Besides titles of dignity, precedence, and prerogative of place, there are
other things that display the prelates’ pomp to mortal eyes. The English
prelates do not go about without a numerous, disorderly crowd of twenty,
thirty, forty, or sixty servants walking before or following behind. Most of
these good-for-nothing men perform no other service than escorting the Lord
Bishop or His Grace the Archbishop out and back. My witness is that excellent
scourge of bishops, Thomas Cartwright. The prelate has some men who stand in
attendance, others who issue summonses, and still others who serve as grooms,
cooks, cupbearers, chamberlains, chaplains, platter-bearers, or train-bearers,
whom one may also designate tail-bearers. These are the men who lift behind him
with their hands the archbishop’s train, several yards long.

[Marginalia: *On Polity*, book 2, p. 335; Milevis, chapter 13; Africa, chapter
53; Carthage, chapter 89; Braga, chapter 24; Toledo, chapter 3; year 451;
*First Reply*, p. 97.]

[1623 printed p. 80 | IA leaf 92 | PDF p. 92]

He also has other footmen assigned to the table, whom it is tedious to
enumerate. When the archbishop passes through St. Paul’s Cathedral in London,
the doctors of law in scarlet wait for His Grace—*my Lord’s Grace*—as an
eyewitness, the author of the book entitled *Abstract*, reports. That celebrated
Synod of Dort did not see the pomp of an English prelate in the bishop of
Llandaff. Godwin of Llandaff reports in his commentary on the prelates that
“the bishopric of Llandaff is by far the poorest in all England and Wales, so
much so that this diocese contains not a few parishes which return much richer
fruits to their incumbents than this see does to its bishop.” See whom they
chose as delegate to the synod. We shall discuss in the dispensation of
commendams the devices by which they replenish poorer bishoprics. If anyone
were to watch in his own country a bishop or archbishop walking “with much
display,” would he not immediately say that bishops formerly went
about accompanied by one or two acolytes, without any solemn display, not
surrounded by dependents with a great company of footmen and attendants
accompanying them? “Nor shall we describe how, when he was lifted up in pride,
Paul of Samosata usurped worldly dignities and wished to be called a
*ducenarius* rather than a bishop; how, while walking through the marketplace,
reading letters and dictating as he went in public, he had around him a
numerous company of attendants, some preceding and others following; with the
result that our faith was made liable to envy and hatred because of the pride
and arrogance of his heart.” The English prelates imitate this Paul of
Samosata, not Paul of Tarsus. At the Fourth Council of Carthage, although it is
spurious, it was decreed that “a bishop should have a little dwelling not far
from the church, plain furniture, a poor table and diet, and should seek the
authority of his dignity in faith and merits of life; and that a cleric should
prove his profession by both dress and gait and should therefore seek elegance
in neither clothing nor shoes.” Likewise, “a bishop sitting anywhere should
not allow a presbyter to stand, and he should use the church’s goods as things
entrusted to him, not as his own.” Nearly all these provisions were inserted
into Gratian’s decree. “Augustine used a frugal and spare table,” Possidius
reports, “which, among vegetables and legumes, sometimes also included meat
for guests or some of the infirm. He used silver spoons only; the other vessels
in which food was brought to the table were earthenware, wood, or marble.”
Likewise, “he made no will, because Christ’s poor man had nothing from which to
make one.” When the emperor’s prefect threatened Basil the Great with
confiscation of his goods, he answered, “None of these things can trouble me. I
have no wealth except a ragged garment and a few books.” English prelates
inhabit palaces whose rooms gleam with gilding and whose cupboards are laden
with engraved plate, as Cartwright reports. When Grandisson, bishop of Exeter,
had built magnificent houses at Taunton and richly furnished them, he said

15, 31, 34, and 45; Distinction 95, chapter 41; *Life of Augustine*; Sozomen,
book 6, chapter 16; *First Reply*, p. 97.]

“he had prepared that place so his successors would have somewhere to lay
their head if perhaps their temporal possessions were taken into the king’s
hand,” as Godwin relates in his commentary and Camden in his *Britannia*. The
reader may see in Godwin’s commentary how great a sum the English prelates
spent in the extravagant construction and repair of episcopal residences.
Today’s prelates possess these magnificent houses; here they lay their heads,
and the foxes have dens. Their daily lunches, says Cartwright, are banquets;
their tables are furnished with such a variety of dishes. Their dinners recall
the feasts of the Salii. Their whole household shines with greater splendor.
Bernard, inveighing against this luxury and pomp, writes: “From where do you
think they obtain this overflowing abundance of goods, splendor of clothing,
luxury of tables, and heap of silver and gold vessels, if not from the bride’s
goods? This is why she is left poor, destitute, and naked, her face pitiable,
unkempt, rough, and bloodless. This is not adorning the bride but despoiling
her; not guarding but betraying; not defending but exposing; not instructing
but prostituting; not feeding the flock but slaughtering and devouring it.”
Jerome likewise says: “It is shameful for the lictors of consuls and soldiers
to keep watch before the doors of a priest of the crucified and poor Christ,
who himself was fed on another’s food, and for the provincial governor to dine
better at your house than in the palace. If you allege that you do this in
order to plead for the wretched and oppressed, a worldly judge will defer more
to a self-controlled cleric than to a rich one, and will revere your holiness
more than your wealth. Or if he is such a man that he does not hear clerics on
behalf of any sufferers except among the drinking cups, I will gladly go
without this kind of favor and will ask Christ as judge, who can give help more
fully and quickly than a judge.” As cattle are fattened in stalls, so they are
fattened in their bishoprics. Edington of Winchester once played on this,
saying, “The papal grate is indeed higher at Canterbury, but the feeding
trough at Winchester is richer.” He meant that Winchester then abounded more
in wealth and revenues, although it was inferior in dignity to Canterbury, as
Godwin explains in his account of Winchester.

Those who formerly lived sparingly from the altar and obtained from the
faithful’s contributions the support necessary for themselves and their
household now grow proud and live luxuriously from the altar. They go out into
the field not as stewards but as lords, men to whom the world is not crucified
but transfixed. In the first edition of his plan for departure, Spalato
promised that in the ninth book of *The Ecclesiastical Commonwealth* he would
discuss “how spare the support of the church’s ministers ought to be.” But in
the English translation, and later in the second edition of the plan prefixed
to the books of *The Ecclesiastical Commonwealth*, the words “how spare” were
struck out, and we have instead “what the support of the church’s ministers
ought to be.” The Roman pontiff is no more solicitous for his triple crown than
the English and Scottish prelates are for preserving their tyranny and pomp.

[Marginalia: p. 469; Camden on Dartmoor, p. 152; *On the Song of Songs*, sermon
77; *To Nepotian on the Life of Clerics*; *Commentary on the Prelates*, p. 104.]

If he had given his work to the first printer he encountered in Germany for
publication, as he resolved in the same plan for departure, we would not have
needed to fear so greatly the counsel and supervision of the English bishops
in his work. But by seeing to the removal also of the words “in Germany,” they
persuade us by their deed to receive that work as issuing from the bishops’
workshop.

By the grant of princes, such as immunities, liberties, and so forth, on their
own broad estates.

By the grant of princes, not only archbishops but also bishops enjoy
immunities, liberties, and privileges for themselves upon their own broad
estates. In 1186 Hugh of Lincoln “gave the king one thousand marks to redeem
the established custom of presenting him each year, at the Feast of the
Circumcision, with certain precious pelts which we call sables,” as Godwin
reports. “Once Hervaeus was bishop,” says Camden, “he had no prior concern to
increasing his church’s dignity. He obtained exemption everywhere from tolls—to
use the words of the Ely book—and freed it from the yoke of servitude and the
guard duty it owed to Norwich Castle.” We related certain things above from the
same Camden concerning the bishopric of Durham. Those permitted to examine
letters of presentation with their own eyes can enumerate more kinds of these
liberties and immunities.

Peculiar to one or the other: namely, for York, to have precedence over all
officers of the realm except the lord chancellor; for Canterbury, by the common
municipal law:

1. To exercise precedence over all officers of the realm, whence he is called
the first peer of the realm.

2. To inaugurate the king at his coronation.

If the inauguration of kings were part of the pastoral office, it would belong
to a presbyter no less than a bishop, and to a metropolitan no more than
another bishop. Ivo of Chartres writes as follows against the bishop of Reims,
who claimed for himself alone the right to inaugurate the king: “Since this
same power belongs to every metropolitan in his own metropolis, it seems
strange that one person aspires to claim as his private right what is known to
be common to many—unless perhaps someone dares to say that the sacraments have
greater power among some than among others, which would be schismatic and
would divide the church’s unity.” They believed the anointing of kings to be a
sacramental act possessing the power to confer the gifts of the Holy Spirit
and inwardly increase what the outward anointing signified.

[Marginalia: *In the Commentary*, p. 346; *Britannia*, p. 391; Ivo, letter 189.]

[1623 printed p. 83 | IA leaf 95 | PDF p. 95]

They believed it possessed the power to confer the gifts of the Holy Spirit
and inwardly increase what the outward anointing signified, as Matthias
Stephani gathers from Hostiensis. Kings and princes were anointed on the arms
and shoulders with the oil of catechumens. “Although other kings—namely, those
of Jerusalem, England, and Sicily—have customarily been imbued with sacred
anointing, they have not been anointed with oil sent down by God in the manner
and appearance of a miracle, as the Most Christian King has,” says Choppin. In
addition to this Jewish ceremony commended to us by a miracle, other rites are
described in the Roman Pontifical. After the metropolitan has anointed the
king’s arm and shoulders with the oil of catechumens, the king is conducted by
his men beneath a canopy, where the oily anointing is wiped away with silk and
he is dressed in royal robes. The metropolitan places an unsheathed sword in
the king’s hand. The ministers replace the sword in its sheath. The
metropolitan then girds the king with the sword. Thus girded, the king rises,
draws the sword from its sheath, brandishes it manfully, wipes it upon his left
arm, and replaces it in the sheath. Then the crown is placed upon him. All the
prelates present hold it after the metropolitan has taken it from the altar;
the metropolitan himself guides it and places it upon the kneeling king’s
head. He then hands him the scepter. As the king rises, the sword is ungirded
and, in its sheath, is given to someone to carry immediately before the king.
The metropolitan and another prelate conduct the king between them to the
throne, bearing the scepter in his hand and crown on his head, and the
metropolitan seats him upon it. I ask, are these pastoral duties?

3. To receive the income from lands held of him by right of homage while the
heir is under twenty-one, even if the heir holds other lands directly from the
Crown.

Camden relates in the English edition of his *Britannia* that William I first
ordained that bishops and abbots, who held their baronies in pure and perpetual
free alms, should henceforth hold from the king by feudal law and military
service; and that he prescribed for each how many soldiers they were to levy
for war. Similarly, bishops who ought to have leased their fields and estates
to tenants, or granted them by emphyteusis for an emphyteutic rent—that is, an
annual payment—to emphyteutic tenants, whom we call leaseholders, instead
acquired vassals holding military fiefs who were bound to military services
and other obligations to them. Thus even nobles held estates and lands from
archbishops and bishops by military service and were bound to render service.
“The earls of Gloucester,” says Camden, “held the manor of Tonbridge by feudal
right from the archbishops of Canterbury on the condition that they serve as
stewards at the archbishops’ enthronement and that their children be under the
archbishops’ wardship.”

[Marginalia: *On Jurisdiction*, book 2, part 1, chapter 1; Choppin, *On Sacred
Polity*, book 2, p. 245; *Roman Pontifical*, fol. 87; *Britannia*, Frankfurt
edition of 1616, p. 146.]

[1623 printed p. 84 | IA leaf 96 | PDF p. 96]

Godwin also reports that at the lavish feast George Neville, archbishop of
York, prepared for his enthronement in 1466, the earl of Warwick served as the
archbishop’s steward. Camden likewise relates that “the barons of Meinel held
certain estates from the archbishops of Canterbury, and in return the Coigniers
and Strangwayses and others descended from them were bound to military duties
and services.” And since by prerogative “the king of England”—I use the words
of the *Prerogative*, says Camden—“shall have custody of all the lands of those
who hold directly from him by military service, of which the tenants were
seised in their demesne as of fee on the day they died, regardless of whom they
held by that kind of service, provided that they held of the king some ancient
tenement of the Crown, until the heir’s lawful age; nevertheless, these fees
and other fees of the archbishops are excepted, so that the archbishops have
custody of such lands even if the tenants held elsewhere from the king.” But
what have ecclesiastics to do with the right of homage, military service,
feudal duties, custody of fiefs—which they call wardship of the property—or
guardianship of heirs—which they call wardship of the person? What have men
devoted to the care of souls and divine worship to do with nobles or others as
their vassals?

4. To hunt with his own dogs in any game preserve within his province.

The bishops also have their game parks, menageries, and fishponds, adding to
the heap of their wealth and pleasures. Camden reports that the archbishops of
York have at Southwell a very large palace with three adjoining game preserves
stocked with animals. Godwin writes that in 1349 Simon Islip, archbishop of
Canterbury, sold the earl of Arundel the right to twenty-six fallow deer to be
delivered annually to him and his successors. He also writes that Henry
Burghersh of Lincoln, in order to have a large game preserve, enclosed the
broad lands of certain poor men along with his own estates; and that after his
death he appeared to a certain nobleman dressed in a short green tunic, holding
a bow, quiver, and arrows in his hands with a hunting horn hanging from his
neck, as if he were keeper of that preserve. Pope Nicholas commands Archbishop
Albinus to convene a synodical council with his bishops and suffragans and by
pastoral authority to command Bishop Landfredus “to remain entirely foreign to
all hunting of beasts or birds and wholly restrain himself from immoderate
familiarity with his son.” In the same place: “For, as blessed Jerome says, we
never read of a holy hunter.” Crantz, reproving the bishops’ indolence and
luxury in his *Metropolis*, says, “Now, if it please the gods, we blush at the
divine ministry in the church, dedicate our dogs in the forest, or adore
secular princes at court.” Although Synesius delighted in hunting before he
undertook the episcopate, he writes that after undertaking it he was obliged to
abandon that practice.

[Marginalia: p. 587; Camden on the Coritani; Distinction 33, chapter 1; book 9,
chapter 44; Synesius, letter 105.]

[1623 printed p. 85 | IA leaf 97 | PDF p. 97]

The archbishop of Canterbury may hunt not only in his own preserves but in any
preserve within his province. At the Lateran Council it was decreed that
“archbishops visiting parishes, according to the diversity of the provinces
and the resources of the churches, must never exceed a retinue of forty or
fifty mounts; bishops, twenty or thirty; cardinals, twenty-five; while
archdeacons are to be content with five or seven horses and deans established
under bishops with two. They are not to set out with hunting dogs and birds,
but proceed so as to appear to seek not their own things but those of Jesus
Christ.” Anyone who desires more on this subject may see the title *On the
Hunting Cleric*, book 5 of the *Decretals*, title 24.

By statute, to grant dispensations from canons and other ecclesiastical laws
through all the dominions of the English empire, which they call faculties.

The Court of Faculties is numbered among the archbishop of Canterbury’s
courts. Camden describes it thus: “The Court of Faculties, in which an officer
is appointed to consider the petitions of those who seek relaxation from
ecclesiastical law, which is sometimes rigid and strict, and a registrar who
records in writing the dispensations granted.” “Finally,” says Mocket, “under
the statutes of the realm, for the sake of necessity or greater advantage,
after first holding a summary examination of this case and that, he dispenses
from canons and other ecclesiastical laws throughout the whole realm of
England”—namely, the archbishop of Canterbury. By a statute enacted in the
twenty-fifth year of Henry VIII, power was given him, under the archiepiscopal
seal, either personally or through the commissioner of faculties, to grant the
faculties, dispensations, and rescripts in any cause that the Roman pontiff had
customarily granted. But if the fee for the faculty or dispensation in the
Roman chancery had exceeded four English pounds, it was to be confirmed by the
Great Seal and enrolled in the royal chancery by an officer appointed for this
purpose whom Camden calls the clerk of faculties. Cartwright therefore rightly
says that, after papal authority was routed, the archbishop was substituted in
the Roman pontiff’s place, only the prince’s right being preserved; and he
asserts that the archbishop conducts a foul and intolerable commerce. “From
canon law,” says Travers, “comes that Roman marketplace in which the most
shameful market of wicked dispensations, as they call them, and every
profit-seeking scheme for destroying the church is practiced.” The authors of
the admonition to Parliament report that almost everything is for sale here,
just as it formerly was in the Roman Curia, whose commerce has neither measure,
bottom, end, nor shame. They dispense not only from human laws but even divine
ones, such as nonresidence, plurality of pastoral benefices and churches,
simony, and so forth.

[Marginalia: *Decretals*, book 3, title 39, chapter 2; *Britannia*, p. 118; *On
Polity*, p. 303; Rastell, *Rome*, p. 3; *First Reply*, part 1, p. 87; *On
Discipline*, fol. 12; p. 3.]

In the divine laws God the lawgiver has provided abundantly for every case. In
human laws, if anything is lacking because particular circumstances were not
specified, relaxation of the strict law for the sake of necessity or greater
advantage is not to be denied. But what does this have to do with the Roman
pontiff or the English pope? Spalato rightly says that “all ecclesiastical laws
are under the rule and power of the churches themselves, so that every church,
where necessity or opportunity so requires, may for a most weighty cause
dispense from universal laws on its own, and even change, moderate, and
completely abrogate them, especially in synods; and the greater the synods are,
the greater the power they possess.” In human laws, therefore, which are merely
ecclesiastical and mutable according to time and place, we are not so
inflexible as to deny that, after necessity and usefulness have been weighed,
the strict law should be relaxed by an ecclesiastical senate. Ecclesiastical
judges are judges both of law and of equity. If necessity or greater usefulness
calls for relaxation of strict law, the dispensation is owed by justice, not
grace; nor should it depend upon the decision of any greater or lesser pope so
that it can be obtained only by paying him a price. Dispensations ought not to
be granted from grace without necessity or usefulness being weighed, for such
dispensations are truly wounds in the laws. Yet nearly all Roman and
Anglo-Roman dispensations are of this kind. The fee schedule of the Apostolic
Chancery says: “Observe carefully that favors and dispensations of this kind
are not granted to the poor, because they have nothing and therefore cannot be
consoled.” In the Anglo-Roman chancery—that is, the Court of Faculties—the same
rule is most religiously observed.

Faculties are considered either particularly or generally. Those considered
particularly, which are rather frequently granted after a summary examination
of the cause, include:

1. To appoint public notaries.

Even if we were to grant the archbishop the right to appoint notaries to
exercise the office of notary in ecclesiastical courts, we say that appointing
public notaries for civil causes is foreign to ecclesiastics. Since so many
losses and lawsuits arise from notaries’ inexperience—as the Council of Trent
says—and, I add, their wickedness, and since, although they are single persons,
they count in giving testimony as two witnesses rather than one, the
appointment of notaries is not to be left to the decision and pleasure of a
single archbishop.

2. To grant permission to eat meat on certain prohibited days, prohibited for
political reasons alone, to those in poor health, women recently delivered of
children, the elderly, and the sick.

[Marginalia: book 4, chapter 10, section 74; Session 22, *On Reform*, chapter
10.]

[1623 printed p. 87 | IA leaf 99 | PDF p. 99]

If it is granted only to those in poor health, women recently delivered of
children, the elderly, and the sick, why not to all such people? Is it because
not everyone’s purse produces coins? And why not to them alone, if only they
are freed from the law’s bond? If eating meat on certain forbidden days is
prohibited for political reasons alone, why is the faculty to eat it not
granted by political officials? They observe the same days as the papists both
in Lent and outside it, and the same form of fasting, restraining themselves
and others from eating meat but not fish. Parish priests are bound to announce
to the people on Sundays during divine service the feast days, vigils, and
meatless days falling in the following week. Eating meat on certain days is
therefore forbidden for the sake of religion—or rather superstition—which we
shall discuss in its place.

3. To celebrate a marriage although the proclamation on three market days,
which they call the banns, has not preceded it.

The book of public liturgy establishes the following concerning banns or
edicts to be published three times: “First, the banns are to be given or
published on three Sundays or feast days during divine services, with the
people present, according to ancient custom.” Walter Reynolds, raised to the
archbishopric of Canterbury in 1313, decreed as follows in a provincial synod:
“In contracting marriage, likewise, on three Sundays or feast days separated
from one another, as if by three edicts, the priests are always to inquire of
the people concerning the freedom of the bridegroom and bride.” Canisius
maintains in his summary of canon law that the proclamations and celebration
of marriage before the church belong to its solemnity, not its essence.
Nevertheless, the order of three proclamations is not to be relaxed rashly,
lest opportunity be given to enter into secret and clandestine marriages. In
the recent constitutions of 1603 the English prelates appear to have taken
abundant care that this order not be violated: “In future no faculty or
indulgence to celebrate a marriage between any persons without the threefold
proclamation of the banns according to the book of public liturgy shall be
granted by any person exercising ecclesiastical jurisdiction or claiming any
privileges in the name of his church, except only by those who possess
episcopal authority; by the commissioner of faculties; while the see is filled,
by the vicars general of archbishops and bishops; or, while the see is vacant,
by the guardians of the spiritualities or ordinaries lawfully exercising
episcopal jurisdiction, and by no others. It shall moreover be granted only to
persons of distinguished and illustrious condition subject to their respective
jurisdiction, with suitable and sufficient security also being furnished.”
The points of the security to be furnished are prescribed in the following
canon: “That there exists no impediment arising from precontract,
consanguinity, affinity, or any other legally prohibited cause whatsoever that
could interfere with or obstruct the said marriage.”

seek a gracious dispensation have means for gifts, they will be enrolled among
the distinguished. Notwithstanding the preceding canon, favors dispensing

4. Concerning beneficial causes, such as:

1. To abolish irregularity contracted without evil intent.

Irregularity is defined by the canonists and theologians as a canonical
impediment by which someone is prohibited from receiving an ecclesiastical
order or ministering in one already received. There are other impediments
founded upon divine or natural law, such as the female sex, lack of baptism,
and lack of bodily members or organs necessary to exercise a duty belonging to
holy orders. Those held back by these impediments from advancement to holy
orders are called not irregular but incapable. These impediments are, as it
were, certain privations and incapacities. Irregularity, however, presupposes
capacity in the subject and deprives him of the exercise or use of that
capacity. All grave offenses also impede if infamy follows, as the canonists
maintain. But irregularity is such an impediment that it hinders even after
repentance until it is removed by dispensation. Some irregularity is founded
upon indecency alone, another kind upon indecency that includes fault, for
indecency is the adequate cause of irregularity among the canonists.

[Marginalia: p. 126.]

[1623 printed p. 89 | IA leaf 101 | PDF p. 101]

Thus if anyone has otherwise blameless defects that diminish the dignity of
the ministry or orders, he is irregular. Some irregularity is contracted
through a person’s own act, another kind without his act. That contracted
through mere suffering without a moral act is free from fault; nevertheless,
an impediment is introduced by canon law, as with defect of birth.
Irregularity contracted through one’s own act, however, is sometimes with
fault and sometimes without it. If a magistrate possessing the power of the
sword and punishment strikes an offender with a sword or axe, he is irregular
but without fault. A murderer who commits voluntary homicide is irregular with
fault. In contracting irregularity, gross fault is equated with intent. “For
supine negligence is compared to intent and induces irregularity, because such
great negligence appears to seek the offense.” Hence there are so many rules
for irregularities distinguishing intent and depraved will from negligence.
Most irregularities arose from a bad imitation of the Mosaic law, as from
Leviticus 21, where certain legal irregularities are enumerated. The blind,
lame, mutilated, excessively long-limbed, a man with a broken foot or hand, a
hunchback, a wasted man, one whose eye has a cataract, or who has psoriasis,
an oozing scab, or crushed testicles are prohibited from approaching to offer
to Jehovah. Thus, on account of certain bodily defects, more recent canons
introduced irregularity, although the person did not inflict this evil upon
himself or arrange for it to be inflicted, but it befell him by violence or
nature. Ammonius the monk cut off his right ear so that he would not be
promoted to the episcopate. I know well that our prelates, who so eagerly seek
bishoprics and invade them against the church’s will, will never contract that
irregularity. If anyone lacks an eye without fault of his own, whether the
right or left, he too is excluded from the sacred ministry. “For according to
the canons, the rights of priesthood cannot be granted to one whose eye has
been put out. It does not help him that he lost his eye unwillingly, since no
one is to be believed to have lost an eye willingly; nor do we find that the
most sacred canons excepted in their rules any case of losing an eye that would
not impede the attainment of the priesthood. Rather, we see that the fathers
considered it sufficient for prohibition that one who lacks an eye cannot
obtain the priestly office,” as Gelasius writes to Bishop Rufinus.
Irregularity arises from a blemish in an eye: “It was objected against you that
you have a blemish in your eye and are the son of a canon. We therefore
commanded the archbishop of Canterbury that, because you are said to be the son
of a canon, he proceed in your promotion by a great dispensation. Concerning
the blemish, however, we commanded the aforesaid archbishop to carry out what
seemed best after counsel with his suffragans, in which we took care to provide
for you by a very great dispensation,” says Alexander III. You see that a
blemish of the eye induces a great irregularity and is not remitted except by
papal dispensation.

One who lacks the power of sight in his right eye is not irregular, provided

lacks sight in his left eye, he is irregular. For although this does not render
the face absolutely deformed, it nevertheless causes the very act of
celebrating—that is, reading the canon—to be performed with a certain
deformity. “For this reason,” says Suárez, “the left eye is customarily called
the eye of the Canon in Roman usage.” A man whose thumb is unable
to break the host is irregular, as is gathered from the final chapter of title
20, book 1 of the *Decretals*. In imitation of the law in Deuteronomy 23:2,
“Let no bastard enter the assembly of Jehovah; even his tenth generation shall
not enter the assembly of Jehovah”—that is, let him hold no public office
among God’s people, as Junius interprets it—they introduced another
irregularity from defect of birth. That law, enacted concerning a *mamzer*,
that is, one born from a prostitute, with an unknown father and certain
infamy, they extended to all born from any unlawful intercourse, such as
bastards and those we call natural children. They established that no one not
born from a lawful marriage could obtain a benefice or order, alleging that
the father’s incontinence should be feared and the parents’ crime punished in
their children. But Augustine says, “From whatever source people are born, if
they do not follow their parents’ faults but rightly worship God, they will be
honorable and saved.” “Jephthah, numbered among the holy men by the apostle’s
voice, was the son of a prostitute,” says Jerome to Pammachius. The same Jerome
writes there: “To be born from adultery is not the fault of the one born, but
of the one who begets him.” “It is proved to be quite perverse and contrary to
ecclesiastical judgment for anyone to be deprived uselessly according to some
men’s wishes when his own fault or crime does not cast him down from the rank
of the office he performs,” says Gregory in a letter to Columba, bishop of
Numidia, as Gratian has it in the *Decree*. The Synod of Middelburg in 1581
therefore rightly decreed: “When bastards and illegitimate persons possess the
necessary divine gifts, there is no reason or cause why they ought not or
cannot be admitted to the offices of deacon, elder, and minister of the word,”
as Scultingius reports. Not only a bigamist—by which the papists mean one who
successively had two wives at different times—but even one who married a widow
is irregular among them. The Mosaic law commanded that the high priest could
marry only a virgin, Leviticus 21. They derive the irregularity of homicide
from 1 Chronicles 22: “You shall not build a house for my name, because you
have shed much blood upon the earth,” that is, you will not have enough leisure
from wars to build it. The shedding of blood was not an irregularity in the
priests of the Old Testament themselves and the ministers of the temple, as
Aquinas wishes. It therefore could not have passed from the Old Testament to
ministers of the New. Indeed, because the Levites killed three thousand,
Moses says, “Consecrate your ministry to Jehovah today,” Exodus 32:29. If any
mystery is present here,

Nevertheless, the papists run riot in irregularities of this kind. One who

Various irregularities arise from the unlawful and improper reception,

irregular; an adulterer is not irregular unless infamy arises from his

irregularities other than the papists do; whether they posit all of them is

ecclesiastical courts which conflict neither with the statutes or municipal
laws of the realm, nor with recent ecclesiastical constitutions enacted by
authority of statute, nor prejudice royal privileges.” From these words I
gather that almost all the irregularities of canon law remain in the English
church because none, or very few, are abolished by statutes of the realm or
recent ecclesiastical constitutions. They either observe or can observe the
rest. I said “can observe” because they neglect certain irregularities of
greater importance which are divine impediments rather than canonical ones and
which they especially ought to observe. The irregularity of apostasy is
numbered among the most serious. Yet very many English presbyters who renounced
the papacy under Edward VI and performed the office of sacrificing priests in
Mary’s time either retained or easily recovered their rank in Elizabeth’s
reign, as the authors of the admonition to Parliament and Cartwright report.
In Josiah’s time the priests who had burned incense in Judah’s high places
were removed, they and their posterity, from their ministry; nor were they
permitted to offer at Jehovah’s altar in Jerusalem, 2 Kings 23:8–9.

Concerning the Levites who went far away from the Lord and followed the dung
gods when the Israelites went astray, the Lord says that “they shall not draw
near me to serve me as priests or approach any of my holy things or the most
holy things, but shall bear their shame and their abominations which they
committed,” Ezekiel 44:10, 13. Although they were from Aaron’s line and God
chose his priests from no source other than the tribe of Levi, he nevertheless
removed them from office because they polluted themselves with foreign
worship. Much more should men be removed from their rank in the Christian
church, where there is no distinction of tribes but a choice may be made from
every quarter, rather than being fully restored or retained. Cyprian writes as
follows to Epictetus and the people of Assuras concerning Fortunatian, formerly
their bishop: “I was gravely and painfully moved, dearly beloved brothers, when
I learned that Fortunatian, once your bishop, after the grave lapse of his
ruin, now wishes to act as if sound and has begun to claim the episcopate for
himself.” A little below: “The wretch still dares to claim the priesthood he
betrayed, as if after the devil’s altars it were lawful to approach God’s
altar.” And later: “Those who have brought grave offenses upon themselves—that
is, who by sacrificing to idols performed sacrilegious sacrifices—cannot claim
God’s priesthood for themselves.” In the first book of his letters he likewise
writes concerning Basilides and Martial, men stained by certificates of
idolatry and convicted by consciousness of abominable deeds: “Such men attempt
in vain to usurp the episcopate for themselves, since it is manifest that men
of this kind can neither preside over Christ’s church nor ought to offer
sacrifices to God. This is especially so since, long ago, together with us and
absolutely all the bishops established throughout the world, Cornelius our
colleague, a peaceable and righteous priest, decreed that men of this kind may
indeed be admitted to perform penance but are to be prohibited from clerical
ordination and priestly honor.”

At the Council of Ancyra it was decreed that presbyters who could not endure
their tortures to the end but, overcome, denied the faith and sacrificed to
idols, if afterward they seriously resisted and performed penance with pure
confession, should be received only to honor and their seat, and should neither
offer nor deliver a sermon—that is, neither teach nor perform anything else
priestly. This is the canon’s meaning according to Balsamon. Therefore, on the
contrary, those who yielded before being tortured or pretended that tortures
had been threatened were considered worthy of neither honor nor seat.
Cartwright reports that those apostates were such men. He distinguishes their
apostasy from Peter’s fall, which Whitgift alleged, as follows: Peter denied
Christ for one hour; they did so for many years. Peter retained in his heart
the one he denied with his mouth; they renounced the truth with both mouth and
hand, signing their apostasy with their own handwriting.

Peter feared for his life; they feared for ecclesiastical honors and

hurled insults and reproaches against the gospel and evangelicals. Peter fell

does not help them, because Peter, called immediately and extraordinarily by
Christ to the apostolate, was restored to his rank—or rather confirmed
anew—by Christ, who has power to dispense from his own laws. Aaron, defiled by
the idolatry of the golden calf, was nevertheless not removed from the
priesthood because he was called immediately by God, and, since Moses prayed
for him in Deuteronomy 9:20, it is probable that God both pardoned the fault
and confirmed him in the priestly function. But we must learn from 2 Kings 23
and Ezekiel 44 what the church ought to do according to ordinary law. What
Cyprian said concerning Fortunatian and his followers can therefore be turned
against those apostate sacrificing priests: “They desire the stipends,
offerings, and profits upon which they formerly lay with insatiable greed, and
even now they gape for the dinners and banquets whose surfeit they daily belched
out with the crudity of the day before still remaining, most plainly proving
that even before they served not religion but rather the belly and gain with
profane greed.” Whitgift maintains that apostate sacrificing priests can
either be restored to their rank or retained, provided that they return to
their senses and their apostasy is cleansed by repentance. Let the Lord judge
their inward return to soundness; we read nothing of outward and public
repentance before the church. Moreover, irregularity is not removed by
repentance but by dispensation. Even after repentance has been performed,
irregularity, the canonical impediment, remains. Therefore, however much a man
repents of his offense, he cannot by the force of any sentence be freed from
such a burden. Absolution does not apply to irregularity, but dispensation
does. According to the canonists, the irregularity of apostasy can be dispensed
by no one beneath the supreme pontiff, who claims divine and human things for
himself. After repentance has been performed and the wound healed, the scar
remains. The Levites and priests discussed above returned to their senses but
were not restored to their rank. The irregularity of apostasy is imposed not
so much as a penalty for the offense as to avoid scandal and for greater
caution and the church’s security, lest those unstable in faith be promoted
to the ministry.
