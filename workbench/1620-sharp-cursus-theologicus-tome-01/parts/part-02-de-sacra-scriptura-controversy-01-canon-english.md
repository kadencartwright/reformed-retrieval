ON HOLY SCRIPTURE

There are ten controversies between us and the Roman Catholics concerning
Holy Scripture: (1) the canon of Scripture; (2) its authentic edition; (3)
translations; (4) by whom it is to be read; (5) conducting sacred services in
public; (6) the authority of Scripture; (7) its clarity; (8) its
interpretation; (9) its necessity; and (10) its perfection, against unwritten
traditions.

First Controversy: The Canon of Holy Scripture

The books of Holy Scripture are called canonical because they contain the rule
of faith and morals. For just as the law of a commonwealth is called a canon
because citizens must live by it in political society, so Scripture is the
church’s canon because every mem—

[1620 Tome I | col. 9 | BSB frame 00019]

—ber of the church must conform life and morals to Scripture.

The church’s canon has a twofold office: first, to teach the truth; second, to
decide all controversies about religion (Deut. 13:1; 2 Tim. 3:16).

The canon in the church is twofold, divine and ecclesiastical. The divine
canon, according to the period, is Old or New. The old canon, given to the
ancient church of the Jews, is contained in the books of the Old Testament. In
substance it was always the same, but it varied with respect to fuller
revelation.

The books of this canon are divided into three classes. The first contains the
law, namely, the five books of Moses. The second contains the prophets, who
are former or latter. Among the former prophets were written the books of
Joshua, Judges, the two books of Samuel, and the two books of Kings. The
latter prophets are either major or minor. The major prophets are four; the
minor are twelve. The third class contains the *hagiographa*. Although this
name is general, the Greeks use it specifically to designate the remaining
books of the Old Testament, for the rest are contained in this class. Thus
Christ divides the books of the Old Testament into the Law, the Prophets, and
the Psalms in Luke 24:44, understanding by the name “Psalms” the remaining
books of this kind.

These books are also divided according to style, for some were composed in
prose and others in measured language. Hence those five—the books of Job,
Psalms, Song of Songs, Proverbs, and Ecclesiastes—are called poetic. They are
also divided according to time: some were written before the Babylonian
deportation, some during the captivity, and some afterward, as is clear from
the books themselves.

The new divine canon delivered to the Christian church is contained in the
books of the New Testament. These are either historical, such as the four
Gospels and the Acts of the Apos—

[1620 Tome I | col. 10 | BSB frame 00019]

—tles; dogmatic, such as all the apostolic epistles; or prophetic, such as
Revelation. The dogmatic books are subdivided according to those to whom they
are inscribed. They are either catholic, written to several Christian
churches, such as the epistles to the Hebrews, of James, the two of Peter, the
first of John, and Jude; or more particular, having been written either to
particular persons, such as Paul’s epistles to Timothy, Titus, and Philemon,
and likewise John’s two later epistles; or at least to particular churches,
such as Paul’s remaining epistles. Nevertheless, the doctrine contained in
all and each of these pertains to the whole church as a rule of faith and
morals, and these two canons, old and new, constitute one most perfect rule of
faith and morals.

The ecclesiastical canon, called *deuterokanon* by the Greeks, contains the
Apocrypha. Although these contain no impious doctrine, when they were examined
by the canon of Holy Scripture, the church found that they did not agree in
every respect with the canon as did the books contained in the true canon, nor
did they savor of divine majesty, wisdom, truth, and the Holy Spirit’s
dictation as the prophetic and apostolic books do. Instead, they appeared to
have been written by a human spirit. They were therefore called
“apocryphal”—that is, hidden and secret—first, because the church did not
recognize them as equal in honor; second, because their authors’ names lay
hidden; third, because they contain certain things that were unknown to, and
indeed contrary to, Moses and the prophets; and fourth, because for these
reasons it was forbidden that these books be read publicly in the church, as
the author of the exposition of the creed among Cyprian’s works testifies.

The apocryphal books are Judith, Tobit, Third and Fourth Esdras, Wisdom,
Ecclesiasticus, Baruch, the Epistle of Jeremiah, chapters 13 and 14 of Daniel,
the So—

[1620 Tome I | col. 11 | BSB frame 00020]

—ng of the Three Young Men added to the third chapter of Daniel, the books of
Maccabees, and the material appended to the book of Esther from verse 10 of
the third chapter.

We and the Roman Catholics agree that all the books of the divine canon of the
Old and New Testaments are to be regarded as canonical. We disagree, however,
because they wish certain apocryphal books to be received with the canonical
books as a rule of faith and morals. In session 4 the Council of Trent adds to
the divine canon those six books—Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, and the
two books of Maccabees—and does so with a grave threat: “If anyone does not
receive as sacred and canonical the complete books themselves, with all their
parts, as they are contained in the old Latin Vulgate edition, let him be
anathema.” Likewise the Jesuits, such as Bellarmine in book 1, chapters 7, 8,
and 9 of *On the Word of God*, add all the rest mentioned above.

The state of the first controversy, therefore, will be this: whether these
books and additions to books ought to be received as Holy Scripture or not.
The Roman Catholics affirm that they should; we defend the contrary position
with the following arguments.

I. All the canonical books of the Old Testament were written by prophets. But
none of these books was written by a prophet. Therefore, they are not
canonical. The major premise is proved from 2 Peter 1:19–21, Romans 1:2 and
16:26, Luke 1:70, 16:30, and 4:27, Ephesians 2:20, and Hebrews 1:1. It can also
be proved by induction. The minor premise is confirmed by three arguments.

1. By everyone’s confession, Malachi was the last prophet of the Jews, and no
prophet intervened between him and John the Baptist. But these books were
written after Malachi, as is clear from Ecclesiasticus and Maccabees.
Therefore, they were not written by a prophet.

2. All the books composed by the prophets were written in the language the
prophets used, that is,

[1620 Tome I | col. 12 | BSB frame 00020]

Hebrew or Aramaic. But these books were not written in Hebrew or Aramaic,
which was then the native and common language, but in a foreign and unknown
language. The Fathers affirm this, the fact itself proves it, and the Roman
Catholics concede it for the most part. Therefore, and so forth.

3. If these books had been written by prophets, then Christ and the apostles
would have used them as witnesses to their teaching—for all the prophetic
Scriptures testify about Christ, and Christ and the apostles used them. But
neither Christ nor the apostles used the testimony of these books. Therefore,
they were not written by prophets.

II. The church received all the canonical books of the Old Testament. But it
did not receive these books. Therefore, they are not canonical. The major
premise is proved as follows. If that church had rejected any part of
canonical Scripture, it would have been guilty of a very great crime in doing
so, and Christ and the apostles would have rebuked it on that account. For if
Christ rebuked them in Matthew 5 because they had interpreted the Scriptures
badly, he would have rebuked them much more if they had rejected a part of
Scripture as noncanonical. But he did not rebuke them on this account.
Furthermore, Romans 3:2 says that “the oracles of God”—that is, Holy
Scripture—were entrusted to the Jews. That the Jews diligently preserved the
entire old canon is testified by Justin in his *Address to the Greeks*,
Augustine in book 1 of *On the Harmony of the Evangelists*, and his Epistles 3
and 59. Finally, if these books were canonical but were not received by the
ancient church, it would follow that many canonical books were not received by
the whole church for as long as that church existed. Yet it is utterly absurd
that the entire church, in which were the prophets and priests, and afterward
Christ himself and the apostles, rejected the will of God. Therefore. The
minor premise is proved by the agree—

[1620 Tome I | col. 13 | BSB frame 00021]

—ment of all the Fathers and even of the Roman Catholics themselves, and it
will be evident below. Therefore.

III. A book is known to be canonical in only two ways: (1) by the testimony of
some prophet or apostle, or (2) by the testimony of those who were living when
that book was published and who could certainly know whether it was published
by a prophet or apostle. But we do not know by either of these ways that the
disputed books are canonical. We do not know it by the first, because there
are now no prophets or apostles; nor by the second, because far from anyone
being able to testify that a prophet or apostle published or received these
books, even in Jerome’s time they had not yet been received into the canon, as
he himself testifies. And if the apostles had received and acknowledged the
books, as Bellarmine maintains in book 1, chapter 10 of *On the Word of God*,
they would have used their testimony as they used that of the other books of
the Old Testament. But they did not use them. Therefore, these books are not
to be acknowledged as canonical.

IV. The principles of theology—which are the canonical books—must be certain,
beyond controversy, immediately true, and of such a character that they
cannot be rejected because of these qualities. But these books are not of
this kind, because they have been and are especially disputed; they contain
many false things; and they were received by neither the ancient nor the new
true church, as will be proved below. Therefore.

Testimonies[^1]

2. Jerome says in the Helmeted Prologue to Paulinus: “This prologue to the
Scriptures may serve as a helmeted introduction to all the books that we have
translated from Hebrew into Latin, so that we may know that whatever is
outside these is to be placed among the Apocrypha. Therefore the Wisdom of
Solomon, Jesus, Judith, and Tobit are not in the canon.”

3. The same author says in his preface to the books of Solomon: “Just as the
books of Judith, Tobit, and Maccabees are indeed read by the chur—

[1620 Tome I | col. 14 | BSB frame 00021]

—ch, but it does not receive them among the canonical Scriptures, so let it also
read these two volumes—the Wisdom of Solomon and Sirach—for the edification of
the people, not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas.”

1. The Synod of Laodicea in chapter 59 forbids noncanonical books to be read
in the churches and permits only the canonical books of the Old and New
Testaments. It then numbers among the canonical books only those received by
us, although it joins the book of Baruch with Jeremiah and omits Revelation.

At the end of his commentary on the historical books of the Old Testament,
Cardinal Cajetan says: “The remaining books—namely, Judith, Tobit, Maccabees,
together with Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus—are placed by St. Jerome outside the
canonical books among the Apocrypha, as is clear in the Helmeted Prologue. Do
not be troubled, novice, if you find these books numbered among the canonical
books anywhere, whether in sacred councils or in sacred teachers, for the
words of both councils and teachers must be brought to Jerome’s touchstone,”
and so forth.

In his prologue to Joshua, Cardinal Hugh calls the disputed books just
mentioned apocryphal and says that they were received not for the proof of
faith but for instruction in morals.

In his book *On Weights and Measures*, Epiphanius numbers the same quantity
and the same books that we receive in the canon.

In the prologue to his exposition of the Psalms, Hilary, like Epiphanius,
numbers the same twenty-two books as canonical according to the number of
Hebrew letters.

1. Objection. The Third Council of Carthage in canon 47; the Council of Trent
in session 4; Pope Innocent I in his epistle to Exuperius; Pope Gelasius I in
his decree on sacred and ecclesiastical books; Augustine in book 2, chapter 8
of *On Christian Doctrine*; Isi—

[1620 Tome I | col. 15 | BSB frame 00022]

—dore of Seville in book 6, chapter 1 of the *Etymologies*; and Cassiodorus in
book 2 of the *Divinations* number these books and place them in the same rank
as the other canonical books. Therefore, they are canonical.

The inference is denied, because whatever certain Fathers and some councils
said is not immediately true; the prerogative of infallible truth is proper to
Holy Scripture alone. Second, that Third Council of Carthage was held in the
year of the Lord 400 and was only provincial; even the Roman Catholics
themselves do not receive all its decrees. For canon 26 forbids the bishop of
the first see to be called “high priest,” “prince of priests,” or anything of
that kind. Third, when that canon was framed in the fourth session of the
recent little Council of Trent, which was convened to confirm papal heresies,
only fifty persons in all were present from among the legates, cardinals,
bishops, and archbishops. Fourth, Gelasius, together with his council of
seventy bishops, recites only one book of Maccabees in the canon, but judges
the other to be apocryphal along with the book of Nehemiah, which is
nevertheless truly canonical.

5. In chapter 23 of the place cited, Augustine distinguishes these books from
the truly canonical books. He does indeed call them canonical in a broad use
of the term, but only in the sense that they were customarily read in the
church for the edification of the people, not because they had the same
certainty and authority as the other truly canonical books. Let the same
judgment be made concerning the others, and even concerning the Carthaginian
Fathers themselves. For this reason, even the Roman Catholics divide the
books of the Old Testament into *kanonikoi*, which are properly canonical,
and *deuterokanonikoi*, which are indeed called canonical by some in a
particular respect but were never received into the Hebrew canon. Sixtus of
Siena makes this distinction

[1620 Tome I | col. 16 | BSB frame 00022]

in book 1 of his *Library*. The canon for reading must therefore be
distinguished from the canon of faith.

They insist: But that Council of Carthage was confirmed by the general
Council in Trullo at Constantinople.

Response: If that decree concerning the canon has standing because it was
confirmed by this council, then the decree concerning the title of the bishop
of the first see was likewise confirmed. But our adversaries do not concede
this.

Second, the papists themselves doubt the authority of this council’s decrees.
Thus Pighius calls its acts by no means genuine, and Melchior Cano says in the
last chapter of book 5 that they do not have ecclesiastical authority. Nor do
any Roman Catholics receive certain of its decrees, such as its equating the
Constantinopolitan bishop with the Roman bishop in canon 36 and forbidding
deacons or presbyters to separate from their wives in canon 13, and so forth.

Finally, this council is convicted of error by the earlier Council of
Laodicea, which was celebrated in the year of Christ 300.

On the second objection. In the Apostolic Canons, three books of Maccabees and
Ecclesiasticus are received into the canon. Therefore, they are canonical.

The inference is denied, because Pope Gelasius himself, around the year of
Christ 495, declared the book of the Apostolic Canons apocryphal. In fact,
these canons are not apostolic. First, the number of the canons was never
certainly established. The Synod of Constantinople receives eighty-five
canons in canon 2, but Gratian says in distinction 16 that there are only
fifty, and that they are apocryphal, on the authority of Isidore, who wrote
that they were composed by heretics under the apostles’ name. Pope Zephyrinus,
who was five hundred years earlier than the Synod of Constantinople,
acknowledged sixty; so did Leo IX. Second, the can—

[1620 Tome I | col. 17 | BSB frame 00023]

—ons themselves teach this very thing. For in the last canon, the Gospel of
John is numbered among the Scriptures of the New Testament. But this Gospel
was written by John after most, if not all, of the apostles had died, namely,
in the time of Domitian. What some say—that the apostles foresaw the Gospel of
John—is ridiculous. Likewise, in the last chapter all the apostles speak and
call them “our acts,” but most of the apostles died before Acts was written.
Although some maintain that at least Clement issued these canons, this is
nevertheless false, since Clement died in the time of Vespasian, as Damasus
and others testify. He therefore could not have seen the Gospel of John, which
is mentioned in these canons, since John wrote his Gospel only after his
return from Patmos during Trajan’s reign, which is the common opinion of the
most weighty authors. Furthermore, these canons approve Clement’s
constitutions and his two epistles, but the Synod of Constantinople, which
received the Apostolic Canons, rejects Clement’s constitutions. Finally, these
canons do not support the Roman Catholics either, because they receive three
books of Maccabees and Ecclesiasticus but reject Tobit, Judith, and Wisdom.

On the Book of Baruch

The book of Baruch is not canonical, for it was not written by a prophet.
Therefore, it is not canonical. The antecedent is proved as follows: if this
book was composed by any of the prophets, then either Jeremiah or Baruch is
its author. But neither wrote the book. For as Jeremiah spoke in Hebrew, so he
also wrote in Hebrew, as is evident from his prophecies. But according to
Jerome, this book did not exist or circulate in Hebrew among the Hebrews.
Baruch also wrote in Hebrew the things that he re—

[1620 Tome I | col. 18 | BSB frame 00023]

—ceived from Jeremiah’s mouth (Jer. 36:4). This book, however, was written in
Greek, as its style itself teaches and as Bellarmine also concedes in book 1,
chapter 8 of *On the Word of God*. Therefore.

Likewise, the ancient councils, the Fathers, and even the popes who list the
canonical books do not mention this one. And in book 1, chapter 4, at the
fourth difficulty of *The Catalogue of Scripture*, John Driedo denies that
this book is canonical.

Objections

Objection 1. A testimony from this book is cited in 2 Maccabees 2. Therefore,
this book will be canonical.

The inference is denied, for no apocryphal book can render another apocryphal
book canonical by its testimony.

Objection 2. Many Fathers cite testimonies from this book. Therefore, it is
canonical.

Response. The inference is denied because the Fathers also cite testimonies
from poets and philosophers, yet their books are not canonical. Indeed, not
even the books that the apostles themselves quote in canonical Scripture are
canonical. Otherwise, Menander, whom the apostle mentions in 1 Corinthians
15:33; Aratus, from whom a testimony is quoted in Acts 17:28; and Epimenides
(Titus 1:12, 12)[^2] would be canonical books. For neither
the apostles nor the Fathers extract such sayings from authors of this kind
to confirm the faith, but only when morals are under discussion, since people
otherwise profane wrote well concerning morals.

Objection 3. The church takes certain readings from this book in annual
services. Therefore, this book is canonical. Response. We deny the inference:
first, because

[1620 Tome I | col. 19 | BSB frame 00024]

a church that does this is antichristian; second, because Jerome and other
Fathers testify that the church was indeed formerly accustomed to read
noncanonical books, yet not for confirming the faith, but only for the benefit
of instructing the people in morals.

On the Seven Chapters Added to the Book of Esther

These chapters are not part of canonical Scripture, for things that contain
material conflicting with canonical Scripture are not part of canonical
Scripture. But these chapters contain such material. Therefore.

The minor premise is proved as follows: what is said in chapters 11:2 and 12:1
in the Apocrypha conflicts with what is said in canonical chapter 2:16.
Likewise, what is said in apocryphal chapter 12:5 conflicts with what is said
in canonical chapter 6:3. What is said in apocryphal chapter 15:7 also
conflicts with what is said in canonical chapter 5:2. Furthermore, what is
said in apocryphal chapter 16:10 conflicts with what is said in canonical
chapter 8:3. Therefore.

On the Additions to Daniel

These added portions about which there is controversy conflict with canonical
Scripture. Therefore, they are not canonical. The antecedent is proved by
comparing chapter 15, verse 45[^3] with the genuine Daniel 2:48 and 6:3, and the
first and second chapters of the genuine Daniel with chapter 13:64. Likewise,
what chapter 14 says about the prophet Habakkuk is wholly false, because
Habakkuk prophesied before the Babylonian captivity, as is evident from his
own prophecy. But if he li—

[1620 Tome I | col. 20 | BSB frame 00024]

—ved at the time mentioned in this chapter 14, he prophesied for more than a
hundred years, which is not plausible. Therefore.

2. These chapters were not written in Hebrew by that true and eminent Daniel.
Indeed, even the Roman Catholics do not agree about who the Daniel mentioned
in chapter 14 was, so that Bellarmine rightly says in book 1, chapter 9 of *On
the Word of God* that it is not improbable that this was another Daniel. For
that great Daniel, a true prophet, was from the tribe of Judah, but this man
was a priest from the tribe of Levi, as the Seventy translators and Jerome
testify. Yet only Daniel the prophet was always received as canonical.
Therefore. For if another Daniel was the author of these added apocryphal
portions, as he was, why are his writings mingled with those of someone else,
or falsely attributed to the other man? Hence Jerome rightly says in his
prologue to Daniel that these chapters are apocryphal, as does the papist John
Driedo in the last chapter of book 1 of *The Catalogue of Scripture*.
Therefore.

On the Book of Tobit

The book of Tobit is not canonical, for this book does not exist in Hebrew.
The Greek and Latin copies that do exist, moreover, are exceedingly corrupt
and contradict each other, as will be evident to anyone who compares them.

2. In his preface to the books of Solomon, and likewise in his prologue to
this book, Jerome says that it is not canonical.

On the Book of Judith

The book of Judith is not canonical. First, the Latin and Greek versions that
exist today differ greatly from each other, as in 1:12, 5:14, 7:9, and chapter
2. According to Jerome, it was first written in Aramaic,

[1620 Tome I | col. 21 | BSB frame 00025]

and its copies were already corrupt at that time; today they have perished
entirely. Hence Jerome says that he translated sense for sense, not word for
word.

3. This book conflicts with Holy Scripture. Therefore, it is not canonical.
The antecedent is proved as follows: in 9:2 it praises what is condemned in
Genesis 34:25 and 49:5–6, and in 9:18 it asks God to bless lies. Likewise, 1:1
says that this history took place in the twelfth year of Nebuchadnezzar before
the Babylonian captivity, but 5:21–22 says that it happened after the temple’s
destruction and even after the people’s return. Therefore, it also conflicts
with itself and consequently is not canonical.

Objection

According to Jerome, this book was numbered among the Holy Scriptures by the
Council of Nicaea. Therefore, at least according to his testimony, this book
is canonical.

Response

It will follow only that Jerome contradicts himself, which nevertheless does
not prove that this book is canonical.

2. If this book is canonical because that council numbered it among the books
of Holy Scripture, then all the other books in question are not canonical,
because that council did not number them among the Holy Scriptures. Thus the
Roman Catholics stab themselves with their own sword.

3. Jerome’s words in his preface to Judith are as follows: “The Council of
Nicaea is read to have counted this book in the number of the Holy
Scriptures.” But he does not relate where this is read. Nor would the Council
of Laodicea, held afterward, have omitted this book from the canon if the
Council of Nicaea had received it into the canon. Finally, there is a great
difference between a book’s being canonical and its being counted among the
Holy Scriptures. For the deuterocanonical books are indeed numbered among
Holy Scripture and read as sacred in

[1620 Tome I | col. 22 | BSB frame 00025]

the church, as we said above from Jerome; nevertheless, they are not truly and
properly canonical.

On the Book of Wisdom

The book of Wisdom is not canonical, which is proved by three arguments.

1. Most of the ancients, and Bonaventure in his commentary on this book, say
that Philo the Jew composed it. But according to Josephus, he lived after
Christ in the time of Caligula. Now since, according to Christ, the Law and
the Prophets—and thus the period of the Old Testament—ended with John the
Baptist, this book cannot be canonical in the Old Testament.

2. This book contains false things; see chapter 14, verse 5. Therefore, it is
not canonical.

3. Jerome calls this book *pseudepigraphos*, at the same time testifying that
it is not Solomon’s but savors of Greek eloquence. For if it had been
Solomon’s, it would exist written in Hebrew like his other books. But it was
not written in Hebrew. Therefore, it is not canonical.

Objections

Objection 1. In chapter 9:10 Solomon is presented composing prayers.
Therefore, it is Solomon’s book and consequently canonical.

The inference is denied, for a writer can present Solomon by imitation.
Furthermore, many papists assert that Solomon did not write this book, such as
Driedo in book 1, chapter 4, at the fourth difficulty. He says that it is
customary in the Scriptures for the one who speaks to speak in the person of
another. And in the special preface to Leviticus, John of Capistrano says that
Philo spoke in the person of Solomon.

Objection 2. Paul used the testimony of this book in Romans 11:34 from its
chapter 9:13, as also in 1 Corinthians 2:16 from its chapter 7:16. Therefore.

[1620 Tome I | col. 23 | BSB frame 00026]

The antecedent is denied. The apostle does not profess to quote a testimony
from this book, since he does not name this book as he does other books of the
Old Testament. Nor does it follow that because the passages cited have words
similar to those in the book of Wisdom, he therefore quoted from it. Nor does
this follow either: the apostle in the cited passages alluded to some writing
of the Old Testament; therefore, he alluded to the passage in Wisdom. For the
words adduced in Romans 11 were taken from Isaiah 40:13.

On the Book of Ecclesiasticus

The book of Ecclesiasticus is not canonical. First, the Greek and Latin
editions conflict with each other, as in 1:14; chapter 27; 3:20; chapter 28;
16:21; and so forth. Therefore.

2. In the prologue, the book’s author asks pardon if he has not translated
something well. Therefore, he was not a prophet or led by the prophetic
Spirit. Moreover, that Hebrew version, if there was one, does not exist today.

3. This book contains false things. Therefore, it is not canonical. The
antecedent is proved as follows: in 46:23 Samuel is said to have prophesied
after death. But from 1 Samuel 28 it is evident that the one who rose and
prophesied was not the true Samuel but rather a demonic apparition. Likewise,
Augustine says in book 2, chapter 23 of *On Christian Doctrine* that an image
of the dead Samuel foretold true things to Saul, and in book 2, chapter 11 of
*On the Miracles of Scripture* that it was the devil. He says the same in
question 27 of the book *Questions on the Old and New Testaments*. So too does
Isidore in Gratian, question 26, chapter 5.

4. This book was written after all the prophets in the time of Ptolemy
Euergetes. Therefore, it is not canonical. The antecedent is evident from the
first chapter of the book itself.

On the Books of Maccabees

The First and Second Books of Maccabees

[1620 Tome I | col. 24 | BSB frame 00026]

are not canonical. This is proved as follows.

1. All the books of the Old Testament were written by prophets. But no prophet
existed either in the very time of the Maccabees or after their time until
Christ, as appears from 1 Maccabees 4:46, 9:27, and 14:41. Therefore.

2. According to Jerome in his *Catalogue of Illustrious Men* and book 2
*Against the Pelagians*, and also Eusebius in his book *On Times*, the author
of these books was Josephus. But he was not a prophet and lived only after
Christ; indeed, he refused to acknowledge Christ. Therefore, they are not
canonical.

3. In 2 Maccabees 12 Judas Maccabeus is praised because he offered a sacrifice
for the dead. But this was done impiously. No sacrifice is praiseworthy except
one that God has commanded, since everything introduced into the worship of
God without his command is to be condemned as *ethelothrēskeia*, or
self-imposed worship. But sacrifices of this kind rest on no command of God,
as is evident from the law. Moreover, it is evident that this sacrifice was
unlawful because it was offered for those who had polluted themselves with
idolatry and sacrilege and had perished in that crime. But according even to
the Roman Catholics, no sacrifice ought to be offered for those who commit a
mortal sin and die in it. Therefore.

4. In 2 Maccabees 2 many things are said about Jeremiah that do not occur in
Jeremiah’s own history. In particular, it is wholly false that he hid the ark,
tent, and altar on a certain mountain, for he was in prison until the
destruction of the city, as is evident from Jeremiah 37 and 38. Therefore,
while the city and temple stood, he could not have removed and hidden them,
nor would the rulers and priests have permitted it. Then, after the city was
