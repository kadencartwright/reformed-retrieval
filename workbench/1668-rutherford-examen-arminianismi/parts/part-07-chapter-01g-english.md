2 TIMOTHY 3:16, 17

All Scripture is divinely inspired and is useful for teaching, reproof,
correction, and training in righteousness, so that the man of God may be
perfect, perfectly equipped for every good work.

From this it is clear that the Scriptures are so full and perfect that not
only the traditions of the Papists, but also all human ceremonies and every
positive observance devised by Antichrist or by false prophets in divine
worship, conflict with the fullness of the Scriptures. You will say, reader,
that the Arminians do not sin in this matter. But I say that they sin
shamefully here, and, as I believe, with a bad conscience, in order to gratify
the English bishops who now hold power. This is so even though the Arminians
in England deliberately defend this error against the perfection of Scripture,
because they teach in the *Apology*, chapter 21, folio 292, that the government
of the church by presbyters and elders is not unlawful and condemnable, but
nevertheless was not instituted by the apostles. They say

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 98 | PDF p. 149]

that it is presumption to condemn the ranks of deacons, archdeacons, presbyters,
archpresbyters, bishops, and archbishops; and yet they do not dare to assert
that these are of divine right or apostolic institution. Likewise, they
maintain that infant baptism is not unlawful, and yet that it has no foundation
in the Word of God. Thus they hold that ministers in the church are lawful,
and that certain things are lawful which nevertheless have no foundation in
God's Word. This detracts from the perfection of the Scriptures.

Hence, against the Arminians either in Belgium or in England, we defend this
thesis:

All new offices, such as the pseudo-episcopate; all sacred rites and ceremonies
of positive observance; all mystical signs, such as the surplice, the square
cap, feast days, and the rest of Antichrist's profane warblings and trifles,
are for this very reason unlawful and condemnable: they have no foundation in
the Word of God.

1. In these matters the negative argument from the authority of Scripture
holds firm: “This is not in the Scriptures; therefore, it is unlawful.” Thus
Acts 15:24: “The apostles commanded no such thing; therefore, you ought not to
be circumcised.” Hebrews 1:13: “God said to none of the angels, ‘Sit at my
right hand’; therefore, it is not lawful to attribute this to angels.” Hebrews
7:14: “Moses said nothing about anyone from the tribe of Judah ministering at
the altar; therefore, it is forbidden for anyone from that tribe to be

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 99 | PDF p. 150]

a minister of the altar.” Nadab's fire is unlawful. Leviticus 10:1. Why? Because
God did not command it. So also Jeremiah 7:31; chapter 9, verse 14; and chapter
32, verse 35: “The high places of Baal and the sacrifice of children to Molech
were not commanded by God, nor did it ever enter God's heart to command them.
Therefore, they are unlawful.” Second Samuel 7:7: “Wherever I went with the
children of Israel, did I speak to any of the rulers of Israel, whom I
commanded to shepherd my people Israel, saying, ‘Why have you not built me a
cedar house?’ Therefore, David was not permitted to build the temple.” In the
same way Jehovah might say, “I have said nothing in my Word about bishops
usurping superiority and jurisdictional power over presbyters; nothing about
the sign of the cross, nothing about the surplice, nothing about feast days,
nothing about archdeacons, archpresbyters, and archbishops; nor did I say to
my pastors, ‘Why do you not serve me in these things?’ Therefore, all these
things are unlawful.” Next, the following argument is of the same stamp: “This
is of human institution in the worship of God; therefore, it is unlawful.” So
in 1 Kings 12:32–33, Jeroboam's feast day is unlawful. Why? Because it was on
the day and in the month that he had devised in his own heart. So Jeremiah
18:12: “According to the imaginations of their own heart”; therefore, they are
vain. Acts 7:43: “The figures which you made”; therefore, they are abominable.
Hosea 10:6 and 13:2: “Carved images of silver, according to their own
understanding”; therefore, they are vain. Hence Zanchi comments weightily on
the same verse 6: “We gather from this that all worship which is not

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 100 | PDF p. 151]

taken from God, from God's will, from his Word and law, but is devised by us
and by other people without God's Word, is to be condemned. For if there is
anything in the church of which the Lord could say,
as he says here through the prophet, ‘This is not from me, but from Israel,
from human beings,’ that thing is disapproved by God.” Jeremiah 18:12: “Let us
follow our own thoughts.” Isaiah 50:11: “Go to the light of your fire and to
the firebrands you have kindled.” Psalm 106:39: “They defiled themselves with
their works, prostituting themselves by their deeds.”

They reply, first, that this argument holds in worship regarded as necessary
in the worshiper's opinion, such as the high places of Baal and Jeroboam's
calves, but not in incidental worship, such as the sign of the cross and the
surplice. We regard these with no necessity and no opinion of necessary
worship, but only as indifferent ornaments of divine worship, since they do
not bind consciences.

Response 1. God's argument in the Scriptures does not proceed from an adjunct
of worship or from necessity, but from the efficient cause of worship, thus:
“This lacks the efficient cause it ought to have, namely, God as its author;
therefore, it is unlawful.” Scripture does not say, “This does not have God as
its author and is regarded as worship binding the conscience; therefore, it is
unlawful.” To argue this way is therefore to do violence to the Holy Spirit's
reasoning, which rests upon the fact that the lawgiver's will is the formal
ground of lawfulness in everything commanded. Thus, the reason why eating the
fruit of the tree of knowledge is unlawful is God's

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 101 | PDF p. 152]

will forbidding it; and the reason Abraham could lawfully sacrifice Isaac is God's
will commanding it. If God had commanded eating the fruit of the tree of
knowledge, it would have been lawful. Whatever our opinion about the necessity
or indifference of the thing commanded may be, God's will alone, when it
commands, makes a thing lawful; and when it forbids, makes a thing unlawful.

2. Otherwise God's inference would be empty: “The calf is not from God;
therefore, it is unlawful. Jeroboam's feast day is not from God but from
Jeroboam's heart; therefore, it is unlawful.” For if the calf and those feast
days had been regarded as indifferent things that did not bind the conscience,
they would have been lawful. But in that case the ark under the New Testament,
circumcision, sacrifices, abstinence from such foods, and all Mosaic worship
would be lawful, provided we used it as worship that did not bind the
conscience.

3. Nor did Jeroboam believe worship of the calf to bind consciences, because
he instituted it only to preserve his kingdom. Yet Jeroboam, in worshiping the
calves, was an idolater because his calves had not been commanded by God, even
though he thought their worship was not a matter of religious necessity but
only of political necessity.

Objection 2. It is enough that ceremonies conform to God's Word in general
and are therefore lawful in this way. It is not required, however, that they
be in God's Word in particular. This inference is enough: “Let all things be
done in order and decently; therefore, ceremonies are lawful.”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 102 | PDF p. 153]

Response 1. If by a general foundation in the Word they mean a major premise
in the Word without an assumption, then I shall demonstrate in this way that
it is lawful to worship the devil: “All worship commanded by God in his Word
is lawful. But worship of the devil is worship commanded by God in his Word.
Therefore, it is lawful.” The major is entirely certain, but I cannot answer
for the minor. If this is what they mean when they say ceremonies conform to
God's Word, they say nothing, because in this way worship of the devil also
conforms to God's Word. But if ceremonies conform to God's Word because both
their major and their minor are found in God's Word, how greatly we long to
hear such a syllogism! Such a syllogism, however, is a city in the sphere of
the moon, or a goat-stag.

2. But suppose the whole foundation of ceremonies lies in this inference:
“Let all things be done decently and in order. Therefore, the surplice, signing
with the cross, feast days, and so forth, are lawful.” This inference either
can be proved by God's Word or cannot. If the former, we ask to hear the proof.
But if the inference can be proved by natural light, then it can also be proved
by God's Word, because the light of reason is contained in God's Word as a
part in the whole. Thus God's Word will still prove the inference. But if the
inference is proved by natural reason that is not in God's Word, how we long
to hear such an invincible demonstration! But if the inference is proved by
the will of the governors alone,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 103 | PDF p. 154]

then this will be a valid inference: “Let all things be done in order and
decently. Therefore, let Aaron's calf be made and worshiped in the temple, provided it
seems good to the governors.” The soundness of an inference is indivisible;
therefore, either it is a good inference or it is not. There is no mixed truth
of an inference here which depends partly upon reason and partly upon the will
of human beings.

But the adversaries say, “Your circumstances are no more approved in God's
Word than our ceremonies.” I therefore add a second argument. Of every action
of which it can be said, according to its specific moral difference, “as it is
written” (καθὼς γέγραπται), that action is determined in the Word either
expressly or by good consequence, even if all the circumstances of place,
time, and person are not expressed in the Word.

But of all our moral actions which are lawful—whether they concern faith or
morals, or the first or the second table—it can be said, “as it is written”
(καθὼς γέγραπται), even if the circumstances are not expressed in the Word.

Therefore, all our moral actions are determined in the Word, even if their
circumstances are not in the Word.

I prove the major premise first. Joseph and Mary's action of offering
turtledoves and pigeons for their son Christ was done “as it is written”
(καθὼς γέγραπται), Luke 2:23. Yet the law in Exodus 13:1–2, 13–14; Numbers
3:11–13 and 8:16–17 says nothing about the circumstances—Joseph, Ma—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 104 | PDF p. 155]

—ry, the priest, the pigeons, or the hour of the offering. So also under the
second table, Amaziah's deed in not killing a son for the father's sin—that
deed, I say, of mercy and justice—was “as it is written in the book of the law
of Moses,” 2 Kings 14:6. Yet the law in Deuteronomy 24:16 says nothing about
Amaziah, that particular deed, or the sons whose lives he spared. Therefore,
the actions themselves are so determined in the Word, even if the Word says
nothing about the circumstances, that it can be said of them that they were
done “as it is written.” Thus Zacharias and Elizabeth walked in all God's
commandments, Luke 1:6, even though their particular actions, their names, the
place, and the time are not found in the Word.

But it cannot be said of ceremonies that they are done “as it is written”
(καθὼς γέγραπται). Thus the public confession of people under public scandal,
made in a public place of the church from which the people can hear their
confession more easily, is “as it is written” (καθὼς γέγραπται), even if the
Word says nothing about a penitential chair. Likewise, a public sermon in a
place from which the whole assembly can hear the Word accords with what is
written in 2 Timothy 4:1–2 and Titus 2:1, even if the preacher's name—John or
Thomas—the particular assembly, the pulpit, this particular passage, and the
day and hour of preaching are not expressed in the Word.

I prove the minor premise. If the Word is the directing rule of a young
person's ac—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 105 | PDF p. 156]

—tions, Psalm 119:9; if it is a lamp to the feet, verse 105; if God's wisdom
causes us to understand justice and judgment, everything right, and every good
path, Proverbs 2:9; if it causes us to travel our way securely and not strike
our foot, Proverbs 3:23; and if the words of God teach the way of wisdom and
lead along paths of uprightness, so that when walking we do not turn our step
aside and when running do not stumble or totter, Proverbs 4:10–12—then the Word
so determines all our actions, both those of the first table, such as whether
pastors ought to wear a surplice to signify pastoral purity and whether
infants ought to be marked with the sign of the cross, and so forth, and those
of the second table, that all can be said to have been done “as it is written”
(καθὼς γέγραπται). But Scripture asserts the former; therefore, it also
asserts the latter.

Now it can be said of all our acts of worshiping God, preaching, singing
psalms, praying, reading, and administering the sacraments that they are done
according to the written Word of God. But this cannot be said of ceremonial
acts. I demonstrate this by the following diagrams, beginning with the
category of acts of divine worship.

1. Worship of God — as it is written.

2. Sacramental worship, or administration of a sacrament — as it is written.

3. Participation in the Lord's Supper — as it is written.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 106 | PDF p. 157]

4. Participation in the Lord's Supper by John and Mary — as it is written. 1
Corinthians 11:23; Matthew 26:26, and so forth.

Likewise concerning ministers of the New Testament:

1. A lawful minister of the New Testament — as it is written.

2. A presbyter preaching the Word of God, or a pastor — as it is written.

3. Archippus, Thomas, and John, pastors — as it is written.

Likewise in ecclesiastical discipline:

1. An act of Christ the King in the church — as it is written.

2. An act of discipline — as it is written.

3. An act of ecclesiastical censure and exercise of the keys — as it is
written.

4. A disciplinary act of the Corinthian church excommunicating the incestuous
man — as it is written, 1 Corinthians 5:4.

Likewise concerning acts of the second table:

1. Love toward the saints — as it is written.

2. An act of charity toward the afflicted — as it is written.

3. An act of charity by which alms are given to poor saints — as it is
written.

4. An act of charity by the Corinthian church giving to the poor—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 107 | PDF p. 158]

—saints at Jerusalem — as it is written, 1 Corinthians 16:1–3.

If a moral action is proved in the last specific degree and according to its
individual being, as it is proved here, all the subordinate degrees and the
highest are also proved. For example, if I prove that this thing is Peter, by
the same operation I prove that it is a human being, an animal, a body, a
living thing, and a substance. But if I prove merely that this is a substance
or a body, I do not thereby prove that it is Peter. For the inference from
lower things in the same category is valid, but not the reverse.

*[Editorial erratum, PDF 52: at p. 107, line 4 the source prints `inviduale`;
the errata directs `individuale` (“individual”), which is adopted here.]*

Now if we apply this evident truth to the adversaries' doctrine of ceremonies,
it will immediately appear that they have written a funeral song for the
Scriptures. Let this, then, be the ceremonial category:

1. Order and decency — as it is written. 1 Corinthians 14:40.

2. Indicative and weighty signs, such as women's veils — as it is written.

3. Ceremonies of human institution — not written.

*[Editorial erratum, PDF 52: at p. 107, line 21 the source prints `sicut
scriptum` (“as it is written”); the errata directs `Non scriptum` (“not
written”), which is adopted here.]*

4. Mystical or symbolic sacred signs instituted by human beings — not written.

5. The surplice, signing with the sign of the cross, and the square cap — not
written.

6. The surplice with which Thomas is clothed, the signing of this infant in
this place and at this time by the man whose whole hope, fixed upon a fat
bishopric,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 108 | PDF p. 159]

is — not written, except in the Papal Code.

You see that our phrase “as it is written” is lacking four times here. Hence
the adversaries' assertion that their ceremonies conform to sacred Scripture
in general is empty. Quite so! This conformity feeds the reader on a vain
hope. Even more futile is their claim that ceremonies conform to the
Scriptures in the same way that all our moral actions, whether of the first or
second table, do. Why, then, are all the degrees in their categorical scale,
especially the last and specific degree, not proved by this middle term,
“because it is so written,” as we contend that all lawful actions must be
proved?

Objection 1. Hooker, *Of Discipline*, book 2, pages 55–57: If wisdom teaches
every good path, then Scripture will teach all arts and sciences, for those
are good ways. But how ridiculous it is to prove that all acts of arts and
sciences—plowing, dancing, singing, constructing mathematical demonstrations,
and speaking Latin—are lawful by saying, “because it is so written in God's
Word.” Is it not enough that such acts are negatively lawful, that is, not
unlawful or contrary to God's Word, since they are neither commanded nor
forbidden by the Word?

Response 1. It does not oppose us that certain acts of arts and sciences are
not taught by the Word. For, first, some actions belong to a hu—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 109 | PDF p. 160]

—man being in so far as he is a living thing and acts. Second, some belong to
a human being in so far as he is an animal. Third, some belong to a human
being in so far as he is this kind of human being, subject to law and a moral agent.

The first kind includes growing, breathing, sensing, hearing, and so forth.
Such things are not regulated by God's Word, for in them the consideration of
lawfulness or unlawfulness has no place.

Actions of the second kind are eating, drinking, and sleeping. These actions
have two aspects. First, they are animal actions and thus natural in
themselves; in this respect they are not capable of regulation by God's Word.
Second, as they are found in a Christian who ought to be ruled by God's Word
and Spirit, they can be regulated by the Word in two ways. First, they are
regulated with respect to the substance of the act in general. The law of
nature, which is a part of the divine Word, commands a person to eat, drink,
and sleep; to abstain entirely from these things would be to kill oneself and
violate God's law. Second, they are also regulated by the Word with respect to
moderation; one must therefore eat, drink, and sleep sparingly and soberly,
according to God's Word in Ecclesiastes 2:24 and 3:12–13; Luke 21:34; and 1
Thessalonians 5:6–7.

Actions of the third kind are those that belong to a person in so far as he is
a human being, or in so far as he is an artistic or scientific agent. Such
actions, as such, are referred to the principles of arts and sciences and to
the ends of intellectual virtues as such: for example, plowing according to
the rules of agriculture, singing ac—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 110 | PDF p. 161]

—cording to the rules of music, speaking Latin according to Despauterius's
rules, and constructing geographical, geometrical, and astronomical
demonstrations according to Euclid's *Elements*. Such actions as such, because
they are not moral and do not proceed from a person as a moral agent but as an
artistic or scientific agent, are not capable of regulation by the Word. For
Scripture certainly does not teach us every good path in this sense, nor does
it transmit the rules of arts and sciences. Hooker is dozing when he raises
such an objection, since our thesis concerns especially actions of a fourth
kind, which belong to a person as a moral agent and a subject of Christ's
kingdom, in relation to the final end. Such actions include worshiping God in
this or that way, in these Aaronic garments or other symbolic ones; all ritual
actions in the sacraments; hearing the Word; helping the poor; and honoring
the king. The common principle of these actions is conscience, which must be
informed and actuated by the theological habits of faith, hope, and charity.
These are the subject of our thesis, which Hooker has understood very badly.
Conscience also has some influence upon actions of the arts and sciences, not
indeed as they are such and flow from intellectual habits in a person, but as
they are—so to speak—moralized or, if the grammarians will pardon the word,
theologized, and reduced by conscience to moral being. Thus actions of the
arts and sciences,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 111 | PDF p. 162]

not indeed according to their artistic and scientific being, but according to
their moral, spiritual, and theological being, are regulated by God's Word.
For example, in order to speak Latin and speak elegantly, I do not consult the
Scriptures but Despauterius's
grammar; in order to construct mathematical demonstrations, I do not consult
the apostle Paul but Euclid. But in order to direct the arts and sciences to
God's glory rather than to shameful profit or empty glory; not to lie while I
speak Latin; and not to defend any false conclusions against the light of
conscience while I make mathematical demonstrations—in these respects the
actions have been moralized and must be regulated by God's Word.

Objection 2. Wisdom does not teach us every good path through Scripture. It
teaches one good way through Scripture, another through spiritual influence,
another through experience, and another through sense, as it taught Thomas
Christ's resurrection and taught the Jews through miracles.

Response. This does not oppose us, because God teaches every moral good
through the Scriptures. We do not say that he teaches people through the
Scriptures alone and through no other means. He teaches fools by blows, but it
does not follow from this that the Scriptures are insufficient to teach us
every moral good. And in alleging the example of the apostle Thomas he
stumbles miserably, as he does with the Jews who were instructed by miracles.
Were the Scriptures not in themselves

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 112 | PDF p. 163]

sufficient both to instruct Thomas about Christ's resurrection and to teach
the Jews apart from all miracles? As though a greater number of means removed the
sufficiency of Scripture! Neither Thomas's sense nor the miracles, but God's
Word alone, was the formal means of faith for both Thomas and the Jews.
Second, let him tell us by what means God instructs us about the surplice, the
sign of the cross, and feast days. Not by Scripture, as is clear from Hooker's
accusation that Scripture is imperfect and his consequent flight to other
means. But are ceremonies shown to be lawful religious rites by sense, sight,
or hearing? Do they instead move the spectators to laughter? Are they shown
by experience? Then again, by sense. By spiritual influence? Then the Holy
Spirit teaches ceremonies. In what school? By the arts and sciences? Nothing
of the kind. Certainly we have been taught ceremonies by the bishops'
superstitious will alone.

Objection 3. Hooker, in the same place: When many dishes are set before me on
the table, it is indifferent to me which I eat. I choose these, not because
Scripture says they are better than the others—for then they would not all be
indifferent to me—but because my discretion is pleased to do so.

Response 1. Eating is a mixed action in a human being. It is partly natural,
and, in so far as a person is a natural agent, he is led by appetite; our
thesis does not concern such acts. But it is also in part an ethical and moral

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 113 | PDF p. 164]

action; and in this respect, eating foods that harm one's health or contribute
to the formation of stones is contrary to the sixth commandment of the Decalogue.
Second, even if the Word provided no light here to those of us who are
unskilled in wholesome foods, it does not follow that in sacred ceremonies
and the worship of God, which is a purely moral action, God's Word leaves us
in darkness.

Objection 4. Parents and masters would command sins to their children and
servants, and the latter would not be bound to obey unless they had a reason
from the Scriptures. Yet masters, under a seal of silence, as Robert Sanderson
says in his English sermon on Romans 14:23, often have to give servants
commands whose equity it is not their place to investigate.

Response 1. Brothers, are you angry with Paul, who commands servants and
children to obey their superiors only in the Lord? Ephesians 6:1; Colossians
3:20, 22–24. Second, when parents and masters command artistic or domestic
actions or works of the sciences, the matter lies outside the question at
issue. When they command moral actions in which God is offended or obeyed,
people must know, even in the most secret matters, as much as concerns their
consciences in practice.

Objection 5. Sanderson, who begs everything in this matter from Hooker, argues
as follows. Many affairs arise daily in our callings concerning which it would
cause even the most practiced theologians difficulty to find testimony in the
Scriptures;

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 114 | PDF p. 165]

these would obstruct the works of our calling and drive consciences to
despair.

Response 1. This vexation torments few consciences, for human ignorance—by
which people cannot see the grounds of lawfulness and expediency in their
actions—ought not to prejudice the fullness of the Scriptures. Second, those
who acknowledge God in all their ways and, in the fear of the Lord, consult
the sacred oracles in the smallest affairs will have an abundant supply of
light in all things.

It is certain that Scripture is as exact a rule of justice and mercy toward
our neighbor as it is of religion toward God. First, although in civil matters
there appears to be greater obscurity in such things as all positive human and
political laws, the human will is no more a rule of justice and equity than it
is of religion. Otherwise what is lawful, equitable, and just in political
laws, contracts, and military stratagems would depend upon human authority,
which is absurd. Second, Scripture is the perfect norm of religion because
the will of God the lawgiver alone, not the will of human beings, is what
formally constitutes something lawful and pious in religion. But this reason
also holds in acts of the second table, because God's will, not Abraham's,
makes the act of killing one's child a just act. Third, self-willed action
(ἐθελόπραξις) is just as unlawful as self-made religion (ἐθελοθρήσκεια).

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 115 | PDF p. 166 | stop before Obj. 8]

Objection 6. Seth, Enoch, Noah, and Abraham, who lived before the written Word
and had revelations in place of the Word, could neither laugh, eat, drink,
walk, nor speak except by revelations made from heaven, if people may dare to
perform no action without a reason from God's Word. For what the written Word
is to us, revelation was to them.

Response. The light of natural reason, which is contained in the Word as a
part in the whole, made their natural actions lawful.

Objection 7. If all particular actions must be conformed to the written Word,
what will become of the peoples of the Indies? They have no written Word. Has
God left them without a law, without a rule for living? But where there is no
law, there is no transgression. Yet if it must necessarily be said that they
sin, then a law has been given to them. But this is the law of nature, not the
written Word.

Response. Who could endure here a neighing horse or a theologian disputing?
For the law of nature stands in place of the written Word for them; indeed, it
is a part and principle of the written Word. Nor do we wish the written Word
to be the rule of all human actions because it is written, but because it is
just. The written Word does not bind the peoples of the Indies, since it has
not been revealed to them.
