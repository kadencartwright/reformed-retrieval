ROMANS 7:18.

“For the will is present with me, but I do not attain to the doing of what is
good. Verse 23: But I see another law in my members, waging war against the law
of my mind and making me captive to the law of sin which is in my members.”

Hence it is evident that Paul asserts about himself that in his most excellent
actions there is a corrupt principle striving in the opposite direction and
retarding the fervor of the action—namely, indwelling concupiscence. Hence

it is asked: Is the conflict between spirit and flesh perpetual in all the
regenerate whenever they perform good works; or is this battle absent in some,
as in those who are perfectly and consummately regenerate? The Remonstrants in
*Confession*, chapter 11, section 6, say that some are perfectly regenerate,
who devote themselves to holiness with pleasure, joy, and a certain delight,
and practice righteousness and truth with their whole heart, their whole soul,
and all their strength. They do not say that these people never sin, but that
they have cast off vicious habits; and if at any time they sin, they stumble
through error, inadvertence, and a

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 525 | PDF p. 576 | split word joined: obnubilationem]

darkening of the mind, but not from indwelling sin. And in *Apology*, chapter
11, folio 128, they deny that the conflict of flesh and spirit is necessarily
and always present in such people, because they have the habit of grace,
through which they readily and willingly choose and perform actions that were
formerly troublesome and burdensome.

We teach that through the infused habit of grace in a person's regeneration,
the acquired habit of sinning is removed, but the natural habit is subdued and
tamed. Titus 3:3; Ezekiel 36:26–27; Hosea 14:8.

But it is false that the habitual propensity to sin—which retards and lessens
the supernatural fervor of actions and inclines the agent in the opposite
direction—is removed. 1. For in the regenerate Paul there is another law
warring against the law of the mind and leading him captive to the law of sin.
Romans 7:23. Galatians 5:17: “The flesh desires against the Spirit, and the
Spirit against the flesh; but these are opposed to one another, so that you do
not accomplish whatever things you wish.” First Peter 2:11: “Abstain from
fleshly desires which wage war against the soul.”

But that this conflict on the part of the flesh is culpable is evident:

1. Because it is prohibited by the Spirit of God as sin. Galatians 5:16: “Do
not carry out what the flesh desires.” Therefore, what the flesh desires is
sin.

2. It is opposed to the Spirit, who inclines toward the good and toward what is
consonant with God's Law.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 526 | PDF p. 577 | split word joined: Exclamat]

3. Paul cries out that because of this conflict he is captive, enslaved and
sold to sin, and wretched; and he seeks deliverance in Christ. Romans
7:14, 13, 24, 25.

2. It is sin in the seed, which is the principle of all moral actions, not a
darkening of the mind. For First John 1:8 says, “If we say that we do not have
sin,” and therefore that we are not affected by an inward habit of sin, “we
deceive ourselves.” Therefore, there is in everyone an inward habit of sinning
from which they are denominated sinners. Proverbs 20:9: “Who can say, ‘I have
purified my soul’?” Therefore, in every person there is a soul that has not
been purified. But this cannot fail to be a propensity striving against and
resisting the propensity of grace and of the Spirit in a regenerate person.
Therefore, habitual conflict, and conflict in first act—as fire is
contrary to water—is always present in every regenerate person; consequently,
no one is free from every natural habit of sinning.

3. James 1:14: “Each person is tempted when he is drawn away and enticed by
his own desire.” Therefore, everyone who sins has inwardly enticing desire;
and consequently he does not sin from a darkening of the mind alone.

4. If some are so perfectly regenerate that they are free from every habit,
that is, concupiscence, and from every acquired habit of sinning, then there
are some who can say: “We know that good dwells in us; we do not have sin; we
have

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 527 | PDF p. 578 | split word joined: purificavimus]

purified our souls.” Nor is it necessary that they say, “Forgive us our sins.”
But this is utterly sacrilegious toward divine grace.

5. Likewise, there would then be some for whom it is not necessary to pray:
“Increase grace in us, Lord; remove the heart of stone; subdue the desires of
the flesh.” For such people have entirely cast off every habit of sinning, both
innate and acquired. And they are in the state in which the good angels and
Adam were in the state of innocence, in whom there is no habit of sinning, no
concupiscence, and no propensity to sin.

6. Indeed, such people could say: “We no longer need Christ as Savior or as
Advocate and Intercessor for us with the Father, any more than the good angels
and Adam before he fell; for we have now cast off every habit and propensity of
sinning.”

7. It is not necessary for such people to crucify the old man and carnal
affections or to subdue the desires which wage war against the soul.

ROMANS 7:14.

“We know that the Law is spiritual, but I am carnal and sold under sin.”

It is asked: Does the Apostle speak here of an unregenerate person existing
under the Law, or of a regenerate person under grace? In his exposition of
chapter 7 of the Epistle to the Romans, Arminius, in order to overturn this
conflict between spirit and flesh in the regenerate, says that Paul speaks in
the person of an unregenerate man groaning under the Law. So the Pelagians
formerly taught, as Augustine says. So also Erasmus, Castellio, and

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 528 | PDF p. 579 | split word joined: Socinus]

Socinus. We, on the contrary, say that he speaks of a truly regenerate person.

1. He continuously carries on the discourse about himself: “I am carnal; I
acknowledge; I will; I do not will; not I, but the flesh in me; I give thanks
to God.”

2. It is false and contrary to God's Word that an unregenerate person can be
said to have died to the Law and to have been married to another, namely,
Christ. Verses 3–4.

3. In verse 5 Paul expressly distinguishes between himself, about whom he is
speaking, and an unregenerate person—or between himself while he was
unregenerate and himself now groaning under this conflict. “For when we were in
the flesh, the passions of sins which arose through the Law were active in our
members to bear fruit unto death.” Verse 6: “But now we have been delivered,”
and so forth. Behold! There is an evident change of state and person; and he
expressly says that he has been delivered from the Law and continues the
discourse about himself as delivered from the Law.

3. To consent to the Law that it is good, and to delight in it according to
the inner man, is proper to the regenerate alone. Psalm 1:2: “The delight of
the blessed man is in the Law of the Lord.” But what blessedness belongs to a
person under the Law, and thus accursed and alien to Christ, in whom all the
nations of the earth are blessed?

4. Only the regenerate, not the unregenerate, have the inner man. Romans 2:29;
Second Corinthians 4:16; First Peter 3:4.

5. It cannot be said of an unregenerate person, as a slave of sin, that he
wills the good, does not will evil, and hates sin. But in the regenerate God
works a new heart. Ezekiel 36:26. He works willing and accomplishing, for his

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 529 | PDF p. 580 | split word joined: gratuitâ]

gracious goodwill, Philippians 2:13. Indeed, all the affections are renewed in
him, as can be seen throughout Psalm 119: delight, joy, fear, hope, confidence,
and so forth. But in the unregenerate the mind is darkened, Ephesians 4:18, and
the conscience is polluted, Titus 1:15. Indeed, the will, affections, senses,
eyes, hands, ears, tongue, throat, feet, and everything that is in them are
polluted. Romans 2:10–20.

6. The unregenerate person does not serve God's Law with the mind; he cannot
say what Paul says about himself here in verse 17: “Not I, but the sin in me,”
because he in his entire extent is sin and enslaved to sin. But it is wrongly
denied that an unregenerate person under the Law who sins against the light of
conscience sins in his entirety, on the ground that by natural light he “sees
and approves the better things, and follows the worse.” For if this reasoning
were valid, the sin against the Holy Spirit could be excused in this way—where
the person sinning has been enlightened by supernatural illumination of by far
the greatest degree that a slave of the Devil can receive: “Not I with respect
to my enlightened mind pour out blasphemies against God, but the sin which
dwells in me.” And thus all the sins of the unregenerate in which enlightened
conscience strives against the lower appetite for sin would be sins of
infirmity, but not reigning sins; and they would be excusable under the title
by which Paul in this chapter asserts that he and all the regenerate sin less
grievously.

7. To the unregenerate person sin is a delight, not a

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 530 | PDF p. 581]

burden. But here he cries out that he is being taken captive—that is, drawn as
if unwilling to sin—that he is wretched and miserable in this body of sin,
that he wishes to be delivered, and that he gives thanks in Christ for
deliverance. But these things do not fit an unregenerate person.

MATTHEW 3:10.

“Every tree which does not bear good fruit will be cut down and cast into the
fire.”

Hence the necessity of good works, if we wish to be saved, is established. But

it is asked: Are good works necessary as causes of justification, and thus
also of salvation? The Arminians say yes. We say no. But for a clearer
determination of the question, these three things must be distinguished: 1.
the right to eternal life; 2. the particular application of that right to
determinate and certain persons; and 3. the actual possession of eternal life.

2. A cause *without which not*—or a cause which Calvin calls inferior—must be
distinguished from a superior cause.

We therefore set forth our position in these assertions.

Assertion 1. The right to righteousness and eternal life is owed solely to
Christ's merits, not to our works. 1. For Christ's blood is the ransom paid for
us, not our works or our sufferings. Romans 3:24; Acts 4:12; Ephesians 1:7;
First Peter 1:18–19; Revelation 1:5. 2. For no meritorious force inheres in our
works. Romans 8:18; Second Corinthians 4:17.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 531 | PDF p. 582 | split word joined: Assertio]

Assertion 2. Nor are our good works prior dispositions and causes qualifying
us for justification under a promise of God. 1. For justification is not from
works, but from mere grace, as was proved elsewhere. 2. For there is no such
promise in Scripture: “The person who has performed such good works will be
justified before God's Tribunal.”

Assertion 3. The cause which applies the right, or the meritorious title, to
righteousness and eternal life is faith alone, because faith alone apprehends
Christ's righteousness and merits. But Christ is our righteousness, and
eternal life has been obtained by his merits and blood alone.

Assertion 4. Nothing prevents us from calling good works an inferior cause of
the actual possession of eternal life. Thus our Calvin says in *Institutes*,
book 4, chapter 14, section 21: “Nothing in these things prevents the Lord from
embracing works as inferior causes. But whence is this? Namely, those whom by
his mercy he has destined to the inheritance of eternal life, he leads into
its possession through good works in his ordinary dispensation. He calls what
precedes in the order of dispensation a cause of what follows. Finally, by
these expressions a sequence is indicated more than a cause, because God, by
heaping grace upon grace, takes from the former graces a cause for adding later
graces.” Hence Bernard's saying, “Good works are the way to the kingdom, not
the cause of reigning,” where he excludes them only as principal causes.
Therefore Bucer in the Regensburg Colloquy of 1547,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 532 | PDF p. 583]

chapter 6, calls them “causes in some manner of the eternal reward.” Zanchi in
*On the Nature of God*, book 2, chapter 5, and in part 2 of *On the
Predestination of the Saints*, question 3, says: “They are an instrumental
cause rather than an efficient cause, and the cause which they call *without
which not*.”

Assertion 5. Good works are understood to have causal force for eternal life
in three ways. 1. As deserving it by condign merit. Against the Papists, we
reject this as blasphemous, as we have proved elsewhere. 2. As having an
inferior force and an instrumental causality conferred upon them from God's
grace. Thus Gisbertus Voetius says in *Thersites Heautontemerumenos*, section
1, chapter 2, that running is a cause of taking hold of the crown; the contest,
of victory; and a regimen, of health. Nor, speaking precisely, is it proper
here to distinguish between a means and a cause or between a way and a cause.
For since good works are means, not passive but active, a means here is an
inferior cause. Hence “our light afflictions produce for us an eternal weight
of surpassingly excellent glory”; the Holy Spirit says that they work or cause
it for us, Corinthians 4:17. Nor can we distinguish here between causes
and signs, because mere signs have no causality. Dawn is in no manner a cause
of day, nor is smoke a cause, even an inferior one, of fire. But our course
through good works is actively related to the actual possession of eternal
life. Yet this causality is 1. not principal; 2. not meritorious; and 3. not
from the works themselves,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 533 | PDF p. 584]

but from God's grace. Therefore, they are made causes rather than innate ones;
they are causes not by their own force, but from God's dispensation. Hence it
follows that in this question there is no need for 1. the distinction between
presence and causality, or presence and efficiency; 2. the distinction between
cause and sign; 3. that between means or way and cause; or 4. that between a
passive and an active means—unless they are explained carefully.

3. Good works are understood to have a principal, ruling, and predeterminative
causal force for eternal life which flows from our free choice, and not from
God's predetermining grace, as the Arminians dream. And in this manner they
wish good works to be causes of eternal life, which we judge blasphemous.

1. For praise and the glory of the prize and victory would be owed to the
runner and contestant who did not receive strength from the Lord of the games,
not to the Lord, who was a mere spectator, if it belongs absolutely to free
choice to determine itself and there is no determination of the free will by
God and his grace. Thus good works will be principal causes of eternal life,
not God's grace.

2. The glory of good works and eternal life would have to be ascribed not to
God, not to the Lamb slaughtered for our sins, and not to divine grace—because
when all these are posited, the free will can determine itself to good works or
not determine itself—but the glory would have to be assigned to free choice
alone.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 534 | PDF p. 585]

3. Conversion, perseverance, and eternal life, rather than non-conversion,
apostasy, and eternal damnation, will belong absolutely to us, not to God and
his grace.

4. For Scripture everywhere denies that we are saved from works, but affirms
that this occurs from God's grace and mercy. Ephesians 2:8: “By grace you have
been saved through faith—and this is not from you; it is God's gift.” Verse 9:
“Not from works, lest anyone boast.” Second Timothy 1:9: “He saved us and
called us with a holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his
own will and the grace which was given to us in Jesus Christ.” Titus 3:5: “Not
from righteous works which we had done, but according to his mercy he saved us
through the washing of regeneration and the renewal of the Holy Spirit.”
Romans 9:23: “The wages of sin is death; but God's gift—not wages—is eternal
life in Christ Jesus our Lord.”

5. For the Covenant of Works, or the Law, and the Covenant of Grace, or the
Gospel, would be confused if salvation were from our works. For the cause of
eternal salvation is the same as the cause of our justification before God's
Tribunal. Indeed, neither do we maintain that legally perfect good works are
necessarily required for the possession of eternal life; it is enough that
the works are evangelically good and pleasing and acceptable to God through
Christ the Mediator.
