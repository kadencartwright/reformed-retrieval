VERSE 2. A man to whom God himself has given riches, resources, and honor, and
whose soul lacks nothing in which it might delight, yet God himself does not
grant this man the ability to enjoy that thing, but a stranger enjoys it: this
is vanity and the worst affliction.

Above Solomon presented to us a man who has riches. Here he presents one who
is laden not only with riches but also with resources of every kind and with
honor—in short, with all the good things that someone might desire not merely
for necessity but also for enjoyment. Yet God has not granted him the power to
use them. It is just as if a starving or thirsty man were surrounded and
enveloped on every side by food and drink, yet had his mouth so wrapped and
stopped up that he could neither touch nor taste them. His misery is more
deplorable than that of a man who suffers hunger and thirst and lacks
everything by which he might allay or break them.

Nor is anything taken away from this evil because another's force does not
prevent him from doing this, but he brings it upon himself voluntarily by his
own avarice and stinginess. For although his insatiable desire to possess makes him
abstain from using those things, the natural appetite to use them for
necessity and enjoyment nevertheless remains. While his health is whole and
unshaken, this appetite cannot be blunted—much less extinguished—by his immense
desire to possess. Besides this, it is a graver evil to bring ruin upon
oneself than to have it inflicted from elsewhere, and it brands him with a
more shameful mark of disgrace. Nor does it make much difference whether he
perishes from hunger or thirst all at once through total abstinence, or is
gradually and step by step destroyed by a restricted participation in these
things, taking them each day more sparingly and harshly than is fitting. For
just as those roasted over a low and slow fire are tortured more grievously
than those who are suddenly

consumed in a vast conflagration, so also those who languish for a long time
in want of the things needed to preserve life and are finally extinguished
are pressed and stung more grievously than those who perish by abstaining from
all food and drink for seven days.

It must be observed that the wicked and those hated by God obtain all things
according to their wish and according to their soul's desire and appetite. In
Psalm 17:14 God is said to fill their bellies with his hidden treasure. What
is more, in verse 7 of Psalm 73 the Prophet affirms that they abound in
prosperity beyond what they could have imagined. This fact so sorely tormented
David that he was almost brought to apostasy from God. Against this temptation
Scripture arms us here as elsewhere. First, they have their portion in this
life, so there is no reason for us to begrudge them this momentary use of good
things. Next, Psalm 37 says that the little a righteous person has is better
than the great wealth of many wicked people. So also in this passage Solomon
says that all these things are stored up for the ruin of their owners. For
what is said here about the grasping and avaricious applies universally,
though in different ways, to all the wicked: their money and resources become
their ruin.

We too should take reassurance from this. For if God supplies all things so
abundantly to the wicked, much more has he furnished what is sufficient to
his own. See 1 Timothy 4:10. Moreover, just as God exceeds the hope and thought
of the wicked
in prosperous things, so he grants this to the faithful in those things that
pertain to eternal life. Ephesians 3:20.

Yet it may be asked how the statement that God supplies such a person with
whatever he desires can agree with the insatiable desire for possessions from
which misers suffer, since he is satisfied by neither honors nor riches,
however great their multitude and magnitude. As far as riches are concerned,
love for them grows as the riches themselves grow. In honors they likewise
set no limit. The merchant aspires to nobility; gentlemen wish to be made
knights; knights, barons; barons, counts; counts, dukes; and these in turn wish
to be created princes. I reply that by these words the Prophet declares not
what a miser, who sets no limit to his desire, is accustomed to seek, but
those things that can amply suffice for all the honorable uses of this life.

It is further asked how honor is said to fall to the miser, when it was said
earlier that nothing is more contemptible and despicable than he. I reply that
misers are often honored with offices of command, and that such is the
flattery of many people that they honor with words and countenance those whom
they despise in their minds.

Next it must be noted that God is the one who grants riches to the miser,
though the miser often gathers them not only greedily and thirstily but also
by fraud. For he obtains them by no labor or deceit unless God gives him the
means to acquire wealth. It does not follow from this that God becomes the
author of injustice. For the person defrauded by the miser either deserves to
be defrauded because of his own foolishness or injustice, or, if neither is
the case, God has determined to test his patience in this way. Whatever the
case may be, God shows himself just in all things.

Moreover, God grants riches and so forth to some people without giving them
the ability and power to use them. If someone should object that this is a
gift that is no gift, indeed that it is instead a plague and fatal to its
recipient, I acknowledge it. But this is through no fault either of the gift
itself or of the Giver; the fault belongs only to the person who receives the
gift, to whom God is in no way obligated also to give the ability to use it.
This should teach us how earnestly we must pray to God that he not confer
riches or honors upon us unless he at the same time gives us the power to use
them rightly. Just so, God must be entreated that when he grants riches, he
would graciously grant their right

use as well. From the contrary evil it is also evident how great and excellent
a good it is when God, in granting riches, at the same time grants their
lawful use. This should also train us to patience in poverty if we remember
that poverty is sent upon us because God sees that we are unfit to use riches.

An attendant circumstance of the wealth the miser has amassed is now set
forth: a stranger—that is, someone who never labored to accumulate it—will eat
and consume it. In Psalm 17 the Prophet teaches that the bellies of the wicked
are stuffed with God's hidden treasures; what they leave over they transfer
to their sons, and the sons in turn transfer it to their little ones, so that
the inheritance of the goods possessed by the wicked reaches their
grandchildren and the third generation. Here, however, their wealth is
assigned like plunder to a stranger after their death.

I reply that those threats which concern the troubles of this age are carried
out only so far as divine wisdom considers expedient, and God remains true to
his word if they are fulfilled for the most part. Furthermore, this threat is
not spoken universally of all the wicked, but of misers, to whom a punishment
of this kind is most justly due: since riches ought to be applied only to the
use of the present life, they are given to others to use when those who
possess them derive neither use nor fruit from them. 1 Timothy 5:6.
