CHAPTER X.

Verse 1. The Proverbs of Schelomon.

A wise son makes his father glad, but a foolish son is sorrow to his mother.

Thus far there has been a preface to the whole book. Now follow the proverbs. In interpreting them, this is to be noted as a general rule: when the parts of a proverb comprehended in a compound maxim are opposed to one another—as often happens—one part serves as the reason and proof of the other.

Verse 1. Wise children, or children imbued with true piety, bring solid joy to both parents; and so the father’s heart is said to be made glad. By contrast, foolish children who are strangers to the fear of God consume both father and mother with grief. Compare Proverbs 23:15.

From this, therefore, two lessons are drawn concerning the duty of
{ Parents.
{ Children.

1. In the procreation and education of children, parents set before themselves some comfort in life. If that hope fails, they must necessarily be saddened and distressed—especially when they are not merely aided by no comfort, but are further burdened with injuries and troubles. To obtain this comfort and avoid its opposite, they must take pains not to satisfy themselves merely by having provided their children with the advantages of this life, unless they further add both teaching and formation, Ephesians 6:4. Indeed, they must also add paternal correction

[1632 witness | PDF p. 102 | printed columns 178–179]

that is moderate, Proverbs 13:24; 23:13–14, and timely, Proverbs 19:18. Its fruit toward parents is described in the same terms as here at Proverbs 29:17. Examples exist in Eli and David. Finally, parents must present themselves to their children as an example of a godly life. This may also be applied to spiritual parents: “I have no greater joy than this, that I may see my children walking in truth,” 3 John 1, &c.

The other lesson concerns the duty of children: they should be willing to render ἀμοιβὰς, recompenses, to their parents and to ἀντιπελαργεῖν, repay their parents’ care, something implanted in everyone by nature. Let them therefore pursue true piety, by which they may revive and refresh parents afflicted by old age or otherwise. An example of this is found in Joseph, of whom his father said: “Now I shall die this time, since I have seen your face,” Genesis 46:30.

Verse 2. Treasures of wickedness do not profit, but justice delivers from death.

Treasures gathered or preserved through wickedness profit nothing. But justice—at whose expense and loss treasures of iniquity are scraped together and turned to account—profits so greatly that it delivers its possessor and owner from death itself, both present and future, much less from other troubles.

By this proverb Solomon condemns human folly in two ways. First, people place such weight and importance upon riches that they hope they can accomplish anything with them, however those riches were acquired. Second, they assign little power to integrity of life, in which there is the greatest importance. That this opinion adheres deeply to human minds is demonstrated by the parable in Luke 12:15–16. And the commonplace saying is worn smooth: money can accomplish everything.

And queen Money bestows birth and beauty.

They boast that they carry the law about in their purse: health can be preserved by money; lost health can be recovered; money opens the approach to honors; gifts construct a road to conferences with kings. In short, what can it not do? The Papists even maintain that heaven can be bought with money.

Here, therefore, we must consider how fragile riches are by their own nature: moth and rust devour them. Consider how inconstant they are: growing wings, they fly away from their rightful possessors, and they are stolen by thieves. All this is summed up by that exceedingly apt saying in Ezekiel 7:19. It is illustrated by the likeness of someone who eats more than is right and vomits it all up, Job 20:15. To this is added that general statement concerning all works of darkness: they are unprofitable, Ephesians 5:11.

The second fault is that people attribute little to justice, as though it had no power either to improve or to prolong this life. Therefore, in their pursuit of riches, they allow justice to lie uncultivated. Hence those complaints of the ungodly in Malachi and the other prophets: that it is vain to fear God and walk humbly with him. But these complaints are refuted here, and also by the frequent promises in the law which promise long life to God’s worshipers. In Luke 12 it is called a purse which does not grow old, and a treasure which does not fail and can neither be eaten by moths nor stolen by thieves. But by far the most excellent statement confirming this matter is 1 Timothy 4:8. Hence Solomon, Proverbs 15:16–17 and Proverbs 16:8; Psalm 37:16. Here see how the things said about the persecution of the just agree. Relate to this the fact that the ungodly man is put in the just man’s place, so that he may pay the penalties prepared for the just, Proverbs 11:8. Indeed, the godly man will deliver even the guilty and offending person, not only himself, Job 22:30; Genesis 18.

Verse 3. Jehovah does not allow the soul of the just to hunger, but he drives away the substance of the wicked.

In

[1632 witness | PDF p. 103 | printed columns 180–181]

נפש (“soul”) there is a synecdoche of a part for the whole: the soul, namely, stands for the whole person. This proverb appears to be an αἰτιολογία, a statement of the cause, of the preceding one—yet in such a way that what is placed second confirms what came first, and what was placed first confirms what came second. In both places he wishes us to lift our eyes to God and his hand. When people do not do this, they fall into those two errors noted above: by attributing somewhat too much to riches, they yield almost nothing to justice.

And since in the latter half-verse he had said that justice delivers from death—because no question arises there about eternal life, since they do not doubt that the just will possess the kingdom of God—he confirms that part of the proposition which concerns the present life. What is said in Psalm 37:25–26 fits this very well; likewise, lions are said to hunger, while those who fear God are nevertheless provided for, Psalm 34:11. See tokens of this in Elijah fed by ravens, in the widow of Zarephath, 1 Kings 17, in the Shunammite woman, 2 Kings 4, and in the Israelites living in the wilderness. As for the posterity of the just, there is a striking example in Ishmael, who was both rejected and begotten by an unlawful union, yet for whom God provided in his extremity of thirst, Genesis 16. Here the question is asked whether the just man may die of hunger; concerning this, see above.

This proverb overthrows that boundless desire to possess and pursuit of gathering riches unjustly. For what purpose are people kindled by this flame of growing rich? Surely everything is directed toward sustaining this miserable life. The shortest route to attaining this purpose is to cultivate justice and piety. And, as the Apostle says, having food and clothing, we ought to be content with them, 1 Timothy 6. God does not promise delicacies and sumptuous provisions to those who love him; he promises only that they will not perish from hunger. Thus those who necessarily live sparingly or harshly have no reason to think themselves despised by God.

For the Lord’s driving away the substance of the wicked, see Haggai. It also frequently happens that badly acquired riches slip away when no causes for their dissipation are apparent—namely, when their owners are sufficiently thrifty and attentive to business and are known to be neither bankrupt nor prodigal. And even if the riches remain, their possessors are carried off by the grasping hands of death, Luke 12. The rich, therefore, must be warned not to trust in uncertain riches or set their heart upon them, Psalm 62.

Verse 4. He becomes poor who deals with a deceitful hand, but the hand of the diligent enriches them.

“Hand” is a synecdoche of a part for the whole. The sense is that someone seeking to grow rich by wicked practices and deceit not only fails to obtain what he desires, but further wears down and diminishes the wealth left to him, even if he does not exhaust himself through thoughtlessness or extravagant generosity. For the very deceit which he expects will be profitable strips him of wealth. But for someone to increase in wealth, it is required not only that he abstain from fraud and wicked practices, but also that he employ diligence and industry in conducting business.

The first judgment of the flesh is condemned in the first half-verse. For how few are found who do not place great protection for this life in lies and frauds, both in words and throughout the rest of life? It goes so far that almost everyone decides within himself—and not a few openly profess—that without vanity of words, abuse of the tongue, and other refuges of falsehood, they cannot endure this life. Hence the seller desires to dispose of his goods for the highest price he can, while the buyer desires to purchase the same goods for the lowest price he can. Hence the corruption and adulteration of merchandise, and unjust weights. Hence

[1632 witness | PDF p. 104 | printed columns 182–183]

solemn attestations to false matters, and even frequent oaths. This is the point of the common proverb, “There is craft in dawbing.” But through Solomon the Spirit of God openly declares, by contrast, that fraud not only fails to advance people to treasuries of wealth, Proverbs 16:8, but draws them very far back. This is also confirmed by Psalm 37:16, and is adorned with a striking likeness in Jeremiah 17. See an example of this in Laban, who profited nothing by his frauds, but whose affairs always grew worse. Set here the examples of moneylenders, innkeepers, and so forth. Consider the opposite proposition, which runs as follows: an honest hand will enrich its owner. Although this is true, yet because it was already readily evident to anyone from the preceding words, he draws another inference which lies further from human understanding. He teaches that, to acquire riches, it is not enough for someone to have injured no one, unless he is also active and ready in conducting business. Although the diligent hand gathers more slowly, it nevertheless at last enriches a man otherwise exhausted, Proverbs 13:14. Everyone favors the diligent and alert, verse 22:29; see the example of Ruth. Yet diligence does not have such power that someone can make his fortune without God’s blessing, Psalm 127. He seems further to note that fraud commonly creeps into commerce because people who are sluggish in their calling seek to make good by fraud and wicked practices the loss contracted through idleness. Hence the proverb common among us: “He is a true fore-labouring man.”

Verse 5. An understanding son gathers in summer; a son who causes shame is sunk in sleep at harvest.

קיץ (“summer”) and קציר (“harvest”) are a synecdoche for every occasion or opportunity. נרדם (“sunk in sleep”) is a metaphor—or rather a synecdoche—indicating everyone who is slack and dissolute in business. The sense is this: the one who gathers the advantages of this life when it is timely and the occasion offers itself is a prudent son who honors himself and his family. But the one who allows the opportunity and occasion to flow past and slip away from him is a foolish son, overwhelming and crushing himself and his family with shame.

Summer is a suitable time for gathering things, for they are conveyed with less labor and less expense. Harvest is suitable for this reason: advantages are then available either to the owner in gathering his crops, to hired workers who hire out their labor for money, or, finally, to those who glean; see Ruth. Thus the merchant observes markets and fairs, and lawyers observe the court recess. Let this truly be said of childhood, adolescence, and youth, which are seasons suitable as summer for gathering the skills by which we sustain life. So it is harvest time when teachers are available and expenses are supplied.

This diligence and seizing of opportunity can most suitably be transferred to the search for heavenly things. For a diligent ear enriches us with spiritual gifts; and while Christ’s schools stand open, and teachers or ministers teach freely and set forth doctrine, there is a kind of harvest of Gospel fruits. Therefore let us imitate Joseph’s prudent household management: in fruitful years he stored up much by which he might endure the scarcity of the following years. See Galatians 6: “Work what is good while there is time.” “Seek him while he may be found; call upon him while he is near,” Isaiah 59. “Walk while you have the light, lest the darkness overtake you.”

Verse 6. Blessings are upon the head of the just, but violence covers the mouth of the wicked.

Blessings—that is, benefits, not one kind or another but of every kind—

[1632 witness | PDF p. 105 | printed columns 184–185]

are upon the head of the just, Genesis 49:25–26; that is, upon the just man. Hence his mouth will be enlarged with glorying in God and with boasting toward men. But violence inflicted from elsewhere will cover—that is, will crowdedly and numerously invade—the mouth of the wicked, Deuteronomy 28; that is, the wicked themselves, who are both talkative and abusive not only toward men, but against heaven.

In the first part, note first that the just man is most blessed, contrary to the common estimation; see Psalm 1. Next, blessings are said to be upon his head. Although this is a synecdoche of a part for the whole, the head is mentioned to indicate, first, that these blessings are from heaven, and, second, that they are conferred openly and in a conspicuous manner, Genesis 49:26. This is clear from the opposites, Psalm 7:16. What is sent down from God out of heaven admonishes us to trust in God even if other supports fail us, Genesis 15:1. At the same time, it reminds us that benefits received from anyone must be referred to God as the one fountain of good things. See an example of this in Joseph. Although he was first assailed and oppressed by his brothers, and afterwards by his master, through false accusations, he nevertheless trusted in God and placed danger after obedience to him; from God he received deliverance from evil and power over the authors of that evil.

That God confers his benefits in a conspicuous manner is confirmed by Psalm 37:6, whence the wicked man is also said to gnash his teeth against the just. Although the supreme fulfillment of this matter is reserved for Christ’s second advent—observe this also in judgments exercised against the wicked—yet even in this life the godly have τεκμήρια, tokens, of divine love that are conspicuous even to the ungodly. So it was with the Israelites long ago in their journey to the land of Canaan; by contrast, as with the Canaanites of old, the heart of the ungodly melts and their knees knock against one another for fear, both from the inward sense and from the outward signs of divine vengeance.

Indeed, violence—or curses inflicted by God upon the unwilling wicked—will rush upon them in crowds and so stupefy them that, deceived by a vain hope of prosperity and left as though confounded and oppressed with shame, they will have nothing to answer those who reproach them; see the penultimate verse of Psalm 107. See Proverbs 26:2. Indeed, the curses of the ungodly will be turned into blessings for the godly, 2 Samuel 16:12. Although the wicked invoke every evil upon the godly, the godly are nevertheless blessed by God.

The cause of this silence is demonstrated: namely, pain and shame. For this silence of the ungodly is opposed to the joy and triumph of the godly. But according to their petition in Psalm 119:42, the just have something with which always to answer their adversaries. It must be noted that the wicked are not brought to silence and stupor by any of their sins, although greatly multiplied and overflowing in magnitude; they are struck at last only when they are pressed by God’s rods and judgments. By contrast, it commonly happens among the godly that they dread nothing more than offending God.

Verse 7. The memory of the just is blessed, but the name of the wicked is rotten.

Blessings follow the just man so abundantly and lavishly that he is not even mentioned except with praise and blessing. By contrast, the very name of the wicked is not uttered among us without detestation and execration of them.

The first part is confirmed by Psalm 112:6–7. From this it is clear that no account is to be taken of the ungodly, who will tear the godly with every charge of wickedness but will praise the ungodly, as Proverbs 28:4 says. Indeed, their censure must be counted as praise. For only then

[1632 witness | PDF p. 106 | printed columns 186–187]

is there great perversity of sin, when not only are evil things done, but misdeeds are further approved by word of mouth. But here the power of God’s truth shines forth: by his blessing the curses of the ungodly become like vain and ineffectual missiles, while weight is added to the praises of the godly so that their memory may be preserved. Next, we must take care not to fail God’s providence, so that the truth of this saying may stand, whenever at the death of the godly we encounter mention of them by an honorable designation.

Some people abuse this passage to confirm prayers for the dead. They do not mention a dead person, especially a friend, without this wish: “May God have mercy on his soul,” or something similar, as though it were not permitted to bless the dead unless we offer prayers for them. This practice is condemned, first, for its uselessness, since it brings no fruit to the dead person but harm to the living, for an account must be given of every idle word, much more of a vicious one. 2. It is false worship of God, sinning against the second commandment, for which neither any command nor any example exists in the sacred Scriptures. 3. It is generally done without zeal or the effect of charity, and so sins against the third commandment. 4. By this kind of prayer an injury is done to the dead person whom we desire to benefit, since we signify plainly enough that he has not yet attained the life for whose attainment we institute our petition; and so one sins against the ninth commandment. 5. We offend our brethren: the godly, by violating the commandment; the ungodly or weak, by confirming their minds in error. But here they ask what, then, is to be said when they happen to mention the dead. By that one question they disclose their perversity: nothing is savory to them except what is insipid before God, although there are so many and such varied expressions by which they may testify to their charity toward the dead person, such as: “He has departed to God”; or, “His memory is pleasing,” or, “is blessed”; “He has rested from his labors”; “He has been gathered to his fathers,” and so forth. This also belongs here: they reach such perversity that they say it is necessary either to pray for good things for the dead or to invoke dire things upon them, as though no middle course could be found between these two.

The name of the wicked will putrefy: either it falls from people’s minds and tongues, or mention is made of them only with the greatest disgrace and detestation. This is clearly seen in the greatest tyrants of the world—Nero, Nebuchadnezzar, and so forth. It is all the more conspicuous in proportion to the greater effort they devote to splendid tombs and to inscribing their names upon their possessions, so as to vindicate themselves and their names from the injury of oblivion. Yet these things are so far from procuring fame or a name for such people that their ignominy and infamy grow fresh again from them. As often as monuments of this kind meet the eye, an occasion is both offered and taken to speak of their ruinous morals.

Verse 8. The wise man receives instructions in his heart, but the fool troubles himself with his lips.

A wise man gladly hears and admits others who instruct him, and thereby procures peace and quiet for himself. But one who is impatient of learning from others and claims for himself the parts of teaching and instructing troubles himself by his own folly.

In the first part it must be observed that the Apostle James commands everyone to be prepared and ready to hear. Although hearing others who teach and give wholesome instructions is commonly considered no proof of great wisdom, since it seems that any dolt can do it, while only the person who delivers a discourse before others is deemed wise, it is nevertheless safer and more consonant with true wisdom

[1632 witness | PDF p. 107 | printed columns 188–189]

to depend upon the mouth of teachers, chiefly from a consciousness of one’s own weakness—at least until one has been more fully instructed to teach and has manifestly been called. In this way he may perhaps learn some wisdom, and he certainly will blurt out nothing foolish. This also belongs here: Christ says, “He who has an ear, let him hear,” and would never say, “He who has a mouth, let him speak.” James also wishes everyone to be slow to speak. Ecclesiastes 5 also belongs here: sin will be no more absent from much speaking than sleep from a multitude of business. Indeed, is not the fool considered wise when he has long kept silent?

Yet those upon whom the office of speaking and teaching is imposed, but who fail in it, are not excused on that account. Although they themselves ought to take hold of the instruction and learn from others what they should teach: “Attend to reading and exhortation.” Apollos, an eloquent man, powerful and instructed in the Scriptures, did not disdain to draw heavenly lessons and instructions from Aquila and Priscilla, who were themselves artisans.

Verse 9. He who walks in integrity walks securely, but he who perverts his ways will be made an example.

The person who orders his life in the highest integrity and sincerity, however exposed to every danger he may seem in human judgment, nevertheless acts in safety. But the person who perverts and variously twists his pursuits and manner of life, although in his own judgment he seems to conceal his way of acting from human eyes so as to escape danger, will at last be discovered and become liable to the danger which he had planned to avoid by his dissimulation.

Here the Holy Spirit recalls the corrupt and perverse judgment of human minds to soundness. By depraved nature people have been persuaded deep within their minds—and by the world’s imposture and wicked suggestion the persuasion has become more deeply rooted—that an upright and uncorrupted conduct of life draws danger and at last destruction after it. The Spirit, on the contrary, teaches that no way is safer and farther removed from danger than for someone to cultivate integrity and sincerity throughout the whole course of his life and adhere to it precisely.

Some translate it, “walks confidently,” which the word itself signifies; but there is no difference, since the person who conducts himself confidently and securely is safe. The reason for this is drawn from the fact that the person who conducts himself uprightly is at the same time persuaded that he lives beneath the shadow and shelter of divine providence. From this his soul and spirit gather more strength than to be struck down by fear arising from the hatred and snares of the ungodly. Indeed, he has a shield against every missile of the ungodly. Thus Genesis 17: “I am God all-sufficient; walk continually before me, upright and sincere”; and Genesis 15: “I am a shield.” See an excellent confirmation of this matter in John 11:9. See David: as long as he lived in Judea, although he seemed to live in the very jaws of his enemy Saul, he was nevertheless farthest from danger there. By contrast, when he went out to foreign nations, where he seemed especially to have reached harbor, danger was then nearer. But the clearest and most evident example of this matter exists in Christ: although he lived continually in the midst of the bitterest enemies, men most thirsty for his blood, he nevertheless remained safe. It may indeed happen that one who walks sincerely suffers affliction for a time; at last, however, it will be well with him. Thus David says excellently: “Observe the upright man; behold the righteous man: his end is peace.”

He delivers to people another παράδοξον, paradox: they suppose that they will be safe through their cunning and transformation into every shape, while the Spirit, on the contrary, teaches that people of this kind, who put on every shape and, like a chameleon, turn themselves into every color except white, will at last be detected and pay the penalty.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 108 | printed columns 190–191]

For first, by driving God away from themselves, they are terrified in their minds by the bite of conscience. Then, by stripping themselves of God’s protection, they cannot but lie open to many evils. The saying is commonplace: the crafty perish in their own craftiness. “I shall destroy the wisdom of the wise,” 1 Corinthians 1:19. See Saul: how many oblique and tortuous schemes he devised to entrap David under the appearance of friendship and concern for the commonwealth. Yet they have now become known to everyone, to his supreme ignominy, although they lay hidden for a time. Conclude this passage with verse 38 of Psalm 37.

Verse 10. He who winks with his eye causes trouble, and a fool troubles himself with his lips.

The person who winks with his eyes and feigns peace and friendship toward his neighbor brings evil or trouble upon others. By contrast, the person who exposes himself to danger by his loquacity must be considered no less at fault, or indeed still more. The sense, therefore, is this: although in the preceding proverb I condemned those who cunningly and covertly turn and conduct themselves in every shape, I do not therefore wish people to suppose that I approve every kind of frankness and open manner of acting. For there is someone who blurts out everything and conceals nothing, and he is no less at fault than the former man, and indeed all the more in proportion as it is more serious to summon evil upon oneself than to cause trouble to another. Here, therefore, as often below, two vices are opposed to one another: one of hypocrisy, and the other of rashness and precipitancy. People are generally so constituted that, when they hear craftiness condemned, they immediately slide down into ignorance and childishness. When they understand that the royal road must be kept and no turning made into byways, they immediately cast themselves upon roads infamous for robberies—among us, Shooters-hill, Stangate-hole, Gads-hill, Salisbury-plaine, and so forth—and will delight always to wander there without any precaution either to avoid the night or to avoid solitude. Thus they turn either to the left hand or to the right.

This teaching is confirmed by the memorable saying of Christ the Savior: “Be simple indeed as doves [but also prudent as serpents],” Matthew 10. Excessive simplicity is no less a sin than excessive fox-like fraud. Joab’s feigned friendship toward Abner and Amasa, under the appearance of which he killed them both, was execrable. But Asahel’s open and inexorable profession of enmity toward Abner must also be greatly condemned. Ziba’s crafty art in capturing David’s favor and betraying the innocence of his master Mephibosheth, 2 Samuel 16, ought to be hateful to all the godly; but Shimei’s insolent and monstrous tongue, and his other enormous insults, are equally hateful to the godly. So also among us, those who immediately rush openly into a fight because of an injury received, or their belief that they have been injured, are just as much at fault as those who conceal the enmities they have conceived until they obtain a suitable opportunity for vengeance. There are many among us who, when they wish to excuse a person, say that he is a good man and an enemy to no one except himself; that he indeed wishes always to profess freely what is in his mind but cannot control himself in anger—as though it were not a serious crime to harm oneself, or rather were not sometimes more serious to harm oneself than another, as is seen in αὐτοκτονίᾳ, suicide. There are also many who, to proclaim their own praise, will boast that they are not among the number of detractors who are accustomed to curse people secretly, but that they will say to an adversary’s face whatever they think and will rage openly before him. Yet there is often more evil in open abuse than in secret detraction. So in religion it is detestably execrable if someone withdraws himself from the true worship

[1632 witness | PDF p. 109 | printed columns 192–193]

of God. But it is also worthy of censure if someone, from hatred of false worship, tears down idols without being furnished with the authority of the magistrate. It is dreadful to deny acknowledged truth; but it is also full of danger if a private person, from hatred of idolatry, assumes for himself license to declaim against idols in the middle of the marketplace.

Verse 11. A spring of life bathes the mouth of the just, but violence covers the mouth of the wicked.

In the first half-verse the singular good of a godly person’s tongue is set forth by its comparison to a spring of unfailing water. For we know the use of a perennial spring and living water in a city, and also at home in private houses: how it revives the thirsty, cools those who are overheated, cleanses the filthy, is useful for extinguishing fires, and so forth. First, it must be observed from John 4 that the just man who drinks from the water of the Holy Spirit will never thirst, but the Spirit becomes in him a spring of unfailing water. A spring serves not itself but only others. Yet the godly person is not useful to himself alone: his mouth is like a spring of waters; for because he believes, he therefore speaks, and when the occasion arises he can no more contain good words than burning coals in his mouth. Isaiah 5.4.57.22.23. But the principal fruit of this matter is seen in the public ministry of the Gospel. Add to this that it is also called a tree of life, that it brings medicine to the sick, and food to the healthy. By contrast, the tongue of the ungodly at home and in private is like a stagnant pool and still water filled with rubbish and dung. It is also like those bitter and poisonous waters and is plainly a tree of death. “For evil communications corrupt good manners,” 1 Corinthians 15. But this is seen above all in the corrupt ministry of the Church, whether mute or heretical.

Verse 12. Hatred stirs up contentions, but charity covers all defections.

Hatred revives contentions that had been lulled to sleep by recalling offenses, much less does it fail to seek out new contentions. But the person thoroughly imbued with charity, turning sins away by a certain voluntary forgetfulness and covering them, settles the dispute that has been stirred up and fosters peace.

Hatred, which everyone shuns in words and profession, is first described here by its effects and then illustrated by its adjuncts. Its effect betrays hatred that lurks in the mind like a serpent in the grass. Its effect is that it stirs up contentions; the instrument by which it does so is the sins or offenses of the people whom they hate, by defaming them, exaggerating them, and drawing them into the worst interpretation. Solomon appears to compare someone inflamed by hatred to a person who renews a fire that is sinking down and beginning to be covered with ashes by thrusting in a poker, and in this way scatters the ashes which restrain the fire’s heat. See an example of this in Esau, who rubbed raw again Jacob’s act of supplanting committed long before, Genesis 27:35. But a more conspicuous example is seen in Doeg, one of Esau’s descendants. Likewise in Moab and Ammon, who sharpened the Babylonians against the afflicted Jews by rubbing raw again their old enmities with inveterate hatred. Next it must be noted that a person is to be charged with hatred if, by recalling and repeating committed offenses, he fosters contention. Yet people commonly consider themselves free from fault if they invent or devise nothing false but only recount what was done. Solomon, however, teaches the contrary. How much more must the person who composes and fabricates lies be held convicted of hatred? See the example of Doeg against David. Next, although a person may judge that, as part of the duty of a Christian man, he ought to bear one or another injury, yet if they multiply, he believes that he may rightly

[1632 witness | PDF p. 110 | printed columns 194–195]

take vengeance. Here let what is said in this passage come to mind: charity covers a multitude. Likewise that saying in Matthew 18: “Seventy times seven.” They say further that if people had sinned less seriously against them, they would easily pass over the injury. The Savior meets them by teaching that charity covers even graver sins, Matthew 6, just as hatred stirs up disputes from trifling offenses. But are none of your sins against God graver? Indeed, among unregenerate people you may find some who will pass over slight and few injuries, perhaps so that they do not cause trouble for themselves by pursuing the injuries. Only that person, therefore, gives proof of Christian charity who covers all defections. The argument by which he exposes hatred is drawn from the opposites: charity covers offenses and graver sins, either crushing them by voluntary oblivion or mitigating them as far as truth permits. He compares charity to a covering by which foul things are removed from sight, lest they render hateful the person in whom those evils are found. See Joseph’s example: as far as truth allowed, he attributed his sale as a cheap slave into Egypt to the paternal providence of God. He did not seize an occasion to mention it, and spoke not even the slightest word about it unless compelled. Thus Japheth and Shem covered their father’s shameful parts, which he had exposed. Thus David, forgetful of every injury done to him, celebrated Saul in song, punished his killer, and treated Saul’s posterity with every kindness in his power. See the example of Stephen and Christ toward their enemies, and of Christ toward the Apostles after he rose again: passing over all their injuries against him, he wished peace to them.

But many people perversely abuse this saying for not a few evil purposes. By this saying they freely assume for themselves a license to sin without accusation or even the name of sin, although God opposes a sharper rebuke to inward hatred of one’s brother, Leviticus 19:17. Indeed, by this saying they will stop the mouth of the preacher of the word so that he does not expose their sins before the congregation. Others try by this clause to undermine the judicial tribunal. All of these spitefully assign a lenient function to charity, although in this passage God forbids people to divulge covered sins or agitate them publicly at an unseasonable time so as to feed the fire of contention. Finally, the Papists foully twist this saying toward the merit of works, although the Prophet, like 1 Peter 4, speaks here not about the remission of sins before God, but only about covering and burying them before people.

This is demonstrated from the opposite member. For just as in the first part of the verse the stirring up of disputes is not referred to God, as though God were incited against the person who is defamed by the uncovering of his sin, so also the covering of sins is not spoken of with respect to divine judgment, but only with respect to people. For we know that the sins of a delinquent may be uncovered in the human forum and nevertheless remain covered before God’s tribunal; and, by contrast, they may be covered among people and veiled in oblivion, yet be summoned to judgment before God. And although the person who stirs up contentions deserves death, Romans 6, it does not therefore follow that the person who composes disputes receives life as a reward. Next, when love of one’s neighbor is said here to cover all transgressions, if this were referred to an abolition of sins earned by merit, what would be reserved for the love by which we pursue God? Since that love is greater than love of one’s neighbor, it would deserve more merit toward eternal life than love of one’s neighbor.

Verse 13. Present wisdom is upon the lips of the prudent, as a rod is upon the body of a senseless man.

“Upon the lips” is a metonymy of the adjunct; next there is a synecdoche of one instrument of speaking for all

[1632 witness | PDF p. 111 | printed columns 196–197]

: likewise, in “back” there is a synecdoche of the part for the whole person, and in “rod” a synecdoche of one kind of punishment for all; finally, in “heart” there is first a metaphor of the heart for the mind (because, as the heart is the principle of life and actions in the body, so the human mind is the principle of all actions that concern the other life); next there is a metonymy of the subject for the adjunct, namely of the mind for the wisdom of the mind. The sense, therefore, is that an intelligent person speaks wisely, which is an honor to him; but an undiscerning person, because he pays little attention to the words of the wise and either does not understand them because of his dullness or, once he has perceived them, passes them by contemptuously, is punished with a penalty that is exceedingly ignominious and full of reproach.

Here, incidentally, it must be noted again in the first half-verse that a person’s speech is, as it were, the offspring of his mind. It cannot happen that, if the mind has conceived well, the mouth will bring forth degenerate offspring. “A good man out of the good treasure of his heart,” and so forth. Likewise there is the saying celebrated in the proverb and commemorated by John: as the man is, so is his speech.

But what Solomon chiefly regards here is what has been omitted here and must be supplied from the opposite in the latter half-verse, namely that it is an honor to him. Christ confirms this in Luke 9:22–23. Even in this life its fruit is very great, Proverbs 21, where he considers its fruit so excellent that he cannot explain it in words. An example of this matter is in David’s reply, by which Jonathan’s soul was united to him and Jonathan afterwards adorned him with marks of honor. By contrast, how great was the ignominy and loss suffered by Rehoboam, who did not listen to Solomon’s most prudent senators, is clear from that history. The same is clear from Amaziah, who despised Joash’s counsel.

Verse 14. The wise store up knowledge, but destruction is near to the mouth of the fool.

The wise store up knowledge, and from it, when the time is suitable, they ward away things that would harm and bring destruction, both from themselves and from others, Psalm 119:11. But speech of such a kind as brings terror both to himself and to others lies ready and exposed in the fool’s mouth.

An excellent example of the first part exists in Elihu. Although he had brought together a great treasure of wisdom in his mind as into a granary, he did not immediately bring it out into the midst, but waited for a suitable time. For just as farmers do not immediately bring out their heaps of grain as soon as they have full granaries, but consider the price at which they may most advantageously sell their grain, so also the wise do not, like σπερμολόγοι, babblers, rashly blurt out whatever they have conceived. Rather, they take particular care to utter at the proper time, for the good of others and their own consolation, the things that they have stored in their minds after careful consideration.

They also make a choice of hearers—not reproving a scoffer, Matthew 7:6; of time, as Abigail did, 1 Samuel 25; and of place, as Elijah did when Ahab was about to set foot in Naboth’s vineyard, 1 Kings 21.

The second part is confirmed by the familiar proverb that a fool’s arrow is shot immediately, and that empty vessels commonly make much noise. It is also said that a fool is, as it were, in labor to speak and walks about as though heavily burdened by it. An excellent example of this exists in Herod, Matthew 14:7, 9, and in Nabal. This is seen not only in the ungodly, but sometimes in the godly when they are carried away too hastily by some passion.

Verse 15. A rich man’s wealth is like his fortified city; the poverty of the poor is their destruction.

The riches of the rich are, in his judgment, like

[1632 witness | PDF p. 112 | printed columns 198–199]

a city of his own strength, 1 Samuel 25:22—that is, they serve him for warding off evils just as a fortified citadel serves cities for repelling enemies. The ruin (or destruction) of the poor, in their own judgment, is their poverty. Although the dispositions of the two kinds of people seem entirely opposed—the dejection of the one and the elation of the other—the sin of each nevertheless flows from one and the same fountain, namely an excessive estimation of wealth. Relying upon riches, the rich person promises himself that everything will be favorable; stripped of them, the poor person cannot hope that he will obtain anything good. In confirmation of the first part one may adduce the parable in Luke 12 and Matthew 19, and hence that saying of the Savior: “It is more difficult for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of heaven.” Its cause is assigned in Mark 10:24. Hence also that excellent exhortation in David, Psalm 62:11: “If riches flow abundantly, do not set your heart upon them”; and that saying in 1 Timothy 6.

Yet what is said of riches may be referred to all other means, aids, and supports for accomplishing some matter: to a multitude of friends; to a multitude of soldiers, chariots, and horses for obtaining victory; or to an abundance of grain for the preservation of life. This can be confirmed by the examples of those who, because they had enrolled a numerous army, promised themselves victory as a certain matter. People advance to such madness that, relying upon riches, they even think that the kingdom of heaven is owed to them. For from the abundance of riches they often infer that God is favorable to them. Thus the Papists formerly gloried in their multitude or universality.

By contrast, the poor are dismayed and broken and cast down in spirit because they lack wealth and those means by which the business of this life is conducted. This comes from their placing supreme happiness in riches, counting those who feast sumptuously and dress splendidly most blessed, honoring them as demigods, and casting themselves down even to hell for the acquisition of riches. Indeed, from this it often happens that they judge themselves unworthy of the kingdom of heaven. Considering the matter hopeless, they therefore pay no attention to the Gospel, because on account of their poverty or scant means they reckon themselves accursed, like Gideon, Judges 6. Thus also the Pharisees pronounce concerning them: “Has any of the rulers believed in him? But this people is accursed by God,” John 7. Although this happens to many poor people, it happens especially to those who were once furnished with wealth and were afterward stripped of it. Hence not a few have been found who, through impatience with poverty, have taken their own lives. By this circumstance Job’s patience is illustrated, Job 1:22: cast down from very ample possessions to a very meager condition, he did not sin in this whole matter.

The same thing happens when other supports of life are lacking, as when one must contend with a few people against great forces. Finally, in religion also, many tremble because those who worship God purely and sincerely are not so great in number, Isaiah 7:2. Christ consoles them when he says, “Fear not, little flock.” As for the advantages of this life, Christ teaches that life is worth more than food and the body than clothing. Therefore one must not fear that he who gave the body will fail to add clothing, or that he who gave life will refuse food.

Here the disadvantages of the rich and the advantages of the poor should be reckoned from Proverbs 27:8. Likewise they may be reckoned from the fact that, as riches increase, disputes and unrest increase, as the French proverb sings: “Qui a de terre, si a de guerre.” He who has lands has wars. Furthermore, in the captivity and devastation of regions, the poor are spared, as is clear from Jeremiah 38:10.

Verse 16. What the just man acquires by his work, he acquires for life; the revenue of the wicked is for sin.

The work of the just man is directed toward the present life, and indeed also toward the future, as is clear from the opposite

[1632 witness | PDF p. 113 | printed columns 200–201]

member: but the revenue of the wicked is directed toward sin. In the first half-verse he teaches that the just man procures the advantages of this life by labor and care only so far as they serve to make the present life bearable, and not to μετεωρίζεσθαι, be anxiously preoccupied. See the last part of Matthew 6; see 1 Timothy 6:6, 7, 8, 9, and so forth, and 17, 18, 19. An excellent example of this exists in Jeremiah 22:14, 15, 16, where it appears that even a king ought to be content with these things. From this it comes about that the just man is content with honorable and lawful gain, because his desires are not so unrestrained and unbelted—not, however, because he devours everything himself; see Ephesians 4:28.

By contrast, the entire revenue of the wicked, however great it may be, serves the commission of sin, whether he greedily gathers and stores it, or directs it toward the luxury and lusts of life. Hence there is no limit to gathering and scraping together. See an example of this matter in Josiah’s descendants, Jeremiah 22.

Verse 17. The way to life belongs to the person who observes instruction; but the person who forsakes correction enters a way that leads into error.

“Way,” here as often elsewhere, is a metaphor taken from travelers. The sense, therefore, is that everyone gathers for himself testimony that he has entered the way to life, and provides others with an indication of the same thing, if he observes the chastisement imposed upon him by God in word or deed, so that he advances from it toward repentance and patience. By contrast, the person who either evades correction or, having once admitted it, later shakes it off, enters a way that leads and drives him into error and thereby into death. The sum and chief point is to commend discipline and chastisement, whether it is exercised by God immediately or through an intervening means. This is done with a twofold argument: the first from the effects that follow the person who embraces it, and the second from the contrary effects upon the person who rejects it.

This proverb is most worthy of observation in this age, which is most impatient of the Lord’s yoke. To such people God’s pleasant yoke seems made of iron, and God’s paternal and saving rod seems like a scourge woven together with scorpions. Finally, of this age that saying of Hosea 4 can truly be spoken: no one reproves his brother, and the people of this age are like those who contend with the priest. They would do the same with God himself and Christ if he were upon earth. They advance so far that not only do they themselves reject correction, but they ridicule as fools those who either try by serious admonition and gentle reproof to recall others to good conduct or permit themselves to be recalled by others. Therefore, as this proverb is never untimely, so now for a certain special reason it is necessary.

First, it must be noted that it is not a τεκμήριον, sure token, of salvation and an undoubted proof of life if someone is reproved by words or circumstances. Yet it is a small sign of health and salvation if God meets a person who is wandering and forsaking the way of life with an opposing and inflicted calamity and affliction. This causes him to hope well that he has not yet been rejected and disowned by God. For we see that Jeroboam, reproved by God both in word and circumstance, persisted in his idolatry. The same can be said of Balaam. And that passage of Ecclesiastes has a place here: from the outcome of outward events it cannot be known whether a person is worthy of hatred or love. But if someone truly receives the chastisement sent and imposed upon him, accommodates and applies it to his soul, considers God’s end and purpose in chastening him, and searches into his own life so as to seek out his stains and sins and wipe away those he has detected, that person may at last congratulate himself upon the calamity laid before him, since it gives the surest assurance that he is standing upon the straight way that leads to salvation,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 114 | printed columns 202–203]

for God’s chastisements bring us to strike our thigh and each inquire with himself, “What have I done?” Jeremiah 31. Thus, when Joseph’s brothers were treated harshly and bitterly by their brother Joseph, they were struck by conscience and confessed that by God’s just judgment this harshness had come upon them because they had been moved by no compassion toward a brother who was innocent and had deserved well of them. An excellent lesson concerning this matter exists in Job 33, where from verse 19 to verse 21 he teaches how at last the afflictions sent upon the godly become useful to them: namely, when a minister approaches them in their suffering and sets forth God’s justice in afflicting, and so forth. From this it appears how necessary God’s ministry is if we are to profit from afflictions. Hence in 1 Peter 1 and James 1 faith is said to be tested by afflictions, whether it is merely shadowy or not.

By contrast, the person who evades chastisement in words or deeds enters the way of error and thereby of death. This is what those people do who avoid rebukes and withdraw themselves from sermons lest their ulcer or abscess be opened. Next are those who depart from the truth so that they may cast away afflictions and the cross. Finally, there are those who, after taking up the cross, are afterward afflicted by weariness or impatience with it. Of the first kind were the Israelites in Jeremiah, who cried, “Preach pleasant things to us”; likewise those in Micah 2:6 who say, “Do not drip,” and so forth. See the example of Ahab, how he kept to the surest road to present and future death because he endured hearing with composed ears only things pleasing to himself, although some may be found who will indeed hear those who rebuke them, but most unwillingly and painfully. An example of the second kind occurs in Matthew 13, in those who fall away when affliction presses in. An example of the third is Alexander the coppersmith, 1 Timothy 4:14.

Verse 18. The person who conceals hatred has deceitful lips, and the person who utters slander is senseless.

There is a person who conceals and dissimulates hatred conceived in his mind against another by false lips, with which he feigns friendship or at least displays no offense of his mind. This indeed is wicked, since it is commanded that no one should hate his brother but should reprove him, Leviticus 19:17. By contrast, the person who reveals his conceived hatred by casting abuse upon his neighbor must be considered foolish: both in God’s judgment, because he exasperates with reproaches the person whom he ought to have healed by brotherly correction, and before people, because by this abuse against his neighbor he begets hatred in the neighbor’s mind, from which that neighbor plots evil against him. An excellent example of the first part is in Absalom. Before his offended sister he appeared as though to excuse and diminish his brother’s unspeakable crime, while meanwhile he carried the injury stored deep in his mind. Moreover, before his father he displayed a singular love toward his brother Amnon, 2 Samuel 13. Likewise there is Joab’s example in the cases of Abner and Amasa. By contrast, examples of revealing hatred through abuse are Shimei and Asahel.

Verse 19. In a multitude of words transgression will not cease, but an understanding person restrains his lips.

Since a slip of the tongue is very easy, and since the tongue sins in many ways, no ground is more slippery or liable to a fall than when someone pours out many words and sets no limit for himself in speaking. See James 1, where he exhorts us to be slow and deliberate in speaking, but eager and awake in hearing. By this he does not condemn every lengthy discourse, since this proverb urges a compression of speech according to the circumstances and admonishes us to pour forth nothing rashly, but first to weigh our words and bring them into the open as though comprehended by number and estimated by weight, Ecclesiastes 12:10. Nor is that all: we must also take account of time

[1632 witness | PDF p. 115 | printed columns 204–205]

and person. For we should not suppose that the discourse condemned in this passage is only that expended in defending evil causes, but also that employed in a legitimate cause, unless someone sets a limit to it.

Besides this, zeal for speaking much very often drives a person into narrating falsehoods. One sins in this kind when one blurts out everything that happens to please one’s mind.

Verse 20. The tongue of the just is like the choicest silver; the mind of the wicked is worth very little.

This excess in speech has two parts:

├─ One before God, Matthew 6:7.
└─ The other before people, Proverbs 2.

“Tongue,” that is, speech, is a metonymy of the subject. The speech of the just is the choicest silver. How much more excellent is his mind, which conceives greater things than can be expressed in words? But the heart of the wicked is worth very little; and for that reason his speech is cheap and contemptible, like dross and refuse.

The speech of a just person is refined seven times, as though cast into a fiery furnace, so that it represents and expresses not only silver, which is desirable in itself, but the choicest and purest silver, smelted and purged of falsehood, vanity, and garrulity as of dross. When it was previously likened to a river of waters, its usefulness in the family, city, and town was described. But now its comparison with refined silver illustrates its necessary use and teaches at what price the just person’s speech is to be valued—namely the highest—and how eagerly it is to be sought and drawn in. If someone were to pour money either into the middle of a city square or onto the floor of a house, who would be so sluggish as not to gather up what was poured out, especially if he himself were poor and needy? Who would not join himself to that household? Who would not desire to be presented with citizenship in that city? With what great force and rush would people, especially the needy, hasten there?

This is all the more to be desired because an abundance of silver may bring satiety, as in Solomon’s time silver was considered of no value, 1 Kings 10; yet choice discourses are commonly more pleasing the more diligently and eagerly they are received and gathered. Therefore, what person of sound mind would not gladly be admitted into that household or enrolled in that city or Church where this vein of silver lies exposed to everyone? What town or society would not procure such a person at a high price? Thus would come to pass what Isaiah prophesies will occur in the happiest times of the Church, Isaiah 60:17: gold will be substituted for bronze and silver for iron.

From this it follows that the thoughts of the just are upright, because he brings forth good things from the good treasure of his heart. By contrast, since the heart or thoughts of the wicked are worth nothing, what proceeds from his mouth, if not harmful, is at least vain and frivolous. Therefore, you should no more be held by desire for his speech than for the most worthless metal. He indeed often has a sonorous and noisy voice, just as baser metals are found to be more resonant; but it is a voice that is weighed least of all by the weight and dignity of its subjects.

Verse 21. The lips of the just feed many, but fools die through folly.

“The lips,” that is, the speech of the just, “feed,” and so forth. A man of pure and uncorrupted speech is likened to a noble head of a household whose gates stand open to everyone passing by so that they may refresh themselves there and sustain themselves with food. The just person not only has the means by which he himself may live, but from his abundance

[1632 witness | PDF p. 116 | printed columns 206–207]

of heart he brings forth healthful and saving discourses into the midst, with which he may feed others, and indeed many in number. But because fools are furnished with no understanding, they not only fail to feed others; they themselves also perish, consumed by their own senselessness as though by hunger.

The third metaphor, and by far the most illuminating, illustrates the benefit of pure and uncorrupted speech. For although a spring of water is useful and silver desirable, yet food and sustenance, without which we cannot endure this life, are something more excellent. Hence Solomon in Ecclesiastes 5:8–9 says that no one can be satisfied with silver, or with any other thing, except the produce of the earth, thus valuing the fruits of the earth more highly than silver. That the just person is said to feed many is confirmed by this: he is like leaven, which, although small, leavens a great lump, Matthew 13:33. The reason is that wherever he spends his time, and among whomever, his discourses are seasoned with salt and gracious: he instructs the unskilled, reproves offenders, and so forth, both in season and out of season. See Isaiah 23, John 17, and so forth.

For the just person is not like a covetous craftsman who sometimes conceals his knowledge from those whom he undertakes to train. Nor is it with spiritual gifts as in the arts, where the more professors there are, the less profit there is. Rather, such is the excellence of spiritual gifts that the more they are communicated to others and shared by them, the more they enrich and gladden their possessor. Therefore, by comparing pure lips to food, a common meal, or nourishment, Solomon has vividly portrayed their excellent power and efficacy. There is no one who does not desire food and, when suffering hunger, give everything for it. If someone moves into another household, he commonly inquires what kind of head of household it has, whether he provides his servants with a just and liberal portion; and he seeks out a household of that kind. How much more should the food of life be sought? Who would join himself to a town or city that is surrounded on every side by deserts and brings its citizens no produce? This should especially be considered if we reflect how hungry we are by nature. If we weighed this properly, we would fly toward this nourishment with no less eagerness than the besieged Samaritans once ran toward food, when the officer whom the king had placed over the grain supply was trampled and crushed to death under the people’s feet.

By contrast, in households, colleges, or Churches where tongues are impure or lips perverse, there are pools and stagnant, putrid waters infected with corpses and with stenches from beasts, these things, and others. There the coin is counterfeit; there the food is pestilential; death is in the pot, in the kneading troughs, and so forth. Here, therefore, a mute and corrupt ministry is especially reproved. Here also let us examine ourselves: whom are we winning for God by our discourses, especially since it is evident that we have destroyed many? Fools, by contrast, consume themselves by their own senselessness; much less are they accustomed to feed others. They are the murderers of their own souls. From this it is inferred that the just person must not instruct others in such a way that he himself meanwhile remains uncultivated in godliness. For it is a grievous thing if someone has commanded that there be no stealing while he himself meanwhile gapes after plunder. Romans 2; Matthew 7; 1 Timothy 4.

Verse 22. Jehovah’s blessing itself makes rich, and he will add no sorrow with it.

Above we considered proverbs by which the perverse judgment of people is rebuked in the use of possessions and riches. Now, however, Solomon notes the perversity of their judgment in the manner of seeking them. For nearly everyone engaged in the acquisition of wealth places, as they say, prow and stern in this: that those who devote themselves to riches should first have skill in the conduct of affairs and then add industry and diligence.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 117 | printed columns 208–209]

But the Holy Spirit establishes a very great—indeed, the one and only—means of acquiring wealth: that God should bless a person and attend his studies and endeavors with his favor. For by God’s blessing things are acquired better, preserved better, and made more effective for every use to which they are applied. Thus a little with God’s blessing will satisfy more people than much with God’s blessing removed. This is indicated by the pronoun היא, as if to say: that alone is the true cause; the other things are only instruments. Then God’s blessing brings another advantage: it does not excessively weary or torment people. While they are, as it were, sleeping and thinking little about the matter, God by his blessing supplies good things to them, Psalm 127:2; Luke 5:7; and he preserves what has been supplied without anxiety, since in Job 1 God is a wall of protection.

The clearest examples are Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Although they wandered here and there, they were nevertheless enriched by God’s blessing. By contrast, see in Haggai 1 what a curse accomplishes.

Meditation upon this matter would drive away all fraud and deceit in commerce, and avarice in the use and preservation of riches. For since riches are acquired by God’s blessing, who is so insane as to believe that he can obtain that blessing from God by violating his commandments?

Next, this will add a spur for people to seek God and ask him to bless our labors and undertakings. Yet the godly must not therefore recline idly and drowsily upon the other side. Rather, in their calling they must labor earnestly σὺν θεῷ, with God.

Verse 23. As it is sport for a fool to commit wickedness, so [it is sport] for an understanding man [to exercise] wisdom.

That is, the exceedingly understanding man derives wisdom from it, or is made wiser. Or: wisdom is like laughter to an exceedingly understanding man. Although this rendering requires a greater supplement of words, because it possesses a more elevated sense and a more striking antithesis, I would prefer this latter rendering to the former and to all others.

In the first part of this proverb the signal wickedness of evil people is declared: with reins released and bridle loosened, they are borne headlong toward sin. For although the commission of even the smallest sin ought to afflict them with grief and bring them to weeping and tears, it is altogether monstrous for them to receive joy from that from which they ought to receive grief—indeed, to break into laughter from that from which it would be fitting to pour forth tears. But it instills the greatest horror if someone regards crime and villainy as jest and sport. To this is added the statement that they do not fall asleep unless they have committed evil, Proverbs 4:16; 2:14. Likewise, in Job 15:16 they are said to gulp down sin as fish do water. Elsewhere they are said to delight in sin just as one who has obtained delicacies holds them beneath his palate for a long while so that he may derive pleasure from the food for as long as possible, Job 20:12. In this they represent the man who wished for himself the neck of a crane. The godly do indeed commit offenses and are sometimes overtaken by a crime, but meanwhile, conscious of their sin, they immediately groan and draw deep sighs. In short, sin adheres to them, but it does not rule, much less triumph. See an example of this matter in Esau. After he had surrendered the right of the firstborn to his brother, he nevertheless ate, drank, rose, and went on his way as though the matter had been conducted well, or at least not disadvantageously. But the most striking example of this matter exists in Joab and Abner, 2 Samuel 2:14. The example of the ungodly Ahab also pertains here: because he could not accomplish his crime, he took to his bed; he revived and was restored to health after he impiously obtained his wish. These things apply to our own times. For you will find very many people to whom it is sport and amusement to watch others quarrel,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 118 | printed columns 210–211]

and who delight in it; who take pleasure in carrying away game by theft and rejoice far more in eating what was seized than what was justly acquired; to whom stolen waters are sweet. Indeed, there are even some who boast that they dare openly to despise the ministry of the word and tread it underfoot. Meanwhile, if you please, they cover themselves with the name of Christ, though they are hated by God.

It will be useful to note the second part of the proverb, which is opposed to the first. It teaches that the godly likewise delight in practicing godliness and performing their duty. These things are therefore their delicacies, their sport, the relaxation of their minds, and their food, John 4:34. For God loves a cheerful giver. Psalm 119; 2 Corinthians 9. Although we are rightly ashamed to be surpassed by the wicked, so that in pursuing depravity they outshine us in the performance of duty, nevertheless the course of the wicked toward evil is swifter and more impetuous than that of the godly toward what is holy. For the wicked are borne along by horses and sails, hands and feet, with every exertion and struggle and without any delay, like an armored horse. But because the godly have the flesh fighting against them, they are impeded in their course as though by a certain corpulence: if the godly walk, the wicked run; if the former run, the wicked fly; if the godly are foot soldiers, the wicked are like horsemen, and so forth.

Verse 24. What the wicked person fears will come upon him, but what the just desire [God] will give.

The fear of the wicked person—that is, what the wicked person fears—will itself come upon him; and God is accustomed to grant the desire of the just. Conversely, the desire of the wicked will perish, and what the godly fear will not befall them. Although the wicked laugh after committing a crime, their laughter is nevertheless like thorns crackling in a fire, which are quickly extinguished. Fear, or dread of evil, succeeds their laughter, arising from the prick of conscience. It makes them restless and represents the sword of divine vengeance hanging over their heads. Nor is that fear empty, since God’s justice does not permit a committed crime to go unpunished. For although the just person, fearing evil for himself, avoids it, Proverbs 14:27, the wicked person, full of fear and dread, gains not the slightest benefit from it and does not resist the evil rushing upon him. What other cause can we assign than that he endeavors to drive away evil by evil, does not pour out prayers to God, and indeed is not even able to pour them out? From this it is also clear how the just person avoids evil: namely, by resolving to flee it through honorable means and by imploring God’s aid in the matter through prayers. Examples exist in Saul, who feared that the kingdom would be transferred from himself and his descendants to David’s family, as he more than once openly affirmed both before Jonathan and before David. Another example is in the Jews, who feared that the Romans would come and take away their commonwealth. Because they endeavored to repel that evil unlawfully, it came upon them. But someone may object here that Job affirms concerning himself that the very thing he feared befell him. It must be known, however, that this was a hidden judgment of God, sent in so unusual a manner that it should not be measured by the common rule. Moreover, Job’s anxiety and fear were not servile, as those of the wicked are. Rather, they arose chiefly lest God’s name should be spoken of badly because of him, a professor of godliness. God grants the desire of the just, 145:19; 25:5; and “ask and you will receive.” See the example of Jacob: when he was possessed by the desire to see Joseph, he saw not only him but also his sons. Likewise, when Solomon asked for wisdom, God also gave him riches and incomparable glory, 1 Kings 3. Yet through excessive indulgence he does not give his children harmful things as foolish parents do. Indeed, he reproaches his own people because they are sparing in asking, John 16:24.

Verse 25. As when a whirlwind passes, the wicked person will not [remain]; but let the just person be an everlasting foundation.

According to the manner in which—or the very brief time in which—a whirlwind passes, so—or in so brief a moment—the wicked person is no more; but the just person is a perpetual and eternal foundation, that is, immovable. See in Psalm 37 how brief the wicked person’s duration is. He indeed appears to live in such a way and to put down roots so deep as though he were immovable, but he has neither roots nor foundation. Hence in a very brief moment he is driven here and there. Therefore he is compared with grass, and particularly grass that grows in a lofty place upon roofs, Psalm 73; Job 21:13; Matthew 7:19. For their good health is neither interrupted nor suspended all the way to the end of life; and when they die, they do not languish for long. There are innumerable examples of this in Herod, Judas, Ahithophel, Absalom, Nebuchadnezzar, and so forth. Yet this likeness does not merely signify their momentary duration. It further indicates how unable they are to resist divine judgment and how suddenly they perish, 1 Thessalonians 5:3. By contrast, the godly are compared to feet founded upon the Rock, standing firm against every assault of rains and floods, while the wicked person cannot sustain the force of even one temptation or another, Matthew 7. See an example of this matter in Job. Though besieged by God’s army and battering rams, he was not conquered, chapter 1. Likewise see Paul in 2 Corinthians 11:23. This passage is excellently illustrated by the last verse of Isaiah 6, where it is taught not only that the just person is stable, but also that he is the support of the rest of the world. Seek the purpose of this proverb from Psalms 37 and 73 and 1 Peter 3:12.

Verse 26. As vinegar is to the teeth and smoke to the eyes, so is a lazy person to those who send him.

By this proverb Solomon defines the lazy person as both useless and harmful to those who employ his service. For just as vinegar dulls the teeth and renders them useless for chewing food, and smoke stings and troubles the eyes, so the lazy person not only fails to help but does great harm to those who permit some task to be entrusted to him. He ought to be exceedingly hateful to us because he is a useless burden, contributing and communicating no assistance to the commonwealth and none to the Church. And when he further brings ruin and loss, then surely he must be pursued with great hatred. Hence no industrious person wishes to support such people, and they are thereby driven to embark upon crooked courses, so that the commonwealth receives great injury from them. Therefore they must be driven to labor by the magistrate’s sword, so that an abundant supply may be available to those who are truly poor. Nor is it any wonder if one who is useless and ruinous to himself is also useless and troublesome to another. But if we exercise discrimination in the ambassadors and messengers whom we dispatch in our own business, how much more in those matters that concern God and his worship? How dangerous it is, therefore, to support a sluggish and idle minister, and much more an unlearned one who also lacks feet and legs, even if he were driven by zeal to discharge his office. See how Christ sent John and Peter to prepare the Passover. Furthermore, we must consider that we have been sent by God into his vineyard so that we may cultivate it and render it fruitful through the husbandry of the Spirit. Therefore we must examine what labor we have bestowed upon our olive tree. We have been sent to engage in spiritual commerce, and consequently we must see what gain we have made for the Lord who sent us. For here it must be feared that we may be found lazy and sluggish servants against whom Christ’s dreadful sentence may be pronounced: “You wicked servant,” Matthew 25. Therefore, let us consider whether empty fear has driven us from our duty, just as fear of a lion drives the lazy man away in the middle of the square and the assembly of people. Finally, let us remember the admonition of Hebrews 12:12–13.

Verse 27. Reverence for Jehovah adds days, but the years of the wicked are shortened.

The fear of the Lord adds—or joins—days, namely beyond the hope that someone is often accustomed to conceive, or beyond the length of life enjoyed by others who do not fear God. But the years of the wicked are cut off sooner than they hope. From this it is taught that people who desire the present life are deluded in the counsels and means by which they seek to prolong it for many years. Likewise, those same people who dread death nevertheless do the very thing by which their life is made shorter.

See above for what the fear of God is. In this passage it is placed by synecdoche for religion and the true worship of God, of which it is a principal part. For just as we are bound to love God because he is our Father, so we are bound to fear him because he is our Lord, Malachi 1. Hence in Psalm 2:11 they are joined in the true worship of God: “Rejoice before him with trembling.” By God’s love toward us we are delivered from despair; by fear, from presumption. Therefore we must dwell upon this all the more because it is difficult to join a sense of divine love with the fear of God, although this must be done. The fear of God, then, is a virtue of the soul by which, from a reverent persuasion concerning God that he is conscious of everything we do and that nothing in which we offend will go unpunished, we are kept from sinning and impelled to perform our duty. For if the sight of people whom we reverence restrains us from sin while they are present, how much more the sight of the omnipresent God, whose pure eyes cannot endure to behold iniquity?

In this there are two things:

├─ The first: an undoubted persuasion concerning God
│  ├─ as knowing all things, Psalm 16:8 (by contrast, the wicked do not see God, Ezekiel 8:12),
│  └─ and as rewarding, Isaiah 6 and Revelation 4, seated upon the throne.
└─ The latter: that which bears fruit from that persuasion—fearing God and departing from evil, Job; Proverbs 8:13.

We therefore know that we truly fear God from a sense of the divine presence when the fear of God has more weight in restraining us from sin than fear of any evil—poverty, disgrace, or even death itself.

Next, we truly fear God when we are impelled toward right conduct more by the fear of God than by any reward of honor, riches, pleasure, and so forth. By contrast, we finally depart from the fear of God when we either forget the divine presence, flatter ourselves that God will not deal so severely with us even if we depart from duty, or, though mindful both of divine justice and of the divine presence, nevertheless depart from duty through fear of disgrace, poverty, or death.

The fear of God is distinguished from the fear of the wicked in this: the wicked so fear harm to themselves from God that they desire him to be extinguished or disarmed. They fear one whom they hate; the godly, one whom they love. The wicked fear in such a way that they do not depart from sin; the godly do otherwise.

This passage has a twofold use. First, that by consciousness of our former depravity in this respect we may be trained toward humility. Second, that henceforth we may stir ourselves up to the duty of divine fear.

The fear of God is commended from its adjunct, the reward of length of the present life, which by synecdoche is placed for every prosperity of this life. See Psalm 34:10–11; see Matthew 10:28: “Fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell.” Here, therefore, a singular proof of God’s mercy is displayed. Though he can command us to do our duty by his own decision and mere will, nevertheless he does so by setting before us the reward both of the future life and of the present, 1 Timothy 4. This reproves our obstinacy and our soul hardened in sin, since we cannot be kept in our duty either by God’s authority in commanding or by his goodness in rewarding. Human beings, 2 Samuel 13:28, claim such authority for themselves over their own people that even

[1632 witness | PDF p. 121 | printed columns 216–217]

at their mere nod they want obedience given them in matters both most unjust and most full of danger. Although they set forth no reward, they command their people to trust in their authority and fear nothing, because, of course, they were the king’s sons. How much more ought it to be enough for us if God commands us to fear no one, Joshua 1:9? Now, however, since he not only commands but also sets forth a most ample reward—something they do not do or, if they do it, cannot provide—he reproves the great hardness of our soul. If they could provide us with the same things that God promises and provides, nevertheless to receive those things from them while passing over God’s generosity and command would be regarded as great wickedness. But these things that God will most surely provide are not in their power; and so it proves signal impiety. Indeed, the greatest impiety and incredible perversity become evident because not only do we fail to pursue through fear of God the good things that God promises, but we suppose that those same things will come to us when the fear of God has been cast off—as though fearing God were not merely unprofitable but even resulted in loss. See the example in 2 Kings 20.

What he proposed above he confirms in the latter half-verse from the contrary, just as the former also renders mutual assistance to the latter. Here it must be observed that wicked people are substituted in place of those who do not fear God, so that being wicked and not fearing God with a true and religious fear are the same thing. Thus Romans 3 identifies the absence of the fear of God as the source of all depravity. From this it is readily apparent how many among us are wicked: namely, as many as fear more harm to themselves from human beings and princes than from God; more from violating human laws than from violating the divine law; and more from a person’s sword and prison than from God’s immense, long, and exceedingly sharp sword and his prisons.

Next, since he says that the absence of fear is punished by the shortening of life and the deprivation of life’s other advantages, still greater perversity and obstinacy appear in casting off the fear of God: we are kept in our duty neither by his authority and benefits nor even by his punishments, although they are exceedingly bitter and severe. Here the question appropriately arises whether a time is appointed by God for human life which it can neither pass beyond nor fail to reach. To this bring the saying of the Psalm, “My times are in your hand.” Likewise, that God numbers our days, and, “My hour has not yet come.”

Verse 28. The hope of the just [is] gladness, but the expectation of the wicked perishes.

Two things are considered here in the first part of this verse. The first is that the just expect good things from God; the second is that this expectation is not frustrated, since the just are gladdened when they receive the good things they expect. In the first we learn that the godly are to be so thoroughly imbued with the fear of God that they meanwhile expect all good things from him. For since God promises good things to the faithful, it is right for us to rest in his promise, even when God threatens the destruction and devastation of all things; to await life in death, heaven in hell, light in darkness, and an ordered state of the Church amid the confusion and tumult of all things. Thus David, when all things were deplorable, strengthened himself in the Lord through expectation of a better condition, 2 Samuel 30; so also Jehoshaphat, 2 Chronicles 20. By contrast, see Ahaz, Isaiah 7, and often the Israelites in the wilderness.

The other point is that they are gladdened upon receiving benefits. For those who hope in God will never be confounded. Furthermore, because they pray that the things they expect may come to pass, when gladdened they sing to the Lord, James 5:13. By contrast, the wicked expect and desire things promised by no word of God; and the things they desire

[1632 witness | PDF p. 122 | printed columns 218–219]

from the word, they nevertheless pour out no prayers to obtain. See James 4:2. Here an exception is made because Psalm 73:7 says that the wicked receive more good things than they can imagine.

Verse 29. The way of Jehovah [is] strength to every upright person, but [it is] destruction to those who work iniquity.

In מחתה (*destruction*) and מעוז (*strength*) there is a metonymy of effect, because the way of the Lord renders people either strong or broken in soul according as they use it rightly or perversely. In דרך (*way*) there is a very common metaphor. The sum is that one who leads a life uprightly and wholly according to the prescription of the divine word, with deceit, simulation, and dissimulation excluded, is strong in soul and acts fearlessly; but continual dwelling in sins breaks the soul and inspires the fear by which the wicked person is thoroughly shaken and crushed. The cause is that God’s administration toward the latter is dreadful, but toward the former friendly and benevolent. For in this way, in Proverbs that are set forth without any connection or continuous train by which the words are bound to one single sense, we judge that interpretation is permitted without introducing violence against the Prophet—especially since it is the praise of a proverb to embrace many things in very few words.

It is deeply rooted in people’s souls that the one who knows how to simulate and dissimulate lives this life most safely and therefore most fearlessly and confidently: like Proteus or a chameleon, he changes himself into every form and conforms himself to the choice and will of those with whom he associates, feigning himself an Evangelical among Evangelicals, a Papist among Papists, and a blasphemer among blasphemers. Some indeed do this to avoid danger, and others to obtain some advantage. This is indeed the worst kind of person. Very few are found who cherish the dovelike simplicity that Christ commends to his people, or who do not think those people foolish or insane who display what they truly are and whose outward countenance reveals what is held within, settled in their mind.

The Holy Spirit refutes this error through the Prophet, who, of all the mortals who ever have been, now are, or will hereafter be, could, if he had wished, have employed the greatest artifice in putting on a false appearance, dissembling what he willed or simulating what he did not will. Yet he teaches that the way of the Lord—namely, piety toward God and justice toward people—however wretched and contemptible it may be in human judgment, however hatefully assailed by them and received with reproaches and insults, is nevertheless not overthrown but emerges unconquered. Its edge is not blunted by these injuries, nor are its zeal and endeavors broken. Rather, when God repeatedly adds new assistance, it becomes stronger and more confident. For while the wicked person flees though no one pursues, the just person trusts fearlessly like a young lion, Proverbs 28.

Nor is this all. From the same good work which seemed likely to create danger and fear from that danger, he derives courage. For when he executes in deed what God had commanded, why should he not trust? See Joshua 1:9. This passage is illustrated by an argument from the lesser, 2 Samuel 13:19. What mortal in whom any trace and sign of humanity appears would not, if he were able, guard and defend his servant when the servant vigorously sweats in his master’s work and is endangered for that very reason? The upright person is therefore strengthened in two ways: inwardly, by the testimony of conscience; outwardly, by God’s providence keeping watch, as it were, for his safety. Here our Machiavellians are refuted, who allege that people imbued with piety are cowardly and trembling when dangers must be undertaken, although no class of people is more fearless in sustaining dangers. It is indeed true that piety

[1632 witness | PDF p. 123 | printed columns 220–221]

deters people from rash undertakings. But in a just struggle undertaken for God’s glory and the benefit of the Church, they show themselves to be men and trust like lions. How much eagerness for joining battle is added by the religious soldier’s knowledge that not a hair of his head will perish apart from the providence of his heavenly Father? What contempt for death is produced when he is certainly persuaded that, after dying, he will live eternally in heaven with Christ and the Apostles? Thus by its own nature it adds great assurance to the confident and creates courage and spirit in those naturally timid and fearful. Hence that saying of Moses: one of those who stand firm in God’s commandments will put one hundred to flight, and one hundred will put one thousand to flight, since God is present with them and equips their hands for battle.

The same way of the Lord—whether the way on which we proceed or that by which God proceeds toward us—results in destruction for workers of iniquity who neglect the way of the Lord. For inwardly they are troubled by the goads of an evil conscience, while God’s avenging hand hangs over them. Therefore this proverb corresponds to the Apostle’s saying in 2 Corinthians 2:15–16, and likewise to Luke 2:34. What then? Is everyone who sins crushed? Far from it. Rather, it is those who practice iniquity as an art and devote themselves to perpetrating sins.

Someone may further object that human beings are by their nature guilty of eternal death; to what purpose, then, is the way of the Lord needed to bring destruction upon them? I answer: they are to be punished more severely and fully because they are disobedient to so clear a light.

Verse 30. The just person will never be moved, but the wicked will not inhabit the land.

The purpose is the same as that of the preceding proverb: to add courage toward holiness of life by turning away from wickedness. For a fuller explanation and illustration of this matter, we must see what kind of person someone not yet regenerated by the Holy Spirit is, from Isaiah 40 and 1 Peter 1. Everyone led by nature and reason establishes a manner of life so that, once it has been comfortably constituted, he may render it as stable as possible and free from change. To accomplish this they enter friendships, contract affinities, acquire clients, and leave no stone unturned to attain this stability. But in this great undertaking and effort they scornfully pass over the chief matter, without which the rest is ineffectual, and regard it as some leftover thing of little or, rather, no value.

Therefore the Holy Spirit teaches that there is no surer or shorter path to all eternity than for someone to attach his soul to the pursuit of piety.

But here the common condition of the godly and the wicked in diseases and death—the way of the whole world—lays hold of us. Then various storms of calamities arise, to which the just person is more exposed than the wicked person. Thus, it ranks among the paradoxes that a longer duration, much less eternity, is attributed to the just than to the wicked.

Verse 31. The mouth of the just abundantly brings forth wisdom, [whereas] the tongue of perversities is cut off.

In לשון (*mouth*) and פי (*tongue* and *mouth*) there is a metonymy of the efficient, or rather a synecdoche of the part in לשון. In ינוב (*abundantly brings forth*) there is a metaphor from a fruitful tree producing much fruit. In תהפכות the plural number stands for a depraved and perverse habit of speaking.

In the metaphor of a fruitful tree there is an elegant description of pure and chaste speech, by which people’s zeal and diligence are sharpened and stirred up to acquire this excellent virtue of speech. For who does not procure at great expense and labor a fruitful tree that produces abundant fruit every year without any interruption? Such a possession is upright and uncorrupted speech. Indeed, it is among those trees mentioned in Revelation 20 that bear fruit in every month—what am I saying? every day and hour. Since, therefore, we purchase a fruitful tree at so great a price, let us be ashamed of our foolishness if we do not acquire the goods of uncorrupted speech, especially since we receive it freely from God without price.

When he says that the mouth of the just brings forth abundantly, and so forth, he teaches that a person’s righteousness is not to be estimated from one or two blessed sayings but from his continual practice and customary manner of speaking. For it often happens that someone speaks well and piously because he is dealing with those who detest corruption of speech, and he conforms himself to their will. Sometimes he feigns the language of Canaan so that he may either receive some good or avoid some evil. Yet he is not to be regarded as just on that account unless he cultivates purity of speech from love and zeal for truth. For a good person brings forth many good words from the good treasure of his heart. As wisdom grows in the soul, its testimony becomes more abundant in good words. To this pertains the Apostle’s command in Ephesians 4:29 that all our speech be free from rottenness and be such as brings grace to the hearers; and in Colossians 3 he admonishes that it be seasoned with salt, so that each person knows how to answer everyone. Therefore, one who wishes to pronounce upon a man’s goodness from one or another chance conversation will conclude the arrival of spring from one swallow.

Here it is objected that sin will not be absent in much speaking and that we ought to be slow to speak but ready to hear. I answer that the former statement means a long prayer to God consisting of many repetitions, which Christ condemns in Matthew 6. It also condemns the zeal and desire for speaking that cannot be free from vice. But zeal to speak many good things for the glory of God and the edification of the brethren differs greatly from an itch to speak. The latter statement, from James, censures those who through their impatience interrupt people teaching useful, though rather harsh, things and who grumble and bark against godly teachers, at least in their minds.

In the latter half-verse he teaches that the wicked abuse their tongues. Here it must be noted that the tongue’s depravity is not defined by one or two words wrongly uttered, but by many, and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 125 | printed columns 224–225 | Chapter X ends before CAPUT XI in the right column]

when someone devotes himself to corrupt discourses, arranging his tongue to deceive, simulate, and dissimulate with God and human beings. Otherwise, curses sometimes escape even a just man; see David’s example, Psalm 119:11; 1 Samuel 25:13; and 2 Samuel 16:4.

This corruption of speech is reproved by the penalty attached to it, namely, that such tongues will be cut off. By these words he plainly intimates that a perverse tongue is like an unfruitful and barren tree that produces either no fruit or rotten and poisonous fruit. Its end is to be cut down. Those who abuse their tongues generally do so with this purpose: to keep themselves safe and secure from the danger of death. But the Prophet teaches that they are deceived and frustrated, since by this corruption of the tongue they enter the path to destruction. Hence that saying of David, Psalm 64:9; Psalm 12, dissuades from perversity of the tongue by its accompanying uselessness. In this passage, however, he employs a stronger argument, namely, that such a tongue prepares destruction for its owner. By this statement he clearly indicates from the contrary that the just person who uses uncorrupted speech acquires for himself a firmer and more enduring condition of life, however much in the perverse and depraved judgment of human beings he may be thought to bring danger upon himself.

Verse 32. The lips of the just will experience favor, but the mouth of the wicked the penalty of perversities.

This proverb is akin to the preceding one, but it also has something further by which uncorrupted speech is directed: namely, that a person undertake the care of speaking rightly and so acquire knowledge of those things by which he may make his speech approved before God and human beings. For good words and pure speech are not ready and prepared at hand for someone merely because he is just. Even at the greatest progress in godliness, because of the sin inhering in us, foolish sayings escape the just person, as was said, unless caution is employed. Hence Psalm 37 says, “The mouth of the just meditates wisdom.” Nor this alone: he also prays to God that he would place a guard over his mouth, lest he stumble in speech. The just person therefore weighs his words and weighs them out in the scales of the divine law before he utters them. It is consequently not enough for him to resolve to use an uncorrupted tongue unless a plan is formed by which this result may be produced. The wicked, moreover, although their vicious nature abundantly suggests every kind of evil deed to them, nevertheless—as though they were not already sufficiently insane of their own accord and trained to perpetrate evil deeds—give themselves as pupils to learn further wickedness. They sharpen still further their tongues, already sharp by nature for wounding, as though pouring oil upon fire. Therefore, even in the night itself, when they ought to rest, they meditate evil.
