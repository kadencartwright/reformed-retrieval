There follow Valencia's arguments, which Navarrete examines.

In Holy Scripture God is said to exist before temporal things; therefore things according to real
being do not coexist with eternity, which they presuppose. First confirmation: otherwise an infinite
length of duration before would not in fact belong to God alone, but would be common to creatures
according to that measure which belongs to God.

Second confirmation: if anyone said that time itself had properly existed from eternity, he would
not without reason appear to assert something against the cited testimonies of Scripture. Therefore
the same must be said concerning temporal things.

[Book I, printed p. 82; BSB canvas 00108]

NAVARRETE.

Navarrete: I. From those passages of Scripture we gather our opinion. For if eternity is said in
Holy Scripture to be before temporal things, it is so either according to its whole self or
according to a part of itself. The first cannot be said, for then it would not be affirmed of
eternity that it exists always. The second also implies a contradiction, for eternity is indivisible
and either exists wholly at once or no eternity exists in itself. It remains, therefore, that in
Holy Scripture eternity is said to exist before time according to that particular coexistence which
it has with time, but not in itself.

II. To the first confirmation it is answered that eternity belongs inadequately to creatures but
adequately to God.

III. To the second confirmation it is answered that neither time nor temporal things are said to
exist from eternity, but to coexist with eternity. There is a very great difference between these
propositions. The first denotes a thing's eternal existence according to its proper measure; the
second denotes the eminence of the measure of eternity, which under an indivisible measure draws to
itself things most widely separated and makes temporal things and things future in time eternally
present to itself.

RESPONSE.

Behold the man's effrontery! From Scripture's saying that God exists before temporal things, he
wishes to prove that God does not exist before temporal things, but that temporal things are as
without a beginning or end of their duration as God himself is—and that according to real being.
The argument on which he relies is by no means founded on those Scriptures, though he most
shamelessly gives it that appearance. It aims only to prove that Scripture cannot be explained
otherwise than with respect to that particular coexistence which eternity has with time. Besides
this, Navarrete invents another particular coexistence which eternity has not only with temporal
things but with time itself in eternity. He finds no trace of this in these passages of Scripture,
although he ought to if he seeks to prove his opinion from those very passages. Note in passing
Navarrete's insane manner of distinguishing when he philosophizes about eternity as existing before
time according to that particular coexistence which it has with time, but not in itself. He conforms
Scripture to this tasteless subtlety by saying that according to Scripture eternity is not before
time in itself, but is before time according to the particular coexistence it has with time.
Scripture certainly makes no mention of such a tasteless distinction, devised beyond all sobriety,
by which it might explain God's eternity to us. Navarrete himself maintains that eternity is not
before time even according to that particular coexistence which it has in time—namely, in eternity.
Yet according to Navarrete's own opinion it must also be asserted for another reason that eternity
is prior to time according to the coexistence which it has with time in eternity. For insofar as it
is prior to time according to coexistence with time in time, it is also prior to time according to
coexistence with time in eternity. To exist in time is to exist in eternity; to exist in time is to
coexist with eternity; to coexist with eternity is to be measured by eternity; and to be measured
by eternity is to exist in eternity.

But let us weigh the argument itself. We say that eternity is before temporal things according to
its whole self, but deny that it follows that it does not exist always. If according to its whole,
always-abiding self it is before temporal things, nothing prevents it from existing always. As it is
without beginning it is before temporal things; as it is without end it is also after temporal
things. Will you deny that eternity is before temporal things future in time, and that according to
its whole self? Does it therefore follow that eternity cannot be affirmed to exist always? Thus I
bind you with your own unhappy subtlety. Yet here the crude error of these Doctors shines forth.
Because eternity is a thing's duration, they fail to observe that it is called wholly simultaneous
insofar as no succession, no earlier and later, is found in the thing it measures. God's very being
admits no mutation. God's volition itself undergoes no succession, not merely because no volition is
subject to motion, but because no volition in God succeeds another; likewise, no cognition either
has succession in itself or succeeds another cognition. God and whatever is in God is wholly
simultaneous without any succession, abiding from eternity to eternity. This eternal permanence of
God coexists with all things succeeding through every change of things. Thus it is true that God
and whatever is in God has endured throughout the span of the whole past world and from eternity
before it, and will endure throughout the span of the whole future world and forever afterward.
These men, however, imagine that all things succeeding through intervals of time are, because of
eternity's indivisible condition, reduced to a single instant in which they exist from eternity—
namely, in eternity.

II. Indeed, according to your opinion eternity belongs as adequately to creatures as to God himself,
and this according to their real being in eternity. For just as God had no beginning and will have
no end, so also, according to your opinion, the real being of things in eternity had no beginning of
duration and will have no end.

III. This distinction is excessively absurd. You maintain not only that temporal things coexist
with eternity, which they indeed do while each exists in its own time, but that they coexist with it
from eternity. If they coexist with eternity from eternity, they therefore exist from eternity, at
least in eternity. What distinction do you invent between being from eternity and existing from
eternity? When you labor to explain their difference, you return to professing that eternity makes
temporal things eternally present to itself. You ought to add: according to their real being. They
are therefore present to eternity from eternity according to their real being. This is objected as
more than sufficiently absurd, and you will wish to remove its absurdity, even though it is from
eternity that they become eternally present to it—a point over which no controversy had been moved,
though it could rightly have been. For nothing is made by God under the notion of eternity. He works
all things according to the counsel of his will; he is nowhere said to work all things according to
his eternity. Indeed, because God's immensity is an active notion—namely according to quantity of
power—it is far better suited to signify the causation of things than eternity, for which

[Book I, printed p. 83; BSB canvas 00109]

you have no account whatsoever on this side, since it contributes nothing to establishing your
fable about the existence of things in eternity. Rather, once a proper comparison is made, it serves
to overturn and utterly demolish it.

VALENCIA.

That which begins to exist in eternity does not exist from eternity in eternity. But all created
things begin to exist in eternity. Therefore no created thing exists from eternity in eternity.

NAVARRETE.

If things coexisted with eternity according to their proper measures, they would also exist
absolutely in eternity. But the ground of this coexistence is taken from the infinite manner of
measuring already explained.

RESPONSE.

Will you then deny that things coexist with eternity according to their proper measures? This
cannot be denied, for when things exist according to their proper measures and eternity itself also
exists, eternity must coexist with things existing according to their proper measures, and things
according to their proper measures must coexist with eternity. I have often taught that the ground
from which you derive this coexistence of yours is merely invented. It is most certain that
eternity's manner of measuring is no more infinite in its own class than the manner of measuring
belonging to immensity is infinite in its class. Yet you do not maintain that all things exist
everywhere, or exist at all, in God's immensity, though you maintain that they both exist and always
exist in God's eternity.

VALENCIA.

What is not verified of things with respect to the measure of their proper duration is not verified
of them with respect to eternity's *now* itself. But that things exist from eternity as present to
God is not verified of things with respect to the measure of their proper duration. Therefore it is
not verified with respect to eternity's *now* either. The major is proved: a common measure does not
attain things except according to the objective conditions which belong to the things in themselves;
otherwise eternity, in attaining things, would change their conditions.

NAVARRETE.

The major is altogether false, as is evident from the examples advanced. To the proof it is
answered that it is one thing for temporal things, even according to their proper measures, to be
attained by eternity, and another for them to exist under their proper measures in eternity. The
first is true, because those proper measures are materially denoted when things are said to be
attained by eternity. The second is false, because things in eternity follow only the nature of
that indivisible measure, and insofar as they are measurable they exist simultaneously, possess the
same coexistence, and neither is prior to the other. Similarly, when the intellect conceives past
and future time as one, the past part of time is not prior to the future part according to its mode
of existing in the intellect.

RESPONSE.

We have already honored the examples you advanced with their due consideration. For temporal things
to exist in eternity is altogether nothing other than for them to be attained by eternity. You have
already taught how they are said to exist in eternity: through a certain elevation, not passively
and intrinsically received, but through a certain containment, insofar as eternity extends itself to
all things future in time. Therefore, for things under their proper measures to be attained by
eternity is nothing other than for things under their proper measures to exist in eternity. If
eternity and the things measured by eternity are simultaneous and neither is prior to the other,
why does eternity not belong adequately to things just as it does to God himself? Yet you judged
this must be denied in your response to Valencia's first argument and first confirmation. Temporal
things existing in time also exist in eternity, for they coexist with it and are measured by it.
But while they exist in time, do you want them also to follow the nature of eternity as an
indivisible measure, and thus to exist simultaneously, neither being prior to the other? I do not
doubt that all past, present, and future things are simultaneously understood by the divine
intellect and thus exist simultaneously before God in known being. Nor do I doubt that they also
exist simultaneously before God in willed being. But this is to exist in eternity solely according
to intentional, not real, being. These illustrations tend as if you wished the existence of things
in eternity to proceed only according to intentional being. But since you judge that they also
exist in eternity according to real being, such illustrations are wholly irrelevant, for they teach
only how all things can exist successively and simultaneously according to intentional being.

NAVARRETE.

From what has been said some arguments which Suárez advances in book 1, chapter 7, *On Knowledge of
Future Things*, to prove that things do not exist successively in eternity are easily answered.
There he intends to prove that representing the entire breadth of time as one present day occurs
rather through the intellect's apprehension than subsists in reality; hence he concedes that things
coexist successively with God. But this author destroys eternity's proper character, which is
essentially to be an indivisible measure, as is clear from Dionysius's definition. It ought rather
to be said that if succession is granted in eternity, it arises from our apprehension attaining
eternity imperfectly after the manner of successive things.

RESPONSE.

I acknowledge that this reasoning of Suárez has no probative force, but again I deny that eternity's
proper character is destroyed when things are held to coexist with it successively. Its
indivisibility is in no way violated, provided we subject neither God himself, nor divine volitions,
nor divine cognitions to any mutation or succession. For this reason alone the eternal duration of
all these is called indivisible and is reckoned as a

[Book I, printed p. 84; BSB canvas 00110]

standing *now*, indeed as a permanent *now* from eternity and forever. The successive coexistence
with a fixed stake of waters successively flowing past it introduces no divisibility or succession
into the stake itself. Durandus long ago illustrated the successive coexistence of things with God
by this familiar analogy, but Navarrete never recalls it for consideration, lest he reveal his
deficiency in some measure to his readers.

NAVARRETE.

See Anselm, *On the Fall of the Devil*, final chapter, and *Why God Became Man*, chapter 18; Bede,
book 1 of *Various Questions*, question 17; Hugh of St. Victor in his *Summa of Sentences*, tract 1,
chapter 12; Caterinus's short work *On Foreknowledge*; and Abulensis on Joshua 11, questions 68 and

RESPONSE.

NAVARRETE.

Suárez nevertheless objects from Thomas, question 17, article 7, who says that if there were no
eternal intellect, no created truth would be eternal. Created truths, he says, are therefore eternal
because they exist objectively in the divine intellect. It is answered that, however much created
truths are really present in eternity, they are called present according to objective being in the
divine intellect, because even a present truth formally possesses the character of truth insofar as
it exists objectively in the intellect. Thomas therefore denotes only the formal mode of existence
of truths themselves. Thus truths agree with other things in their mode of existing really in
eternity, but differ in their proper mode, namely that of existing objectively. Hence we concede
that if, per impossibile, the measure of eternity were given in the divine being but there were no
intellection measured by eternity, truths would not be said to exist really in eternity according
to their proper formalities. Nevertheless, other things which are not formally called such because
they possess objective being in the intellect would then exist really in the eternity which
measured the divine being. It is certainly astonishing that Suárez adduces in his favor St. Thomas,
question 87, article 4, where he says: “Future things cannot indeed be known in themselves except by
God, to whom they are also present while they are future in the course of events, insofar as his
eternal gaze is borne simultaneously over the whole course of time.” What could be said more clearly
in favor of our opinion? What is it for God to know future things in themselves except for his
cognition to be actually terminated at such future things in themselves, and not only in the Word
or in the divine intellect? When he adds, “insofar as his eternal gaze is borne simultaneously over
the whole course of time,” this “insofar as” does not state a diminishing condition but explains the
ground of intuitive knowledge. Thus it does not denote the ground of formally existing in eternity,
nor is it taken reduplicatively in relation to eternity, but specifically, even if it denotes the
formality of intuitive cognition.

RESPONSE.

The Suarezian reasoning is by no means persuasive to me. Nevertheless, unless I am mistaken, an
argument of this kind can also be restored on this side from Aquinas. For if things existed from
eternity in eternity according to real being, it must certainly be admitted that some created truth
existed from eternity according to its real, not merely objective, being. Yet according to Aquinas
no created truth is eternal. Again, Aquinas acknowledges no eternity even of universals except that
which is in the intellect, in I, question 16, article 7, reply 2. Finally, in question 14, article 8,
Aquinas professes that it was not in God's knowledge that things existed from eternity. But
according to your interpretation of Aquinas it must be conceded that it was in God's knowledge that
things existed from eternity, at least in eternity. When you ask what it is for God to know future
things in themselves other than for his cognition to be actually terminated at such future things
in themselves, I acknowledge that it is nothing else. Yet we say that God's cognition is terminated
at future things in themselves not as existing in eternity but as existing in time, as what follows
indicates when he says, “insofar as his eternal gaze is borne simultaneously over the whole course
of time.” He does not say, “insofar as his eternal gaze is borne over the whole of eternity.” Just as
his eternal gaze is borne simultaneously over the whole course of time, it is also borne
simultaneously over all things existing throughout the entire course of time. If it is asked how
both the whole course of time and all things distributed according to the various differences of
time become present to God from eternity, nothing can truthfully be answered except that they were
present to GOD in willed being—that is, solely according to their intentional and not their real
being.

Nor do we say that “insofar as” denotes a diminishing condition, but rather a ground—as you rightly
say, provided you understand a formal ground—of intuitive knowledge, though a partial and not an
integral ground. The formal object of intuitive cognition before God is not present things alone,
but future and past things as well as present ones. This is also evident because God is said to
behold future things by beholding the whole course of time. By beholding the whole course of time,
God sees things as future for one definite part of time, as present for another, and finally as past
for a third. Thus these statements concern the formal ground. But if the issue is not the formal
but the radical ground of this cognition—which, I admit, is called intuitive by reason of the
condition of its object—Aquinas openly professes that God sees all these things not in themselves
but in himself. For he says in I, question 14, article 5, in the body: “God sees himself in himself,
because he sees himself through his essence. But he sees things other than himself not in
themselves, but in himself, insofar as his essence contains the likeness of things other than
itself.” Add the disjunctive phrase from Aquinas himself in the same place, article 11, in the body:
“or as their active principle.” Let us now return to Penottus.
