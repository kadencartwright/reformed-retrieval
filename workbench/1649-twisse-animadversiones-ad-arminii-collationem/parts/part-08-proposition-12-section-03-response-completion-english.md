Junius first acknowledged a meritorious cause of this act outside God; afterward he seemed to deny
that any cause of it was given other than God’s will. Yet immediately afterward he professes that
iniquity is the cause why a man is distant from God and God from him, and no one doubts that this
occurs through the denial of blessedness. And beyond doubt, a man’s iniquity is the cause of every
punishment inflicted by God upon human beings. Afterward he returns to his earlier statement, in
which he had said that sin is indeed a cause of this act, but not its only cause. Without doubt,
whatever is denied to a man by way of punishment—as I professed some time ago—has a meritorious
cause. Yet it by no means follows from this that the decree to punish in this way requires or can
admit a meritorious cause. For the conferral of salvation by way of reward presupposes good works
as a cause—not indeed a meritorious but a dispositive cause. Nevertheless, it by no means follows
from this that the decree to confer salvation also presupposes good works as its dispositive
cause; for then the election of human beings would have been made from works. Nevertheless, it
cannot be denied that God can deny blessedness by his mere good pleasure to whomever he wills. For
according to Arminius, he can annihilate even the holiest person; how much more can he deprive that
person of supernatural blessedness? And if it is from God’s mere will that you do not live in the
age of Adam and Methuselah, is it not from God’s mere will that you do not live eternally? Even
those who do not exist could both exist and be blessed; yet in fact he denies them both existence
and blessedness, and that by his mere good pleasure. But let us return to Arminius.

1. “The act of the divine good pleasure by which God determines to deny supernatural blessedness
to a particular man arises from a meritorious cause, which is sin,” he says. But we reject that
proposition as utterly false. We acknowledge no cause of the divine will with respect to its act
of willing, of divine predestination with respect to its act of predestining, or of divine
reprobation with respect to its act of reprobating. We acknowledge a cause only with respect to
the things prepared by the will of God as it predestines and reprobates, in accordance with the
most amply attested doctrine of Aquinas and the most celebrated Scholastics, as we have discussed
in particular digressions in their proper places. The reasons are many. The act of the divine good
pleasure is something eternal, whereas sin is something temporal; but the temporal cannot be the
cause of the eternal. Again, the act of the divine good pleasure is an immanent act and
consequently identical with God himself. Therefore to seek a cause of the divine act outside God
is the same as seeking a cause of God himself outside God. Again, if sin were the cause of the
decree to deny supernatural blessedness, it would altogether presuppose the decree to permit sin.
The permission of sin would therefore be intended by God before the denial of blessedness;
consequently, the permission of sin would assume the character of an end and the denial of
blessedness would take on the character of a means to that end. For priority and posteriority have
no place in intention except on account of the corresponding relations of an end and a means to
the end. But blessedness is by no means denied for the end that sin might be permitted. It is much
more plausible that sin is permitted for the end that blessedness might be denied to someone,
although it by no means pleases me to formulate the matter in that way. But let Arminius prevail by
even a single argument and win his case. Let us therefore examine the reason he appended. “The
reason for the major,” he says, “is that this denial cannot be considered otherwise than as
punishment; but sin necessarily precedes it as its proper cause according to the manner of merit.”
Arminius concludes everything by his customary non sequitur. Sin, I admit, precedes the denial of
blessedness, but it does not precede the act of the divine good pleasure by which God determines
to deny blessedness—and that alone was what Arminius had to prove. In the same way, good works
precede the conferral of salvation. Yet far be it from you to infer from this—as you could have
inferred with equal force of consequence—that good works precede the decree to confer salvation,
or the act of the divine good pleasure by which God determines to confer salvation upon human
beings.

2. Arminius proceeds to confirm the major in this manner: “What God’s providence prepared for a man
under a condition, preterition does not deny him unless the condition has not been performed or
has been violated.” There would not be even the appearance of plausibility to commend this reason,
were it not for verbal ambiguities somewhat favorable to the Arminian cause. First, preparation is
either eternal or temporal. Temporal preparation can depend upon the performance of the condition;
eternal preparation cannot. Therefore, if he is speaking of eternal preparation, the sense of the
antecedent must be this: “What God’s providence prepared to be conferred upon a man under a
condition”—that is, the conferral of the decreed good depends upon the condition, but the decree to
confer it does not. Again, preterition is either eternal or temporal. Temporal preterition is the
denial of grace; eternal preterition is the decree to deny either grace, glory, or both. But the
decree does not deny; it decrees to deny at the appointed time. The proposition, freed from
ambiguities, therefore stands thus: “What God’s providence prepared to be conferred upon a man
under a condition, this he does not deny him unless the condition has not been performed or has
been violated.” We say that this proposition is true. But if it concerns the decree to deny, it
stands thus: “What God’s providence prepared to be conferred upon a man under a condition, this he
does not decree to deny him unless the condition has not been performed or has been violated.” If
the nonperformance of the condition is posited as the cause of the decree, it is a most false

proposition; but if it is posited as the cause of the denial to be made in time, it is most true
according to the present course of divine providence. For God does not deny blessedness to created
human beings except on account of sin. Yet it by no means follows from this that the decree to deny
was also made on account of sins. That alone was what Arminius had to prove; he always attempts its
proof, but always meets an unhappy outcome. I acknowledge that the passage in Isaiah indicates
that God would not have deserted the Jews unless they had merited it by their iniquity; neither
does Junius say otherwise. The prophet is speaking of desertion by God with respect to their
protection from temporal evil. We say that the same truth also holds with respect to their
protection from eternal evil, that is, from damnation. For he condemns no one except on account of
sin. From this it follows, I admit, that sin is a cause of damnation, but it by no means follows
that sin is the cause of the decree to damn. For good works too are dispositive causes with
respect to the conferral of salvation, but they are not dispositive causes with respect to the
decree to confer salvation. Meanwhile there is still another desertion, with respect to their
protection from sin. But it is utterly false that sin is the cause of this desertion, since it
should instead be held to be a consequence of that desertion. Arminius tacks on a proof of the
reason which he had added to confirm the major. But we did not at all require a proof of it; for we
acknowledge that supernatural blessedness is denied by way of punishment. We profess, however,
that the inference of the reason is altogether invalid. It does not follow: the denial of
blessedness, since it occurs by way of punishment, presupposes sin; therefore the decree to deny
blessedness presupposes sin. For the conferral of salvation too, since it occurs by way of reward,
presupposes the obedience of good works. But it does not follow from this that the decree to confer
salvation therefore presupposes good works, even in God’s consideration. For then election would
manifestly be established as arising from works, in spite of the Apostle. But Arminius wishes to
display his scholastic ability in proving things which need no proof. In proving things which
ought to be supported by reason, however, he utterly fails—or rather, he does not even undertake
the task, either because he does not perceive the invalid inference at all, because he hopes it can
remain hidden from and deceive an unskilled reader, or finally because he utterly despairs of
providing another reason to support it. We do not altogether contend that the denial of blessedness
can be considered otherwise than by way of punishment, nor do we deny that it is prepared to be
inflicted upon none but sinners. It follows from this that sin indeed precedes its denial. But no
rack can wring from this the conclusion that sin also precedes the decree to deny. For, as I have
already said, good works too precede the conferral of salvation, since it is conferred by way of
reward; but it by no means follows from this that good works also precede the decree to confer
salvation.

“My syllogism did not infer from a particular,” he says. Indeed, I say, it did infer from a
particular. Because God did not have a meritorious cause of that negative act, you inferred from
this that God had no cause at all of that negative act. When you add that the act exists only from
that cause, and then serve up the same stale dish again by saying that the act would not have
existed if that cause had not existed, these assertions contend only that sin is a cause *sine qua
non*. But causes of this kind are never efficacious causes in themselves, nor do they have any
consequent effect at all unless another, more efficacious—or rather, simply efficacious—cause is
added. When you say that the particle “alone” does not serve to exclude God’s will, you act
craftily, as is your custom. Certainly the nature of a meritorious cause requires another cause in
addition, one which weighs the merit and inflicts punishment in proportion to it. This is so even
among human judges who are obliged before God to treat people according to their deserts and do
not have it within their power to absolve guilty people according to their own good pleasure.
Those who are bound by office to punish the guilty are said, with respect to the nature of their
act, to derive its cause from the sins of the guilty, and not from their own will or good pleasure.
But where it lies within someone’s discretion to punish or pardon sin according to his good
pleasure, whenever he punishes sins, not only the sin of the person punished but also the good
pleasure of the one who punishes comes into consideration as a cause of no less weight. Indeed, so
that you may see how easily Arminius is accustomed to contradict himself: at times he maintains
that the cause why someone is punished is not so much the guilty person’s sin as the will of
whatever judge there may be. For thus he discusses reprobation in Section 6, number 2: “Nor will
reprobation therefore depend upon human beings, if God reprobates no one unless that person has
merited reprobation and desertion.” And please notice the reason which he appends: “For an effect
cannot be said to depend upon a cause which, when posited, does not produce the effect. But all
human beings are sinners, and the elect are not deserted. Indeed, sin is not the cause of
desertion unless the Judge’s condemnatory sentence intervenes, by which sin is determined to be
punished according to its demerit. And who does not know that a sentence depends not upon the
accused but upon the Judge, even if the accused has merited that sentence by his own act, without
which the Judge could neither conceive, pronounce, nor execute the sentence?” Indeed, in Section 4
of the same place he professes that, notwithstanding a meritorious cause given on the human side,
God’s mere good pleasure can be called the cause of desertion. “Scripture,” he says, “teaches
everywhere that every desertion occurs on account of sin”; and although this is so, it nevertheless
does not follow from it that God’s mere good pleasure is not a cause of desertion. He relies upon
the reason which he next proposes in these words: “For God is free either to desert or not to
desert a sinner who has merited desertion,” and so forth. But let these arguments of Arminius be
transferred, if you please, to the decree to damn, where they have the greatest force of all; it
will readily become apparent how frivolous and clumsy they were. How much less will they prevail
with respect to the decree not to elect?
