### QUESTION VIII

#### Who was that Darius in whose second year the temple is said to have begun to be rebuilt and in whose sixth year it was completed, in Ezra 4:24 and 6:15?

Answer. Almost all the ancient Hebrew, Greek, and Latin writers take him to
be Darius Hystaspes. But after Sulpicius Severus alone, Joseph Scaliger
drew many modern writers, especially in the Reformed Church, into a new
opinion—more by his own authority than by arguments which, as it seems to us,
by no means outweigh the foundations of the ancient opinion. Sethus Calvisius,
Scaliger’s amanuensis, arranged those arguments in chapter 42 of his
*Isagoge* and polished them as far as he could. Junius, Rainolds, and Emmius
follow this side; Nisbet, Howe, and the English annotators in both editions
also incline toward it, but with such hesitation and uncertainty that they
seem drawn there against their convictions. Diodati passes over this quite
serious doubt entirely, apparently uncertain what should be determined. We,
with Capellus, Ussher, Petavius, and Henricus Philippi, embrace the ancient
and common opinion, chiefly because of the arguments that may be seen in the
English annotations on Ezra 4:7 and in other authors of the opposing party,
without an adequate answer. We shall present some of them.

> Marginalia, printed p. 159: *Scaliger, the author among us of the new
> opinion*.

First, from Cyrus’s second year, when the temple was founded, to the sixth
year of Darius Nothus, when they hold it was completed, there are, as they
admit, 112 years. During this entire period—and for some time both before and
after—Zerubbabel administered the principate and Joshua the high priesthood.
In no age has so long a period of administration in either church or
commonwealth been heard of in the case of any mortal.

> Marginalia, printed p. 159: *Against Scaliger, it is proved that this Darius
> was not Nothus*.

Calvisius’s answer comes down to this: the same absurdity presses our opinion.
For although no one reckons more than twenty-two years from the second year
of Cyrus’s monarchy to the sixth of Darius Hystaspes, and our opinion is
therefore free enough from difficulty on this side, they nevertheless say
that we are forced to admit in the cases of Nehemiah, Ezra, and Sanballat the
same thing they admit in the cases of Zerubbabel and Joshua. For they hold
that Nehemiah lived until the time of Alexander the Great because in chapter
12, verses 11 and 21, he names the high priest Jaddua, who certainly lived at
Jerusalem in Alexander’s days. Yet from the twentieth year of Artaxerxes
Longimanus to Alexander’s first year are 114 years.

Answer. First, one difficulty does not solve an argument. Arguments *ad
hominem* alone establish no opinion. Although they wrap us in thorns, they do
not thereby extricate themselves.

Second, they permit us to affirm that the Artaxerxes under whom Nehemiah was
appointed governor of Judea was Mnemon, not Longimanus. But from the twentieth
year of Longimanus to the twentieth year of Mnemon are at least sixty years,
which must be subtracted from the alleged longevity of Ezra, Nehemiah, and
Sanballat according to their position.

Third, the aged Nehemiah could have seen Jaddua as a young man. Our opponents
hold that Joshua entered the high priesthood contrary to the law when barely
twenty years old. But suppose Jaddua became high priest according to the law
in his thirtieth year and was ninety in Alexander’s time, an age not rare
among high priests. On these assumptions Nehemiah could have died sixty years
before Alexander’s time, and even before the death of Artaxerxes Mnemon,
although he had lived for a time with Jaddua.

> Marginalia, printed p. 159: *Concerning Nehemiah’s age*.

Fourth, the extraordinary longevity of Nehemiah rests on this one foundation:
that he himself wrote the words cited from his twelfth chapter. But who will
prove this denied assertion? Although Deuteronomy was written by Moses and
the books of Joshua and Jeremiah by Joshua and Jeremiah, in all three there
are various verses that the named authors certainly could not have written.
The eight verses of Deuteronomy’s last chapter, the last five in Joshua, and
four in Jeremiah were written after the authors of the books had died. What
if we deny that any verse in the whole book was written by Nehemiah? Would
not the entire argument then collapse? How reasonably this may be denied can
be seen in the English annotations on Nehemiah 1:1.

As for Ezra,

[Printed p. 160 | IA leaf 190 | access PDF 190 | OCR object 189]

Calvisius holds that he lived near the end of the Persian Empire because Ezra
10:6 says that he entered the
room of Johanan, father of Jaddua, who was high priest in the time of
Alexander the Great.

Answer. At that time Johanan was a very young man, for his grandfather
Eliashib was still serving as high priest, and the action of Ezra mentioned
there was his last recorded in Scripture. Given the ages of many high
priests, more than a hundred years could easily have elapsed from Johanan’s
youth to Jaddua’s old age. Further, it can be denied that Johanan son of
Eliashib was Jaddua’s father, for his father was Jonathan son of Joiada and
grandson of Eliashib, in Nehemiah 12:10.

But after Broughton, Nisbet presses this argument, especially from its earlier
limit. “Ezra,” they say, “was a son of Seraiah, according to Ezra 7:1, but
Seraiah was killed by Nebuchadnezzar before the captivity, according to 2
Kings 25:18. Ezra was therefore born before Jerusalem was destroyed and must
have been more than two hundred years old in the seventh year of Artaxerxes
Mnemon, when he returned to Jerusalem.”

Answer. First, sixty years must be cut from the end of this calculation,
namely the years that elapsed from the seventh of Longimanus to the seventh
of Mnemon. For, as we shall shortly see, the Artaxerxes under whom Ezra and
Nehemiah came to Jerusalem was Longimanus, not Mnemon. Second, much must be
shaved from the beginning of the calculation. As Rainolds rightly says, Ezra
was not the immediate son of Seraiah. Even granting that the Seraiah mentioned
in Ezra 7 was the high priest whom Nebuchadnezzar killed—which can
nevertheless be doubted, since Ezra and Nehemiah also name several Seraiahs
from the priestly family—Ezra does not appear to have been his immediate son,
as is rightly observed on Ezra 7:1 in the latest English annotations against
the excessively rash annotation in the earlier edition. For why may not the
first verse of Ezra 7 be explained as everyone admits the third must be,
where Azariah is called the son of Meraioth although no fewer than seven men,
omitted there for brevity, intervene between Azariah and Meraioth in 1
Chronicles 6:7–9? Ezra’s purpose is to trace his lineage to Aaron and display
his ancestors in the priestly family. He accomplishes this by indicating that
he descended from Seraiah, which is true whether he was a son, grandson,
great-grandson, great-great-grandson, or a later descendant at any number of
degrees. But if, with Nisbet, you require Rainolds to provide some scriptural
foundation for applying this distinction to the present passage, we answer
that Nisbet may equally be required to provide some sound reason for denying
the application. The monstrous age of Ezra, far beyond two hundred years,
which Broughton and Nisbet press from one side, seems to us sufficient lawful
foundation for this fact; so does, on the other side, their equally absurd
shortening of the Persian Empire by at least a hundred years contrary to all
learned writers, ancient and modern, together with some delirious Jews. All
their arguments tend to that result. But besides these points, Scripture
seems to provide us an express foundation for applying that distinction to
the present passage, because elsewhere, whenever the genealogy of Seraiah the
high priest is described, his son is named as Jozadak, father of Joshua, but
never Ezra. From these things we conclude that nothing appears which proves
Ezra’s extraordinary age.

> Marginalia, printed p. 160: *And Ezra’s*.

As for Sanballat, in whom Scaliger and Calvisius place their chief hope, we
answer: assuming that Nehemiah came to Jerusalem in Mnemon’s twentieth
year—which is indeed the opinion of Scaliger and his followers—Sanballat’s
age will not be extraordinary. Suppose he was twenty in Mnemon’s twentieth
year. From then to Alexander’s fifth year are no more than fifty years; when
Sanballat died during the siege of Tyre, he was no older than seventy. But if
he opposed Nehemiah in Longimanus’s twentieth year, he must have been 130
during the siege of Tyre. Scaliger and Calvisius here defend longevity of this
kind as sufficiently ordinary in that age; but Capellus, Petavius, Torniellus,
and Henricus Philippi, rather than admit this, posit two Sanballats: one who
opposed Nehemiah in Longimanus’s twentieth year and whose son-in-law was
expelled from the temple by Nehemiah in the days of Darius—not Codomannus, as
Scaliger holds, but Nothus, according to Nehemiah 12:22. In his days Jaddua
could have entered the high priesthood and easily continued to Alexander’s
time. But they hold that the other Sanballat was the son or grandson of the
first. His daughter Nicaso was married to Manasseh, brother of Jaddua the high
priest; therefore Manasseh was expelled from the temple and the city not by
Nehemiah but by his brother Jaddua, as Josephus has it. For his sake the
second Sanballat obtained authorization from Alexander the Great and built
the Samaritan temple on Mount Gerizim.

> Marginalia, printed p. 160: *And Sanballat’s*; *Two Sanballats*.

For our opinion we argue, second, as before, from Haggai 2:4. It states that
some Jews saw the splendor of the first temple and compared the glory of the
second with it. But this could not have happened if the second temple had not
been built before the sixth year of Darius Nothus, for on that account those
who saw both temples must have lived more than 180 years. So great a
difficulty cannot be admitted. The passage of Haggai concerning those who saw
both temples cannot be evaded, as we said above. The contraction of the time
from Cyrus to Darius

Nothus, which either the Jews or Beroaldus—and after him Broughton and
Nisbet—contrive, is so absurd that all learned men abominate it.

We shall present only one other argument. From the laying of the foundations
of the second temple to the completion of the work there were no more than
forty-six years, as stated in John 2:20. Most scholars indeed reckon fewer,
but more cannot be admitted. But if the Darius under whom the second temple
was built was Nothus, not Hystaspes, that number of years from the beginning
to the end of the work will be more than doubled. For from Cyrus’s second
year to Nothus’s sixth are at least 112 years, and thus the temple would have
lain in ruins and the people abstained from the solemn worship of sacrifices
not merely for seventy years but for nearly three periods of seventy years.

> Marginalia, printed p. 161: *3*.

First, they argue as follows for the opposing opinion. The Darius under whom
the temple was completed stands between two men called Artaxerxes, as is
evident by comparing Ezra 4:23–24, 6:14–15, and 7:1. But Darius Hystaspes does
not stand between two men called Artaxerxes, for before him reigned Cambyses,
son of Cyrus, and after him his son Xerxes. Darius Nothus, however, had
Artaxerxes Longimanus before him and Artaxerxes Mnemon after him.

Answer. We deny the minor premise. Cambyses, whom Darius Hystaspes had before
him, is called Artaxerxes in Ezra 4:23, as we proved above; and after him,
though not immediately, he had Artaxerxes Longimanus, under whom Ezra and
Nehemiah departed for Jerusalem. Moreover, the Artaxerxeses who surround
Darius Nothus are not those whom Ezra mentions in the passages cited. The
former of these was Longimanus and the latter Mnemon; but the former of
Ezra’s two was Cambyses or some other enemy of the Jews who had hindered the
construction of the temple—which is certainly not said of Longimanus—and the
latter Artaxerxes was the one who sent Ezra and Nehemiah to Judea. This fits
Longimanus well, since he stood after Hystaspes with Xerxes between them, but
not Mnemon, who follows Nothus, as will soon appear.

> Marginalia, printed p. 161: *Calvisius’s first argument for Scaliger*.

Calvisius’s second argument is this. The interval from Cyrus’s first year to
the Darius under whom the temple was built was so long that Cyrus’s edict
concerning the release of the Jews’ captivity and the building of the temple
was forgotten by all the Persians and found in the archives only with
difficulty after a great search. But from Cyrus’s edict to Darius Hystaspes
there were very few years, no more than eleven. And Darius Hystaspes was one
of Cyrus’s counselors, who must have remembered well that recent edict about
building the Jews’ temple, since he himself had framed it together with Cyrus.

Answer. All this is sheer invention by Calvisius. From Cyrus’s first year as
monarch to Darius’s second there were nineteen years, and no Darius was among
Cyrus’s counselors. Indeed, in the Massagetan war, when Darius’s father
Hystaspes followed Cyrus’s camp, Darius himself was left behind in Persia as
a youth. During nineteen years in the courts of princes, how many matters
more relevant to the state than the temple of a foreign and exceedingly poor
Jewish nation are buried in deep oblivion—especially in turbulent times,
such as those in Persia from Cyrus’s first year to Darius’s second. Yet the
edict about the Jews had not been so completely forgotten that it was not
found with little trouble in the archives at Ecbatana in Media.

What Calvisius advances third for this opinion from Daniel’s weeks will be
weighed in its proper place. We answered his other supports in our first
argument.
