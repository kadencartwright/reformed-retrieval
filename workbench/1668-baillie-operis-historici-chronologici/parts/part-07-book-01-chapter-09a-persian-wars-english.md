## CHAPTER IX

### Containing the events of the fifth epoch, from the overthrow of the first
temple to the death of Alexander the Great

After divine indignation had crushed the remaining tribes of the Israelites,
who were obstinately rebellious against the most explicit warnings of
Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the other Prophets, and had buried them in the ruin of
Jerusalem and all the cities of Judaea, that most wretched people lay drowned
in the sea of divine fury throughout the whole appointed seventy-year term of
the divine good pleasure (as some maintain). The entire visible Church of God
on earth settled, exiled and captive, by the rivers of Babylon for no shorter
an interval. A great multitude of the people, who had fled from fear of the
Babylonian sword into the hiding places of neighboring nations, returned home
with the captives after Nebuchadnezzar's departure and settled in Judaea under
the provisional government of Gedaliah, the Chaldeans' trustee. But divine
wrath scattered all of these as well. When Gedaliah fell through Ishmael's
treachery, Johanan rescued the crowd from Ishmael's oppression and carried it
off to Egypt together with Jeremiah. All of it either perished there by plague
or sword, or was carried away to Babylon together with the Egyptians by
Nebuchadnezzar, so that Judaea, wholly empty of inhabitants, might enjoy the
Sabbath rest of a complete century.

This was a rare and unparalleled display of divine justice: the most fertile
region in the entire world, most productive of people and livestock, was so
thoroughly cursed by God that for so long a time it was trodden by no trace of
man or beast. Although situated at the heart of the earth and surrounded by
needy and rapacious peoples—Arabs, Idumaeans, Syrians, and others—it was left
untouched by any wild animal or bird, like a corpse infected with plague.

Throughout that time the public worship of God, or at least solemn
sacrifices, was interrupted. A portion of the people sometimes met with
Ezekiel and others for the word and prayers, but after the temple's overthrow
there was no altar, no offering, and no solemn ceremony until a new altar was
restored beside the foundations of the second temple.

Although the greater part of the people was battered by God's dreadful
punishments, it by no means returned to good sense. Some, mindful of God, hung
the lyres that consoled them upon Babylon's willows and kept their minds fixed
day and night upon desolate Zion's ruins. Yet divine mercy found most still
persevering in rebellion and impenitence even in the seventieth year, and
anticipated them with kindness.

The crimes of the Jews had indeed most deservedly provoked divine justice to
inflict the whole Iliad of calamities they endured; nevertheless, they had not
deserved such monstrous blows from any mortal. God used the Chaldeans' arms to
subdue his people's obstinacy in sin. Yet God did not thereby approve their
proud and cruel tyranny; indeed, he poured out the severest vengeance for his
overthrown temple and ruined people upon the Chaldeans' own heads.

While all the nations neighboring the Jews, and almost the entire known
world, groaned silently beneath Nebuchadnezzar's harsh yoke, and no one dared
even open his mouth against the tyrant, divine vengeance suddenly arrived.
The monarch of the world, erecting trophies in his Babylonian palaces over
conquered, plundered, and triumphed-over peoples, was cast down by God's right
hand—not merely into the lowest abyss of human misery, but below every human
condition into the order of beasts. Among brute animals he lived a wild life

yoke shaken from Jewish necks was placed upon Chaldean ones, never to be

experienced foreign masters as harsh and dreadful as those whom foreigners
had once endured in them. This happened as follows.

When Arbaces, governor of Media, and Belochus, governor of Babylonia, conspired
against Sardanapalus and, after many adverse battles, finally destroyed the
Assyrian monarchy together with Sardanapalus, Belochus and his descendants
reigned in Babylon, while Arbaces granted freedom to the Medes and neighboring
peoples. Hence the Assyrians soon recovered, if not the monarchy, at least a
fairly powerful kingdom, and established it in the city of Nineveh. There
Tiglath-pileser, Shalmaneser, Sennacherib, and Esarhaddon—harsh scourges of the
Israelites—reigned for many years. The Medes, tired of liberty, persuaded
Deioces, a descendant of Arbaces, to rule them. Eventually Cyaxares, son of
Phraortes and father of Astyages, contracted a marriage alliance with the
Babylonian Nabopolassar, giving his daughter Amyatis as wife to
Nebuchadnezzar, Nabopolassar's son. With their combined forces they besieged,
captured, and destroyed Nineveh together with the Assyrian kingdom, just as
the combined Babylonian and Median forces had previously overthrown the
Assyrian monarchy. With Nineveh destroyed, Babylon's fortunes greatly
increased until, under Nebuchadnezzar, they grew into a monarchy after Syria,
Arabia, and Egypt had been subdued, overflowing or at least overshadowing the
Median kingdom. That kingdom had been severely weakened at that time in
disastrous battles with Scythians who overran most of Asia; but after the
Scythians returned home and Nebuchadnezzar died, the Medes began to emerge
from eclipse once again. Their king Astyages (the Ahasuerus of Daniel) gave
his daughter Mandane in marriage to the Persian Cambyses and through her had
Cyrus as grandson. Under Cyrus's auspices, and those of his own son Cyaxares
II—who is Darius the Mede in Daniel—he fought many successful battles with
neighboring peoples.

After Astyages died, Cyaxares and Cyrus ventured not only to take up arms
against the Armenians, Lydians, and most peoples of lower Asia, but also to
attack Babylon in open warfare. In one of the battles the Babylonian himself
was killed. Authors disagree over who he was, a matter discussed more fully
below. Many report that Evil-merodach was killed in the second year after his
father's death and that his brother-in-law Neriglissar, whose wife was
Nebuchadnezzar's daughter and Evil-merodach's sister, seized the kingdom. They
believe he was the man Cyrus killed in battle. These authors say that
Neriglissar was succeeded by his son Laborosoarchod, Nebuchadnezzar's grandson
through his daughter, whom the Chaldean nobles killed after he had ruled for a
few months because of his depraved character. They placed Evil-merodach's son
Belshazzar upon his paternal and ancestral throne. Some years later, when
Babylon was taken by force, Cyrus deprived him too of life and empire. Thus
all Nebuchadnezzar's family was rooted out: not only his son but both his
grandsons, one descended through his son and the other through his daughter,
died violently, some at the hands of their own people and some at those of
enemies. The Babylonian nobility and the entire people who had helped destroy
Jerusalem, together with all their descendants whom the sword spared, were
delivered into wretched bondage—first to the Persians, then to the Greeks, and
finally to every tyrant of subsequent ages—without ever being manumitted. Thus
the Jews' blood and oppression were amply avenged, to the great praise of
divine justice before the eyes of every nation.

Although Cyrus played the principal role in overthrowing the Chaldean
monarchy, all his military action at the time occurred under the auspices of
his uncle Darius the Mede, or Cyaxares. He therefore voluntarily yielded the
Babylonian empire to him as long as he lived. When the Mede died two years
later and Cyrus received the scepter of the Median and Persian monarchy into
his own hands in the seventieth year of the Jewish captivity, one of his first
concerns was to carry out what the Prophet Isaiah had predicted about him by
name two hundred years before he was born. He promulgated an edict freeing the
Jews and, with thoroughly royal generosity, assisted every Jew who wished to
return home. He restored the temple and the city as well.

It is true that the devices of the Cuthaeans and others hostile to the Jews
delayed the building of the temple, the restoration of Jerusalem, and the
people's return to full liberty. Cyrus's edict was nevertheless the foundation
upon which God in his own time raised all these things in the face of every
plot by every malicious person. Although Cyrus presided over Persian and

Granted that many writers regard Herodotus's account of the Massagetan war as
fabulous, and Xenophon's more truthful account has Cyrus die peacefully at
Persepolis rather than be crucified by Tomyris, and buried at Pasargadae,

nevertheless, there was such an abundance of the greatest affairs in so vast
and new an empire, Cambyses had so depraved a character, and Persian courtiers
corrupted by Samaritan gifts had such perverse dispositions, that it is no
wonder the Jews' complaints over their enemies' wrongs found no path to
Cyrus's ears. Once he was removed and the reins of the entire empire came into
Cambyses's hands alone, it would have been close to a miracle if any sacred
thing had flourished under the rule of such a monster.

As soon as Cambyses ascended the throne, his foremost ambition was to reduce
the Egyptians to their old obedience. Although they had once contested the
supremacy with the Assyrians and all their other very powerful neighbors,
Nebuchadnezzar had devastated them so savagely that, despite frequent
attempts, they could never recover their former power. Cambyses therefore
dealt with them easily. After subduing Egypt, he next cast his eyes upon
Carthage. Since it could not be subdued without a fleet and naval battles and
the Persian naval forces were in Phoenician hands, while Carthage was a
Phoenician colony of Tyre and Sidon, those mother cities refused to provide
Cambyses their ships to destroy their offspring. Compelled by this obstacle,
he abandoned the expedition.

The restless character of ambitious men does not allow them to be still until
they cast themselves into the pit of an ignominious death. Cambyses desired to
subdue the Ethiopians. While preparing this campaign, he selected fifty
thousand infantry from his numerous army and dispatched them to plunder the
temple of Jupiter Ammon. Traveling through the sands, every one of them was
buried and entombed beneath heaps of sand when a wind arose. Cambyses
nevertheless continued into Ethiopia. His supplies failed en route; after the
horses and every pack animal had been eaten, the legions cast lots and chose a
fixed number each day to supply their comrades' provisions with their own
flesh. Forced by necessity, Cambyses returned to Egypt. But raging because his
teeth had been deprived not only of Carthaginians and Ammonians but also of
Ethiopians, he vented his spleen upon the wretched Egyptians. Not content with
devouring human beings, he declared war upon their gods: he broke many idols
and burned their shrines. With his own hand he inflicted a mortal wound in the
thigh of their greatest deity, the red calf Apis, from which the brute god soon
wasted away and died. Contrary to Persian law and custom he married both his
sisters; he put his only brother to death; and he kicked to death the sister
and wife who was already pregnant because she lamented her brother's
undeserved murder. The Magus Sphendadates was extremely like Smerdis,
Cambyses's brother, in the entire appearance of his body. Seeing that many
people greatly regretted the murdered Smerdis and that everyone utterly hated
Cambyses, he proclaimed himself Smerdis still alive and offered himself as
leader to those who wished to cast off Cambyses's naked tyranny. While
Cambyses hurried from Egypt to Persia to extinguish this spark in the ashes
before it burst into flame, his sword slipped from its sheath as he mounted
his horse and wounded the same part of his thigh in which he had previously
wounded Apis. He died of the wound a few days later.

The Magus, falsely posing as Smerdis, then sat on the Persian throne for
almost a full year and earned the reputation of an excellent prince because
his rule was gentle, kind, and wholly unlike Cambyses's. But as truth emerged
in time, and many were now certain that the man reigning was not Cyrus's son
Smerdis but an impostor Magus, seven of Persia's leading men conspired and
stabbed the Magus to death. Darius son of Hystaspes was chosen from among them
to succeed to the empire. The Jews found him benevolent toward them. Whether
for the reasons recently offered by Ussher or those once given by Josephus, we
shall consider in the proper place. Ussher thinks Darius Hystaspes was
Esther's husband and bestowed benefits upon the Jews because of his wife. In
the questions, however, we shall weigh the foundation of this conjecture.

hands. At that time Darius followed Cyrus's footsteps in everything so as to
gain popular favor for his new and unexpected rule. Emulating Cyrus in this
matter too, he encouraged the Jews with a new rescript to

My mind will not let me pass over Josephus's great error (as it appears to
you) in *Antiquities* 20.4, which some recent writers borrow from him: namely,
that under this edict of Darius Zerubbabel and Joshua brought with them from
Babylon into Judaea—in addition to those who had already returned under
Cyrus—no fewer than forty times a hundred thousand, six hundred and
twenty-eight thousand men, and these from the tribes of Judah and Benjamin
alone, an utterly incredible total. I would have thought this a typographical
error had our Genevan Roverian printing not been corrected according to the
Palatine Greek edition made from the best manuscripts, and had not both the
very learned Latin translator Gelenius expressed the number explicitly as
four million six hundred and twenty-eight thousand, and the Greek text itself
given its numbers not only in letters but also expanded in a sum as follows:
*μυριάδες. υ. ξ. β. κ. ὀκτακόσιοι* (“myriads … eight hundred”). So vast was
this multitude that we cannot credit any human authority for it. Indeed, when
we examine Josephus's passage more closely, we find other things that, if they
do not demonstrate the author's drowsy inattention, at least make him highly
suspect: the number of both servants and camels returning under Darius's edict
is exactly identical, down to the last one, with that of those who had returned
some years before under Cyrus's edict. Moreover, to so vast a multitude of men
he adds only 40,742 children and women, although children and women usually
far outnumber men.

He further says that these later returnees not only laid the temple's
foundations but also built its altar, in the second year and second month after
their return. There was no need to repeat everything already done in the
first return under Zerubbabel. Far from believing Josephus that so many
returned under Darius's edict—when all desolate Judaea at that time could not
have fed even a tenth of them—it appears that no one at all migrated from
Babylon into Judaea then, so far as can be seen from Ezra, Nehemiah, or Esther,
who described that century's Jewish affairs much more accurately and reliably
than Josephus.

Just as Darius's benevolence shone upon Jerusalem through divine goodness, so
through divine vengeance for Zion his severity blazed against Babylon, which
he afflicted with every calamity of war and finally reduced to a village.

Noting throughout the reigns of Cambyses and the Magi, and even at the
beginning of Darius's, the great confusion in Persian government, the
Babylonians raised their spirits in hope of recovering their former freedom
and empire. They fortified their city with such skill and force that for
almost two full years they scorned Darius besieging it with his entire army.
But when Zopyrus's craft and treachery opened access to the city after a great
slaughter of those encountered, Darius leveled to the ground the walls—a
wonder of the world—which Cyrus had spared. He crucified three thousand of the
leading citizens. Before the siege they themselves had slaughtered so many of
their own women that Darius had to gather fifty thousand women from nearby
places as wives for the surviving men.

With Babylon reduced from a city to a village, Darius's next concern was to
subdue the Scythians. Darius ruled far and wide from India to Thrace and from
the Caucasus to the Ocean, but the man's boundless desire refused to be
confined within those limits. A great ambition to subdue the Scythians seized
him. He did not dare attack the eastern Massagetae, who had destroyed Cyrus's
life and army, but took up arms against the western Scythians—today the
Perekop Tatars—who lived beyond the Euxine Sea and the Danube. He constructed
two bridges of wooden rafts, covering with one the four-stade-wide Thracian
Bosporus and with the other the Danube. There were then no cities among the
Scythians; all their movable dwellings were in tents. They treated the
Persians' arms as nothing, moved camp into the country's interior, and left
Darius empty fields. After many days of fruitless wandering, they compelled
the Persians, almost dead from lack of provisions, to flee back to the bridges
over the Ister in great fear and no less danger. Not a Persian would have
escaped the Scythians' bloody hands had they not hurried across the bridges;
but divine goodness willed to reward Darius's beneficence toward the Jews and
vengeance upon the Babylonians by delivering him from this immense and
immediate danger.

How it happens that James Ussher—usually accurate in much smaller matters—does
not mention even in a word either this Babylonian or Scythian war of Darius,
both described at length by Herodotus and other ancient writers, I cannot
conjecture.

power of both Greeks and Persians. The Greeks had led their greatest colonies

Although the cities of the Ionians in Asia and the islands were either wholly
free or governed by tyrants under their own laws, Persian power in Asia was so
great—especially that of the neighboring Lydian satrap residing at Sardis—that
the Asiatic and island Greeks, partly from fear and partly from friendship,
reverenced the majesty of the Persian empire almost as subjects. A great
number of them followed Darius's camp as far as the Ister in the Scythian war
and were ordered to halt there to guard the bridges under Histiaeus, tyrant of
Miletus, while the Persians wandered through Scythia without sighting an enemy
or scarcely even an animal. The Scythian king Indathyrsus had come with his
forces to the Ister bridges against the Persians. He earnestly asked the Greeks
to break the bridges, promising with certainty that he would destroy every
Persian by famine or sword. Many Greeks opened willing ears to the Scythians,
both to recover and secure their liberty from Persian fear. But Histiaeus and
the other rulers of Greek cities feared that if the Persian monarchy fell,
their thrones would fall too, since each people would claim liberty. They
therefore remained faithful to the Persians at a most opportune moment. Darius
then acknowledged the supreme service of Histiaeus and the Ionians, though on
bad advice he carried Histiaeus with him to Susa.

Darius heaped upon Histiaeus the greatest wealth and honors. Nevertheless,
because of every Greek's remarkable native longing for his homeland, the man
grew weary of the Persian court and desired above all to return home to
Miletus. Since he could accomplish that wish in no other way, he secretly used
his arts to stir seditious tumults among the Ionians against Persia. As he had
foreseen, Darius sent him to Ionia to settle them. Far from suppressing the
Ionian revolt so that he would not be dragged back to the Persian court far
from his homeland, he made it as prolonged a war as he could, using his
Athenian friends to draw the people into an armed alliance. The Ionian forces
were easily crushed by the much larger Persian ones, and whatever Greek
liberty survived in Asia was destroyed by Histiaeus's ill-fated undertakings.
Darius's anger against the Greeks did not end there. Conceiving an implacable
hatred especially for Athens, he assembled great forces by land and sea to
place all Greece under the yoke. Again he crossed the Thracian Bosporus on a
wooden bridge with more than one hundred thousand troops under his son-in-law
Mardonius, and at the same time sent a great fleet to the Hellespont. When the
camp was pitched by Mount Athos and the fleet stood nearby, a great storm from
heaven sank many ships and twenty thousand men. The Thracians also mauled the
Persian camp so badly in nocturnal raids that the terrified Mardonius hurried
the remnants of his forces back into Asia as quickly as he could.

These calamities inflamed Darius still more to destroy the Greeks. He
therefore assembled new and much larger forces and fell upon Attica with his
whole army, led by the Persian Datis and the Greeks Hippias and Demaratus. The
Athenians, abandoned by the other Greeks except for one thousand Plataeans,
opposed ten thousand of their own men under Miltiades to the Persians on the
field of Marathon. Greek courage shone so brightly there that the Persians,
although ten times more numerous, turned their backs and fled to the ships;
the entire fleet too, confounded by panic, hurried to Asia.

Darius's spirit did not falter. The flame of his anger against the Greeks,
chilled by these disasters, burst out into still greater fires.

Yet while he raged against the victorious Greeks, the Egyptians, raised to
hope of liberty by these Persian defeats, rebelled. Darius resolved to lead an
army against both.

To provide for his kingdom amid the uncertain chances of war, he thought it
wise to designate a successor before departing Susa. He had various sons by
seven wives. Artabazanes, the eldest by birth, thought the Persian throne
should belong to him after his father, but Darius preferred Xerxes although
younger, because he was the first son born to Darius as king, by Atossa,
Cyrus's daughter, whereas Artabazanes was his first son born as a private man.
This dangerous domestic dispute was settled very amicably. While Darius
immersed himself entirely in preparations for war against Greeks and
Egyptians, fate suddenly broke off all that this magnanimous man had begun
after a reign of thirty-six years. He deserved a better epitaph than those
Athenaeus and Strabo assign him.

In his reign the kings were expelled from Rome, and Pythagoras left his native
Samos and migrated to Croton in Magna Graecia—that is, Calabria in Italy. Some
exiles who fled from nearby Sybaris to Croton were not surrendered when the
Sybarites demanded them, because Pythagoras judged it unjust to hand over
refugees who sought in neighbors' justice an asylum from the violence of
tyrants at home.

About the same time the Carthaginian Hamilcar brought three hundred thousand
Africans and Spaniards into Sicily against the Syracusans; the tyrant Gelon

Persian hatred of the Greeks did not die with Darius. As soon as Xerxes
ascended the throne he followed his father's footsteps. His first concern was
to restore the Egyptians to their former yoke; Persia had little trouble with
that then-feeble nation, but found Greek hearts made of entirely different
metal.

After three years' preparation Xerxes reviewed enormous forces at Abydos on
the Hellespont's eastern shore. They continually increased until he reached
Thermopylae in Thessaly, as many thousands from neighboring nations joined
along the way.

Authors both ancient and modern vary greatly over the size of Xerxes' army.
Nevertheless, Ussher's figure from Herodotus and others—2,641,610 soldiers,
with the same number serving the camp and fleet, so that no fewer than
5,283,220 men followed Xerxes into Thessaly—does not seem entirely
improbable, although no one records even a third of that number ever following
a single camp, apart from Semiramis's army alone.

In that prodigious multitude no one equaled Xerxes in height or bodily beauty,
and when real danger came, no one was smaller in spirit. Among so many myriads
of men, none surpassed a woman, Artemisia, queen of Halicarnassus, in true
courage. Commanding her three triremes in the fiercest naval battle, she so
far surpassed every Persian in martial glory while Xerxes watched that the
king declared his men had become women and the woman a man.

It is no wonder that Xerxes, surrounded by so vast and well-equipped an army,
poured an offering to the Persians' sole god so that he might look kindly upon
him until he conquered Europe's remotest coasts, not only Greece's. Reports
that Xerxes' army drank the Scamander and rivers larger than it dry are wholly
credible. Yet, so that the divine right hand might give a brilliant proof of
its power in human affairs, this thrice-greatest and far most powerful army
ever to exist on earth was easily scattered and destroyed within a few months.

Xerxes wintered at Sardis in Lydia, three days' journey from the Aegean but
three months from Susa. Before he moved camp at the beginning of spring, the
sun failed one clear, cloudless morning until stars appeared—not (as Ussher
maintains) through the moon's interposition, the visible cause of ordinary
eclipses. Both this prodigy and the other were taken as evil omens and struck
many with such fear that Pythius the Lydian, who had generously feasted the
whole army at his expense and offered Xerxes an immense mass of gold, begged
that one of his five sons be excused from service. Xerxes granted his request,
but ordered the eldest son—the one the father wished to keep as comfort and
support in old age—cut in half and the halves displayed on either side of the
road as the army passed.

While camped beneath Trojan Ida, although offerings were poured to the heroes
buried nearby, nocturnal terror swept the army and they left very early next
morning. At Abydos, now the Dardanelles, Xerxes laid twin bridges where the
Hellespont is seven stades wide. The Thracian Bosporus between Byzantium and
Chalcedon—now Constantinople and Pera, or Scutari—where his father bridged it,
is only four stades, or half an Italian mile, wide.

### A more accurate account of the foregoing history

But let us not race through the beautiful history of the Persian monarchy so
dry-footed. Let us relate somewhat more fully from the Greek sources, the
fathers of ancient history, what historians of every nation commonly tell
from them, especially about Cyrus, Cambyses, Darius, and Xerxes.

hard to gather from either, but Herodotus seems likelier to me. Though on
Cyrus's death I think Xenophon chiefly should be followed, I shall relate the
common opinion from Herodotus.

After destroying the Assyrian empire, Arbaces did not call himself king but

acted otherwise, continuing the Assyrian monarchy at Babylon and compelling
as many southern and western peoples as possible beneath its yoke.

After some years congratulating one another on liberty, the Medes discovered

abandoned, although elsewhere he ranks among the foremost chorographers. He
places the Massagetae on the eastern side of the Caspian Sea, also east of
Media, and in this he has Ptolemy, the prince of geographers, as a follower;

Georgians now dwell. I would say Herodotus erred only in the name, calling
that river the Araxes which now, and even in ancient times, bears the name
Oxus, were it not that what he relates about the Araxes does not agree with
the Oxus either. The sources of the Oxus are indeed extremely far from the
sources of the Euphrates, Gyndes, and Tigris; its two mouths lie to the south
of the Massagetan nation, the Caspian Sea to its west, and the river Jaxartes
to its north.

on that response, he securely expected a late death at home in Media. But now,
turning aside to a neighboring Syrian city to have his wound treated, he heard
that the town where he then was bore the name Ecbatana. He no longer doubted
his present fate. He therefore summoned the foremost men of the kingdom,
revealed the previously concealed murder of his brother, and explained that
he had once seen Smerdis in a dream seated upon the throne, touching heaven
with the crown of his head. Because this dream had made him fear a rebellion
by his brother, he had ordered the murder whose execution he now deeply
regretted. The man presently lying about his brother's name, he said, was as
far as possible from being Smerdis, son of Cyrus; he knew very well that the
man was a Median Magus. He called down the most wretched death upon them all
if they allowed the audacious fraud of the vilest little man to go unpunished.
With these words he breathed out his unhappy spirit.

The Persians did not believe their dying king, for they supposed that what he
said had been fabricated out of jealousy toward his brother. Their disbelief
was especially strong because Praxaspes, the well-known perpetrator of the
murder, swore most solemnly that he had never laid hands on Smerdis. If he
confessed, he feared that, now Cambyses was dead, the Persians would avenge
the great crime he had committed. He therefore stoutly denied a deed he had
carried out with very few witnesses. Consequently, the Magus, passing himself
off as Cambyses' brother, ruled Persia in peace for a full seven months. At
last, however, the truth came out in this way.

Together with Cambyses' empire, his wives also passed to the Magus. Among them
was a daughter of Otanes, a man second to no Persian in power. Otanes observed
that, contrary to the custom of the Persian kings, the new emperor kept
himself shut up at home and never willingly granted the magnates access to
him. From this he suspected that the report could be true: it was not Cyrus's
Smerdis but a Magus who possessed the kingdom. To discover the truth, he sent
men to warn his daughter that, while she slept with the king, she should feel
the ears of the sleeping man; for he remembered that Cyrus, while alive, had
cut off the ears of Smerdis the Magus for a grave crime. Otanes' daughter
reported that the man who reigned had no ears.

Now certain of the truth, Otanes told the whole matter to five of the foremost
men. As they deliberated, a seventh joined them, Darius son of Hystaspes. When
Cyrus was about to cross the Araxes to meet his fate, he had seen Darius in a
dream ruling Asia. Upon waking, he summoned Hystaspes, who was in the camp,
told him what he had seen of his son in the dream, and ordered him to fly at
once to Persia and bring the young man to Cyrus as soon as he had arrested
him; but Cyrus's slaughter broke off these plans. The seven nobles in question
now deliberated about removing the Magus. Otanes wanted the affair postponed
for several days, but Darius declared that unless they undertook it
immediately he would make their plans public. He knew perfectly well that
seven men could not keep a secret of such importance suppressed for long, and
he was therefore unwilling to be forestalled by anyone in carrying the news
to the Magi.
On the way they learned that the Magi had won Praxaspes over and induced him,
by great gifts and greater promises, to persuade the people that the genuine
Smerdis of Cyrus was reigning and that Praxaspes had never inflicted any
violence upon Cyrus's son. Praxaspes promised to perform excellently. He
ordered the Persians to assemble and a high platform to be set up for him.
Ascending it, he declared to his many hearers, very far contrary to the Magi's
expectation, that he truly had slaughtered Smerdis, son of Cyrus, and that no
one other than the vilest Magus held the reins of the kingdom. With these
words he threw himself headfirst from the platform and died with a broken
neck.
This report added a spur to the running conspirators. They reached the palace
doors, and the majesty of the men opened a way through the guards. The
eunuchs, however, did not likewise admit them to the chamber. Suspecting the
truth, they called them to arms, but in vain; for the nobles easily drove the
half-men aside with their swords and broke into the chamber. There the Magus
brothers armed themselves, one seizing a bow and the other a spear. At close
quarters the bow was a useless weapon, so the man armed with it fled into the
inner rooms. Darius and Gobrias pursued him. Gobrias held the fugitive in his
arms; the Magus struggled, and the two were entangled in a mutual embrace.
Darius stood irresolute, afraid to strike lest he injure his friend. Then
Gobrias said, “What are you doing, Darius? Run the Magus through, even through
my side.” Encouraged by this command, Darius by good fortune transfixed the
Magus without touching Gobrias.

In the history of Darius, passing over the rest, we shall explain three things
especially worthy of remembrance: the capture of Babylon, his unhappy journey

within a few years convenient for rebellion. The man who now possessed the
kingdom was young and inexperienced in ruling; they, who had formerly been
accustomed to command others, should no longer endure the yoke. Commanders
were appointed, weapons manufactured, and the city prepared for a siege.
Everyone of the female sex within the walls was summoned into one place. The
mothers were driven into the fields; from the rest, each soldier chose one
woman for himself as a servant. The remaining crowd was strangled as useless
for war and harmful to the supply of grain.

At the report of rebellion, Darius appeared with his forces at the gates and
urged them to return to their former obedience. The rebels laughed at his

birth—that is, never. After the nineteen months during which Darius had sat
before the walls in vain, it happened in the camp that a certain mule among

prodigy with his own eyes, he was raised to hope of taking the city. In order
to bring that result about himself, he devised this plan: he cut off his own
ears and nose and inflicted many bloody wounds upon himself. In this condition
he presented himself to the king and explained that Babylon could be taken by
no means other than these wounds. After revealing his plan, he took to his

He told them who he was and what he had suffered from Darius's cruelty because
he had advised that the siege be abandoned; he promised to reveal every plan
of the king that he knew well.
The Babylonians believed him and placed a man of well-known courage over a
part of their forces. Accompanied by them, on the seventh day after his
arrival he destroyed one thousand Persians, just as he had agreed with Darius;
after ten days he killed two thousand, and after a further interval four
thousand. Having obtained three victories in succession, Zopyrus alone flew
from mouth to mouth among the people. The city's safety was thought to depend
upon this one man; the citadels, walls, and soldiers were placed in his hands
alone. Having thus obtained his hope and desire, he opened the Belidas gates
to the Persians at the appointed time. As soon as they had gained possession
of them, they mastered the conquered city. Darius the victor leveled the walls
of Babylon, one of the wonders of the world, to the ground; fastened three
thousand of the most illustrious citizens to crosses; and ordered the
neighboring peoples to supply fifty thousand women as wives to the rest. He
gave all Babylon to Zopyrus to rule for life, free of tribute, and often said
that he would rather have one sound and whole Zopyrus than a thousand
Babylons.

A single vanity of spirit moved Darius against the Scythians, though he put
forward a semblance of justice as a pretext for that vanity. Some centuries
before, the Scythians had crossed the Caucasus and descended into Media. Like
a most rapid torrent, they swept everything along with them through all Asia;
nothing could check their onset except the gifts of King Psammenitus, who met
them peaceably at the borders of Egypt. Darius wished to avenge this insult
inflicted upon Asia and especially upon Media, although there was no need to
exact vengeance from men who had already paid abundant penalties at home for
all their crimes. For after this rabble of plunderers of the world had ruled
almost all Asia for twenty-eight years, the Medes buried them in wine and
destroyed the greater part by the sword. The survivors hastened on the road
home; but after passing through Armenia and Colchis, and before reaching the
Maeotis, they found an immense ditch cast before their faces, beside which
stood an endless battle line of the strongest young men.

When the Scythians left home for Asia, they had deprived all their slaves of
their eyes, lest during their masters' long absence their own wives should
turn to those slaves. Yet this cruel device did not overcome lust. When the
Scythian women saw very little hope that their husbands would return, they
gave themselves to the blind slaves rather than allow the race to perish.
From this came a new offspring. When they heard that their mothers' husbands
and their fathers' masters were returning home, they feared that the slave
fathers, the adulterous mothers, and they themselves, the illegitimate
offspring, would be severely punished. They took up arms, ran out to meet the
old Scythians, and resolved with all their strength to keep them from their
homeland. A bloody battle was fought. The old men could by no force cross the

the mouths of the Ister, where he joined new bridges and left the Ionians with
their fleet as guards. He gave them permission to go home if he had not
returned in triumph as victor over all Scythia before the sixtieth day. The
appointed day arrived, but without any report of Darius except what the
Scythians brought.

At the king's first arrival, the Scythians—not the eastern Massagetae, but the
western Scythians, today the Perekop Tatars—called a common council. A large
part did not think themselves attacked, but supposed that the Persians were
pursuing only those who had invaded Asia long before; they therefore denied
that they wished to be the first to fight against the Persians. The rest, who
approved the alliance in arms, thought it advantageous to refrain from battle
until, by fleeing here and there through inhospitable rocks, they had
exhausted the enemy and at the same time reduced him to lack of provisions.
Thus they led Darius after them beyond the river Tanais without any gain,
except that the fields of those who had refused alliance in the war were
ravaged by both camps. At last, weary of following, the Persians sent an envoy
to the Scythians to urge them either to battle or to a confession of weakness.
King Indathyrsus replied that he paid no regard to Darius, since he had no
cities or fields to defend with arms; his single concern was the tombs of his
ancestors. If the Persians attempted to violate these, they would learn the
strength of the Scythians' right hands.

Meanwhile famine gained strength in Darius's camp. An envoy came from
Indathyrsus without instructions in words, but carrying Darius as a gift a
mouse, a frog, a bird, and five arrows. He told the Persians to conjecture by
their own cleverness what these things meant. Darius believed the Scythians
had sent him, as an acknowledgment of submission, not only earth and water
but also the air and all the strength they possessed: for the mouse lived in
the earth, the frog in the water, and the bird in the air, while all Scythian
power rested in the arrows. Gobrias, however, came far closer to the
Scythians' meaning when he explained it thus: *“O Persians! Unless you are
turned into mice and go beneath the earth, or become frogs and submerge
yourselves in the waters, or become birds and fly into the air, you will
perish miserably, pierced by these arrows.”*

Darius regarded this interpretation of Gobrias as the most probable and
resolved to flee from the Scythians as quickly as possible. That very night,
therefore, he left his camp and the infirm to the enemy and hastily set out on
the road. The Scythians pressed upon his heels; because of their knowledge of
the roads, they far outstripped the Persians and reached the Ister bridges.
There they found the Greek fleet waiting beyond the appointed day to guard
the bridge. They addressed it with kindly words, urging the Greeks to depart,
seize their liberty, and leave the Persians to them. They promised to handle
the Persians in such a manner that after that day they would strike fear into
no one. Very many of the Greeks thought the Scythians' advice profitable; but
Histiaeus set before the eyes of the other petty rulers the danger in which
they would find themselves if Darius were destroyed. Without fear of Darius,
each of their peoples, madly in love with liberty, would undoubtedly remove
them from power. By this argument he kept the Ionian petty rulers at the
defense of the bridges. To deceive the Scythians all the more, however, he
pretended to follow their advice. As though about to break the bridge, he drew
back the portion touching the Scythian bank and suspended it with cables,
ready to return it to its former place when convenient; meanwhile he withdrew
everything of his beyond the range of Scythian weapons.

From that point the Scythians retraced their steps to search for the Persians.
But the Persians followed an unexpected track through the devastated roads
and, eluding the Scythians, at last reached the banks of the Ister by night.
When they found not even the shadow of a bridge, they nearly went mad with
fear. Darius nevertheless ordered a certain Egyptian, a man with a Stentorian
voice, to shout the name of Histiaeus toward the bank. Histiaeus heard the
voice, hastily replaced the removed section of the bridge, and joyfully
carried the Persians across to the other bank of the Danube—the land once of
the Getae, now of the Moldavians.

The beginnings of the greatest affairs are very small. The wars that nearly
crushed the Greeks and reduced Persian greatness to absolute nothingness had
a contemptible beginning. Among the tyrants of Ionia was Histiaeus, lord of
Miletus, a man of singular prudence and the one man to whom Darius owed his
safety at the bridges over the Ister. The satraps did not think it expedient
for the king to leave a man of such courage and power in Ionia, lest he attempt
some innovation while the king was far away in Persia. Darius therefore, at
his men's persuasion, took Histiaeus with him to Susa under the appearance of
honor, as though by his prudence he were to serve the king as a counselor in
every affair of great importance. Histiaeus took this honor very badly. For in
ancient times the desire for their homeland was so deeply implanted in every
Greek that even the meanest of them preferred to live at home in the least
fortune rather than abroad with the greatest honor in the courts of barbarian
princes. Histiaeus certainly left no stone unturned to recover his beloved
homeland by any means whatever.

When Histiaeus departed from Miletus for Susa, he left his brother Aristagoras
as deputy in the principate. Certain Naxian exiles fled to Aristagoras so that
they might be restored home through his aid. Aristagoras alone was unable to
subdue Naxos. He drew Artaphernes, Darius's representative at Sardis, into an
alliance in arms, received from him two hundred excellently equipped ships,
and set out against the Naxians. He besieged the city, but without any
success. The Persians, indignant that their labors and expenses had been
wasted, turned their anger upon Aristagoras, the author of the expedition. He
began to tremble and look about for some means by which he could defend
himself against Persian force.
While he pondered this, a messenger from Histiaeus arrived with no instruction
other than that Aristagoras should inspect the messenger's shaved head.
Histiaeus had written on the skin of his head that he wished Aristagoras to
incite the Ionians against the king; for he was certain that, if some
disturbance arose in Ionia, he would be sent home to suppress it. This message
impelled the previously wavering Aristagoras. He diligently prepared
everything for rebellion and, to make the people more favorable to him, laid
down the tyranny of Miletus himself. By counsel in some cases and force in
others, he moved the neighboring tyrants to grant liberty to their subjects.
He drew into alliance not only all Ionia in Asia but most of the islands of
the Aegean, Cyprus too, and even the Athenians themselves.
This was the outcome of their plans: Darius sent Histiaeus to put Ionia in
order; Histiaeus joined the rebels; these were broken and defeated by the
Persians in various battles; Aristagoras fell; Histiaeus was put to death;

into deserts; and Darius conceived an implacable hatred for Athens and Euboea,
ordering one of his servants to mention Athens to him every day as he rose
from bed.

placing the Plataeans on the left wing and the Athenians densely on the right,
while leaving the central line thinner. Opposite them the barbarians were
drawn up in battle order, surpassing the Greeks in number more than tenfold.
The signals sounded, and the Greeks burst upon the enemy at a run. The
Persians received them without moving. The center of the Athenian line was
routed, but each wing put the forces opposed to it to flight. Having done so,
the wings drew together into one body, surrounded the Persians who were
pressing the central line, and cut them down completely. Indeed, the whole
barbarian multitude turned its back and fled in panic to the ships. The
Greeks followed, burned the ships they could, and dragged some ashore.
Cynegirus seized one ship with his right hand and held it until the hand,
severed by a battle-axe, fell into the sea. Deprived of his right hand, he
placed his left upon the ship; when this too had been cut off, he fastened his
teeth into it, and did not let go until, beheaded, he fell lifeless. In the
heat of battle, a certain Greek soldier lost his sight without any other pain,
through the breath, as he said, of some apparition. He had seen an immense man
standing opposite him, whose chin overshadowed his whole shield; at this
apparition one of his comrades fell lifeless, while he himself was deprived
of the use of his eyes.

Although the Persians had lost only about six thousand in the battle, the
Greeks' marvelous courage so terrified them that, not daring to return to a
new fight, they went back to Asia as quickly as possible. Yet this victory,
like a fresh firebrand, kindled still greater flames of anger in Darius. He
ordered forces three times greater than ever before to be prepared against
the Greeks. Meanwhile Egypt rebelled. While both concerns troubled his mind,
the fates compelled the man to rest who had never rested in the thirty-six
years of his rule.

By his courage at Marathon, Miltiades drove barbarian servitude away from

the slightest cause the Athenians compelled the man to die in the foulest
manner. Immediately after the battle of Marathon, Miltiades promised that if
they followed him where he led, they would obtain rich spoils and immense
booty. In this hope, a great crowd of the people followed the most renowned
man. They went to the island of Paros and besieged the city for some time in
vain. Indeed Miltiades himself, while attempting to break into the city over
the walls of Ceres's temple, fell and shattered his thigh. The Athenians did
not think the man had paid enough for this rash expedition with so grave a
wound unless he was fined fifty talents in addition. Since he did not possess
them among his goods, they ordered him to furnish them with his body. They
threw him into prison, where the pain of his wound, the stench of the place,
and the unaccustomed chains wrung the life from this excellent man.
It is astonishing that the mad populace was not satisfied even by the man's
death. They refused to give his dead body over for burial on any condition
other than that his son Cimon should succeed to his father's place and remain
in prison in chains until he paid the fine down to the final farthing. Cimon
accepted the condition and wished by his filial devotion to overcome the
Athenians' ingratitude.

As Darius prepared for the journey into Egypt which the fates did not allow
him to undertake, a great controversy arose in the royal household. Some sons
had been born to Darius before he was king; he received others while already
reigning from Atossa, daughter of Cyrus. Xerxes was the eldest of the latter,
Artabasenes of the former. Artabasenes judged it equitable that the kingdom
should pass to him after his father, since nature had granted him the right of
primogeniture. Xerxes, on the other hand, did not begrudge his brother their
father's private goods, since Artabasenes had been the first son born to
Darius as a private man; but he contended that the kingdom belonged to him by
right, because he had been the first son born to Darius as king. Either right
or the favor of his mother Atossa caused Darius to judge in favor of Xerxes.

As soon as Xerxes sat upon the royal throne, nothing was more important to him
than completing what his father had begun. The slightest effort sufficed to
reduce Egypt to obedience, but greater exertion was needed to wash away the
stain impressed by the Greeks in the battle of Marathon. Artabanus, Darius's
brother, strongly dissuaded him from every war with the Greeks, and by the
weight of his arguments drew Xerxes himself to his opinion, although at first
Xerxes had been inclined toward the Greek expedition. But the divine mind
presides over the counsels of princes. Once and again, a nocturnal apparition
threatened both Xerxes and Artabanus in their dreams with the gravest
disasters if the abandoned plans against the Greeks were not resumed as
quickly as possible. The king did not dare resist God.

He spent three years making preparations. He ordered an army to assemble at
Sardis from every corner of the empire. There they counted one million seven
hundred thousand infantry and eighty thousand cavalry. A private man in
Lydia named Pythius, as rich in livestock and money as anyone ever was,
provided at his own expense a royal banquet for all of them. He freely offered
Xerxes an exceedingly great present sum of gold and silver. The king's
magnanimity refused to touch a single coin from it; instead, in return for
Pythius's generosity, he ordered the amount of gold he lacked to make four
million to be supplied to him. Yet soon afterward matters did not go so well
between Xerxes and Pythius. Five sons from among Pythius's children were
following the king. Their father as a suppliant requested exemption from arms
for the eldest. Enraged by these prayers, Xerxes ordered that son of Pythius
for whom his father had begged release from the war to be cut in two, and the
parts of the divided body placed in the public road by which the forces had
to pass, one on the right and the other on the left.

Xerxes resolved to cross into Europe, not where his father had at the
Bosporus, but lower down at the narrows of the Hellespont. There he joined
Abydos in the Troad and Sestos in the Chersonese with a double bridge. The
Hellespont, stirred by a storm, destroyed the rafts with which he first joined
them. Raving at this audacity, the king ordered lashes to be inflicted upon
the sea and shackles thrown into it, as though they could restrain the furious
Hellespont. But soon repenting of this madness, in order to appease the
hostile sea he cast into it with his own hand, along with libations, a golden
phial, a silver mixing bowl, and a sword.

On one of the new bridges the armed forces passed in military order for seven
complete days, without any interruption of the journey even at night; on the
other passed the pack animals, camp followers, and all the baggage. When
Xerxes looked down from the top of the royal pavilion upon the endless
multitude of men following him, he is said to have shed tears. When Artabanus
asked the reason, Xerxes replied that he wept for the human lot, reflecting
that of the many thousands of brave men present, not one would survive to the
hundredth year.

The Persian army continually increased on the journey. For when they came
into Europe, not only the various nations of Thracians joined the king but
also the Thessalians, Macedonians, Thebans, and the greater part of the other
Greeks. Xerxes was therefore seized a second time by a desire to review his
army when the fleet lay off Samothrace and the camp was pitched at Doriscus,
at the mouths of the Hebrus.

For many centuries the Lacedaemonians had easily been foremost among the
Greeks, with the Athenians then in second place; but in Sicily Gelon, who
reigned at Syracuse, was second to neither in power. The common council of the
Greeks sent men to him to request aid against the public enemy. He promised
abundant assistance, namely thirty thousand soldiers for as long as the war
lasted, provided the Greeks would choose him as commander. The Spartans
rejected the condition, teaching that the command belonged to them. Gelon
then said, “At least grant me the leadership of the fleet”; the Athenians
answered that they would not admit even that. Because of these responses,
Gelon refused to join his arms to theirs—or rather, because at that time he
necessarily had to attend to his own affairs. For to this period is referred
the immense Carthaginian fleet which, under Hamilcar's command, landed three
hundred thousand armed men against Gelon. Yet Gelon routed them all and put
them to flight.

The magnanimity of the Greeks is said to have been such that they continued
their customary games even at the time when the Persians were pitching camp
in Macedonia. But when the Athenians learned that Xerxes was already standing
in Pieria, they anxiously sent men to Delphi to ask what plan should be
adopted in so desperate a danger. The Pythia's first response threatened the
city with disasters so great that the envoys rejected it and as suppliants
asked the god for another. The second oracle gave the city a dim shadow of
safety, provided that its citizens defended themselves with wooden walls.
There was much dispute over what these wooden walls were. At last the opinion
of Themistocles was approved, in which he persuaded them that the god ordered
them to abandon the city, construct a fleet, and defend themselves against
the Persians in ships. The outcome proved that this was Apollo's meaning, and
for the present it was confirmed by the departure from the temple of the
serpent to which monthly offerings of honey were poured at Athens, as to by no
means the least of the gods.

The Greeks first resolved to meet the Persians at the narrow approaches to
Thessaly. But those who came there with Themistocles, warned by Alexander of
Macedon about the multitude of the Persians, fled anxiously away. Afterward,
however, they returned to nearby narrows at the exit of Phthiotis in Thessaly,
beside Mount Oeta, where the rocks opened scarcely half a *jugerum*. Here
Leonidas, king of Sparta, settled with three hundred Spartans and three
thousand others. The place was named Thermopylae, not far from the shore where
the Greek fleet of three hundred triremes lay at Artemisium in the Euripus,
which separates Euboea from Boeotia.

When Xerxes reached Thermopylae, he ordered that the small band of Greeks
stationed before the narrows be brought to him alive. But they performed their
work in the battle so vigorously that the king himself is said to have leapt
from his seat three times in fear that these few men would defeat all his
forces. Certainly for two full days they sustained the entire strength of the
Persians—three million soldiers, as the old epigram has it—delivering about
twenty thousand brave men to death, together with two of the king's brothers.
But on the third day, when Xerxes now despaired of making the passage, a
certain Phocian traitor led a Persian column—the so-called Immortals—by an
unknown path around to the Greeks' rear. When Leonidas perceived his men were
surrounded, he dismissed the rest to their homes and with his three hundred
Spartans defended the place until at last they were overwhelmed and crushed
by the multitude, every one except two. When those two returned to Sparta,
one was punished because he had refused to die for his homeland; the other
was heaped with so much disgrace that he ended his own life by hanging.

Meanwhile the proud Persian fleet lay off Magnesia, consisting of more than
five thousand vessels, among which were one thousand two hundred beaked
triremes. The Athenians slaughtered victims to Boreas, and a violent tempest
arose. The Persian fleet was battered for four days, and four hundred ships
were destroyed; but in so great a number this was a slight loss. With the rest

whether he wanted his possessions hidden. He replied that his own things

near the shrine, so many thunderbolts sounded from heaven and so many roars

Xerxes continued this war against the gods of the Greeks and other nations.
For when he returned to Asia, he ordered many shrines belonging to many idols
to be overthrown, and at Babylon he removed the twelve-cubit-high statue of
Bel made of solid gold. This was indeed right and royal, had it proceeded from
true knowledge of God and zeal, not from mere greed and atheism. We do not
read that the sacred things of the Jews were harmed by him. Although the
things Flavius Josephus relates about his beneficence toward the temple at
Jerusalem plainly concern his son Artaxerxes, the very numerous things
Josephus Ben Gorion tells seem to me Talmudic and utterly stinking nursery
tales.

When Xerxes came to Athens, he found the city empty. Only a very few remained
in the citadel, stubbornly clinging to the oracle's wooden walls, until the
numerous swords of the Persians dragged the wretches away at once from their
stubbornness and from life. When the Persian now sat peacefully in Athens, he
sent messengers home to Artabanus to report his complete victory; but he soon
learned at his own peril that the enemy had not yet been conquered.

As soon as the Greek fleet at Salamis perceived the Persians following it, it
was so filled with terror that it was on the point of flight. Themistocles
restrained it only by telling Eurybiades in plain words that he was resolved
to desert to the Persians with his men unless the contest were begun in that
very place. This fear kept the allies together until news was brought from
Xerxes that Athens had been taken. The report made them all absolutely
determined to return to their homes. Yet the prudence of Themistocles held
them back, however unwilling, and compelled them to fight. He sent a man to
inform the Persians secretly that the Greek fleet, divided by quarrels, was
preparing for flight. Before it fled, the whole fleet could easily be
surrounded and so thoroughly conquered that not a single ship would escape.
No sooner said than done, the royal forces surrounded the Greeks. No place
remained for avoiding battle. The fight began, and Xerxes watched every
detail from the shore. Among five thousand barbarian ships, no one snatched
the palm for courage from Artemisia, queen of Halicarnassus. But no courage of
the barbarians could resist the valor of the Greeks. The Persians turned
their backs under the very eyes of Xerxes; a very great slaughter of men and
sinking of ships followed. So great a fear struck Xerxes that, although he had
endless forces on land still untouched, he nevertheless adopted a plan for
immediate flight.

Themistocles could not persuade the allies to pursue the fugitives, much less
to break the bridges over the Hellespont. The Athenians were indeed prepared
to attempt these things even alone, but Themistocles held back from the
undertaking, either because he did not think it safe to shut off every means
of flight from Xerxes, to whom so many myriads of forces still remained
unharmed, or because he desired to prepare for himself some place in the
king's favor. He certainly sent messengers to whisper in Xerxes' ear that he
owed his safety to Themistocles, who, solely to win the king's favor, had
turned the Athenians away when they were ready to break the bridges.

While Xerxes was wholly inclined to flight, Mardonius obtained from him three
hundred thousand chosen men out of the entire force, with whom he promised to
subdue the rest of Greece. The king proceeded to the bridges with the rest,
with such haste that very many of the infirm were left on the road, many died
from lack of provisions, and of the rest, after they had crossed into Asia in
ships—for the sea's tide had broken the bridges—a good part died upon their
first abundance of food. A report gained currency among some writers that at
the mouths of the Strymon the king, in order to become secure from fear of the
Greeks more quickly, boarded a Sidonian ship. When a storm overtook it in the
middle of the strait, the pilot informed the king that there was little hope
of safety unless a large part of the passengers was cast into the sea. Xerxes
declared his peril to the Persians present. At once a great company of the
most noble men threw themselves headlong into the waves to secure the king's
safety. When the ship reached the shore unharmed, the pilot received a golden
necklace as the reward for saving the king, but was beheaded as punishment for
the lost Persians.

When Mardonius had no hope left of winning over the Athenians, he moved his

but before this the whole nation had thrown itself into ships and sent men to
Sparta to request aid. There the only concern was to complete the walls upon
the Isthmus and attend to the solemn games. When the Athenians saw that the
Spartans had no concern for them, they prepared to return to their own people,
threatening to join arms with the Persians. This fear drew the Spartans and
their allies out into the field. An army of both heavy and light equipment was
formed, amounting to eleven myriads. Nowhere had a greater number of Greeks
appeared in a single camp; and, what is remarkable, they all appear to have
fought on foot.

When Mardonius learned that the Greeks were approaching, he withdrew from
Attica, as a less level region, into Boeotia, to the farther bank of the river
Asopus. The Greeks settled on the nearer bank. They sat beside the river for
eleven days without doing one another harm, neither side daring to cross
first. The sacrifices of each side gave favorable omens for its own defense,
but those of neither side for invading the enemy. At last Mardonius resolved
to despise the Greeks' sacred rites so that he could come to blows. Alexander
the Macedonian crossed the river in the untimely hours of night and disclosed
this Persian decision to fight to the commanders of the Athenians. They
communicated the matter to Pausanias, the commander in chief.
Pausanias and the Spartans immediately began to tremble at the prospect of
fighting the Medes the next day, since they had never met them in battle.
They begged the Athenians, who had routed these men at Marathon, to exchange
stations with them, so that the Thebans and Thessalians might fall to the
Spartans as opponents, while the Athenians would have to deal with the
Persians and Medes. The Spartans' request was most welcome to the Athenians.
At first light, therefore, the Athenians passed to the right wing of the line
and the Laconians succeeded them on the left. When the Medes observed this,
they likewise moved themselves from the left to the right wing in order to
join battle with the Spartans alone. But to avoid meeting them, the Laconians
returned to their former station, and the Medes did the same. Mardonius sent
an envoy to Pausanias to reproach the Spartans for their fear, since they, the
Greeks most renowned for courage, dreaded joining battle with the Persians.
When the envoy returned without an answer, Mardonius ordered the cavalry to
cross against the enemy. They harassed the Greeks the entire day and disturbed
the spring—the only place from which it was possible to draw water safely—so
thoroughly that they utterly ruined it. The Greeks therefore judged it
necessary to transfer their camp to the city of Plataea, ten stades away.

As the others moved, Momphoretus, a Laconian tribune, could not be persuaded
by any arguments to allow his cohort to move a single foot. Day dawned while
Pausanias was disputing with him. The Athenians were advancing on their march;
the others, under the pretext of marching, were proceeding in flight. The
Persian cavalry pressed upon those who were departing, and the whole camp of
the Medes rushed upon the Lacedaemonians. Deserted by their other allies, they
sent to the Athenians to beg them to run to their assistance; but the traitor
Greeks who served with the king gave the Athenians enough trouble of their
own.
There were about fifty-three thousand Spartans. After showing marvelous
patience and at last obtaining favorable sacrifices, they turned upon the
Persians, trampled down everyone in their way, killed Mardonius himself, and
drove the rest into their camp. The same courage and fortune attended the
Athenians. Not one man from the royal forces stood his ground; all fled to the
camp, which they had excellently fortified with a great quantity of timber.
The valor of the Athenians crossed these walls, and a dreadful slaughter
followed. On that day fewer than one hundred Greeks perished, but more than
two hundred and fifty thousand barbarians. As soon as he sensed the first
breeze of fortune turning against the Persians, Artabazus had led forty
thousand men away from the battle out of Mardonius's thirty myriads. By a
hasty march he outran the report of the disastrous battle and conducted the
greater part of them to the Hellespont. Scarcely three thousand of all the
rest survived.

On the same day the Greek naval forces won a distinguished victory over the
Persians. At the urging of the Samians, they were led against the royal fleet
and routed it. For as soon as that fleet saw the Greeks, it was struck with
terror and put itself ashore above Ephesus at the city of Mycale, where
Xerxes' land forces maintained a standing camp to hold the Ionians in check
if they made any move. The Persians drew their fleet up there and joined
themselves to the land army. The Greeks attacked both; and when, as though by
divine means, they received news of the Plataean battle that had been fought
that very morning, they flew upon the enemy with marvelous eagerness, defeated
them with great slaughter, burned the ships and the city of Mycale, and freed
a great part of Ionia and the Hellespont from Persian servitude. This was the
catastrophe of the expedition by far the most terrible since the world was
founded.
