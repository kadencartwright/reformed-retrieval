# Anthony Tuckney, *Clergy Sermon for the Doctoral Degree — Fourth Part*

[1679 new-series fol. 40 | IA leaf 360]

Let us therefore take care that, while we have been diligent in asserting that dignity, we are not meanwhile more sluggish in performing our office. This was the final point, and that most fitting metaphor of salt clearly indicates both what ought to be in us ourselves and what ought to be done to others.

1. First, then, if we are salt, it is entirely reasonable that we ourselves should first have savor, so that we may be able to season others. “Have salt in yourselves,” Mark 9:50. He said “in yourselves,” commending to us a seasoning of conduct no less than of doctrine, so that you may be not merely a channel but also a basin, possessing within what you bring forth without. He wants oil in the vessel, lest the flame in the lamp be extinguished, Matthew 25; water in the spring as well as in its flowing stream, Proverbs 5:15–16. Christ himself began to do before he taught, just as his forerunner taught first by his life and then by his discourse. The priests of old were themselves first washed in the bronze sea and only afterward washed the people's sacrifices in the bronze laver. Ezra also first set his heart to seek and perform the Law, and only then to teach Israel.

[Marginalia: Acts 1; Brugensis on Matthew 4:4; chapter 7:10.]

He was like Polyclitus, who, after writing a sufficiently elegant book on sculpture, also fashioned a statue according to its rule and called it the *Canon*. Let this living canon be our perpetual canon, this the clergy's truly canonical institution, this the best method and rule of preaching. “I taught nothing that I myself did not do,” said that old man as he was drawing his last breath in Cassian. “They live as they speak, and speak as they hear,” as we read in Augustine. To be Chrysostom or Chrysologus not merely in speech, but Eusebius and Polycarp in life—this is at last truly to play the teacher. For, to use Tertullian's words, those who undertake to commend anything must first be found practicing it themselves, and they must direct the constancy of their admonition by the authority of their own manner of life, lest their words blush when deeds fail them. Therefore let your deeds not confound your sermon, lest, while you speak in church, someone silently

[Marginalia: † *Conference*; * on Psalm 101; *On Patience*, chapter 1.]

[1679 new-series fol. 41 | IA leaf 361]

answer, “Physician, heal yourself. Why do you yourself not do what you say?” The words are excellent, but not when spoken for Tiberius. The utterance for the republic is honorable but doubtful in his own case, since his other conduct did not conform to this pattern, as Tacitus says of Galba. He is a dainty teacher who debates fasting with a full mouth. Even a robber can denounce greed, as Jerome says to Nepotianus. But shall I judge him a suitable man who gives counsel to me but gives none to himself? “I hate a sophist who is not wise for himself.” I hate those who, though they cannot discern a path for themselves, show the way to others. They are like the wild fig tree, which, though it never itself produces ripe fruit, nevertheless helps other fruit to ripen. Or rather, they resemble John of Crema, once the pope's legate among us and, one might suppose, exceedingly like His Holiness: after declaiming during the day in London for priestly chastity until he was hoarse and sweating, the following night he was caught in the very act in the embraces of a notorious prostitute.

[Marginalia: Ambrose, *On Duties*, book 2, chapter 12.]

This is the ancient “this is not this,” to speak with the prophet; “the salt becomes saltless.” Salt is not salt but becomes tasteless if, first, it has no savor—if a minister of the Word savors only the flesh and the world, while lacking every taste of spiritual grace, prudence, or learning. I readily acknowledge that Spyridon and Alexander, bishop of Constantinople, though not especially learned, were nevertheless useful to the Church. Yet let us not on that account bring a charge against Saint Augustine because he wished all who would expound weighty questions to be thoroughly instructed in the liberal disciplines.

[Marginalia: Ezekiel 21:26; *On Order*, chapter 10.]

Since by office it falls to a pastor to feed the sheep, drive off wolves, protect the souls entrusted to him from the Devil's snares, supply them counsel and defenses against every assault, and stand ready in danger and in the very agony of death—“who is sufficient for these things?”—these duties require someone not merely lightly dipped, nor merely sprinkled as though with a sparse scattering of this salt, but thoroughly drenched and imbued with it. He needs to have vinegar in his breast if he is to be salt.

[Marginalia: 2 Corinthians 2:16.]

Not someone fresh from the academy, not a novice in the Church—though our precocious age has produced very many such men, whom one may see everywhere fluttering around the churches, miserably chirping and still unfledged. Aaron's rod was made from the almond, a tree called *shaqed* by the Hebrews because it is the first of all to put forth flowers, just as the rod itself produced buds, flowers, and ripe fruit in a single night. The Church accordingly had men like Timothy the evangelist, Origen the teacher, and Aurelius the presbyter, young in years yet advanced in commendable faith, as Cyprian says concerning the last of them. Indeed she had Cyprian himself, who, as Pontius says of him, was through the premature swiftness of his piety almost perfected before he had learned. But what has this to do with those mushrooms, closely resembling Jonah's gourd, which arose in one night and perished in one? Just as ambition in the papacy manufactures infant cardinals, and superstition boy bishops, so shameless rashness produces our little preachers, in whom there is not a grain of salt, not without the greatest scandal to the Church and loss to the truth. The government of souls is the art of arts. Let these modern men at last learn this, who come forth as Doctors before they are even Masters of Arts.

[Marginalia: Epistle 5.]

I would have those who follow the camp understand this too. They do not pass, with the most learned and equally holy Ambrose, from secular military service to heavenly service. They are not discharged after service, but wholly untrained men: barbarians no less in learning than in conduct, they rush into the most sacred ministry of the divine Word, wielding both swords at once. In this respect at least they are very like the Roman pontiff, to whom they would wish to be thought most hostile. God indeed wished the prophet to lift up his voice like a trumpet,

[Marginalia: Isaiah 58:1.]

[1679 new-series fol. 42 | IA leaf 362]

but who ever appointed Mars's flute-player to be Christ's trumpeter by office? Who transformed a herald of war into a bearer of the caduceus and an ambassador of peace? If even under the Law God did not want David himself to build him a temple because he was a warrior and had shed much blood, who but a bloodthirsty man would not shudder at a soldier, soaked and still warm with human gore, announcing the gospel of peace and offering the Lord's blood to the Christian people with a bloody hand? We do indeed read several times in sacred Scripture of such men, after carrying out their slaughter, sowing the ground with salt so that it would become sterile. But that they were called the salt of the earth so that it might become more fruitful, we have never heard tell.

[Marginalia: 1 Chronicles 22:8; 28:3; Judges 9:45.]

Yet there are others even more inept than those men, though no less audacious, since they thrust themselves still more arrogantly into this holy office:

“Companies of female flute-players and quack physicians,

actors, beggars, buffoons, and all that breed.”

Craftsmen from barbershops and cobblers' shops, the cheapest hawkers of trifles—such as the priests whom Jeroboam once took from the lowest dregs of the people, and the demagogues and hierophants whom the Anabaptists and other sectarians choose for themselves today. These, if you please, are the “most reverend,” the most savorless animals of all, whose soul, as with swine, serves them only in place of salt; and if you bought them all at a fair price, “you would not give even salt” for them. Nevertheless, these petty peddlers openly hawk and disperse their scraps everywhere. A foolish and witless crowd, so that like lips may have their matching lettuce, looks upon and venerates them in astonishment no less than the Ephesians did their heaven-fallen image or the Samaritans Simon as “the great power of God.”

[Marginalia: Varro, *On Agriculture*; *Odyssey* 17; Acts 19:35; 8:10.]

I do not reluctantly confess that Amos was made a prophet from being a herdsman—and a slow-tongued one, as Drusius notes that his name implies—and Peter an apostle from being a fisherman. Yet the former was called immediately by God and the latter by Christ; both were full of the Spirit, as we read Bezalel was, and both were abundantly furnished with gifts for discharging their offices. We rightly honor them. But now at last a Mercury is fashioned from any block of wood whatsoever, just as Varro relates that a certain salt—black and tasteless indeed—is made beside the Rhine from burned coals. Away with such nonsense! O most wretched of all men that we are, for whom, as the saying goes, “the treasure has proved to be charcoal.” From charcoal we have received tasteless salt, soon to give us truly collier's faith. Finally, I would have these sordid men understand how great a sacrilege they are guilty of, since they rob not only the priesthood of all dignity but also this saying of Christ himself of the adornment of its most elegant trope. For in the plainest sense, “the salt has become foolish” precisely when the pastor himself is foolish and senseless.
