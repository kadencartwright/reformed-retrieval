As to the third question, the adversaries assert that Timothy and Titus were
bishops, while our men assert that they were evangelists, and consequently
that whatever was permitted or commanded to Timothy and Titus must not be
extended to ordinary offices. Their journeys and travels through various
regions, both with Paul and apart from Paul's company, testify that they were
evangelists. They reply that they were indeed evangelists and traveled through
various regions, but were at last assigned to particular churches and
furnished with episcopal power. For Paul wished Timothy `προσμένειν`, that is,
to remain perpetually or stay continually at Ephesus; and he left Titus in
Crete not to correct matters for a moment, but to restore what was lost and
always guard that church as something needing repair, as the force of the word
`ἐπιδιορθώσασθαι` shows, 1 Timothy 1:3; Titus 1:5. On the contrary, however,
the verb `προσμένειν` does not of itself signify a perpetual stay, but
sometimes a stay of three days, as in Matthew 15:32, *They have now remained
with me three days and have nothing to eat,* and Mark 8:2; sometimes an
indefinite and brief, not perpetual, time, as in Acts 18:18, *But Paul, after
he had remained yet many days,* namely at Corinth. `Προσμένειν` also means

[1623 printed p. 149 | IA leaf 161 | PDF p. 161]

to await someone's arrival, which agrees beautifully with the apostle's words
in 1 Timothy 4:13, *Until I come, attend to reading.* Augustine thus rendered
it, *I asked you to wait for me at Ephesus.* Paul stayed, `ἐκάθισε`, at Corinth
for a year and six months, Acts 18:11—a word, says Theodore Beza, which the
ancient bishops made their own, though it must not therefore be inferred that
Paul was bishop at Corinth. When he says, *Until I come, attend to reading,*
1 Timothy 4:13, he intimates that he had not intended Timothy to remain there
perpetually as bishop of that church. Had he been appointed bishop, the apostle
would not have said, *I asked you to remain at Ephesus,* or *to wait for me at
Ephesus.* For if he was bishop of Ephesus, why did he need to be asked to stay
at Ephesus? Did not the nature of the episcopal office and function require
that he attend to himself and to the flock over which the Holy Spirit had
appointed him? Next, the word *remain* must be taken comparatively, not
absolutely. Timothy's stay at Ephesus is opposed to Paul's journey: he did not
want Timothy as a companion because at that time Timothy had to supply his
place in the Ephesian church. Anselm therefore interprets the words thus: *That
you should remain at Ephesus, that is, that in my place you should act in
person and order the church at Ephesus, which I in my absence could not govern
sufficiently.* But his rendering *remain*, like the Vulgate edition, does not
answer to the Greek word. Rather, when setting out for Macedonia, Paul asked
Timothy to stay at Ephesus. From this another argument is supplied: in
apostolic times those ordained as pastors were appointed with the churches
present and consenting. Finally, Timothy's own journeys after the first
epistle was written prove that he had not previously been appointed bishop of
the Ephesian church. For the first epistle was written while Timothy was still
young, as is clear from these words: *Let no one despise your youth,*
1 Timothy 4:12—that is, conduct yourself so that no one is given occasion to
despise your youth. It matters little whether, when Paul undertook his journey
from Asia into Macedonia in Acts 19, or when he undertook the journey from
Greece to avoid the plots of the Jews, Timothy was asked to stay at Ephesus
for a time. For it is clear from the later epistle—which Paul certainly wrote
as a prisoner at Rome—that the first epistle was written before Paul's bonds
and captivity, indeed at the beginning of the two years Luke mentions in
Acts 28:30, as Baronius teaches in this manner. Not long after his arrival in
Rome, namely immediately after his first defense before Nero, he wrote and
informed Timothy of the things that had most recently happened to him, of the
different condition of his companions, and of who had deserted him. And after
he had, besides these things, admonished Timothy about many things he desired
him to perform and foretold many evils that would befall, he finally begged
him, once and again, to make haste to Rome before winter—which he earnestly
desired—and to bring with him the cloak that Paul himself had left at Troas
the year

before, as well as the books and especially the parchments. Thus Spondanus
reduced the words of Baronius to an epitome. Paul had traveled on foot from
Troas as far as Assos, Acts 20:13, and for that reason perhaps left the cloak,
books, and parchments when he set out for Syria. Paul also mentions Alexander
the coppersmith, 2 Timothy 4:14, who, as it appears from Acts 19, had assisted
Demetrius the silversmith in stirring up the tumult against Paul. *Trophimus,*
he says in 2 Timothy 4:20, *I left sick at Miletus,* namely when he sailed past
the coast of Asia, Acts 27. For Trophimus had gone up to Jerusalem with Paul,
Acts 21:29. Since, therefore, the apostle recalls events that were recent, it
is unlikely that he wrote the later epistle after the course of many years.
Moreover, he commands Timothy to bring Mark with him, 2 Timothy 4: *For he is
very useful to me for ministry,* he says. It is clear from the epistle to
Philemon, also sent from Rome, that Mark had been brought, Philemon 24. From
this it is sufficiently evident that the second epistle to Timothy was written
before those to Philemon, the Philippians, and the Colossians, all of which
were certainly sent after Timothy's arrival, since in every one of them
Timothy is named with Paul in the address, says Baronius. Some think that
Second Timothy was the very last of all because Paul says in 2 Timothy 4:6,
*For I am already being poured out, and the appointed time of my departure is
at hand,* as if by the prophetic Spirit he foresaw his approaching martyrdom.
But this interpretation about an imminent martyrdom does not cohere with
verse 17: *The Lord stood by me and strengthened me, and I was delivered from
the lion's mouth, and God will deliver me from every evil work,* etc. Why does
he command Mark to be brought because he is useful to him for ministry, and
the books and parchments to be brought, if martyrdom was at hand? They report
that in the later imprisonment Paul was thrust into prison and kept there for
nine months until his death. He could not therefore conveniently write or send
epistles as before, when for two years he stayed in his own rented house. He
would also have written to Timothy in vain that he should come to Rome, bring
the cloak, books, and parchments with him, and bring Mark because he was very
useful for ministry; nor would he say that he had been delivered from the
lion's mouth. And it is certainly unlikely that Nero was as humane toward Paul
in the later as in the earlier imprisonment. If, then, Timothy was bishop of
Ephesus, as they wish, when Paul wrote the later epistle, why does he call him
away from the flock? Why also, after his arrival at Rome, does he say that he
will send him to the Philippians as soon as he has seen how his affairs stand,
Philippians 2:19, and meanwhile send Epaphroditus? We also read somewhere that
Timothy was imprisoned and, once released from bonds, was to be sent to the
Hebrews, Hebrews 13:23. It is therefore established that Timothy was not
appointed bishop at Ephesus, and that the word `προσμένειν` does not signify a
fixed and perpetual stay or residence, and

that Timothy, as before, traveled through various regions after he had stayed
at Ephesus for a time. Much less can they infer Titus's episcopal residence
from the expression `ἐπιδ. ορθοῦν`. For the apostle means nothing other than
that Titus should complete the building which he himself had begun, and should
correct and supply `τὰ λείποντα`, the things which are lacking or remain. He
indicates what was lacking when he says, *that you should appoint presbyters
town by town, as I directed you,* Titus 1:5. He did not leave him in Crete so
that he might keep the churches in good repair and make provision for all
future events and new cases that might thereafter arise, but so that he might
bring to its summit and raise up the building whose foundations the apostle
had laid. *He left the disciple,* says Anselm of Canterbury, *so that he might
strengthen the first beginnings of the newborn church and correct anything
that appeared lacking.* In the same place: *Everything corrected is
imperfect. That you should correct the things that are lacking,* he says,
*that is, that the things corrected by me but not yet brought to the full line
of truth should be corrected by you and receive the rule of equality.* Titus's
journeys also show that he was an evangelist, not a bishop. He went up to
Jerusalem with Paul and Barnabas, Galatians 2:1; came from Corinth into
Macedonia, 2 Corinthians 7:5; was sent again to the Corinthians,
2 Corinthians 8:6, 23; was left by Paul in Crete, Titus 1:5, and only for a
time, for he commands Titus to come to Nicopolis, Titus 3; and went from Rome
into Dalmatia, 2 Timothy 4:10, while Paul was in bonds at Rome. The epistle to
Titus, however, was written before Paul's bonds. After the apostle had traveled
through Macedonia he came into Greece, Acts 20—not by an overland journey, as
Baronius conjectures, but sailing through the Cyclades in the Aegean Sea, he
first reached Crete, where he left Titus, and in the following year wrote the
epistle in which he commands him to come to Nicopolis. But let us grant even
this, that Timothy and Titus remained, the one in Crete and the other at
Ephesus. It still will not follow that they were ordinary pastors and bishops
unless they prove that they were fastened to their sees by reason of an
ordinary office and function, and that an evangelist can be cast down to the
lower rank of an ordinary pastor. We said above that the apostle James retained
his apostleship notwithstanding his fixed station at Jerusalem. We say the
same of the evangelists Timothy and Titus. Indeed, when writing from bonds,
the apostle expressly commands Timothy to do the work of an evangelist,
whether he was at Ephesus or elsewhere, 2 Timothy 4:5. Those who wish this
epistle to have been written by Paul in the later imprisonment strengthen our
argument still more: namely, that even after they were assigned to particular
churches, they were nevertheless not created bishops but discharged the
office of evangelists in regions or provinces assigned to them. Let us see how
Tilenus evades this argument. *For the rest, I do not wonder that what seemed
persuasive to me from the Genevan masters seems so to you also, taught in the
same school. They divined that Timothy and*

[Marginalia: *In Epist. ad Titum*, c. 1; *An.* 57.]

[1623 printed p. 152 | IA leaf 164 | PDF p. 164]

*Titus and those other `συνεργούς` of Paul were evangelists. A support for the
collapsing divination is applied from 2 Timothy 4:5, where Timothy is commanded
to do the work of an evangelist. By a not dissimilar vote Timothy could also
be admitted into the college of the apostles from 1 Corinthians 16:10, where
he is said to do the work of the Lord, just as Paul the apostle.* We do not
regret having been taught in the Genevan school. He confesses that he was
taken by the divinations of the Genevans, and for that reason does not wonder
that we too were taken—as if it were surprising that someone should not
stumble over the same stone over which Lynceus-eyed and, to be sure, most
keen-sighted Tilenus had stumbled. But, leaving aside the sectary's arrogance,
let us come to the matter itself. Timothy did the work of the Lord as Paul did
because he was Paul's fellow worker and true companion in the work of the
Lord, just as in Philippians 4:3 he commends another fellow worker with this
title, as though pulling under the same yoke in the Lord's field. Timothy was
young; therefore, to procure authority for him among the Corinthians, Paul
exhorts them not to regard him as nothing—that is, as elsewhere, not to hold
his youth in contempt—and says that he does the Lord's work, *as do I also.*
Moreover, he does not say *as I*, but *as I also*, `ὡς καὶ ἐγώ`, which are far
different. Both did the work of the Lord, but not the work of an apostle.
Timothy did the Lord's work, as did the apostle, but not as an apostle. Grant
that the apostle said, *he does the Lord's work as I do*; it would not follow
by the force and power of that expression that he worked by the same and equal
function. Paul says to the Galatians, *You did not reject me, but received me
as an angel of the Lord, as Christ Jesus,* Galatians 4:14. Yet in the judgment
of the Galatians the apostle was not equal to an angel of the Lord or to Christ
the Lord. Thus not parity or equality between Timothy and Paul but likeness is
marked, namely that they agreed in one third thing, the work of the Lord. But
to do the work of an evangelist is not to do the Lord's work as an evangelist
does, but to work as an evangelist—indeed, to act earnestly as an evangelist.
What is it to do the work of an evangelist, asks Downham, except to evangelize,
that is, to preach the gospel, which any preacher and minister of the gospel
can do? But it is one thing to evangelize in the broader sense and another to
do the work of an evangelist. The latter is to evangelize as an evangelist;
otherwise it will not be the evangelist's proper work. The apostle urges
Timothy to consider the dignity of the function to which he was called so that
he might proceed more courageously, since he was now about to lose his
faithful teacher—a point observed by the Reverend Calvin, the Genevan master
of pious and happy memory. Let *evangelizing*, then, be common to all who were
endowed with the power of teaching and preaching; nevertheless, nowhere in
the Scriptures is the title of evangelists given except to those who were
evangelists properly so called, who preached the gospel not in one but in
several churches. If nowhere is this title taken in a broader

[Marginalia: *Defens.*, l. 4, c. 4, s. 12.]

[1623 printed p. 153 | IA leaf 165 | PDF p. 165]

sense, and nothing compels us to depart from the same meaning here, they argue
wrongly from the common work of evangelizing against the title of evangelist
peculiar to the apostles' auxiliary ministers. For in this manner they can
also abolish deacons, because the verb *to minister* is attributed to the
apostles and evangelists themselves. Moreover, Downham admits that Timothy was
an evangelist; nothing therefore prevents him who actually was an evangelist
from being called one. Tilenus, taught in Saravia's school, proves as follows
that Timothy and Titus were not evangelists properly and strictly so called:
*It is established from the authentic documents of the New Testament that
evangelists properly and strictly understood were called no less immediately
by Christ than the apostles themselves were. But we expressly read in
Acts 16:2–3 that Timothy, while he was a disciple, was adorned by the testimony
of the brethren who were at Lystra and Iconium and was taken by Paul into the
ministry of the Word. As for the presbytery of 1 Timothy 4:14, whether it means
the assembly and college of presbyters, as Beza wishes, or the office entrusted
to Timothy by Paul through the laying on of hands, as Calvin wishes and I
agree, it contributes nothing to an evangelist properly so called. For an
office that is only extraordinary and temporary cannot be entrusted to anyone
by the ordinary ministers of the church; nor can a presbyter who is ordained
be called the same as an evangelist, such as those seventy were. For these,
called and appointed by the Lord himself, have no need at all of the bare
appointment and commands of a man, of which Timothy received not a few.*
First, he says that evangelists were called no less immediately by Christ than
the apostles. He means the seventy disciples, whom he says were called by the
Lord himself and appointed evangelists. Both claims are false. No evangelists
were appointed while the Lord lived on earth. From the numerous crowd of
disciples the Lord chose seventy, whom he sent like heralds, two by two, before
his face into every city and place to which he himself was about to come,
Luke 10. *So that, to be sure, these men might not so much preach the gospel,
which had not yet been fulfilled, as prepare the peoples in some fashion by a
general announcement that the Messiah had now come and the kingdom of God was
drawing near,* says Spalatensis. *Nor is a certain solemn preaching of the
gospel appointed here, such as was employed by the disciples when they were
now establishing churches; rather, these are sent as Christ's heralds to
signify that he was coming to Jerusalem and to proclaim the true and final
jubilee, namely so that Christ might have very many witnesses of his final
sermons and passion, as well as of his resurrection,* says Theodore Beza. You
see how Spalatensis and the Genevan master agree. Spalatensis thinks that these
things were done on Christ's last journey, when he set his face to go to
Jerusalem. He calls that proclamation imperfect and limited. That mission was
also temporary, since it was commanded to be undertaken only once, and
therefore

[Marginalia: *Paraen.*, c. 5; l. 1, c. 12, num. 3; *Annot. in Luc.* 10. 7.]

[1623 printed p. 154 | IA leaf 166 | PDF p. 166]

they had not yet been called to any stable and fixed office. *He wished the
twelve apostles, now properly so named by a firm and constant election, to be
his ordinary ministers while they lived. He did not reduce these seventy
disciples into any definite and stable body of that number, but wished them to
be his forerunners. Once this first office was completed, we do not read that
they were sent again by Christ into the ministry of the Word, nor that a new
commission with extension was repeated to them, as we know that after the
resurrection the twelve apostles received anew from Christ the mandate and
general commission of their perpetual and universal apostleship throughout
the world. Of those seventy we have no further mention in Holy Scripture under
this fixed number, but only under the most general name of disciples.* When
some attempted to set out one by one the names of the seventy disciples chosen
by the Lord, they recklessly patched together almost all whom Paul mentions in
his epistles and counted them among the Lord's disciples; Dorotheus was the
first author of this after Hippolytus, says Baronius. Eusebius, though he seems
to have expended no little labor searching for them here and there, admits
that their catalogue is found nowhere. The disciples said in John 4:2 to have
baptized were not from the number of the seventy disciples. Indeed, not all
the apostles had yet been called to discipleship, much less to apostleship.
They were still untrained and ignorant, truly disciples and not masters; and
therefore their baptizing was nothing other than dipping at the command of
Christ, who was present and evangelizing. Thus in John 3:22 Jesus is said to
have baptized because at his command they administered the outward act of
dipping. Nowhere do we read that the seventy disciples were confirmed again
by Christ before his ascension into heaven. Augustine and Ambrose, according
to Spalatensis, think that they deserted Christ because Christ turned to the
twelve and said, *Do you also wish to go away?* John 6:67. Epiphanius thinks
that they deserted but that at least some returned. Christ indeed said to
them, *Rather rejoice that your names are written in heaven,* Luke 10:20. But
Epiphanius did not think that they returned to their office and calling, for
he believed that the seven deacons were chosen from their number; that,
however, is not probable. For because of the murmuring between the Hellenists
and Hebrews in Acts 6, it is likely that not all the deacons were Hebrews. We
see, nevertheless, that not only the Genevan masters but also the Fathers deny
that the immediate calling of the seventy disciples to the office of
evangelist was confirmed again by Christ. It does not follow, therefore, that
Timothy and Titus were not evangelists properly and strictly so called because
they were not called `ἀμέσως`, immediately, by Christ, since we read that no
one was called by Christ to that office before his ascension. Yet we
acknowledge that the calling of evangelists was extraordinary. For an
extraordinary calling occurs not only immediately from God or Christ
addressing one by his voice, as Moses, Samuel, and others were called in the
Old

[Marginalia: *Spal.*, l. 1, c. 3, num. 3; *Ann.* 34; *Hist.*, lib. 1, c. 12.]

[1623 printed p. 155 | IA leaf 167 | PDF p. 167]

Testament, and the apostles by Christ, but also through the mediation of men
and the employment of messengers, who do not confer the right but announce
God's will and command. Every immediate calling is extraordinary, but not the
converse, for an extraordinary calling is either mediated or immediate. An
immediate calling is made apart from human ministry, either by an addressing
voice or by the inspiration of the Spirit. Evangelists were called either
immediately by an inward inspiration of the Spirit or through the mediation
of human ministry. Philip the evangelist seems to have been called by an
inward inspiration of the Spirit. For before the dispersion he was only a
deacon; after the dispersion, without the apostles' knowledge, he went into
Samaria to preach the gospel and was afterward called an evangelist. Others
who first evangelized in Phoenicia and Cyprus from the same dispersion, and
who laid the foundations of the church at Antioch without consulting the
apostles, Acts 11:19, seem to have been called in the same way. Timothy was
called extraordinarily through the mediation of human ministry. *Do not
neglect the gift that is in you, which was given to you through prophecy,*
1 Timothy 4:14. *How through prophecy? Because, of course, the Holy Spirit by
an oracle had destined Timothy to be admitted into the order of pastors. For
he had not merely been chosen by human judgment, as commonly happens, but the
Spirit's declaration had gone before,* says Calvin on this passage. And lest
anyone perhaps think Calvin's view was that Timothy was not an evangelist but
an ordinary pastor, let him know that in his commentaries and sermons on this
same passage, and in the fourth book of the *Institutes*, he proves by several
arguments that Timothy was an evangelist. There he says: *By evangelists I
understand those who, though lower in dignity than the apostles, were yet next
to them in office and thus acted in their place. Such were Luke, Timothy,
Titus, and the rest like them.* Others also infer from 1 Timothy 4:14 that
Timothy was called extraordinarily. *You were made a bishop not by human vote
but by prophecy, that is, by divine revelation,* says Theodoret; *at the
command of the Holy Spirit,* says Theophylact; *by the Spirit's command,*
Oecumenius interprets it. *What does “through prophecy” mean?* says
Chrysostom. *Through the Holy Spirit. For it is the office of prophecy not
only to predict future things but also to tell present things, since Saul too
was revealed by prophecy when he hid among the baggage. For God reveals to the
just. It was likewise prophecy when the Holy Spirit says, “Separate Paul and
Barnabas for me”; so Timothy also was chosen. He speaks, however, of many
prophecies, perhaps including that through which he had received him, when he
both circumcised and ordained him, just as he himself writes: “Do not neglect
the grace that is in you.” Therefore, to arouse him and make him sober and
vigilant, he reminds him who chose and ordained him, as though saying more
plainly, “God himself chose you; he himself entrusted the church to you; human
judgment did not advance you. Therefore, do not bring God's sentence into
reproach.”* If he had been designated bishop of Ephesus by the declaration of
prophets,

[Marginalia: *Cap.* 3, s. 4; *Homil.* 5 in 1. Timoth. 1.]

[1623 printed p. 156 | IA leaf 168 | PDF p. 168]

what need was there for Paul to ask him to stay at Ephesus? It is not likely
that Timothy would have failed to obey divine revelation without any request
or entreaties from Paul. Therefore this declaration of the Spirit must be
understood of his first assumption into the office of evangelist. Was Timothy
not furnished with gifts until he stayed at Ephesus, though he had preached
the gospel for so many years before in the most celebrated churches? Let us
proceed to others. Anselm of Canterbury: *The apostle seems to call prophecy
the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, through which he knew what he would do
about this man and the others.* Through prophecy, says Piscator, *that is,
through a singular calling attested by some prophet from a revelation of the
Holy Spirit, such as the calling of Paul and Barnabas to preach the gospel in
Greece described in Acts 13:2–4.* *It also seems probable to me that divine
revelation is meant by prophecy,* says Spalatensis. We see, therefore, that
not only the Genevans but ancient and more recent authors acknowledge in
Timothy an extraordinary calling, though a mediated one, the Holy Spirit
commanding through the mouths of prophets. The hierarchical party itself
acknowledges it: Downham in his sermon at the end, and Bilson in *The
Government of the Church*. For he says: *I find three manners of choosing in
the New Testament: by the Spirit, by lots, and by votes. Matthias was chosen
by lots, the deacons by votes, Paul and Barnabas were sent to teach the
Gentiles by the uttered voice of the Spirit, and Timothy was designated by the
Spirit speaking in the prophets,* 1 Timothy 4; 1 Timothy 1. And in the same
chapter: *Thus Paul took Timothy, approved and commended by the testimony of
the prophets, into his company and wished him to travel with him, and through
the laying on of his hands enriched him with the Holy Spirit.* Here too you
see that Bilson understands the prophets' predictions of Timothy's first
assumption. And it would certainly be absurd to think that Timothy undertook
the office of bishop only after prophecies had preceded, but not the office of
evangelist. Bilson adds to the prophets' predictions another extraordinary
mode of calling pastors in the primitive church, namely the inward inspiration
of the Spirit in the apostles themselves. *The Holy Spirit,* he says,
*directed the apostles in the choice of pastors by voice, prophecy, or
inspiration: on whom they should lay hands and whom he himself should adorn
with his heavenly gifts worthy of ministry,* Acts 19. Likewise: *The apostles
laid hands on those whom the Spirit himself had named, whether by inward
impulse, by voice, or through prophets.* Likewise: *The same Spirit directed
Peter and John in Samaria, upon whom they should lay their hands. In the same
way he inspired Paul to impart the manifold gifts of the Spirit to those
twelve Ephesians for planting the church there.* Eusebius also reports that
the apostle John, on returning to Ephesus from the island of Patmos, chose
certain men for the clergy whom the Holy Spirit indicated. If, therefore, in
designating pastors the apostles were directed by the inward inspiration and
impulse of the Spirit, much more so in taking fellow workers and in their
commissions,

who were to proclaim the gospel not to one church but to several, throughout
many provinces. Would the Holy Spirit direct the apostles in their journeys
yet abandon them in choosing companions? Lest anyone perhaps wonder that
evangelists were commonly called extraordinarily in so many and various
ways—by the testimony of prophets, by an inward inspiration of the evangelist
called or of the apostle calling—I would have him observe a similar variety in
the choice of apostles. He will see that some were called to the apostleship by
Christ's living voice on earth, others by Christ's voice addressing them from
heaven, and Matthias through the intervention of human ministry and by lots.
How Barnabas was called is not established. Yet he was extraordinarily
admitted into the number of the apostles. For it appears from many indications
that he was an apostle of the same rank as Paul. Without any restriction and
`ἁπλῶς`, simply, he is called an apostle in Acts 14:4, 14. He is sent to the
Gentiles in Acts 13 furnished with equal authority with Paul. Others were in
their company, yet Barnabas is named as an equal, not among subordinates. The
people of Lystra thought Barnabas was Jupiter, Acts 14:12, and Paul Mercury;
and Barnabas is often named before Paul. He is always distinguished from
Paul's other companions, both on that journey among the Gentiles and when they
went up to the council at Jerusalem. The sharp disagreement that arose
between Paul and Barnabas, so that one departed from the other, Acts 5:39, and
his power of taking the fellow worker John surnamed Mark show that he was an
apostle, not an evangelist. Saravia says that Silas was an evangelist properly
and strictly so called, like Barnabas. But he cannot demonstrate his imaginary
distinction between evangelists. In this very passage, Acts 15:40, Paul chose
Silas and departed, commended by the brethren to the grace of God; Barnabas
took Mark and sailed to Cyprus. You see that Silas was not equal to Barnabas
and did not take a fellow worker but was taken by Paul. In Acts 11:22 Barnabas
appears to have been sent to Antioch for the same purpose for which Peter and
John were sent to Samaria. Casaubon says that Barnabas was admitted into the
number of the apostles. Baronius writes that Paul and Barnabas were chosen
above the number of twelve, like senators admitted to the senate by favor
above the fixed number, whom Suetonius writes were customarily called
“abortive senators.” Calvin says: *Unless perhaps, because Barnabas was added
as Paul's colleague, we place both in an equal rank of office; thus the title

Philip, although an evangelist, did not lay hands on the converted Samaritans,

ministers of sacramental confirmation. Now they adduce it to establish that

conferred the wondrous gifts of the Holy Spirit. *For when the churches were
first planted, even those who believed were such novices and so untrained in

people except those whom the apostles equipped through the laying on of their

office,* says Bilson. *Those in Samaria newly converted to the faith were
altogether unarmed and unfit for the preaching of the gospel and government of

pastors, others teachers, adorning each with the gifts necessary for his

first kind, as they wish, and was not furnished with this power, it does not
follow that Philip was prohibited from every ordinary laying on of hands.

*From that text it is not clear whether the apostles confirmed as bishops or

or whoever is the author of the *Questions of the Old and New Testament*, says
they laid hands on them as presbyters.

the apostle is treating offices and functions instituted by God for the work

ministry any more than writing Acts of the Apostles or epistles. Writing
Gospels was incidental and common both to apostles and evangelists, something
they also performed only once. Here, however, some are distinguished from
others by a certain special and peculiar function. As for Downham, he speaks
hesitantly, and we showed above that the invention about the seventy-two
disciples being called by Christ to the office of evangelist rests on no
authority of Scripture. But grant it to be true: why does he nevertheless

sent or taken by the apostles they were called by an inward inspiration of the

therefore, that he was more than a bishop, even if for a time he preached in

to the rank of presbyter; but to move evangelists from their own place and
cast them down to the rank of bishops is a greater sacrilege. But they will
say that they were evangelists and bishops at the same time, and were not
therefore diminished in status because they exercised the episcopate.
*Indeed, they were evangelists rather and bishops,* says Bilson. *For as soon
as they were fixed to definite sees with the power and authority I described,
they ceased to be the apostles' companions. What else could they then be but
bishops? When the apostles were present, indeed, they assisted them; when they
were absent, they supplied their places and preserved in the same condition
the churches the apostles had left. Now, if the apostles by reason of that
power were bishops when they remained in some places, much more were the
evangelists.* I answer that these two offices are different: those of
evangelists and pastors, under whom they include their hierarchical bishops.
*He gave some,* he says, *as evangelists, others as pastors* and thus,
according to them, as bishops. The extraordinary is opposed to the ordinary.
One and the same man cannot at the same time and once for all discharge both
an ordinary and extraordinary office in the same matter; the exercise of one
or the other must be suspended. As an ordinary pastor he is bound by office
to one definite church, which he cannot desert; as an extraordinary man he is
not bound by office to one church, although perhaps he might have remained
there until death, but is bound to devote his labor to other churches and
always to be prepared to follow the leading of the Holy Spirit or, if an
evangelist, an apostolic command. Whitaker said above that whatever James did,
he did as an apostle, not as a bishop. So I say that an evangelist did
everything as an evangelist, not as a bishop, even when he remained for
several years in a certain church. James, staying at Jerusalem, is called an
apostle; Philip at Caesarea and Timothy at Ephesus are called not bishops but
evangelists. The names *evangelist* and *apostle* designate the function they
exercised, namely that it was not ordinary but extraordinary. Moreover,
evangelists preached the gospel with a different and greater authority than
ordinary pastors: discharging their embassy as extraordinary legates endowed
with extraordinary gifts and as commissioned agents of the apostles, which
ordinary pastors could not do. If the function of a bishop and of an
evangelist is not formally the same, neither is the act of a bishop and an
evangelist formally the same, says the Englishman Baynes, a man of most
polished judgment and Perkins's successor, in a certain manuscript codex. As
for what some object, that lower

everywhere show apostles who are not evangelists, evangelists who are not
apostles, and presbyters who are neither apostles nor evangelists. But they

evangelist. It is therefore a begging of the question when they say that
Timothy as evangelist contained a bishop under him, because no such bishop
distinct from an evangelist, who might be contained under him, existed in the
nature of things. Moreover, there would have been no need of a new ordination

bishop superior to a presbytery—there was no such person—but as apostles and
evangelists placed extraordinarily and temporarily by the Lord in a higher

the condition of a thing in generation is one thing and in constitution

while it was being constructed, so neither is there need of apostles and their

called `ἀμέσως`, immediately, by Christ, but they were also endowed with

perpetually and immutably, he devised this distinction: *In every

*title or application of power to the person, and use must be distinguished.*
The power itself to be exercised, and the order by which inferiors are
subjected and subordinated to superiors, are essential and immutable in every
government. But the form or mode—that is, whether the power is to be exercised
monarchically, aristocratically, or democratically—the title, whether rulers
received a right to power by succession or election, and the use, how they
exercise it, are mutable. If these things are to be distinguished in ordinary
government, which they assert is of apostolic institution, much more must they
be distinguished in the church's extraordinary rulers. The extraordinary
function of apostles and evangelists, therefore, must be distinguished from
the power of jurisdiction and ordination. The function of evangelists is not
perpetually necessary, since it was only extraordinary and temporary, but the
power of ordination and jurisdiction which they exercised is perpetually
necessary until the glorious advent of our Lord Jesus Christ. The apostles
could not by themselves fulfill Christ's command to preach the gospel to all
nations, baptize, and proclaim Christ's death in celebrating the Eucharist
until his return; rather, it had to be accomplished and fulfilled through
their successors. Yet the successors did not have to be apostles. Thus
evangelists and apostles exercised the power of ordination and jurisdiction
in the church, yet apostles and evangelists did not have to endure in the
church until the end of the age. Moses and his successor Joshua led the people
of God through the wilderness and brought them into the land of Canaan. Did
they therefore, after God's people were brought into the land of Canaan, have
to choose successors who would succeed Moses and Joshua in the same rank? The
apostles and evangelists led the faithful through the wilderness of paganism
and planted churches. Did they therefore have to choose others to succeed the
apostles and evangelists in the same rank of authority? Moses instituted the
ordinary government of the commonwealth, although he himself was raised up
extraordinarily by God; and by God's institution he left the supreme ordinary
power with the supreme Sanhedrin. So too the apostles and evangelists lodged
the ordinary government of the Christian commonwealth, which they decreed
would remain, not with men like themselves but with the presbytery or senate
of presbyters. *Christ never instituted succession to the apostles in the
second rank in the church,* says Junius. *Had this been so, the function of
the apostleship would have to be called ordinary. But this conflicts with
truth and reason. All God's servants succeeded to the apostles' doctrine; God
adopted no one into their rank.* Succession, says Bañez, is either into the
same rank or into the head. It is into the same rank as when a brother succeeds
a brother in the right of inheritance; into the head when someone legally
succeeds not in the same line but from another. Thus no one succeeded into the
rank of apostles and evangelists, because it was extraordinary and temporary.

among later writers who described the provinces of the Roman Empire when it
was already declining. Thus from this passage too it can be established that
subscriptions of this kind were added long after these epistles were written,
generally by men either unlearned or certainly not sufficiently attentive.
The Codex Claromontanus mentions no place. At the end of the later epistle it
is said to have been written from Rome to Timothy, who was the first ordained
bishop of Ephesus. But this clause is absent from certain ancient codices, the
old Vulgate edition, and the Syriac interpreter. Paul would have written *to
Timothy, bishop at Ephesus* or *of the Ephesian church*, not *to the first*,
since it was not yet certain whether a second would succeed; nor *to the first
bishop who was ordained*, but rather *who is ordained*. The last clause does
not exist in the Syriac. Paul would have written, *when I appeared a second
time before Nero Caesar*, not *when Paul appeared a second time before Nero
Caesar*. Moreover, from the apostle's words in 2 Timothy 4:16, *At my first
defense no one stood with me,* some infer his release from the first bonds and,
almost ten years later, the second bonds in which he appeared again before
Nero. I do not labor much over the matter itself, but I deny that any such
thing can be inferred from that verse—as though after nine or ten years he
would write what had happened to him at his first defense, especially to
Timothy, whom they report he had met after the first imprisonment and appointed
bishop at Ephesus. We proved above by several arguments that this epistle was
written in the first imprisonment, and consequently this final clause of the
subscription was not added by the apostle or annexed to the epistle. If,
therefore, Timothy was appointed bishop after the first imprisonment, from
which this epistle was written, as they wish, what is said here—*written from
Rome to Timothy, who was the first ordained bishop of Ephesus*—must be an
addition by some unskilled writer. For he had not yet been ordained bishop,
except after the first imprisonment, as they wish. Meanwhile, it must be
remembered that Theodoret's rule is false, or Paul ought to have written *to
Timothy, apostle of the Ephesian church*. The subscription annexed to the end
of the epistle to Titus says it was written from Nicopolis of Macedonia. But
he was not there when that epistle was written. *When I send Artemas to you or
Tychicus, make haste to come to me at Nicopolis, for I have determined to
winter there.* He does not say *here*, as if he were there when writing, but
*there*, a place he had not yet reached on that journey. Moreover, *ordained
the first bishop of the church of the Cretans*—and who, I ask, was the second
bishop of the Cretans? Indeed, was there ever one? We read of a bishop of some
diocesan church, but not of all the Cretans. Next, he would have said bishop
*of the churches of the Cretans*, not *of the church of the Cretans*. For Luke
in Acts and Paul in the epistles always call the Christian churches of some
people or nation *churches* in the plural,

[1623 printed p. 178 | IA leaf 190 | PDF p. 190]

never *church* in the singular. Finally, according to Beza, the Codex
Claromontanus has nothing other than *The epistle to Titus is ended.* The
Syriac has: *The epistle to Titus, which was written from Nicopolis, ends.*
The old Vulgate edition has nothing about Titus's episcopate. As to the
testimonies of the ancients, they produce none of those who flourished in the
first three hundred years except Pseudo-Dionysius, who was unknown to the
Fathers for almost six hundred years. The writings of others are either
supposititious, such as the catalogue of writers attributed to Jerome and the
book *On the Life and Death of the Saints* attributed to Isidore; or fabulous
and conflicting with one another, such as those of Nicephorus, Dorotheus, and
Antoninus. Dorotheus says: *Beginning Christ's gospel at Ephesus, he preached
as far as Illyricum and throughout all Hellas, where he both died and was
honorably buried.* If that Dorotheus had ever been persuaded that Timothy was
appointed bishop at Ephesus by Paul, he would never have written these things.
For by these words he depicts for us an evangelist rather than a bishop. The
master of scholastic history reports that he was bishop of Lystra. Or those
testimonies of the ancients are more recent, such as those of Theophylact,
Primasius, and the like. Or they wrote according to the custom of their own
age, when bishops occupied a higher rank akin and similar to the rank of
evangelists, and did not speak properly. How many ancients does Bellarmine
cite who call Peter a bishop? Yet even the hierarchical party, such as
Sutlivius against Bellarmine *On the Pontiff* and Downham, asserts that Peter
was not a bishop in the narrow sense, and that those ancients who called Peter
a bishop spoke improperly. Did those who called an evangelist a bishop not
also speak improperly? Eusebius reports that in the first succession after the
apostles there were very many evangelists. Since, therefore, evangelists were
still needed as long as any of the apostles survived, why would Timothy and
Titus cease from their evangelistic function? Thus, because after the
foundation of other churches Titus was needed to build up the structure, Paul
writes to him that when he had sent Artemas or Tychicus—namely one of the two
who had been with him—to Crete to fill his place, he himself should come to
Nicopolis. He sent the same Tychicus from Rome to Ephesus when he orders
Timothy to come to him. Histories hand down that in the division of provinces
John received Asia by lot, etc.; that he lived for the most part at Ephesus
until his exile to Patmos; and that afterward he also returned to Ephesus. How
then was Timothy bishop? For they appoint him bishop there until John's exile.
If they wish Timothy to have been appointed bishop before John came to Ephesus,
although they extend Timothy's years to the year of Christ 90, and others to
108, they must have exercised the episcopate together. But they admit that the
apostles appointed bishops not in those places where they themselves stayed
for a time, but in others, when

[Marginalia: Lib. 3, cap. 34.; *In Tit.* 3.]

[1623 printed p. 179 | IA leaf 191 | PDF p. 191]

they moved elsewhere. For where the apostles were present, they say the
apostles reserved all episcopal power to themselves. Let it therefore remain
settled that Timothy and Titus were always evangelists, not hierarchical
bishops, as were very many others who, like planets, wandered through their
spheres while ordinary pastors remained in their places like stars fixed in
the firmament. If we granted that Titus and Timothy were bishops, it would not
nevertheless follow that he exercised the same episcopate, as Tilenus says,
as the English. For an English bishop by his singular power rules alone over
churches, either by himself or through his chancellor, official, or
commissioner; Titus or Timothy did not. Indeed, we who assert that they were
evangelists deny that they exercised singular power in constituted churches.
The things written to Timothy are of three different kinds, like those said to
Peter. Some were written to him for himself alone; some for himself and all
Christians; some for himself and all the other bishops of the Ephesian church
of whom Acts 20 speaks, as can be inferred from their differing rationale. For
whatever belongs to common right is rightly said of each man individually,
`κατ᾽ ἰδίαν`, and is understood of others `κατὰ συνεκδοχήν`, by synecdoche.
And so much for Timothy and Titus, whom they endeavor to prove from Scripture
were appointed bishops by the apostles. Bilson admits that if our answer about
their evangelistic function has sufficient weight, the overthrow and ruin of
the episcopal office and power follows from it. *For plainly,* he says, *if
this fails, bishops may claim their authority by the custom of the church, but
they may not claim it by any divine right or testimony of the Scriptures.*
