2. The second point was the dignity and excellence of the ministry. For since our Savior called his apostles the light of the world and the salt of the earth, what could be said or devised that would more greatly exalt not only their supreme dignity but also magnify their usefulness in every way? Nothing is more useful than salt and the sun, according to the proverb; and, if Pliny is to be believed, human life cannot be carried on without salt. Without it, neither the wisest nor the strongest man could feed a household in peace or long defend even the best-fortified city in war. Moreover, it is commonly held in such esteem by everyone—I had almost said veneration—that both now and formerly many superstitious people regarded spilling it as an evil omen. Indeed, according to the ancient custom which endowed things of the greatest usefulness with divinity, Homer designated salt “divine,” and Plato called it a “body dear to God.” If, then, the priesthood is a deifying order, as Saint Ambrose judged, and we do not discern in the presbyterate that “body most dear to God,” or in the theologian—whose very name itself sounds of divinity—that “divine salt,” we are plainly blind; if we do not joyfully acknowledge it, we are by far the most ungrateful of all men.

[Marginalia: book 31, chapter 7; *Timaeus*; *On the Dignity of the Priesthood*, chapter 6.]

And here my spirit swells with the apostle in Romans 11:13 to “magnify my ministry”—that is, to adorn the ministry which we have received from the Lord, and to raise still higher the sublimity of our most sacred order, though today, alas, it possesses neither place nor rank of any kind. The holy fathers were permitted to do this; the earlier centuries approved it. But this present day brings another way of life and demands new manners, and at last matters have come to such a point that those who undertake to assert the dignity of the most sacred ministry are called worshipers of Baal, while those who spit upon it as filthily as possible, being plainly godless, are accounted the most godless. But let the broken be allowed their indignation. As for us, meanwhile, let us stink in others' nostrils, provided that we are a sweet savor to God; let us be the disgrace of men, provided that we are also the glory of Christ; let us be the world's refuse and the earth's hatred, provided that we are at once the light of the world and the salt of the earth.

This is the highest summit of our dignity; hence comes the most profitable advantage of the people, whose good we serve at our own expense, just as salt consumes itself while seasoning meat. And in this respect at least we may compare the salt of the earth with those shields of the earth of whom the psalmist speaks, and indeed prefer the former. Bodies are protected by those shields; by this salt souls are consecrated to eternity. Truly the mountain of God is a mountain of salt—as historians relate concerning Oromenus among the Indians—from which kings receive more revenue than from gold and pearls. Without salt, Pliny testifies, we human beings cannot live. Without this salt, Augustine attests, people can neither become nor live as Christians. Therefore whoever casts it out of the house of God would by that same deed snatch salt from the table, the sun from heaven, rain from the earth,

the shepherd from the flock, the laborer from the harvest, the physician from the sick man, and chariots and cavalry together from the camp. Once these are removed, what will remain but hunger and stench, darkness deeper than Egypt's, the most deadly melancholy, or a life—if any survives—that is scarcely alive? Then the most lavish delicacies of even the richest will be as tasteless as the white of an egg or the poor man's beet. The broadest estates and most august basilicas will resemble the territory of Jericho, whose site was pleasant enough, but whose soil miscarried and whose water was bad until Elisha cast salt into it. The people therefore have no reason to envy us the twofold honor God has granted us, since they ought to credit us with the manifold blessing of the gospel.

[Marginalia: Job 6:6; 2 Kings 2:19–22.]

1. First, the honor shown by reverence. This has not been freely granted to us as though we were undeserving; rather, it is owed to us by every excellent reason, as to God's angels, as fathers in Christ, as those who lead and preside in the Lord. I pass over how even princes once kissed the priests' right hands, as Ambrose relates; I pass over how kings themselves submitted their necks to the priests' knees. That earlier age indeed saw itself trodden under the feet of pontiffs when the Antichristian tyranny arrogantly raised itself “above every so-called god or object of worship”; but at last, in our own most unhappy age, even the lowest rabble trample underfoot the ministers of the gospel, whose very feet sacred Scripture proclaims beautiful. Whoever views this with glad eyes has cast off every sense of divinity; whoever views it with dry eyes has cast off all humanity.

[Marginalia: Hebrews 13:17; 1 Thessalonians 5:12; Romans 10:15.]

Certainly, if Jeremiah were alive again and saw his Nazirites, once whiter than milk and snow but now blacker than coal, he would compose a new lamentation far sadder than the former one, and would groan out between sobs, sighing from the depth of his breast, “I am the man who has seen affliction under the rod of his indignation.” But as for us wretches who see and endure all these things together, God—as the same prophet says—has commanded silence, since he has made us drink the juice of hemlock and rendered us contemptible and cast off before all the people. Let us therefore put our mouths in the dust and rest under the penalty of our iniquity. Let us bear men's most undeserved insults with a strong spirit, and God's most righteous chastisements with deepest humility, repeatedly reciting with the most submissive obedience of mind that word of the prophets: “I will bear the indignation of Jehovah, because I have sinned against him, until he pleads my cause and executes my judgment; he will bring me forth to the light, that I may behold his righteousness,” Micah 7:9.

[Marginalia: Lamentations 3:1; Jeremiah 8:14; Malachi 2:9.]

Meanwhile, let the man who has no taste for the Salt himself acknowledge that he is tasteless; nor will it be counted as that superstition if someone takes it as an evil omen when another spills this Salt. Whoever treats the ministers of the Word, who are the salt of the earth, like slaves purchased with salt—truly let him forever be called a son of the earth.

2. But let the pastoral staff have the staff of bread joined to it as well. Otherwise, to bend the knee to a priest, as also to Christ, while he carries a reed scepter in his hand is truly to mock him in earnest. That twofold honor of which we spoke requires support for sustenance just as it requires the homage of reverence; it demands not only observance for the father but grain for the farmer, wages for the laborer, and pay for the soldier. If the word *salary* derived its name from salt, who but a sacrilegious man would deny it to this salt of the earth? How could the one who has seasoned the meat be unable to eat from it? We read that the tribe of Levi, though the smallest of all, was also the richest of all, abundantly endowed with cities, estates, first fruits, tithes, sacrifices, offerings, and the best things in every category. The mountain of God

[Marginalia: Pliny, in the passage cited above; Psalm 68:15.]

[1679 new-series fol. 40 | IA leaf 360]

was then indeed the mountain of Bashan, a word which signifies fatness. But at last the Church now groans and cries, “Leanness is mine, leanness is mine; woe is me! The treacherous act treacherously, and the treacherous deal most treacherously,” Isaiah 24:16. For they go in droves to trample the most sacred ministry of the gospel underfoot, and they flock together as though to extinguish a common conflagration at the most holy fire of the altar, which should be kept burning continually even more than that fire of Vesta. To accomplish this the sooner, they strive above all to remove its fuel. There is scarcely anyone from the lowest dregs of the petty populace so stupid and so Boeotian in wit that he does not know how to misappropriate something from the Church's endowment—should I say skillfully, or rather wickedly? Those who wish to be regarded as holier measure piety by sacrilege. Or, to ensure that we are very well provided for, they will assign their preachers, who are to give them their portion of the Word, whatever ration they please—and I do not fear that I speak too extravagantly—so that they may have parasites rather than pastors.

Therefore let the priests of our Lord weep between the porch and the altar, and let them say, “Spare, Lord, spare your people, and do not give your inheritance over to reproach. For who knows whether he will turn and forgive, and leave behind a blessing, a sacrifice and a drink offering for the Lord our God?” We pray individually that the Merciful One may at last cause this; let us all pray together with most ardent vows that it may happen quickly.

[Marginalia: Joel 2:14, 17.]

3. And lest it be through us that this does not happen the sooner, it is fitting—as Saint Ambrose asserts—that the dignity of the priesthood first be known by us and then be preserved by us; that what we are be worthily recognized, and that we show what we are by profession and action rather than by name, so that the name may accord with the action and the action answer to the name.
