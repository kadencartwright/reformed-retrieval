QUESTION III

ON THE UNITY OF GOD

Is God One? Affirmative against Gentiles and Tritheists.

I. Opinions about God can be reduced to three principal positions, as Gregory
of Nazianzus notes in Oration 35: to anarchy (ἀναρχίαν), which the atheists
press; 2. to polyarchy (πολυαρχίαν), which the Gentiles maintain; and 3. to
monarchy (μοναρχίαν), which Christians teach.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. *One in number* is spoken of in two ways: either affirmatively only, or
also exclusively. In the former way, that is one which is undivided in itself
and divided from anything else, but besides which there can nevertheless be
other things of the same order and nature. In this way each individual human
being is one in number. In the latter way, that is one which is unique and
singular (μοναδικὸν), besides which there is no other such thing,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 164 | IA leaf 218 | PDF p. 218]

as the sun is called one because it is unique. The question here does not
concern the unity of God in the former sense, but in the latter, because God
is one affirmatively with respect to indivision in such a way that he is also
unique with respect to the exclusion of all others.

III. The question does not concern a numerical unity of person, because, as
will be proved later, there are several persons in the divine nature. It
concerns instead a numerical unity of essence: whether God is one in number
with respect to essence. We uphold this against the polytheism
(πολυθεότητα) of the Gentiles and the error of the Tritheists.

IV. The question is not whether there are many gods *so called*
(λεγομένως), or according to designation, in keeping with the vain and false
human opinion which prevailed among the nations. Rather, it is whether there
are several in reality and according to being. Nor is the question whether
there are several participatively and improperly. For we acknowledge that in
this sense angels and magistrates are often called gods in Scripture because
of a certain participation in dominion and dignity, Psalm 82; Hebrews 2; John
10. Rather, it is whether there are several properly and originally. We deny
this.

[Marginal heading: It is proved from Scripture that God is one.]

V. Scripture everywhere asserts that God is one, and reason demonstrates it:
Deuteronomy 6:4, “Hear, Israel, Jehovah our God is one Jehovah”; 1 Corinthians
8:4–5, “For us there is one God, the Father from whom are all things”;
Galatians 3:20, “God is one”; and 1 Timothy 2:5, “There is one God and one
Mediator.” Moses denies that there are, or can be found, several besides this
one, Deuteronomy 32:39: “See now that I myself am, and there is no God with
me.” So does Isaiah 43:10–11: “I am Jehovah, and there is no Savior besides
me,” as well as 37:16 and 44:8.

[Marginal heading: From reason.]

VI. Reason also confirms the same truth. For it involves a contradiction that
there should be several infinite, eternal, omnipotent, and most perfect beings
such as God must be, or that there should also be several rulers of the world:
“Many rulers are not good; let there be one ruler” (οὐκ ἀγαθὸν
πολυκοιρανίη, εἷς κοίρανος ἔστω). Hence Tertullian says in book 4 of
*Against Marcion*, “If God is not one, he is not God.” For if there were
several gods, either they would be equal, and thus neither would be first and
most perfect; or they would be unequal, and thus the inferior would not be
God; or one would be the cause of the others, and thus he alone would be the
true God; or not, and thus neither would be God, because neither would be the
cause of all things.

VII. Nor was this wholly unknown even to the Gentiles. They handed the reins
of the world to one supreme God, whom they call the Father of human beings and
gods. When they subject the others to him and maintain that they arose from
him, they strip of divinity those very beings whom they call gods. Hence
Hermes Trismegistus, the father of the philosophers, determined in chapters
2, 3, and 4 of the *Poemander*, and elsewhere, that there is one God, one
unity, the root of all things that exist. He says, “The monad is the beginning
and root of all things” (ἡ μονὰς πάντων ἀρχὴ καὶ ῥίζα). Pythagoras,
according to Cicero and Plutarch, says, “God is one, not, as most people think,
outside the administration of the world, but whole in the whole.” Orpheus,
who, if we believe Justin, was the first author of polytheism
(πολυθεότητος), since he was the first to publish the names and race of the
gods, nevertheless acknowledges one God in Clement's *Exhortation*:

One, who is self-begotten; from one all offspring have been fashioned.
(Εἷς δ᾽ ὃς αὐτογενὴς, ἑνὸς ἔκγονα πάντα τέτυκται.)

The sounder Gentiles hold the same opinion: Plato, Aristotle, Epictetus,
Cicero, Seneca, and others. See their testimonies in Plessis, *On the
Christian Religion*, chapter 3, and in Justin, Tertullian, Clement of
Alexandria, Lactantius, Arnobius, Augustine, and other ancient writers who
prove the unity of God at length against the Gentiles.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VIII. The variety of divine names and attributes does not imply a plurality
of gods, because they are employed to denote the perfection of the one God.
Thus through several inadequate conceptions we may grasp in some measure what
we cannot grasp through a single adequate conception.

IX. Although there are several persons in God, there are nevertheless not
several

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 165 | IA leaf 219 | PDF p. 219]

natures, because they all share one and the same infinite nature, not by
division but by communication. Nor, if three persons among human beings are
called three human beings, must three persons in the divine nature imply
three gods. Nor should the unity of the divine essence imply the unity of
person, as Socinus would have it. For the three human persons have three
singular essences distinct in number, since even the one subsistence of each
exhausts and terminates its finite singular essence. But the three divine
persons have one undivided and singular essence which, because it is infinite,
can be communicated to several.

X. Polytheism (Πολυθεότης) and atheism (Ἀθεότης) are rightly regarded as
the offspring of the same madness. Hence the apostle calls the Gentiles
“without God” (ἀθέους) because they were polytheists (πολύθεοι), Ephesians
2:12. For one who does not have the one God has no God at all, because he is
destitute of the true God.

XI. If it is asked how the polytheism (πολυθεϊσμὸς) of the Gentiles could
have arisen when reason and nature lead us to one God, the answer concerns
those who worshiped “a multitude of gods” (δῆμον θεῶν) in place of one
God, as Nazianzen says in his sermon on the nativity of Christ:

Whatever earth, sea, and wondrous heaven display,
they called gods—hills, straits, rivers, flames;

as Prudentius sings about them. Hesiod states that there were thirty thousand
gods:

For three times ten thousand immortals are upon the all-nourishing earth.
(Τρὶς γὰρ μύριοι εἰσὶν ἐπὶ χθονὶ πουλυβοτείρῃ / Ἀθάνατοι——.)

Various causes can be given. The principal one is forgetfulness of the true
God together with human need. For a human being, pressed on every side by his
need, seeks helpers and therefore makes many gods for himself. Pliny explains
this elegantly in chapter 2: “Frail and laborious mortality, mindful of its
weakness, divided these things into parts so that each person might worship
in portions whatever he most needed.” Thus, because they were pressed by
manifold need, they sought manifold gods who might remedy their needs, as
though the one supreme God could not do all things.

XII. Second, the veneration and worship of those who had benefited the human
race by certain distinguished benefactions introduced a multitude of gods.
Those who were illustrious for splendid deeds or had commended themselves by
some exceptional benefits—whether because they had profited the human race by
a noble invention, had performed some splendid deed in war, or had founded a
nation or cities—were enrolled in the number of the gods. Hence Cicero says in
book 2 of *The Nature of the Gods*, “Human life and common custom adopted the
practice of raising men outstanding in benefactions to heaven by reputation
and consent. Hence Hercules, hence Castor and Pollux, hence Aesculapius, hence
Liber, and so forth.” Pliny also observes in book 2, chapter 7: “This is the
most ancient custom of repaying gratitude to those who have deserved well: to
ascribe such people to the deities. Indeed, the names of all the other gods,
including those transferred above the stars, arose from human merits.”
Tertullian in chapters 10 and 11 of *To the Nations*, Minucius in *Octavius*,
Lactantius, and Arnobius show this at length: namely, that the gods who were
held in greater esteem among the Gentiles were nothing other than human
beings. And if we examine the matter more deeply, we readily discover that
the philosophers and poets borrowed most of these things from the Hebrews.
Yet they were so corrupted by Satan's craft and wrapped in fables that an
enormous heap of manure must be moved in order to find a few specks of gold.
Let Saturn with his three sons, dividing the dominion of the world among
themselves, be an example for us. Learned men long ago observed that Noah and
his three sons lie concealed in them, as Bochart and Vossius solidly prove.

XIII. Third, another occasion of polytheism was not only the multitude of

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 166 | IA leaf 220 | PDF p. 220]

divine names, but especially the variety and abundance of God's attributes
and works. By them human beings were gradually led to think of several gods,
and they sought so many and such great perfections divided among several
rather than joined together in one.
