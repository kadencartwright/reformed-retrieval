# Whether It Is Lawful to Make Images to Represent God

This is a fresh English translation of Calvin's complete French response at CO
X/1 199–202. It answers the position in the immediately preceding Horrys
paper, but remains a distinct document. Biblical wording is translated from
the French witness; no wording from Beaty and Farley's modern translation or
a modern English Bible supplied the English.

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 199]

The question is whether it is lawful to make images to depict God and to
worship them in honor of the one whom they represent.

In the first place, it cannot be denied that God expressly forbade this in his
law when he said: You shall not make for yourself carved images or any
likenesses. You shall not worship them or pay them honor.

Brother Matthieu Horris, in order to demonstrate the contrary, alleges that
we are not bound to observe the law of Moses, since it has been abolished as
far as Christians are concerned, and that those who wish to give it authority
among us Judaize.

That such a response is wicked and wholly contrary to the truth is evident
for very many reasons. When Moses published the law, he solemnly swore that
life and death are contained in it. Again: this is the way; walk in it.
Moreover, it is more than evident that God gave the Ten Words as a perpetual
and immutable rule of righteousness. For this reason it is said: the person
who does these things will live by them. Accordingly, our Lord Jesus does not
say that the law must be trampled underfoot. Rather, by expounding it he wills
that it be held and kept in its purity. And when someone asks him what the way
of living well is, he answers: if you wish to enter into life, keep

### [CO X/1, p. 200]

the commandments. When Saint Paul speaks to Christians, he does not give them
a new form for serving God, but instead brings them back to the law. Thus he
says in Romans 13: whoever loves his neighbor has fulfilled the law. For these
commandments—You shall not commit adultery; you shall be neither murderer nor
thief—are contained in this summary: you shall love your neighbor as yourself.
Again in Galatians 5: serve one another in love, for the whole law is fulfilled
in this word: you shall love your neighbor as yourself. Again in Ephesians 6:
children, obey your fathers and mothers, for this is right, as it is written:
honor your father and your mother, which is the first commandment with
promises.

In short, Scripture is full of such testimonies. Whoever denies that the law
is common to all the faithful, Christians as well as Jews, plainly renounces
God and his righteousness, for the whole perfection of holiness is contained
in the law.

Now, when Scripture says that the law came to an end at the coming of Jesus
Christ, this is understood of the ceremonies. This doctrine is so clear that
it has become almost a proverb: the moral law endures forever, although the
ceremonial law has been abolished.

Again, when Scripture says that Christians have been freed from slavery to
the law, this is understood with respect to the conditions and qualities
attached to it, so that it no longer holds us under its rigor in order to
condemn us. Its teaching nevertheless does not cease to have force. Whoever
wishes to obey God must conform his life to it and subject himself to it.
Therefore one must conclude that Brother Matthieu Orris, by banishing the law
of the Ten Words from the Christian church, blasphemes most vilely against
God, who is its author, and tramples his authority underfoot. For we must
remember that Saint James, wishing to show that it belongs to God to save and
condemn, brings us back to this point: God gave his law to govern our souls.

It is true that the Sabbath commandment is partly ceremonial. But in its
substance and truth it remains whole and ought to have force among Christians,
although what concerned shadows and figures has ceased.

This very fact also argues against this hypocrite. In all the Ten Words one
will find nothing belonging to the ancient ceremonies except the observance
of the Sabbath. Saint Augustine also speaks expressly of this in *On the
Spirit and the Letter*. It follows that the prohibition against making images
is included in the moral law. All who refuse to be subject to that law reject
God's yoke.

Brother Matthieu Oris adds consequently:

### [CO X/1, p. 201]

That none of the law's commandments are valid among Christians except insofar
as they conform to natural reason.

To this I answer that if he takes reason as it exists in those descended from
Adam—namely, corrupted by sin—he is gravely mistaken. For that is just as if
he said that judgment should be made by the sight of the blind. Yet I also say
in response that this second commandment of the law is taken and drawn from
the order of nature: namely, one must not depict God by any visible images.

For God did not merely forbid in express words that he should be depicted. He
also immediately added the natural reason why this ought not to be done, as
is written in Deuteronomy 4, and again in Isaiah 46: to whom have you made me
like? Therefore all idols are so many monsters contrary to nature, disguising
the truth of God and turning it into a lie. Accordingly, both the prophet
Jeremiah and Habakkuk say that images are nothing but falsehood.

As for Brother Matthieu Oris's allegation that it is lawful to paint all the
forms in which God has appeared, one would have to be quite foolish to grant
him so stupid an argument. God has never shown himself in a visible form with
the intention that paintings should be made of it. Moreover, in the visions
which the holy fathers had, he always impressed some mark of his majesty which
cannot be expressed by human hand or craft. Therefore those who wish to paint
God's majesty under color of the visions he has given falsify it.

In another argument Brother Matthieu Oris is still more ridiculous. He says
that it is lawful to depict God by the things to which he compares himself.
From this one would fall into exceedingly gross absurdities: he would have to
be painted in the forms of mountains, a lion, and a bear, and the judgment of
our Lord Jesus would have to be painted in the form of a thief. Thus
everything this hypocrite advances fails to show that idols are anything but
wholly contrary to the reason and order of nature.

Indeed, in the sermon Saint Paul delivered in Athens, which Saint Luke records
in Acts 17, Paul does not put forward the law of Moses to prove that God must
not be depicted by visible images. Addressing his discourse to pagans, he
instead brings them back to the order of nature: because we are God's
offspring, we must not suppose him to be like gold, silver, stones, or anything
fashioned by human beings.

### [CO X/1, p. 202]

As for what Brother Matthieu alleges concerning the cherubim, it is astonishing
that he does not perceive the manifest contradiction in his statements. But
this is how God makes his enemies senseless. He confesses that idols were
forbidden to the Jews. Whether he likes it or not, he must also confess that
the cherubim existed only for the time of the law. It follows, then, that they
were not made as a representation of God, as the thing itself also shows. For,
being placed in the sanctuary to cover the mercy seat, they signified that
God's majesty is invisible. By hiding their faces with their wings, they
warned people of the reverence and restraint they ought to maintain when
seeking God. Nothing is more contrary to this than attempting to make a bodily
memorial of him.

The reason he alleges—that images were forbidden to the Jews because they
were inclined to idolatry—argues against him. This has been a vice common to
all peoples and is rooted in human hearts, for the source of idolatry is that
we are fleshly and apprehend God according to our imagination. I therefore
conclude the contrary: because the reason on which the prohibition against
making images is founded still endures today, the same prohibition pertains
to us and must be applied to every age until the end of the world. Until human
beings are completely renewed, it is impossible for them not to give
themselves to idolatry when they have some object for it. This is in fact how
Lactantius, Saint Augustine, and other ancient teachers spoke about it.

Meanwhile, Brother Matthieu Oris is far too brazen in saying that no passage
condemning idols will be found anywhere in the New Testament. In his canonical
epistle Saint John not only draws Christians away from idolatry, but commands
them to guard themselves from idols. Moreover, even if it had been lawful
under the law to have some signs or memorials because of the people's
rudeness, this ought to have no place today, according to the statement of
Jesus Christ: the hour has come when God's true servants will worship him in
spirit and in truth. Anyone who carefully considers the whole context of the
passage will find there a stricter prohibition against having outward images
than was ever made to the Jews.

And when Saint Paul condemns people for turning God's truth into a lie, when
they have turned his glory into the likeness of corruptible man and into
images of birds or beasts, he is not speaking only for one period. He gives a
universal rule: an injury is done to God by wishing to represent him in this
way.

## Translator's notes

1. CO identifies this as Calvin's response to the preceding Horrys paper. Its
   principal witness is in Charles de Jonvilliers's hand, not Calvin's
   autograph; another copy in the same codex spells the inquisitor's name
   *Oris*. The body itself varies among *Horris*, *Orris*, and *Oris*.
2. On p. 201 CO prints *qu'il ne fault iuger par la veue des aveugles*, which
   literally negates the necessity of judging by blind people's sight and so
   defeats the analogy. Its footnote asks “*nous? me?*” The translation gives
   the sense required by the argument—“judgment should be made by the sight of
   the blind”—while `french.txt` retains CO's printed *ne* and the README
   records the editorial alternatives.
3. *Cafart* is deliberately abusive polemical language. “Hypocrite” conveys
   its historical charge without making the term neutral.
4. Biblical statements and patristic titles are translated directly from the
   French presentation. The body supplies no date, place, personal addressee,
   signature, or closing.
