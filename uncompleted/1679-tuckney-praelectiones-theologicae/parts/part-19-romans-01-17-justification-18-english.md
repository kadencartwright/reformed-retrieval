Considering and refuting the second objection may contribute no less to
explaining justification. Bellarmine presents it in book 1, chapter 19 of
*On Justification*, drawing it from several statements of Paul: If the
inheritance is from the law, it is not from the promise (Galatians 3:18).
Indeed, faith is emptied and the promise abolished (Romans 4:14). If
righteousness is through the law, Christ died in vain (Galatians 2:21). You
who are justified by the law are severed from Christ and have fallen from
grace (Galatians 5:4).

These inferences are very firm, Bellarmine says, if justification excludes
works done without faith and grace by knowledge of the law and the powers of
free choice alone. If the law and nature and their works suffice for
justification, they also suffice for the inheritance; what need would there
then be of a gratuitous promise for obtaining it?

[1679 printed p. 132 | IA leaf 140]

Indeed, what need would there be of Christ, faith, or grace, when nature, the
law, and free choice abundantly suffice?

But if, as we maintain, justification excludes works performed after faith
and from grace, these inferences are extremely weak and accomplish nothing.
For it does not follow, if we are justified by works done through grace and
faith, that the inheritance is not from the promise, that the promise is
abolished, or that faith is emptied. On the contrary, without the promise
there would have been no grace, without grace no faith, and without faith no
righteous works for which the inheritance would be rendered.

Nor would it follow that Christ died in vain, that we are severed from him,
or that we have fallen from grace, because works of this kind necessarily
require Christ and his grace. Without them such works would not exist at all,
much less justify.

To all these claims we reply, first in general:

1. These inferences are indeed very firm, as Bellarmine rightly says, and
most effectively overthrow the view of those who establish justification by
works done without faith and grace. But who are such people? Gentiles and
Jews: Jews sought justification by ceremonial or moral works of the law;
Gentiles by moral works of nature or habits acquired by their own powers.
Neither relied upon Christ either alone or at all; both impiously cursed him.

Yet these were not the Galatians or Romans whom Paul disputes against or
better instructs in these passages. They did not reject Christ, grace, and
faith or exclude them from justification, but embraced and professed them
and freely acknowledged that they were necessary for justification. They
wished only to add works of the law to them so that justification would be
more fully and perfectly constituted by both. This is exactly what Roman
Catholics do today, against whom Paul therefore disputes no less than he did
against the senseless Galatians.

2. Bellarmine secondly says that Paul's inferences are very weak if even
works performed from grace and faith are excluded from justification, as we
hold. Such works do not empty or exclude Christ, his faith, or his grace,
since all are necessary causes from which truly righteous works proceed. We
maintain, notwithstanding, that the inferences are very firm.

Christ, faith, and grace are emptied in justification not only when they are
wholly excluded, as Gentiles and pure Jews exclude them, but also when
anything is added alongside them as assistance. Galatians 5:4 was therefore
said not to Gentiles or Jews, but to the Galatians themselves: “You have
become empty, severed from Christ, whoever are justified through the law.”
Likewise Paul said to the believing Galatians, or perhaps even to Peter, “If
righteousness is through the law, Christ died in vain.” Christ, who is all
in all especially in justification, is nothing unless he is all. Grace is
in no way grace unless it is wholly gratuitous. Faith which

[1679 printed p. 133 | IA leaf 141]

justifies without works is either alone or nothing here, though it is not
solitary. Thus, although Christ, grace, and faith are necessarily required
for good works and so are not wholly denied, as Bellarmine objects, they are
entirely emptied with respect to the matter Paul so carefully disputes—free
justification—if justification is divided between faith and works.

Grace is certainly emptied. Though they object that truly righteous works
are done from grace, grace is not alone.

1. Insofar as justification is from works, a wage is demanded by debt and
justice, as Paul argues in Romans 4:4. The worker does not acknowledge grace
in this respect, whether he performed the work with his own spade or a spade
given to him.

2. Indeed, on the Roman view such works do not arise from grace alone but
partly—and in determining the act and producing its actual operation, either
solely or chiefly—from human free choice. Grace is emptied if in its chief
work it has something superior or even equal joined to it, as in human
government Caesar suffers no superior and Pompey no equal.

Finally, the whole nature, rationale, administration, and formal distinction
of the covenant of grace from the covenant of works are emptied, confused,
and overturned. Under the covenant of works our whole justification was to
arise from good works, which through the fall became nonexistent. In his
supreme mercy and kindness, God willed that under the covenant of grace it
should depend wholly upon faith alone apprehending and applying Christ, who
by his obedience fully satisfied the law in our place. Our works are not
expiatory or justifying, but only the way to the kingdom and a display of
our gratitude.

The Roman invention of justification by works done through faith entirely
reverses this new-covenant method of justifying sinners and leads us back to
the covenant of works by sending us to works for justification. Roman
Catholics grant that Christ, his faith, and grace are necessary for our
justification, and from them derive pardon of sin and the power to do good,
though not wholly, since they attribute it partly to human free choice. Yet
on their view this entire provision merely prepares the way and supplies
helps by which we perform the works that formally justify us, just as was to
occur under the covenant of works.

This answers the adversaries' objection in general. We need not pursue each
of Bellarmine's particular instances and inferences separately, since what
has been answered generally readily dissolves whatever he attempts to
extract from them.

1. Bellarmine says that if works following faith are excluded from
justification, Paul's inferences—Christ died in vain, you are severed from
Christ, and you have fallen from grace—have no force. So far from making
Christ die in vain or severing those who hold our view from Christ and
grace, Christ's death gives them this grace together with both faith and the
works flowing from faith.

[1679 printed p. 134 | IA leaf 142]

I answer:

1. This reasoning does not make Christ die in vain with respect to
sanctification and the inherent righteousness flowing from his Spirit.
Chemnitz writes that such renewal “is not a work or effect of human powers,
but the gift and operation of the Holy Spirit. Good works are therefore
called the fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5, and renewal is a benefit of
God the Mediator, for whose merit believers are renewed in the spirit of
their mind,” and so forth (*Examination*, part 1, p. 134).

But with respect to justification, our present subject, Christ certainly
would have died in vain. He died to satisfy divine justice for us and thus
present us justified before God. If the Roman view were correct and we were
justified by works done through faith, Christ's death and satisfaction would
be wholly superfluous in this respect and would merit or effect nothing more
than acquiring for us the ability to justify ourselves by works. Whether or
not the Roman Catholics see or wish to see how much this disturbs and even
destroys the divine economy of justifying a sinner, Scripture has taught us
better. We know plainly and firmly believe that it is utterly alien to
gospel truth. Paul's inference therefore fully stands: if we are justified
by works, even works done from faith, as the adversaries hold, then in the
sense explained Christ died in vain, we fall from that grace, and are
severed from Christ in this respect, because we adopt a method of
justification Christ never instituted.

2. Paul's inference that we fall from grace and are severed from Christ if
we seek justification by works, even those done through faith, is further
confirmed because these works are good and acceptable to God insofar as
they are done by Christ's Spirit and through faith in him, and by his
indulgence lead us straight toward eternal life. Nevertheless, Paul testifies
that whoever wishes to be justified by works of the law becomes a debtor to
keep the entire law (Galatians 5:3). Even the person who works best from
faith does not pay the debt of perfectly fulfilling the whole law, since he
sins in many things and falls short of fulfillment. If he expected
justification from those works or salvation from their merit, he would fall
from grace by falling under condemnation.

2. The two remaining inferences are: If the inheritance is from the law, it
is not from the promise; indeed faith is emptied and the promise abolished.
Bellarmine contends that these have no force if works done from faith are
excluded. Even if we were justified by such works, faith would not be
emptied nor the promise abolished, because without the promise there would
have been no grace; without grace, no faith; without faith, no works. In
other words, justification by these works does not empty faith and the
promise because faith and the promise cause the works. Positing an effect
does not remove but establishes its cause.

I answer:

1. I grant that good works are done only through faith and that both good
works and faith itself are promised to the elect. In this respect good works
flowing from faith and the promise do not remove faith and the promise but
establish and confirm them.

Yet we assert that although the good works themselves do not remove faith
and the promise, justification from those works does.

[1679 printed p. 135 | IA leaf 143]

First, it removes faith, if not in its absolute existence, then with respect
to the office assigned to it in justifying a sinner. Faith performs this
office by believing, not by working.

Indeed, Roman Catholic justification by works to this extent removes faith
in its very existence. No faith is true faith because it produces good works
and supposedly justifies us through them; it is true faith insofar as it
apprehends and applies Christ's righteousness freely imputed to us.

2. Nearly the same may be said of the promise. Though these good works are
promised, and good works and the promise do not conflict in that respect,
the promise of which Paul speaks in Romans 4 and Galatians 3 was neither an
indefinite promise nor any definite promise that justification would be
obtained by works, for no such promise appears anywhere in Scripture. It was
the promise of Christ and, through him, blessing upon all Abraham's genuine
children. As Paul himself interprets in those passages, it was the promise
of justification through Christ and faith freely imputed without works—not
works absent from justifying faith, but works contributing nothing to
constitute justification itself. Roman Catholic justification by works
entirely removes the promise as Paul expounds and explains it.
