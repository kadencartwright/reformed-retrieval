# CHAPTER VII

## The Decalogue's precepts are proved to be moral from the Decalogue's privileges

In order to prepare the way for discussing our principal question concerning
the morality of the fourth precept about the Sabbath, this question concerning
the whole law of the Decalogue must first be addressed: namely, whether it is
moral or not.

The papists, so that they may more easily defend their idolatry and worship of
images, erase the second commandment from the list of natural precepts. Our
opponents the Anti-Sabbatarians, so that they may make their opinion about the
abolition of the weekly Sabbath more plausible, deny that the fourth precept is
moral. And why might not others in like manner—the Libertines, the *Equalizers*,
commonly called *Levellers*, and people of the same sort—reduce other precepts
of the Decalogue to the rank of things ceremonial and abrogated? It is therefore
necessary for us to establish this law firmly.

It must be noted, 1, that the Decalogue can be considered in two ways: either
absolutely, in itself and materially, insofar as it contains the ten words or
precepts, that is, the heads of the precepts; or relatively, insofar as it bore
a relation to the people to whom it was then solemnly promulgated. Considered
respectively or relatively, it can again be viewed in two ways: either insofar
as it had a place in that economy or administration of the covenant of grace;
or insofar as it formed part of the instrument of civil government by which
God, as their special Governor, ruled them. Here we take the Decalogue in the
former sense, materially, that is.

[Printed p. 86 | IA PDF 94 | IA leaf 94 | IA decausadei02brow]

It must be noted, 2, that the Decalogue may be considered either simply and
abstractly, insofar as it presents bare precepts; or comprehensively and, as it
were, concretely, insofar as besides the precepts it also contains the preface,
promises, and other things of that kind that were said to have a special
relation to that evangelical economy. Here we take it in the former sense.

It must be noted, 3, that the sense of the question is this: are those ten
precepts so distinguished from all the remaining precepts which were given to
the Israelites and are commonly called ceremonial and judicial that they have
not been antiquated and abolished along with them, but still remain in force
and bind us Christians no less than they once bound the Israelites themselves?

It must be noted, 4, that when it is asked whether those precepts still bind
and remain in force, the question is whether they retain the power to bind in
the literal and grammatical sense as regards their substance, and not in some
general spiritual and mystical sense. For as regards a general or spiritual
sense, it cannot be denied that even the judicial and ceremonial laws still
retain the force of laws. The general equity of the judicial precepts and the
spiritual and mystical sense of the ceremonial precepts bind now and bound
even before those judicial and ceremonial laws were enacted. That force of
general equity and that mystical and spiritual sense do not depend upon those
particular precepts, and consequently are not the proper senses of those
precepts. We are therefore speaking of those precepts according to their own
proper sense, namely the literal and grammatical sense that expresses the
substance of the law.

With these points premised, we say that the precepts of the Decalogue were not
abrogated with the judicial and ceremonial precepts, but still remain in force
and bind us, and hence that the precepts of the Decalogue are perpetual. We
prove it as follows.

First, this law was honored above all the other laws delivered to the Israelite
people with various distinguished and peculiar privileges. This is a sure sign
that these precepts are moral and perpetually binding. To make the force of
this argument evident, we propose the following considerations.

1. God set many and various laws before the Israelite people, and no one says
that they were all of one kind or that they were all to be abrogated alike.

2. It is certain that these precepts of the Decalogue were distinguished from
all the others then sanctioned and delivered, both in their promulgation and
in their preservation.

3. Indeed, it is certain that God never promulgated any law to any people in
the manner in which he promulgated this law.

4. It is acknowledged that God willed at least some of these laws which he gave
to the Israelites to be perpetual, retaining their power to bind until the end
of the world.

5. It is certain that God willed the Israelite people to distinguish these
precepts, which he marked with a peculiar sign, from the others, to set them
before all the others, and to give them the first place among them. Without
doubt he willed them to esteem these more highly than any others, whether
ceremonial or judicial; otherwise he would never have distinguished them from
the others as we read that he did. For when legislators set some precept before
all the rest by a particular distinguished mark, by this sign they declare
their will that their subjects should have special regard for the precept thus
particularly marked.

6. The respect in which these commands surpassed the others, which were not
adorned with these ornaments,

must ultimately concern their obligation and observance. A precept's excellence
is its usefulness for securing the intended ends, and this usefulness implies a
greater necessity of observance.

7. Since, therefore, it was not within the Jews' power to reject or alter even
the least command of God, but they were bound to observe all of them in a holy
manner, it is certain that the excellence of these precepts consisted in this:
that they were to be perpetual.

8. This becomes more evident when those marks of excellence by which these
precepts are distinguished from the others indicate their perpetuity plainly,
as is the case here. This will appear from a brief explanation and vindication
of those privileges.

But this point must first be premised: these precepts are several times called
by the Holy Spirit *par excellence* the *ten words* (Exodus 34:28)—*those ten
words*; likewise Deuteronomy 4:13 and 10:4, עשרת הדברים. This demonstrates
quite clearly the special excellence of these commands.
