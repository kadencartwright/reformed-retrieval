PHILIPPIANS 2:12.

--- In fear and fear, work the work of your salvation.

It is asked whether, because exhortations by their nature serve to engender
fear, the force of the promise by which God absolutely promises perseverance
therefore disappears. The Remonstrants affirm it, *Synodical Writings*, p.
278. We deny it.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 580 | PDF p. 629]

1. For moral exhortations and persuasions directed toward engendering fear of
not persevering would be ridiculous when applied to a physical and brutish
perseverance devoid of all cognition. It would be as if God exhorted heaven
and earth to persevere in their being, though they will persevere by physical
necessity. Psalm 119:19: “They continue to this day.” Moral exhortations
directed toward engendering fear, however, are most fitting means for those
who, by the divine promise, will absolutely persevere morally, ethically, and
with sure knowledge.

2. The force of the promise of absolute perseverance is not removed when a
saint piously fears that he may fall away, because the fear that I may fall
away, as a means of not falling away, is itself also absolutely promised,
Jeremiah 32:40–41.

3. The fear that I may fall away does not conflict with the promise that I
shall never fall away, any more than David's faith by which he believed that
he would become king of Israel conflicted with the pious caution by which he
fled Saul's wrath and the hands with which Saul intended to kill him.
Similarly, the faith of Joseph and Mary—by which they believed that God, by
his irresistible power, would preserve the infant Christ alive until, having
grown to more advanced years, he would offer himself to God upon the wood of
the cross as a sacrifice for the sins of the world—by no means conflicted with
the pious caution by which Joseph and Mary fled into Egypt with the

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 581 | PDF p. 630 | split word joined: puero]

child. They did so by God's special command, lest Herod kill the infant Christ
along with the other infants. For the faith by which I believe that I shall
absolutely persevere and the fear by which I fear that I may not persevere do
not have contradictorily opposed objects, nor is the contradiction in the same
respect. The object of faith is God, by his irresistible grace producing
caution in me so that I do not fall away. The object of fear is defection on
the hypothesis that I shall be culpably negligent and less cautious in
persevering. Here the object of faith is God's promised irresistible action,
for my action of persevering is not properly the object of faith. The object
of fear is my culpable negligence and want of pious fear.

You will say that you need not fear that you may fall away, because God will
irresistibly bring it about that you do not fall away. Response: 1. He brings
it about that I fear I may fall away, so that this fear is a means of securing
my not falling away. 2. God's not producing pious fear in me is not the object
of pious fear. Indeed, it is sinful to fear that what must be believed by
divine faith will occur. The formal object of fear is instead my natural
negligence and security, not my apostasy. Thus fear properly so called has no
place here with respect to the outcome, but only caution and pious care for
the means. 3. Fear is twofold: one kind concerns the end, namely, eternal
death; the other concerns the means tending toward that end, namely, my

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 582 | PDF p. 631 | split word joined: peccata]

sins. Fear concerning the end is twofold: one kind is absolute and the other
conditional. Absolute fear concerning the end exists when someone fears
eternal death with respect to its actual infliction. This fear is not
commanded to the regenerate in exhortations or threats. It is the fear of
despair, to which God exhorts no one, and it is formally the fault of
unbelief: the fear that an outcome will occur whose prevention rests with God
alone. This accords with the common saying, “Our part is to care for our
duties, but God's to care for outcomes.” For fear properly so called obliges
us to believe that God is truthful in his threats and will therefore actually
inflict the evil he threatens. Yet to believe that God will actually inflict
eternal death is diametrically opposed to the faith by which the elect person
is bound to believe in final perseverance and eternal life.

The other kind is conditional fear. It is horror and fear at eternal death
being inflicted upon me, not simply, but upon me as one who voluntarily
neglects the day of salvation, or if I should neglect the day of salvation.
This fear cannot be absent from one who, with legal faith, believes both God's
legal and his evangelical threats. The former fear is called despair and,
because it formally includes sin, cannot be commanded to the justified. The
fear of horror, however, which is called conditional, can very well exist in
someone certainly persuaded that, as to the outcome, he will escape the evil
to be inflicted. For who

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 583 | PDF p. 632]

does not shudder and fear when he sees another person's head cut off by a
sword, even if he is persuaded that he himself will escape that death? Who
could stand on a riverbank beside the threatening abyss of a very deep river
and not shudder, even if he knows with certainty that he will never be
submerged there? Thus 2 Corinthians 5:11 says, “Knowing therefore the terror
of the Lord, we persuade men,” and so forth. Paul was most fully persuaded
that no contrivance of the world, flesh, or devil could separate him from the
love of God which is in Christ Jesus, Romans 8:53–39. Nevertheless, Paul did
not lack an experiential horror and trembling at the last judgment.

There is also fear concerning the means, and this too is twofold: one kind is
conditional and the other absolute. Conditional fear exists when a regenerate
person, on the hypothesis that he is deserted even for a time, doubts and
fears concerning God's favor and his own perseverance. While that doubt
remains, not to fear but to be secure is evil and sinful, although considered
simply, he is bound to put away his doubting conscience and fear. Hence on
that hypothesis this fear is a rational matter (*chrēma ti*). The other
kind is absolute fear concerning the means, when a regenerate person fears
that he may indulge his own sloth and pay the penalties of his drowsiness and
supine negligence. When the object of this fear is final drowsiness and
carelessness accompanied by impenitence, this fear is the same as despair of
one's own perseverance and is damnable. The person who fears in this manner

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 584 | PDF p. 633]

doubts about the outcome that properly belongs to God, and doubts whether God
will fulfill his promises to put pious fear in the heart, Jeremiah 32:40. But
when the object of this fear is one's native inclination to defect, and the
fear is nothing other than care to do what God commands and caution and flight
from sloth, drowsiness, and rebellion, then it is pious fear and the genuine
effect of exhortations and threats.

From this it is easy to answer the Synodists' argument in article 5, p. 292:
Things that can in no way produce the effect for which they are by nature
suited are employed in vain as means for producing it. But if the faithful
cannot fall away, the threats of eternal death made to them can in no way
produce the effect for which they are by nature suited. Therefore, and so
forth. The minor premise is proved as follows. Threats are suited to engender
fear of evil, namely, of eternal death. But the fear of eternal death cannot
be engendered in faithful people who are persuaded that they will never fall
away, for they possess a sure confidence that God neither wills nor is able to
inflict upon them that evil of eternal death, because of the absolute decree
of election that he has made.

Response: Threats of eternal death, with respect to the truly regenerate, are
improper threats, for they have something peculiar which human threats do
not, just as promises do with respect to reprobate

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 585 | PDF p. 634 | split word joined: non-credentium]

unbelievers. With respect to the regenerate and insofar as they are made to
them, they are not threats properly so called. For this threat, “I will
inflict eternal death upon Peter if he is impenitent,” is equivalent to this:
“I will inflict eternal death upon Peter if I deny him the efficacious grace
of believing and repenting.” This in turn is equivalent to: “I will that Peter
die eternally if I reprobated him from eternity.” But this supposition posits
nothing at all, because a condition posits nothing when the conditioned thing
is nonbeing and nothing. Peter, however, was not reprobated from eternity.
Therefore the minor premise and its proof are false, because God does not will
that Peter, as a believer, fear eternal death. Nor are threats suited to
engender fear of eternal death. They are suited instead to caution and the
anxious use of means in the manner of a person fearing eternal death. For this
threat, “If David does not flee from Keilah, Saul will kill him,” is not suited
to engender in David the fear that he will never be king of Israel or to make
him distrust God, who said through the prophet that he would become king of
Israel. If God says to Christ, “Flee the people attempting to stone you,” it
is not lawful to infer from this, “Therefore fear that you may never redeem
the world through death on the cross.” What has been said elsewhere about the
evangelical promises preached to each and every person in the visible church,
but actually pertaining only to the sons

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 586 | PDF p. 635]

of the promise, as Paul speaks in Romans 9, must also be said here about the
evangelical threats preached among a mixed multitude but, in God's intention,
pertaining only to the reprobate and unregenerate.
