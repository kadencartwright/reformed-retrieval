# CHAPTER XXV

## Dr. Burmann's principal argument is refuted

Dr. Burmann proposes and pursues at greater length another argument against
the moral character of the fourth precept in *Inquiry concerning the morality
of the weekly Sabbath*, page 17, in this manner: [The precept (*he says*)
concerning the external weekly Sabbath is not moral but ceremonial, as is
evident even from its parts. For whatever is in it is either the
*determination of the seventh day*, the *rest commanded on that day*, or, as
some add, the *sanctification of that rest* for sacred worship.]

*Response 1.* I wish he had distinctly explained to us what he understands by
the *external* Sabbath. In the preceding discussion he does indeed speak of a
certain spiritual and

[Printed p. 259 | IA PDF 267 | IA leaf 267 | IA decausadei02brow]

mystical Sabbath, and this external Sabbath must be something distinct from
it. Yet he does not indicate what he properly understands by it. If by it he
understands the sanctification, or holy observance, of a principal and solemn
time instituted by God, that is, of one day in the weekly cycle, we say that
the precept concerning this weekly Sabbath is moral. But if he understands
something else, we cannot acknowledge it as the weekly Sabbath whose precept
we say is moral. 2. The determination of the seventh day can be taken in two
ways: either as the determination of the *seventh day in number*, that is, of
one day in the weekly cycle; or as the determination of the *seventh day in
order*, or the last day in the week. If he understands the *latter*, the
argument will amount to nothing, because it will not contain a complete
enumeration. For we say, and will prove later, that the fourth precept requires
one day in the weekly cycle to be consecrated to God. Let us now see what he
says about the individual parts.

[1) The determination of the seventh day (*he says*), as was seen before, was
an entirely positive matter. For it is indifferent by its own nature to
assemble at such a time and place; and since no reason can be given from the
nature of the matter why the assembly should occur on this rather than
another day, it depended entirely upon God's free good pleasure and
ordination. Thus, although this determination rests upon natural equity, it is
itself only positive.]

*Response 1.* We concede that the determination of the seventh day, whether
the *seventh in order* or the *seventh in number*, was entirely positive with
respect to God. Yet it does not follow from this that such a determination is
not, or cannot be, natural or moral to us; or that everything which is
positive with respect to God must also be positive or non-moral to us. 2. We
concede that, by its own nature and before the divine determination, it is
indifferent to assemble at such a time and place. Yet it does not follow that
it is equally indifferent to assemble at such a time or place after the
divine determination has once been made, or that the divine determination
cannot render it moral for us to assemble at such a time to celebrate his
praises. 3. We deny the supposition that everything which depends upon God's
free good pleasure and ordination is merely positive and not moral for us. 4.
We also deny that nothing positive can be moral for us, for above, in Book I,
Chapter XV, we showed that there is such a thing as moral-positive.

What, then, does he say about the second member of his proof, namely, *rest*?
[As for *rest* (*he says*), that matter is indifferent in itself and is not of
itself moral or a virtue, as is evident because it applies even to beasts,
according to the fourth precept, as well as to unbelievers. Nor does it become
moral when a precept under which it falls is added to it, for nothing
indifferent in itself can become moral through a supervening precept.]

*Response 1.* I concede that rest considered in itself is an indifferent
matter. But sheer rest, or idleness, is one thing, while ceasing from daily
works is another. The former is not commanded by this precept, but the latter
is enjoined. 2. Again, ceasing from works, or not working, considered purely
in itself, is one thing; not working here and now, when God commands it and
when the solemn worship of God and the celebration or sanctification of a
Solemn Time necessarily require it, is another. 3. Sheer rest does indeed
apply to beasts, but not working according to a precept does not apply to
beasts. Therefore, although sheer rest is a matter indifferent in itself and
is not moral or a virtue, a precept concerning not working can be moral. For
sheer rest and not working differ: the *latter* falls

[Printed p. 260 | IA PDF 268 | IA leaf 268 | IA decausadei02brow]

under a precept, the *former* does not. The latter is directed toward a good
end, the former is not. The latter conduces to promoting the solemn worship
of God, the former does not. 4. Furthermore, although not working, considered
in itself and absolutely, is indifferent and non-moral, not working according
to the fourth precept, that is, in order to celebrate and consecrate a solemn
time to God, can be moral, since it is necessarily required for a moral end.
For not working is not enjoined here for the sake of sheer idleness or mere
rest, but for the sake of a solemn time that is to be sanctified and
consecrated to God. One who is purely idle, or merely does no work, on the
Sabbath day does not obey this precept. Rather, one who does no work in order
to sanctify the Sabbath, to celebrate the solemn time appointed by God, or to
extol God solemnly at a solemn time obeys this command. Therefore, although
not working is not moral in itself, not working in such a manner is moral, a
part of our moral obedience, and something religious pertaining to the
worship of God and the first table of the Decalogue. This *not working* is far
different from natural cessation or rest, which is pure non-action, because
it is a commanded cessation or non-operation enjoined for a special end:
namely, that a solemn time be sanctified and God's solemn worship be
celebrated at a solemn time. It is thus a solemn and religious cessation or
non-operation. 5. It is false that nothing indifferent in itself before a
precept is given can be made moral by a supervening precept, as is evident
from what was said in Book I, Chapter XIV.

The celebrated Essenius, in his *Vindications*, page 54, responded as follows
to the points just refuted: [Response 1. Rest, as commanded by God, is moral
by virtue of the command and possesses the character of virtue, although in
other respects it is indifferent in itself and non-moral of itself. 2. As it
is commanded, namely, for sanctification, it does not apply to beasts,
although God at the same time commanded the rest of beasts so that people who
are otherwise accustomed to employ animals for labors might rest all the
more. Yet he did so in such a way that human beings render rational obedience
in their rest and thus devote themselves to pious and sacred exercises, while
beasts rest in their own manner, that is, irrationally. 3. Unbelievers, while
they remain such, cannot rightly render obedience to any divine precept.
Nevertheless, they are bound by the moral law to moral obedience, including
in the fourth precept.]

To this Dr. Burmann says in his *Apology*, page 62: *Therefore a thing that is
not moral of itself becomes moral and the image of God, so that to rest is to
be like God.* He adds: *as it is commanded, namely, for sanctification, it
does not apply to beasts. Correct, when an internal sanctification is
invented; but since rest according to the fourth precept also applies to
beasts, being given to them by human beings according to the precept, and
that rest is the sanctification of that day according to the letter of the
precept, it is clear that no sanctification according to the fourth precept is
understood here except that which consists in separating the day for rest,
which includes beasts also. Where, then, is the moral character of the rest?*
*Response 1.* Just as the image of God is said to consist in obedience
rendered to God and his precepts, so a thing that is not moral in itself can
become moral and a part of the image of God. 2. To rest according to the
precept is to comply with God and to imitate God's example, and it is no less
being like God than obeying parents according to the fifth precept is. 3.
When the most illustrious Essenius said that rest, as commanded for
sanctification, does not apply to beasts, he invented no such internal
sanctification as Dr. Burmann imagines, but understood exercises of religion,
both private

[Printed p. 261 | IA PDF 269 | IA leaf 269 | IA decausadei02brow]

and public, by which the Sabbath day is sanctified and spent to God's
glory. This rest is necessarily required for that sanctification of the
Sabbath, and for this end the precept requires that rest, or cessation from
our works. 4. Rest according to the fourth precept, that is, rest ordered
toward sanctifying the Sabbath, does not apply to beasts but to human beings.
They, indeed, are commanded not to employ animals in their labors; beasts are
not commanded to rest. 5. That rest is not the sanctification of that day
according to the fourth precept, but only a means to that sanctification. 6.
It is therefore false that no sanctification according to the fourth precept
is understood here except that which consists in separating the day for
rest. For the day is not separated for rest, but for worshiping God solemnly
throughout that entire day, both privately and publicly. Rest is consequently
required, and it includes beasts only to this extent: that human beings, for
whom it is fitting to devote the day to God, are not permitted to employ them
for labor.

Dr. Burmann proceeds in his *Inquiry*: [A precept indeed makes a thing
necessary, but not moral if it was not moral before. For the nature of the
thing itself is not changed by the precept; rather, since it was indifferent
and non-moral in itself, it remains such afterward and does not lose its
nature.]

And *Response 1.* It has not yet been established that anything is morally
good or evil antecedently to God's good pleasure in prohibiting *this* and
commanding *that*. See Book I, Chapter II. And whence, I ask, is an action said
to be morally good or evil? Is it not because it conforms to, or differs from,
God's law? A thing's physical nature indeed is not changed by a precept, but
the moral nature of the thing is changed by a precept. For an action that in
itself is neither morally good nor evil, but indifferent, is made morally good
by a divine precept and morally evil by its prohibition. It is vice and sin
to do what is prohibited, or to omit and not do what is commanded. It is
virtue and obedience to do what is commanded and avoid what is prohibited.
Yet I do not say that everything God bids to be done is moral in an equal or
every respect, or moral *par excellence*. For some things are political, some
ceremonial, and some moral by preeminence. Nevertheless, they all assume some
moral character and are no longer indifferent but morally good or evil
according as they are commanded or prohibited. It depends upon God's good
pleasure that they are moral and moral to such a degree: that they are
prescribed only to certain people and only for a time, or enjoined upon
everyone and always; that they are peculiar to a particular administration of
the covenant, or common to all administrations of the covenant of grace; that
they are given to form and regulate morals, or for a political end, or,
finally, instituted for a ceremonial or peculiarly ecclesiastical end. 3. It
would certainly be astonishing if a thing indifferent in itself remained
such even after a precept, since a precept makes an indifferent thing
necessary. For whatever is necessary, insofar as it is necessary, is not
indifferent: what is indifferent may be done or not done, but what is
necessary morally cannot be left undone. 4. Yet it is not only God's command
enjoining cessation from daily works that enters into consideration here, but
also the end for which such cessation is commanded, namely, the sanctification
of the Sabbath and celebration of a solemn time. For the Lord did not simply
command cessation from our works (although that would be sufficient

[Printed p. 262 | IA PDF 270 | IA leaf 270 | IA decausadei02brow]

to render that cessation or vacation morally good), but commands that
cessation occur for this end: that the Sabbath be sanctified and God be
worshiped so much more solemnly. This end is moral and always necessary:
namely, that the Solemn Time which God instituted so that he might be solemnly
worshiped in it be observed. And this means is always equally necessary for
attaining that end.

The words the celebrated Essenius offers in reply above, page 55, are in
agreement: [What is indifferent in itself (*he says*) becomes necessary
through a supervening precept. Correct. But what, pray, is that necessity? It
must undoubtedly be known from the opposite indifference. What, then, is this
indifference? It is not viewed *in the category of being*, whereby a thing can
absolutely exist or not exist, but *in the category of morals*, so that moral
goodness and moral evil are absent from the matter, and it may therefore be
done or omitted without sin and without the character of virtue. Generally
and abstracted from circumstances and the moral norm, waking, sleeping,
eating, drinking, lying down, standing, sitting, walking, and so forth are of
this kind. This is moral indifference, which has a place in all those things
that have not been determined by divine law, whether natural or positive. By
the same operation the nature of the opposite necessity arising from a divine
precept is known: namely, a necessity, or determination, *in the category of
morals*, so that the thing now definitely and necessarily has moral goodness
if it conforms to the precept, or moral evil if it differs from the precept.
This is clear. I concede that whether the precept exists or not, the nature of
the thing *in the category of being* is not changed through the precept; but
its quality *in the category of morals* is indeed changed. Thus it becomes
entirely moral through the precept, so that it now has either moral goodness
or moral evil. A precept that introduces that moral distinction is truly
moral; and if it introduces it universally, not restrictively—for example, to
the Old Testament polity or until the time of reformation—it is moral *par
excellence* and must and customarily is distinguished from the political or
forensic law of the Old Testament and from the ceremonial-typical law.]

What does Dr. Burmann say to this in his *Vindication*, page 62, section 6?
[The dilemma paraded on page 55 has long since been removed: *what is
indifferent in itself becomes necessary through a supervening precept*. But
just as the *indifference*, so also the *necessity* or *determination* here is
not in the *category of being* but in the *category of morals*; and so rest,
which is indifferent in itself, becomes *moral* through the precept. In place
of the dilemma he should have posited a trilemma and added a third member:
rest becomes necessary through a precept neither in the category of *being*
nor of *morals*, but of *decree or ordinance*. He would then have seen that
neither horn of his dilemma strikes its target.] *Response.* A novel doctrine,
as I see it, requires novel terms and novel distinctions, unheard of until
now; otherwise it cannot be defended. Who ever before spoke this way or said
that an action, although conformable to God's law, is nevertheless not morally
good but only good by decree? Therefore, it seems, all moral matters are
indifferent—that is, neither good nor evil—by decree, and what is good or evil
by decree is morally indifferent: mere evasions.

Dr. Burmann proceeds in his *Inquiry*: [Nor does it become moral because it is
directed to a moral end and use. For if whatever is done for a moral reason
and referred to a moral end thereby becomes moral, then all ceremonial things
instituted for a moral end would also have been moral, such as circumcision,
sacrifices, and so forth. Those things were indeed means, aids, and instruments
toward a Moral end, yet they themselves were not moral; for the action itself
is one thing, its direction toward a good end another.]

[Printed p. 263 | IA PDF 271 | IA leaf 271 | IA decausadei02brow]

Dr. Essenius responded as follows in his *Vindication*, page 56: [What is
properly moral arises from God's precept, whether it flows immediately from
his most holy nature, or a free determination of the will toward this or that
part intervenes, *in the category of morals*. The nature of a precept is truly
moral, and things subordinate on account of the precept must be referred to
their moral end. 2. The ceremonial things themselves, such as circumcision,
sacrifices, and so forth, besides an evangelical relation, in which they were
“a shadow of things to come, but the body is Christ's,” also had a legal
relation. They thus had a moral determination, so that they had to be
performed according to the divine prescription and from conformity with it
possessed moral goodness, but from nonconformity, moral evil. This was a
certain common character of morality. But since the evangelical relation in
those things itself carried a determination to a definite period, until the
time of reformation and fulfillment, and since they were accordingly not
commanded from the beginning of the creation of humankind, nor all at once,
nor to all nations, nor especially to those who live under the New Testament
administration, they are therefore contradistinguished from things called
moral *par excellence*. 3. Nor does it help to repeat that *the action itself
is one thing and its direction toward a good end another*. We are by no means
ignorant of that. We know it, and in addition we know from God's word that the
action itself concerning which God gave a precept must not only be referred
to a good end, but must itself also be conformed to that precept, so that it
may thereby be morally good. If it is done otherwise, it is morally evil,
even if it were otherwise directed toward a good end. For what else is an
action morally good before God but the action of a rational creature as such,
conformable to the divine law or precept? And what is morally evil but such an
action nonconformable to that norm? I speak plainly so that I may be
understood. I have no desire to play with ambiguities or practice disputes
over words.] Thus far he; I see nothing Dr. Burmann offered in reply to it.

Allow me to add these points. We do not say that this cessation from our works
is moral because it is directed toward a moral end and use. For we know that
the action itself is one thing and its direction toward a good end another.
Someone can direct an evil and prohibited action toward a good end, but the
prohibited action always remains evil. Nor do we say that this cessation is
moral because it is instituted by God as some means and aid toward a moral
end, but because it is instituted as a means of such a kind that it is
necessary—always and everywhere—for attaining and promoting that end, and is
not a ceremonial or political means but rather a natural one. Hence it is
evident that the nature of this means differs from that of ceremonial means.
For the latter were not means always or everywhere necessary to that moral
end, but only for a time, while that old administration endured, and among the
Jews alone and the few proselytes converted to that religion. This cessation
from works, however, is always and everywhere necessary for sanctifying a
solemn time to God. In its orientation toward that moral end it has no
ceremonial relation, but a moral or natural one, which cannot be said of the
ceremonies. For the latter indicated no moral or natural relation to God's
solemn worship, but only a ceremonial one. God could be solemnly worshiped
without all of them, and is so worshiped today. But God never could, and
cannot now, be solemnly worshiped at a solemn time without this cessation from
our daily works.

What does Dr. Burmann say further? [In that way all our actions, even natural
ones, and all

[Printed p. 264 | IA PDF 272 | IA leaf 272 | IA decausadei02brow]

others, such as eating, drinking, and whatever we do, would be moral, since we
must refer all things to the good end of God's glory, 1 Corinthians 10:31.]

Dr. Essenius answers in this manner: [Actions of eating, drinking, and all
similar things which are subject to reason must be referred to the good end of
God's glory, 1 Corinthians 10:31. Correct, for God himself prescribed this;
and it is similarly prescribed that they be done from faith, without a
reluctant or doubting conscience, Romans 14:5, 14, 22, 23; with love, without
offense to others, Romans 14:13, 15, 16, 19, 20, 21; 1 Corinthians 8:9–13;
and temperately, so that strength is refreshed, not oppressed, Luke 21:34;
Romans 13:13, 14; Titus 2:12. Observance of all these things makes those
actions morally good in their circumstances and manner of performance,
through conformity with the rule of moral goodness; but in any departure from
that norm there is sin or moral evil. Furthermore, he calls them *natural
actions*, but I think he understands those which pertain to our natural and
animal life, although they also are subject to reason. Otherwise things are
also called *natural actions* which are not subject to reason and proceed
without our consciousness or deliberation, such as the digestion of food in
the stomach, the motion of the heart and blood, the pulse of the arteries, and
so forth. Such actions do not proceed from a rational creature insofar as it
is such, and consequently rational obedience or disobedience, moral goodness
or evil, have no place in them. Although they possess natural goodness, or
the strength of nature, they nevertheless are and remain indifferent in the
*category of morals*.] Thus far he, and here too I see nothing offered in
reply by Burmann.

I will add this. Actions that lie within our power, so that we may do or not
do them here and now at our discretion, and which, whenever we do them, we
must direct toward God's glory, are one thing. Actions necessary for attaining
a good end, and therefore specially commanded, which we cannot omit or
neglect without violating a divine precept, are another. Furthermore, actions
we are bound, whenever we perform them, to direct toward the general good end,
namely, God's glory, are one thing. Actions which we cannot omit or even defer,
and which by God's special command we must direct toward a special end as
necessary, fitting, and divinely enjoined means, are another. Hence it is
evident that even if the former actions were not to be called moral, this
vacation from our labors so that we may solemnly worship God and observe a
solemn time will nevertheless be moral, and a necessary and moral means toward
that moral end, because it too is specially commanded for this special end.
To eat and drink ordinarily is one thing; to eat and drink sacramentally is
another. We direct the *former* toward the general end, but the *latter* toward
the special end for which it is specially commanded. So too, ordinarily to
cease from our labors on working days is one thing, while to cease from them
on the Sabbath day is *another*. The *former* must indeed be directed to God's
glory whenever it is done, and lies within our power. The *latter* does not
lie within our power, but is specially commanded so that it cannot be omitted;
and it is specially commanded so that it may be directed toward a special
moral end, namely, the worship of God. Therefore it is not only moral in the
way other things may be called moral which are directed toward the general
moral end and conform to God's law, but has this added besides: that it is
done by the force of a more specific divine precept. And so this cessation

[Printed p. 265 | IA PDF 273 | IA leaf 273 | IA decausadei02brow]

is moral not only in respect of the more general end, as are our common
actions, which we can do or omit when we wish, but also because it is itself
specially commanded as a necessary means for celebrating a solemn time,
sanctifying the Sabbath day, and solemnly worshiping God on the Sabbath day.

Dr. Burmann proceeds in his *Inquiry*: *Therefore the rest itself and its
observance were only bodily exercise, which is contradistinguished from piety
or moral virtue*, 1 Timothy 4:8.

*Response.* Mere rest is not exercise but rather lack of exercise, while
vacation from our labors at a solemn time is a setting apart for the solemn
worship of God. Or, as Dr. Essenius has it above, page 57, to rest according
to the precept, as is said in Luke 23:56, *is not simply bodily exercise, but
an act of piety and moral virtue; indeed, an act of religion and love for God,
which, although not elicited, is nevertheless commanded on the supposition of
such a divine precept, to which obedience must be rendered.*

What does Dr. Burmann say to this? [Here (*he says*, page 63) he is in harmony
neither with the matter itself nor with his own words. *Paul calls piety
lawful and spiritual worship, with which he compares bodily mortifications.
He calls bodily exercise not the immoderate kind, much less the superstitious,
or rather impious, kind, but nevertheless a strict regimen useful for
subduing the flesh.* Thus the venerable Beza. Yet is not such mortification
of the body according to the precept in 1 Corinthians 9:27? Therefore,
according to our author, it will be an act of piety, which, according to Beza,
Paul calls bodily exercise. The same must be said of rest and every bodily
exercise rendered according to a precept. For if it were contrary to a
precept, it would not be useful for *a little* but would be useful for nothing.
Indeed, he ought to have acknowledged this very thing when he admits that
such acts are not elicited acts of love for God but commanded ones. See our
eighth axiom. Therefore they are not *spiritual worship*, and thus not piety,
according to Beza. Since he admits this, and yet wishes those acts to be acts
of piety and not bodily exercise, he is also contrary to himself.] *Response.*
I ask whether obedience rendered to the second table of the law is to be
referred to piety. If this is conceded, then Paul calls *piety* something
other than spiritual worship; otherwise that obedience too will be called
spiritual worship. And why not also vacation from works on the Sabbath day?
If it is denied, then those precepts of the second table will not be moral,
but the obedience rendered to them will be mere bodily exercise. For the force
of Dr. Burmann's argument is placed in this: that whatever is referred to
bodily exercise and contradistinguished from piety is not moral. 2. Is every
act that is not an elicited act of love for God mere bodily exercise? We will
indeed not deny that vacation from our works should be called *bodily
exercise*, but we will add further that notwithstanding this it can be, and
is, moral, just as all other acts of piety and obedience that are not elicited
acts of love for God are. 3. We examined and considered long ago in Book I
what he said in the eighth axiom. 4. Beza does not say that all acts which are
not elicited but only commanded acts of love for God are to be referred to
bodily exercise rather than piety. He refers there only to bodily
mortifications, a strict regimen for subduing the flesh, and similar things,
which, as Calvin says on the passage, are nothing but the rudiments of a
childish discipline: vigils, prolonged fasting, thirst, recitations of
prayers, readings, lying on the ground, or similar bodily afflictions
undertaken for the sake of religion. From

[Printed p. 265 (repeated; sequence requires p. 266) | IA PDF 274 | IA leaf 274 | IA decausadei02brow]

these things it is sufficiently manifest that Dr. Essenius is contrary
neither to himself nor to the matter itself.

Dr. Burmann adds in his *Inquiry*: [And how could observing it have been
moral, since such things could not *perfect with respect to conscience* the
person who did them—“make the worshiper perfect according to conscience,”
Hebrews 9:9—which is nevertheless proper to moral virtue?] *Response.* How
will it be proved that observance of the Sabbath cannot perfect with respect
to conscience? In the cited passage the apostle indeed says that sacrifices
and the other gifts which were offered according to the ceremonial law could
not perfect the worshiper in conscience. But we do not read this said by the
apostle about observing the Sabbath or ceasing from works on the Sabbath day.

Let us hear what the celebrated Essenius answered, so that we may better judge
Dr. Burmann's vindication. He says as follows in his *Vindication*, page 57:
[In Hebrews 9:9 the apostle denies that the typical ceremonies of the Old
Testament could *perfect the worshiper in conscience*, just as in verse 13 he
also asserts that they *sanctified for the purity of the flesh*, insofar as
those things were considered in themselves, that is, apart from Christ, and
had no efficacy but a carnal, external, and temporal one in that
administration. Nevertheless, when referred to Christ they certainly
possessed the character of a sign, and thus a moral instrumental efficacy
toward the conscience, or the inward and eternal state of a human being. But
what is denied of the typical ceremonies considered in themselves is asserted
of Christ as the truth and body of those shadows. Thus verse 14: *How much
more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself
blameless to God, cleanse your conscience*, and so forth. Yet this perfection
in conscience is not ascribed to our moral virtues. Indeed, after the fall,
perfection cannot be perfected or obtained in this way, just as justification
cannot be obtained from our own works. Thus the Inquirer accomplishes nothing
by his argument. But why should I not turn it back upon its author? That we
are perfected in conscience through Christ and his most holy offering does
not pertain, nor is it customarily referred, to God's nature absolutely and
as contradistinguished from decree, but to his will and good pleasure, Hebrews
10:9, 10: *Then* Christ *said, Behold, I am present to do your will, O God—by
which will we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus once
made.* Ephesians 1:7, 8, 9: *In him we have redemption through his blood, the
remission of offenses, according to the riches of his grace, which he caused
to abound toward us in all wisdom and prudence, after he made known to us the
mystery of his will*—note well—*according to his benevolent good pleasure*,
or decree, *which he purposed in himself.* Nor did Christ, in preparing and
sanctifying himself for that blameless offering, render obedience only to
those precepts which pertain to the law natural to God himself, but also to
those which pertain to positive law. He himself speaks plainly enough about
the Baptism which he wished to receive in Matthew 3:15: *for thus it becomes
us to fulfill all righteousness.* So also concerning the observance of the
Sabbath in Christ. See what was cited from the most excellent Cloppenburg
above, page 15, number 10. These are his words: [*For our present purpose we
assert against the Anabaptists* that the external Sabbath commanded in the
fourth precept was not taken away by Christ or his apostles, either by
doctrine or example. *For Christ did not even assail the Jewish Sabbath in the
days of his flesh; but in this too he presented himself as the fulfiller of
the law, who had come to fulfill the law, so much so*

[Printed p. 267 | IA PDF 275 | IA leaf 275 | IA decausadei02brow]

*that when he was accused by the Jews of violating the Sabbath, he appealed
to a strict judgment without exception for his person*, John 7:24. *And
although he deservedly asserted himself to be Lord of the Sabbath, that is,
the Lawgiver himself and, by that title, the self-authenticating Interpreter
of the law, he nevertheless wished to be judged according to that exactness, or
literal sense, of the written law which could be learned from Moses himself*,
Matthew 12:6, 8; Mark 2:28; Luke 6:5, and so forth.] In these things also,
therefore, there is moral virtue, for to *perfect with respect to conscience*,
according to the inquirer himself, *is proper to moral virtue*.

He has similar things later, pages 67, 68: [For the apostle (*he says*) in
Hebrews 9:13 and 9, *sanctification* and *perfection* denote that efficacy
against our sins which is denied to the other sacrifices and typical
ceremonies of the Old Testament but ascribed to Christ's sacrifice once
performed. Properly, therefore, the expiation of sins is understood here, and
thus the obtaining of salvation and deliverance from sins and their guilt
with respect to the inward state before God and the eternal state. Thus
expiation and the obtaining of salvation belong only to Christ's true, unique,
and all-sufficient sacrifice as a ransom. But this did not at all belong to
the other sacrifices and typical ceremonies of the Old Testament, any more
than the reality and efficacy of a body belong to a shadow. Yet by divine
institution a certain sprinkling, cleansing, and so forth did belong to them
with respect to the flesh and the outward and temporal state in the Israelite
polity. Thus those expiated were no longer considered ceremonially unclean,
but clean, and so forth. This itself pertained to the character of the type,
but in itself by no means reached to the conscience, that is, the inward and
eternal state, so as to extinguish guilt or cleanse the mind. This is what the
apostle teaches, and by this argument he recalls the Hebrews from shadowy
types to the truth in Christ. Application, which is performed subjectively in
the faithful, corresponds to that obtaining. Christ thus applies by calling,
regenerating, justifying, sanctifying, and so forth, where chiefly the real,
supernatural efficacy of the Holy Spirit must be regarded. Nor must the moral
cooperation of the word, sacraments, and so forth, and the instrumental,
receptive efficacy of our faith be omitted, nor the declarative and
confirmatory operation of our gratitude, new obedience, and so forth. In this
way subjective application in believers under the Old Testament was also
accomplished in its own manner through the faithful and pious observance of
those types. For those types, by morally signifying and prefiguring, taught
them the truth which is in Christ; through faith they at the same time
instrumentally received Christ with his benefits; and through the obedience
which they rendered to God from faith, even in those ceremonial, typical
institutions, their righteousness in Christ and true holiness were declared,
because the certainty of righteousness and inward holiness itself also
received a subjective increase. Throughout, Christ and his Spirit retained
their own principal and real efficacy with respect to the application itself.
Meanwhile, let the reader weigh and compare these sacred testimonies: Acts
15:11; Romans 4:11; 1 Corinthians 10:1–4; 2 Corinthians 4:13; Hebrews 4:2;
11:4, 5, and so forth.]

Let us now see what Dr. Burmann offered in reply. In his *Apology*, page 63,
section 8, he answers as follows: [*On pages 57 and 67, 68 he is prolix in
explaining* Hebrews 9:9, *and we will not reply to each point individually.*
We say only this: for Paul, *to perfect according to conscience* is to be
justice—there is perhaps a typographical error here for *just*—or to cause
the worshiper no longer to have a consciousness of sin, Hebrews 10:2, *as a
means of one who has been ex-*

[Printed p. 268 | IA PDF 276 | IA leaf 276 | IA decausadei02brow]

*piated, and that the conscience be cleansed from dead works to serve the
living God*, Hebrews 9:14, *which also pertains to inward sanctification, as
he himself admits more than once on page 68. But what prevents precepts which
are decrees from perfecting or cleansing the conscience? If those works are
morally good, righteousness and remission of sins are said to be attached to
them as morally good works. This is the opinion of the Jews, whom Scripture
refutes. Surely, how does what is good and in which God takes pleasure not
cleanse the conscience? But the apostle says that those things are only for
purity of the flesh. Surely the precepts in which one lives cleanse the
conscience; and according to our author, we live in obedience to decrees.
Therefore those too perfect the conscience. Yet both Paul and he himself say
the contrary; let him teach us how it does not follow from what has been
said.*]

*Response 1.* Absolutely nothing appears here to prove that observance of the
Sabbath does not perfect with respect to conscience just as much as other
moral things, or that it is to be numbered among those things which could not
perfect with respect to conscience the one who performed them, in the sense
in which the apostle understands it. Dr. Burmann had to prove this; with it
unproved, he accomplishes nothing. 2. Does Dr. Burmann think that, now after
the fall, righteousness and remission of sins are attached to morally good
works? Or does he think that works cause a person no longer to have a
consciousness of sin, expiate sins, and cleanse the conscience from dead works
to serve the living God? Then he will certainly hold, if not the opinion of
the Jews who attributed this efficacy even to ceremonial works, at least the
opinion of the Papists, which Scripture also refutes and which is diametrically
opposed to our Reformed doctrine. 3. How will he prove that the apostle is
speaking here about ceremonies in opposition to moral matters, and thus
ascribing to moral matters what he denies to ceremonies? The text itself—as
Dr. Essenius also made manifest—and the scope of the entire epistle prove that
the apostle compares these ceremonial things, which were shadows, with the
body, namely, Christ, and denies to ceremonies and claims for Christ the
things in question. He does not compare them with moral actions, for then he
would have contradicted himself and established justification by works, which
he condemns elsewhere. 4. He asks how what is good and in which God takes
pleasure does not cleanse the conscience. What, is that teacher in Reformed
Israel ignorant of even this, or does he ask as though doubtful? Let him know,
then, that this is the true reason: it is not the blood of Christ. For only
Christ's blood meritoriously cleanses us and our conscience, and only
meritorious cleansing is under discussion here. 5. It is true that the
apostle says that sacrifices and the other shadows of Christ—about which
alone he is speaking—are only for purity of the flesh, because he
considers them apart from Christ, whom they and his complete sacrifice
foreshadowed and represented, as Dr. Essenius also explained very well. What,
then, will Dr. Burmann infer from this? Will he conclude that moral things
cleanse, purify, or perfect the conscience itself? Yet this was the error of
the Jews and today is the error of the Papists. The apostle, however, is not
speaking about ceremonies in opposition to moral things. 6. Let him show how
those precepts in which a person lives, that is, moral precepts, cleanse the
conscience; and at the same time let him show how he holds an opinion
different from that of the Jews and the Papists, and how evangelical doctrine
is not overthrown by it. We do indeed live in obedience to decrees just as in
obedience to moral precepts. With respect to justification before God—that
is, neither the former nor the latter is our righteousness before God or the
meritorious cause of our justification—both nevertheless are obedience, and
obedience keeps us free from external blows to the same extent that

[Printed p. 269 | IA PDF 277 | IA leaf 277 | IA decausadei02brow]

disobedience and contempt for divine authority, whether in moral matters or
in decrees, expose us to divine wrath.

What else does Dr. Burmann have in his *Inquiry*? [Indeed (*he says*), if that
external rest had been moral and the moral element of the Sabbath, no other
Sabbath-rest would have remained. Rather, the Jews would already have had it
in the land of Canaan, and Joshua would have settled them in that rest,
contrary to the apostle's teaching.] *Response 1.* We do not say that the
external rest was the moral element of the Sabbath, as though the precept
concerning the Sabbath required nothing else. For we have already said, and
will prove later, that this precept requires something far different. 2. Does
he understand by the Sabbath-rest of which the apostle speaks in Hebrews 4
only that which was the moral element of the fourth precept, and nothing else?
Why does he not prove this? For we deny it. 3. The Jews indeed did not possess
that arduous Sabbath-rest which the apostle said remained, but they did
possess the moral element of the fourth precept.

Dr. Essenius responds at greater length in his *Vindication*, page 58: [The
apostle (*he says*) proposes a threefold Sabbath-rest in Hebrews 4: first, the
weekly Sabbath-rest in verses 3, 4. Thus he demonstrates its divine
institution and primeval antiquity, and hence its moral character. Next he
proposes a certain typical, shadowy Sabbath-rest in the land of Canaan, to
which he immediately opposes the true heavenly rest obtained for us through
Christ and to be fully applied in the next age, as in verses 8, 11. It now by
no means follows from the moral character of the weekly Sabbath, instituted
from the laying of the world's foundation and repeated in the Decalogue, that
no heavenly Sabbath-rest remains. Moral observance is one thing; the reality
prefigured by a type is another. In the Sabbath-rest of the land of Canaan
there was an eminent type. But when we call the Sabbath of the Decalogue
moral, we by no means thereby say that it was the true reality prefigured by
that typical Sabbath-rest. Indeed, the rest of the Christian Sabbath may also
quite suitably be referred to the heavenly Sabbath-rest as a preparation or
disposition having a certain analogical correspondence with it. Yet such a
comparison and correspondence no more overthrows the moral character of our
weekly Sabbath than the moral character of our charity in this age is
overthrown by its comparison and correspondence with the charity which will
exist in heaven.]

To this Dr. Burmann responds in his *Apology*, page 63, section 9: [He dictates
that in Hebrews 4:3, 4 Paul proposes the *weekly Sabbath-rest*, already in
force from the beginning of the world upon the completion of God's works. But
the apostle there speaks expressly not about the rest of human beings, but
about God's rest, which is proper to God; he does not speak about the weekly
Sabbath rest which he commanded to the Israelites. See the illustrious
Gomarus's *Investigation of the Origin of the Sabbath, Defense*, page 302, on
these words, and the lucid commentary of the illustrious de Dieu upon them.]
*Response.* Dr. Burmann dictates that Paul there speaks of God's rest, which
is proper to God, but he does not prove it. Let him see what the illustrious
Rivet answered to Gomarus's exposition. We said enough about this above. I do
not have the commentary of the illustrious de Dieu at hand.

These are the things Dr. Burmann says about the *second* member of his
argument. He proceeds to the *third* member and says on page 17 of his
*Inquiry*: [Nor did the *sanctification of that rest* for worship itself make
it moral.] *Response.* A certain Anti-Sabbatarian, Alting, thinks otherwise.
He says that the sanctification of rest is a religious exercise enjoined in
the first table. Yet we do not admit his meaning, for he says that this rest
was a sign between God and the Israelites, and that its observance

[Printed p. 270 | IA PDF 278 | IA leaf 278 | IA decausadei02brow]

as a sign, by the force of a divine precept, was its sanctification and a
religious action. Indeed, Burmann himself asserts something of the kind, as we
will see later. But we say that rest is a necessary means to sanctification of
the Sabbath, or to celebration of a principal and solemn time; and when it is
observed in relation to God's solemn worship to be celebrated at a solemn
time, by the force of the fourth precept, it can be said to be sanctified. In
this respect we said that it is a moral part of this precept, or something
moral commanded by this precept. Let us now hear Dr. Burmann's Reasons.

[For (*he says*) first, no mention is made of such sanctification in the
fourth precept, where not rest but the day itself is commanded to be
sanctified; the sanctification of the day properly consisted in rest.]
*Response.* It is true that the fourth precept does not mention sanctification
of rest. Therefore the opinion of those who wish this precept to enjoin the
sanctification of rest is false, if by that they mean observance of rest as a
mere sign or type prefiguring some spiritual rest which the Israelites ought
principally to have kept in view. But when the fourth precept says that the
Sabbath day is to be sanctified, it also says that holy rest must necessarily
be observed, because without this rest, or cessation from our works, the
Sabbath cannot be sanctified or a solemn time observed. 2. It is false that
sanctification of the day properly consists in rest. Although rest is required
as a means to sanctification of the day, sanctification of the day nevertheless
required something else, as will become manifest later.

The most illustrious Dr. Essenius responded as follows in his *Vindication*,
page 59: [We derive moral character from God's precept. 2. The day must be
sanctified; that is expressly commanded. But that sanctification did not, and
does not, properly consist in rest itself, as was proved above, pages 51, 52,
53. Through rest from other labors properly ordained to sustain this temporal
life, therefore, they ought properly to have given their attention to those
things which pertained to the sanctification of themselves and of God in them;
and we likewise ought to do so under this administration.] I see nothing
offered in reply to this by Dr. Burmann.

Dr. Burmann proceeds in his *Inquiry*: [The fourth precept does not mention
public worship, but only that strict rest which was mysterious.] *Response.*
Although the fourth precept does not mention public worship, both public and
private worship are presupposed for celebration of a solemn time; and this
precept requires rest in relation to celebration of that solemn time. 2. It is
not yet evident in what sense this rest can be called mysterious. We
acknowledge no type in this rest insofar as it was first instituted, but say
that it was a moral means for celebrating a solemn time. If, however, some
mysterious significance was attached to the rest or its observance under the
Old Testament, it does nothing against us.

The illustrious Essenius has similar words above: [I concede that the fourth
precept does not specifically express public worship, but it comprehends it
under the general command of sanctification. This should be considered no
more foreign than the command in the first precept of knowledge, love,
reverence, worship, and so forth. 2. The mysterious character of that rest
and observance, peculiar to the Old Testament, does nothing against us.]

[Printed p. 271 | IA PDF 279 | IA leaf 279 | IA decausadei02brow]

Dr. Burmann responds in his *Apology*, page 64, section 10: [He aptly teaches
*public worship*, and so forth. The inference is this: knowledge and love of
the true God, and so forth, are comprehended under the spiritual worship of
the first precept. Therefore the command of public worship is comprehended
under external sanctification, that is, the separation of the seventh day for
rest. One who commands rest commands solemn worship; the latter is a part and
species of rest, just as knowledge, love, and so forth are a part and species
of natural worship commanded in the first precept.]

*Response.* The celebrated Essenius spoke aptly enough, although he did not
institute such a comparison or form such an inference as we see formed here.
He says only that comprehending public worship under the general command of
sanctification ought to seem no more foreign than the fact that other things
not expressed in their terms are commanded by other precepts. 2. Even if he
had made such an inference, he would have said nothing absurd. For one who
commands not rest but sanctification of the Sabbath, or separation of a
solemn time to God, also commands solemn worship whenever it is possible. And
this solemn worship is a part or species—not of rest, but—of the
sanctification of the Sabbath, just as knowledge, love, and so forth are a
part or species of the natural worship commanded in the first precept.

What else does Dr. Burmann say in his *Inquiry*? [We readily believe,
nevertheless, that the Jews performed public worship on that day, for nature
itself taught that God was to be glorified in that rest.] Dr. Essenius
responded in his *Vindication*, page 59: [How much more, therefore, is it to be
comprehended in the command of that sanctification?] Dr. Burmann offers
nothing in reply in his *Apology*. I add that the Jews did not perform public
worship on that day purely because nature taught that God was to be glorified
in that rest, but because it was a solemn time separated to God and thus to
be spent entirely in exercises of piety. They also had an express command to
offer a doubled sacrifice on that day, which nature did not dictate.

Furthermore, Dr. Burmann has the following in his *Inquiry*: [Even on the
express supposition that the fourth precept intended this, nevertheless that
sanctification of rest for worship was not moral. The worship itself is
indeed moral—indeed, most moral—but that worship, with the manner in which it
is to be performed, is described by the first three precepts, while the fourth
prescribes only the time of that worship. The time and the application of rest
in it to moral worship is not therefore moral. For, as was just said, the
action is one thing and its direction toward some end another.] *Response.*
Since the fourth precept necessarily requires God to be worshiped on the
Sabbath day both publicly and privately, the observance of rest in relation
to promoting this worship and celebrating the day will be a moral means and
our moral duty, because it is commanded by this precept and has so close and
necessary a connection with celebration of that day, as was shown above. 2.
Just as the worship is moral, so too the observance of the solemn time
prescribed here and of rest in relation to observing that solemn time is
moral, because observance of that solemn time is always equally necessary in
every state of a human being in this life. 3. This is not merely some general
direction, but a special one flowing from the nature of the matter, as has
been said.

Dr. Essenius briefly responds as follows above: [1. Why is that sanctification
not moral by reason of the precept? 2. The worship to be performed with its
manner according to the preceding precepts

[Printed p. 274 (following p. 271 in the witness) | IA PDF 280 | IA leaf 280 | IA decausadei02brow]

is moral; similarly, worship to be rendered at a definite time according to
the fourth is moral. 3. Compare what was said above on page 56, numbers 3, 5.]
What does Dr. Burmann say to this?

[On the same page (*he says*) he compares the manner of worship commanded in
the first three precepts with the time of worship prescribed in the fourth
precept—the reading should be *prescribed*—“worship to be performed with its
manner,” and so forth. But that manner of worship is so connected with it
that it is not only inseparable from it but also moral in itself. The time and
place of worship, however, are indeed connected with worship in general by
natural necessity, but specifically it is entirely arbitrary to worship God
at this or that time and place. An excellent comparison! See the seventh axiom
concerning morality.]

*Response 1.* We said above in Book II how great the difference is between a
Solemn and determined Time and a Common and undetermined Time, whether
considered generally or specifically. The fourth precept, however, speaks of
this solemn and determined time, not of an undetermined and common time. 2.
Furthermore, although this solemn and determined time was arbitrary with
respect to God, it is moral for us once the divine determination has been
made. Much more will the worship to be rendered at that definite time be
moral. 3. We examined long ago what was said in that seventh axiom. And above,
in Book II, Chapter XX, we abundantly showed how great the difference is
between *Place and Time*.

Next Dr. Burmann turns to his reasons, saying in the *Inquiry*: [For that time
and that rest are in themselves indifferent and are only means of worship,
not worship itself.] But Dr. Essenius responded above: [Yet to observe and
spend the time holily according to the precept is worship and is moral.] Dr.
Burmann offers nothing in reply, nor indeed can any reply be offered. For
although time and rest considered in themselves are indifferent, that time
enjoined here—namely, a principal, solemn, and sufficient time—and that rest
commanded by this precept are not indifferent but moral. To observe them
holily according to the fourth precept is a part of worship, because the
divine precept constitutes such worship.

He proceeds: [Worship is not better or more efficacious because it occurs at
a definite time or on a particular day; for then it would be more moral to
worship God on the Sabbath day than on any other day, although God can always
be worshiped in Spirit and truth, and the Spirit of God, who breathes wherever
he wishes, can sometimes operate just as efficaciously, if not more
efficaciously, on other days than on that particular day. Worshiping God is
therefore indeed moral, but worshiping God on a particular day or with the
sanctification of a particular rest for worship is not moral.]

*Response.* We do not say that worship is better or more efficacious because
it occurs at a definite time or on a particular day. For we know that the
Spirit of God, who breathes wherever he wishes, can sometimes operate just as
efficaciously, if not more efficaciously, on other days than on that day.
Nevertheless, one who worships God on the Sabbath day according to God's
prescription performs a greater moral duty than one who worships him on
another day, both because he worships him more solemnly and because he complies
with more moral precepts: namely, with the *first*, the *second*, and the
*fourth*. In the same way, one who prays solemnly on a solemn fast day performs
a greater Moral duty than one who prays on any other day. Therefore it is
also moral to worship God on a particular day and with the sanctification of
a particular rest, because God also constituted this as moral by his precept.

Let us hear what the illustrious Essenius replied above, page 60: [Where (*he
says*)

[Printed p. 275 | IA PDF 281 | IA leaf 281 | IA decausadei02brow]

worship is performed on a particular day according to a precept, there is a
greater moral character, because the matter is not only commanded absolutely
in the first precept but is also restricted to that particular day in the
fourth. 2. God must be worshiped in spirit and truth frequently, without
intermission, 1 Thessalonians 5:17. That matter is always moral, but in worship
performed on a particular day according to a precept, that special obedience
which cannot be omitted without sin is added. 3. Apart from the use of the
Lord's Supper, the Holy Spirit can preserve our faith, spiritual life, union,
and communion with Christ, and sometimes confirms them more efficaciously
than in the use of that Sacrament. Nevertheless, use of the sacrament, when
occasion for it is given, has its necessity from the divine precept and cannot
be omitted without sin. We say the same about observing the Sabbath, and this
is its moral character.]

Thus far he, and Dr. Burmann had nothing to answer.

He nevertheless proceeds in the *Inquiry*: [It is indeed virtue and obedience
to sanctify that rest in this way, and this is always moral. Yet the action
itself, or the thing commanded which arises from that obedience, is not always
moral or a virtue, but only when the thing rendered from that obedience is in
itself moral or a virtue.] Dr. Essenius answered: [I do not see how doing a
commanded thing out of obedience to the precept is not an act of moral virtue.
2. Things otherwise indifferent, when a precept has been placed over them,
cease to be indifferent to that extent and have a moral determination.] But
Dr. Burmann offers nothing in reply. Why, then, will it not be moral to worship
God on a particular day and with the sanctification of a particular rest? If
sanctifying that rest in this way is a virtue and moral, we have what we seek.
For then this precept will be moral, because the thing commanded which arises
from that obedience will also be moral: the sanctification of such rest is the
thing commanded. Finally, what kind of expression is this: the thing commanded
is not moral or a virtue except when it is itself moral or a virtue? The same
is surely always the same. But perhaps he will explain himself in what follows.
Let us hear him.

[For out of obedience to God (*he says*) we practice not only moral virtues,
such as believing God, loving him, and so forth, but also other external,
indifferent actions which are not virtues in themselves. Thus by God's precept
to labor, sow, reap, and so forth is a moral virtue, but the actions of sowing,
reaping, and so forth are not moral, although the virtue of obedience from
which they are elicited is moral.] *Response 1.* We do not say that observing
the weekly Sabbath is a virtue in itself, but that it is one by reason of
God's precept commanding that observance. 2. We also concede that all the
actions we perform out of obedience to God are not equal or equally moral.
Some are necessary and always remain in the same relation; others are
indifferent in themselves and within our power, so that sometimes they can be
done and sometimes not. Yet even in the latter a moral character appears
whenever they are done in the due manner according to God's law, for they are
then morally good, neither evil nor neutral. He himself concedes this when he
says that by God's precept laboring, sowing, reaping, and so forth are a moral
virtue. Indeed, moral virtue is nothing other than what is done by reason of
God's precept. But how he applies this to the point must now be seen.

[Thus also (*he says*) to rest and to apply that rest to worship out of
obedience to the fourth precept is a moral virtue. Yet the application of rest
to worship itself, rendered out of obedience, is not a moral thing, since the
rest and its sanctification for worship

[Printed p. 276 | IA PDF 282 | IA leaf 282 | IA decausadei02brow]

is only a means to performing moral worship, and is not worship itself or
anything moral in itself.] *Response 1.* Can any action be called moral and a
virtue when it is not considered insofar as it is done from obedience? 2. It
is well that he says that to rest and apply that rest to worship out of
obedience to the fourth precept is moral and a virtue, for this is enough for
us. 3. Nor do I see how it should not also be said that the application of
rest to worship itself, rendered out of obedience, is moral. For what, I ask,
is the difference between the *application* of rest to worship out of
obedience and *applying* rest to worship out of obedience? 4. I concede that
rest and the application of rest are not in themselves worship or a part of
worship. But when a divine command is added, no one will deny that this can
occur, for it depends upon the divine will that this act rather than that one
be worship pleasing to God, as we explained above. Dr. Essenius adds above,
page 61: [Indeed, this application according to a precept is an act of rational
obedience, and to that extent moral, and not merely a means to performing
moral worship, as though that means lacked the moral character arising from
the precept to which obedience is thus rendered.]

What follows? [Therefore (*Dr. Burmann says*) the worship itself that is
rendered is moral, as commanded by force of the first precept. But to render
it on the Sabbath day by force of the fourth precept is moral insofar as the
thing itself that is rendered is considered, namely, the application of that
rest to worship. Otherwise sacrifices, circumcision, and all ceremonies would
be moral in the same way, because they are done from moral obedience. Yet the
things themselves rendered from that obedience will not therefore be moral.]

*Response 1.* The worship rendered is moral not only because it is commanded
by force of the first precept, but also because it is commanded by force of
the second or third. 2. If worship is moral because it is commanded by force
of the first or second precept, why will it not also be moral to render worship
on the Sabbath day because it is commanded by force of the fourth precept? 3.
No action can be called moral or morally good except in relation to conformity
with a divine precept. 4. It does not follow that sacrifices and ceremonial
things of that kind are to be called moral simply, because, as we recently
explained, we do not say that every command of God constitutes something moral
*par excellence*. [Indeed (*Dr. Essenius says*), according to the legal
relation of the ceremonial precepts, obedience even in such matter was moral
in a certain common respect. But according to their evangelical relation,
those things rendered in this way under the Old Testament possessed a
ceremonial, typical character.]

Finally, Dr. Burmann concludes as follows on page 19: [Since, therefore,
everything in the fourth precept is not moral, the precept itself certainly
will not be moral either.] [I would rather respond (*the illustrious Essenius
says*): since that observance by force of the fourth precept is truly moral,
the precept itself is undoubtedly moral.]

And thus I believe this objection has been answered.
