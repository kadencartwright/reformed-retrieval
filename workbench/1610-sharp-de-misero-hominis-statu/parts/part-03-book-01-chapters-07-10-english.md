Chapter VII

The Division of Sin, Where the Sin of Angels Is Discussed

First division. Sin belongs either to human beings or to angels. The sin of
angels is either the first sin or one that arose from the first. The first sin
of the angels was that they did not remain in the truth, John 8:44; Jude 6,
but abandoned their own origin, John 8:44; Jude 6.

The scholastics raise the following questions concerning the sin of angels.

Question 1. What was the first sin of the angels?

Response. There are various opinions on this matter among the scholastics. 1.
There is the opinion of those who said that it was rebellion against God and
the aspiration to a higher state, and that because rebellion arises from
pride, it was pride. 2. There is the opinion of those who said that it was
nothing other than envy, because they envied the gift given to man when he was
created in the image of God, Wisdom 2:24. 3. There is the opinion of those who
infer from John 8:44, where it says, “They did not stand in the truth,” that
their sin was failure to preserve the faithfulness owed to God, since
“truth” sometimes signifies the faithfulness subjects owe their prince. 4.
There is the opinion of those who, because “truth” signifies integrity, as in
John 2:3, think their sin was failure to remain in the integrity in which they
were created. 5. There is the opinion of those who, because truth is opposed
to falsehood, think their sin was the lie the devil devised to deceive Eve.
6. The

[1610 printed p. 53 | Google Books PA53]

true opinion is that it was pride with envy and rebellion, arising from the
occasion of the recognized truth of the gospel that was to be manifested in
Christ. It was pride because they resented that no one, not even they
themselves, could be blessed without Christ. It was envy because they envied
Christ the God-man this dignity and the fact that he would have to minister
to them. It was rebellion because they refused to assent and submit
themselves to this will. This is proved as follows. 1. From the beginning the
devil has endeavored by his lies about Christ—that is, by heresies—to destroy
the truth of the gospel. 2. In John 8:44, the lie the devil speaks is opposed
to the truth in which he did not stand; but the chief lie he speaks or has
spoken consists in the heresies that fight against the gospel. 3. Because the
Jews did not remain in the truth but resisted the gospel, they are said to be
of their father the devil. 4. The sin against the Holy Spirit, which is of the
same kind as the devils’ sin, is opposition to the recognized truth of the
gospel, Hebrews 6 and 10.

Question 2. Did one angel sin before the others, whom the others afterward
followed?

Response. I affirm it. The rest are therefore called the angels of that first
angel, John 8:44; 2 Peter 2:4; Jude 6.

Question 3. Did as many angels fall as remained steadfast?

Response. The scholastics infer from 2 Kings 6:16 that the numbers are equal,
but Scripture determines nothing.

Question 4. In what place did the angels sin, in heaven or in the air?

Response. In heaven, as may be inferred from 2 Peter 2:4. 1. They abandoned
their dwelling, but the angels’ dwelling was not the air but the heaven of the
blessed. 2. They were cast down into the air. Hence the devil is said to have
been cast down and to be the prince

[1610 printed p. 54 | Google Books PA54]

of the air, Ephesians 2:2.

The sins of the angels that arose from the first are each and every sin that
has been or will be committed after that first one.

Chapter VIII

The Sin of Human Beings

Thus far we have dealt with the sin of angels. The sin of human beings
follows, and it is either the first sin or one that arose from the first.

The first sin of human beings was that by which Adam and Eve, voluntarily and
at the devil’s deceitful persuasion, transgressed God’s command by eating the
forbidden fruit, Genesis 3. The causes of this sin in both Adam and Eve are
twofold: 1. internal; and 2. external. The external causes are likewise
twofold: principal efficient causes and instrumental causes.

The principal external cause that enticed Eve was the devil. He assailed Eve
first because of the weakness of her sex, 1 Timothy 2:13.

There were two instrumental causes. 1. The serpent, whose service the devil
used for the following reasons: 1. because the serpent was an animal more
familiar to man; 2. because it was craftier than the others; and 3. because it
could secretly creep wherever it wished out of the man’s sight. This serpent
was a real serpent.

The following heresies must therefore be rejected: 1. that of the Ophites,
whom Irenaeus mentions in book 1, chapter 34 *On Heresies*, who said that the
serpent was the wisdom of God; 2. that of the Manichaeans, who, according to
Augustine in *On Heresies* 46, said that the serpent was Christ; and 3. that
of Origen, who, according to Ambrose in chapter 2 *On Paradise*,
wanted pleasure to be understood by the

[1610 printed p. 55 | Google Books PA55]

serpent.

2. The second instrumental cause was the temptations themselves, which were:
1. the slander that God begrudged them the happiness that could become more
excellent; 2. the twofold lie: first, when he said that they would not
certainly die, and second, when he said that they would become like God; 3.
the slander and lie together when he spoke about the tree, Genesis 3; and 4.
the beauty of the fruit, verse 6 of the same chapter.

There were five internal causes in the woman: 1. her willing hearing of the
temptations; 2. doubt about God’s truth; 3. partial forgetfulness of the
command God had given; 4. the incitement of ambition, by which she desired to
be more blessed; and 5. the credence given to Satan’s temptations, until at
last she actually ate the forbidden fruit contrary to God’s command.

The external causes that enticed Adam were remote or proximate. The remote
causes were the suggestions of Satan imposed upon Eve through the serpent;
the proximate cause was Eve through her enticements.

The internal causes that enticed Adam were either those common to Adam and
Eve, such as those discussed above, or causes particular to Adam—namely, an
immoderate love for his wife, as a result of which he was immediately taken
by her enticements.

Question. What was Adam’s sin?

Response. From Genesis 3:17 and Romans 5:14, it was disobedience—that is, a
voluntary turning away from rendering obedience to God’s revealed word—which
was followed by the corruption of the whole nature and to which Christ’s
obedience is opposed. This disobedience contains very many sins: 1. unbelief,
the root of the rest; 2. ambition and pride, which was followed by contempt
for the divine majesty; 3. transgression of God’s will; 4.

[1610 printed p. 56 | Google Books PA56]

ingratitude; 5. disparagement of God’s faithfulness; 6. listening to Satan’s
lies, slanders, and blasphemies; 7. accusing God of falsehood, envy, and
malice; and 8. aspiring to divinity against God’s will, and so forth.
Accordingly, this sin was the most serious of nearly all sins except the sin
against the Holy Spirit.

The following did not judge quite correctly. 1. Thomas, in question 14,
article 2, wanted this sin to be gluttony.

2. Rupert of Deutz, in book 2, chapter 29 on Genesis, held it to be the crime
of an ungrateful spirit, because Adam never gave thanks for the benefits he
had received.

3. Brenz, on John 8, said it was a defection from the Son of God,
because Adam was unwilling to submit to Christ.

4. Augustine wanted it to be only pride and presumption concerning himself.

5. Scotus, book 2, distinction 22, wanted it to be an immoderate love for the
friendship of his wife, because Adam was unwilling to grieve her. He believed
that without his consolation she would waste away and, being alienated from
him, perish altogether.

6. Others maintain that Adam first sinned in forestalling the use of the tree
of the knowledge of good and evil.

Finally, some maintain that it was only pride, like that of the angels, by
which Adam desired to be blessed, but without God.

Question I. Was the forbidden fruit an apple, a pear, or another fruit?

Response. Scripture says nothing about this.

Question II. At what time did man fall?

Response. Although Scripture says nothing expressly about this, it is
probable that he fell on the same day on which he was created,

[1610 printed p. 57 | Google Books PA57]

for the following reasons. 1. This follows from the devil’s envy, since it is
not probable that he wanted to bear man’s remaining for long in that state of
integrity. 2. If man had remained steadfast for several days, the marital
blessing would have taken effect, Adam would have known his wife, and he would
have begotten children without original sin. 3. The Sabbath was instituted
not so much for recognizing and meditating upon the works of creation as for
redemption. 4. It appears from the words of both the serpent and the woman
that she had not yet tasted any fruit. 5. When the Holy Spirit speaks of the
sixth day in Genesis 1 and of the fall in chapter 3, he says היום, with the
emphatic ה prefixed, by which he seems to signify one and the same day.
Finally, Adam fell so quickly that the work of redemption might be more
illustrious, because although the angels and man received excellent gifts
from God, they were nevertheless so powerless that they could not remain
steadfast even for a day without Christ the Mediator.

Chapter IX

Original Sin

Thus far we have dealt with the first sin of human beings. The sins that arose
from the first follow, and they belong either to Adam or to his descendants.
The sins that arose in Adam are all the other sins Adam committed.

The sin that arose in Adam’s descendants is twofold: original and actual. In
Adam himself, however, there was no original sin.

The term “original” does not occur in Scripture, but the thing itself does,
Genesis 3 and 6:4; John 3:6; Romans 5:12.

It is called original in order to distinguish it from actual and personal

[1610 printed p. 58 | Google Books PA58]

sin. It is so called not because it is the fountain and origin of other sins,
although it really is, but because by continual propagation and inheritance
it stains our birth and inheres in us as soon as we begin to be human beings.
Four matters concerning it must be treated by us: 1. Does it exist? 2. What
is it, and how many kinds are there? 3. How is it propagated? 4. To what
extent does it remain in the regenerate?

Chapter X

Does Original Sin Exist?

The Pelagians said that all sins occur by imitation, and therefore entirely
denied that original sin exists. The Anabaptists said that original sin was
merely Augustine’s invention.

Some of the papists, however, held it to be the very least sin. William,
bishop of Paris, said in chapter 7 of *On Vices and Sin* that it is not properly
sin. In his controversy on original sin, Pighius contends that it does not
have the true character of sin but is only liability for another’s crime, and
so forth.

We, however, assert that it is truly and properly sin. It must be noted that
individual human beings are considered in two ways: 1. as individual persons;

[1610 printed p. 59 | Google Books PA59]

and 2. as members of a society or multitude. In each way an act can pertain to
an individual. For example, if someone is a citizen of a commonwealth, the
acts he performs by his own judgment pertain to him alone insofar as he is an
individual person. But insofar as he is a member of the commonwealth, the
acts performed either by the whole city, by its greater part, or by the
city’s ruler pertain to him. Thus the Philosopher says, “What the ruler of a
city does, the city is said to do.” In the same way, the whole multitude of
human beings who receive human nature from the first parent must be considered
as one body of one man. In this multitude each person, including Adam himself,
is considered either only as an individual person or as a member of this
multitude, which is derived by natural origin from one man. Adam’s acts
considered as those of an individual person pertain to him alone and cannot be
said of or propagated to others. But Adam’s acts insofar as he is the head or
root from which the remaining multitude of human beings flows are rightly
said of the entire multitude. For the supernatural gifts divinely given to
Adam were granted to him not as an individual person but as the principle of
the whole human nature, so that they might be derived by origin to his
descendants. Through the fall, however, he lost these gifts on the very terms
on which he received them—namely, for himself and his posterity—so that every
descendant of Adam, at the very moment he is a human being, exists deprived of
these gifts and infected with that

[1610 printed p. 60 | Google Books PA60]

sin, as will be explained more fully below. Original sin is therefore proved
to be truly and properly sin as follows.

Argument I. Scripture testifies that all are corrupted and depraved by sin
from the first moment of conception, Genesis 5:3; 6:5; 8:21; Job 14:4; Psalm
51:7; Isaiah 48:8; John 3:6; Romans 3:10; 5:12; Ephesians 2:3. Therefore.

Argument II. If death has overtaken everyone who does not sin actually, then
original sin exists, because death is the wages of sin, Romans 6:23, and every
sin is either original or actual. But the former is taught in Romans 5:12,
where Paul teaches that both the guilt and the punishment of that sin passed
to all. Therefore.

Argument III. If original sin, as the papists maintain, is lighter than any
venial sin, if it does not merit death, and if those who die in original sin
are punished with the penalty of loss rather than of sense, then death
signifies the penalty of loss, not of sense. For many die because of this sin,
and in eternal life, which is opposed to death, there will be no sense of
glory and happiness. But the latter is absurd. Therefore the former is also.

Argument IV. If infants are baptized for the remission of original sin, then
original sin is properly sin, for what does not exist is not remitted. But the
former is true by the testimony of our adversaries. Therefore. Hence Thomas
rightly says in question 4, article 1 *On Original Sin*: “If original sin does
not exist, the necessity of baptizing children is excluded, yet the common
custom of the Church holds this practice to have been derived from the
apostles. Therefore

[1610 printed p. 61 | Google Books PA61]

it must be said simply that sin is transmitted by origin from the first
parent to his descendants.”

Argument 5. Through one man sin entered the world and rendered everyone a
sinner and subject to death. But that sin could not be actual in everyone,
because they could not all perform one action before they existed, and many
die before they can do anything.

Argument 6. We are unjust through Adam in the same way that we are just
through Christ, Romans 5:19. But we are just through the imputation of
Christ’s righteousness. Therefore we are unjust through the imputation of
Adam’s sin, which is original in us.

Argument 7. What the head does insofar as it is the head is imputed to the
whole body. But Adam, who was the head of the human race and in whom all were
representatively present—and who is therefore called a type of the Christ to
come, Romans 5:14—truly sinned insofar as he was the head. Therefore.

Objections

Objection 1. Ezekiel 18:20 says that a son does not bear his father’s
iniquity. Therefore Adam’s sin is not imputed to his descendants.

Response 1. I deny the consequence, because that sin is now ours.

Response 2. The proposition is true if the sin is removed through
regeneration, or if we do not follow the father’s sin.

Response 3. A son does not bear personal sins, that is, sins pertaining to one
individual. Adam’s sin, however, was not the sin of one individual, because he
represented the whole species. For just as he received gifts to be continued
for himself and his descendants if he remained steadfast, so he lost them for
himself and his descendants.

[1610 printed p. 62 | Google Books PA62]

Moreover, we were in his loins. Thomas therefore rightly says in question 4,
article 1 *On Original Sin*: “The sin of the first man is the common sin of
the whole human nature. Therefore, when someone is punished for the sin of the
first parent, he is not punished for another’s sin but for his own.”

Objection 2. What is not voluntary is not sin. But original sin in infants is
not voluntary. Therefore.

Response. I deny the proposition, because it is true only in civil matters,
and not always even in those. Moreover, it is true only of actual sin, and not
always even then, for we commit some sins contrary to our will, Romans 7:15.
Finally, just as we sinned in Adam, so we willed in him. Hence Thomas says in
the same place that original sin is voluntary by reason of its principle,
namely, the will of the first parent.

Objection 3. Romans 5:19. If people were constituted sinners by propagation
because of Adam’s disobedience, Paul would have said “all,” not “many.”

Response. Augustine says in book 6, chapter 12 *Against Julian* that “many”
signifies “all,” and that Paul said “many” because of the antithesis.
Moreover, Scripture uses “many” and “all” interchangeably, as in Genesis
17:5 and 22:18. Compare these passages with Romans 5:12, 18 and verses 15 and
19.

Objection 4. Romans 5:12. If sin is propagated to all by generation, Paul
would not have said “through one” but “through two,” namely, the man and the
woman.

Response. The manner of propagation will be discussed below. He says
“through one” because Adam, not Eve, was the cause of original sin, and
because Adam’s sin, not Eve’s, is transmitted to their descendants.

Objection 5. Second Corinthians 5:10 says that God renders

[1610 printed p. 63 | Google Books PA63]

to each person what he himself has done. Infants, however, do nothing.
Therefore.

Response 1. The passage speaks of adults.

Response 2. They committed that sin in the manner in which they were in
Adam’s loins.

Objection 6. An accident of one individual is not propagated to an entire
species. But Adam’s sin was an accident of one individual. Therefore.

Response 1. I deny the assumption. Thomas rightly says in question 4, article
1 *On Original Sin*: “Adam, insofar as he was the principle of human nature,
had the character of a universal cause, and thus through his act the entire
human nature propagated from him was corrupted.” And in the same place:
“Original sin, speaking in itself, is a sin of nature, not of a person except
by reason of the infected nature.”

Response 2. The proposition is true unless those accidents belong to natural
impotence and are propagated continually and universally, as mortality is.
The argument is therefore drawn from particulars.

Response 3. The proposition is true of separable accidents, but not of
inseparable ones.

Objection 7. Sin is liability. But when Adam begot a son he was no longer
liable, but had been reconciled to God. Therefore sin was not propagated from
Adam to his children.

Response. The conclusion contains more than the premises. The begetting of
offspring does not occur according to grace, John 1:13, but according to
nature as it exists after the fall. Immunity from liability, however, does not
come from nature but from grace.

Objection 8. Like begets like. But Adam was righteous not only by imputation
but also by the beginning of righteousness in him. Therefore he begot people
like himself.

[1610 printed p. 64 | Google Books PA64]

Response 1. The proposition is true of natural generation.

Response 2. Vice, or corruption, remains even in believers after regeneration
and is propagated. Moreover, those born from holy parents are not holy because
of carnal generation, but because of spiritual generation.

Objection 9. Romans 11:16. If the root is holy, the branches are also holy.
Therefore the children of the holy are holy and thus without original sin.

Response. There is an equivocation in the word “holy,” because in the cited
passage it does not signify regeneration or justification, but separation
from other peoples through a particular covenant.

Objection 10. First Corinthians 7:14 calls the children of believers holy.
Therefore.

Response 1. I deny the consequence.

Response 2. There is an equivocation in the word “holy.” In the cited passage
it does not signify that those children are free from all sin. Instead, they
are called holy with respect to communion with the Church because of the
covenant, “I will be your God and the God of your offspring.” It therefore
signifies a relation, not a quality.

Objection 11. Original sin is the want of original righteousness. Since
original righteousness was something spiritual, its defect is likewise
spiritual, while the corruption of the flesh is something corporeal. But what
is spiritual cannot cause a corporeal effect. Therefore the corruption of
nature is not contained in Adam’s first sin.

Response 1. Original sin is not merely the absence of original righteousness,
as will be explained below.

Response 2. The defect of original righteousness is neither a corporeal nor a
spiritual thing, but a privation, just as the

[1610 printed p. 65 | Google Books PA65]

corruption of the flesh is not something corporeal.

Response 3. It is false that what is spiritual cannot cause a corporeal
effect. Boethius therefore rightly says in his book *On the Trinity* that the
forms which are in matter came from forms which are without matter. Thus in
us the will is moved by the intellect, the lower appetite by the will, and a
corporeal change of this kind follows.

Question 1. Since it is certain that original sin exists, is there only one
original sin in one person, or are there several?

Response. Only one. Thus Augustine in the *Enchiridion*, although some
scholastics taught the contrary.

Objection 1. If there were only one original sin, then every evil act would
arise from that sin. But many sins arise from things other than original sin.
Therefore.

Response. I distinguish the assumption. Immediately, it is true; but
mediately all sins arise from original sin.

Objection 2. Psalm 51:7 says in the plural, “I was conceived in iniquities.”
Therefore.

Response. Augustine says in the *Enchiridion* that the psalmist uses a plural
number for the singular, as in Exodus 32:31. Moreover, the Hebrew text has a
singular noun signifying iniquity. The antecedent is therefore false. The
Vulgate edition has thus translated it badly in the plural, and this false
translation furnished an occasion for the error concerning the plurality, so
to speak, of original sin.

Question 2. Is original sin a punishment or a fault?

[1610 printed p. 66 | Google Books PA66]

Response. It is both in different respects. It is a fault, and is accordingly
called sin, a fall, a transgression, and so forth in Romans 5. It is a
punishment, as Augustine says in book 2 *On the Baptism of Infants, to
Marcellus*, where he says that sin dwelling in our members is sin in such a
way that it is the punishment of sin. He says the same in book 5 *Against
Julian*. Finally, considered in individual human beings without respect to
their common nature, it is a punishment. But if you consider individuals in
their common nature and as they existed in that principle in which they
sinned, it is a fault.
