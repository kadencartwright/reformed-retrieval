QUESTION III.

THE NECESSITY OF THE INCARNATION.

*Was it necessary for the Son of God to become incarnate? We affirm.*

[Marginal heading: The terms by which the incarnation is designated in
Scripture;]

I. Before we discuss the question, we should briefly note the various terms
by which the mystery of the incarnation is customarily designated, both in
Scripture and among the ancients. In Scripture it is called φανέρωσις ἐν
σαρκὶ, *manifestation in the flesh* (1 Tim. 3:16); σώματος καταρτισμός, *the
fitting or forming of a body* (Heb. 10:5); κένωσις, *emptying*, and the
assumption of the form of a servant (Phil. 2:7); ἐπίληψις, *assumption*, of
Abraham’s seed (Heb. 2:16); ἐπιφάνεια, *appearing* (2 Tim. 1:10); and so
forth.

[Marginal heading: And among the fathers.]

Among the fathers it is sometimes called οἰκονομία, *economy*, as distinct
from θεολογία, *theology*, because it was the work of a marvelous
dispensation. At other times it is called ἐνανθρώπησις, *becoming human*;
ἐνσωμάτωσις, *embodiment*; ἔνσαρκος παρουσία, *coming in the flesh*; and, more
commonly, σάρκωσις or ἐνσάρκωσις, *incarnation*. This term was undoubtedly
drawn from John 1:14, where the λόγος, *Word*, is said to have *become flesh*.
There the word *flesh* is not taken strictly for one part of a human being,
but broadly and synecdochically for the whole human nature, which includes
the soul along with the flesh. Thus the greatness of God’s love and mercy
toward human beings is more clearly intimated. God deigned to lower himself
so far that he willed to unite to himself not only the soul, the nobler part
of a human being, but also what is lowest and weakest in us, namely, this
frail and mortal flesh.

[Marginal heading: The necessity of the incarnation.]

II. Since this mystery is impenetrable to reason and is known only by the
benefit of revelation, various questions are customarily raised about it.
The first concerns its necessity, and this can in turn be divided into
several heads. 1. Would the Son of God have become incarnate even if man had
not sinned? 2. Was it necessary for the Son of God to become incarnate for
us, and was no other manner of salvation possible for God? 3. Was it
necessary for our Mediator to be θεάνθρωπον, *God-man*? We will discuss these
separately under three propositions.

[Marginal heading: The first proposition. The incarnation was not necessary
unless sin is posited.]

III. The first proposition is: *Just as the Son of God became incarnate only
because of sin, so it would not have been necessary for him to become
incarnate if man had not sinned.* This is opposed by the older scholastics,
who rashly and without the authority of Scripture asserted the contrary,
including Alexander of Hales, Ockham, Bonaventure, and others. The Lutheran
Osiander refurbished their error in the preceding century, and most recently
the *Socinians* have revived it for no other purpose than to seek from it
some support for the most pestilent heresy of Christ’s metaphorical
redemption and improper satisfaction.

[Marginal heading: It is proved, 1. From Scripture;]

IV. Our reasons are: 1. No other end is ever proposed for Christ’s advent and
incarnation, whether in the Old or the New Testament, than that he might save
his people from their sins. If he is promised, he is promised only after the
fall, in Gen. 3:15, and for crushing the serpent’s head. If he is
foreshadowed, he is foreshadowed by sacrifices appointed to expiate human
sins. In the New Testament this is plainly confirmed by the angel (Matt.
1:21), Zechariah (Luke 1:67 and following), Simeon (Luke 2:30, 34), John the
Baptist (John 1:29), Christ himself (Matt. 9:13; 20:28), and the apostles:
Paul (Gal. 4:4–5; 1 Tim. 1:15; Heb. 2:14) and John (1 John 2:1–2; 3:8).

[Marginal heading: 2. From his office, which is concerned only with sinners;]

V. 2. His office is concerned only with sinners. As Prophet, he teaches
sinners and calls them to faith and repentance (Isa. 61:1–2; Matt. 9:13). As
Priest, he gives himself as an ἀντίλυτρον, *a ransom in exchange*, for sins
(1 Tim. 2:6; Isa. 53:10; 1 John 2:2), and prays for transgressors (Isa. 53:12;
John 17). As King, he governs his own and protects them against the devil, the
world, and the flesh so that they are not snatched from his hands (John
10:28; Rom. 8:35, 38–39). In a word, he became incarnate so that he might be
Mediator, but there would have been no need for a mediator while no sin yet
existed.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the impulsive cause of Christ’s mission, which was
love toward fallen human beings;]

VI. 3. The impulsive cause of Christ’s mission was God’s love and charity
toward human beings who were not upright but fallen (John 3:16). This love is
commended far more lavishly when it is maintained that he was sent for the
sole end of freeing us from the chains of sins than it would be if he had
come for other causes (Rom. 5:6–8).

[Marginal heading: 4. From the fathers.]

VII. 4. The fathers often confirm this. Irenaeus says in book 5, chapter 14:
“If the flesh did not need to be saved, the Word of God would by no means
have become flesh.” Augustine says in sermon 9 *On the Words of the Apostle*:
“There was no reason for Christ the Lord to come except to save sinners.
Take away diseases, take away wounds, and there is no reason for medicine.”
Gregory says in book 4, chapter 1: “If Adam had not sinned, our Redeemer would
not have needed to assume our flesh,” and so forth.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VIII. Christ is called the *firstborn of every creature* (Col. 1:15), not as
though rational creatures were produced according to his example, but both
by reason of his eternal generation and by reason of the dominion over them
which belongs to him. All things created in him and for him

[Printed p. 266 | Internet Archive PDF 294 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

are said to have been created in relation to him as God, not as man. He is
called first in all things in verse 18 by dignity, not by time. Nor is man
said to have been made in God’s image because he was formed according to the
example of Christ, but because he was created in God’s essential image,
which consists in wisdom and holiness, as Paul teaches (Eph. 4:24; Col.
3:10).

IX. It is one thing for the marriage of Adam and Eve to have been a sign and
type of Christ’s marriage with the church according to the Holy Spirit’s
hidden intention, but another for this to have been known to Adam and
proposed by him as a prediction of such a marriage. We grant the former, not
the latter. The fact that the apostle applies Adam’s words to Christ in Eph.
5:31 does not imply that Adam spoke them for that purpose, but only that they
can properly be referred to Christ allegorically because of the resemblance.

X. If Christ was not to become incarnate before sin was taken into
consideration, the church would not therefore have been ἀκέφαλος, *headless*,
because he would have been its Head just as he would have been the angels’
Head—not by reason of his humanity, but by reason of his divinity and the
right of creation.

XI. A demonstration of love toward the human race through the Son’s becoming
incarnate without respect to sin and the expiation of sins through the same
incarnate Son cannot be regarded as subordinate ends. Such a subordination
conflicts with Scripture, which proposed no other end for Christ’s mission
than the salvation of sinners, and it is not lawful to inquire further when
Scripture is silent.

XII. God does not withhold from human nature a good of which it is capable by
a natural capacity arising from the principles of that nature itself. But he
can withhold what it is capable of according to an obediential potency, or a
lack of repugnance to receiving all that God wills. The capacity for
hypostatic union in human nature is of this kind.

XIII. God’s self-communicating goodness could indeed have been a sufficient
motive for God to produce creatures, but not for the Son of God to become
incarnate, for that further looked to the restoration of his creature.

[Marginal heading: The second proposition. Given sin and God’s decree
concerning human salvation, it was necessary for the Son of God to become
incarnate.]

XIV. The second proposition is: *It was not only fitting but necessary, given
sin and God’s decree concerning human redemption, for the Son of God to
become incarnate to accomplish this work.* 1. We do not ask about *simple and
absolute necessity* on God’s part. For if God had willed, he could have left
man in his ruin no less than the devil. We ask instead about *hypothetical
necessity*: once the will to redeem human beings was posited, was the
incarnation necessary, or could this have been accomplished by another
means? 2. Again, we do not ask about the *necessity of the decree*. No one
denies that, once it is posited that God had so decreed, it necessarily had
to happen that way. We ask instead about a *necessity of nature*: setting the
decree aside and considering what precedes it, was it necessary for the Son
of God to become incarnate for our redemption? 3. We do not ask about a
*necessity of fittingness*, because everyone acknowledges that this was most
fitting to the divine Majesty, lest his precepts be said to be violated with
impunity. We ask instead about a *necessity of justice*: could God’s justice
have been satisfied and our liberation accomplished in no other way? This we
affirm.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

XV. I know that some of the ancients held another opinion—that God was not
without other possible means—and various scholastics follow them here.
Augustine says in book 13 of *The Trinity*, chapter 10: “God was not without
another possible means, since all things are equally subject to his power,
but there was not and could not have been another means more suitable for
healing our misery,” and so forth. Various scholastics agree with him here,
including Thomas, part 3, question 1; Lombard, book 3, distinction 2; and
Bonaventure, on book 3 of the *Sentences*, distinction 20, question 6. Nor are
some of our own lacking who incline to it: Martyr, *Common Places*, class 2,
chapter 17, section 19; Tilenus, *Syntagma*, part 2, on the incarnation;
Chimedontius, *On the Redemption of the Human Race*, book 1, chapter 5; and
above

[Printed p. 267 | Internet Archive PDF 295 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

all Twisse, who strives laboriously to prove it. Yet there were also fathers
and scholastics who acknowledged this necessity: Athanasius in his treatise
*On the Incarnation of the Word*, Anselm in the book *Why God Became Man*, and
Ambrose on Hebrews 9. Most of our theologians agree with them, especially
after the rise of Socinus, and we embrace their opinion as both truer and
safer.

XVI. Our reasons are: 1. Since God cannot deny his own justice, he could not
liberate human beings unless satisfaction had first been made to it. Infinite
justice could be satisfied only by an infinite λύτρον, *ransom*, and that
infinite ransom could be found nowhere but in the Son of God. In Part I,
Locus III, Question XIX, on God, we proved that the exercise of this justice
is necessary, not free or arbitrary and dependent solely upon God’s good
pleasure.

XVII. 2. If the matter could have been accomplished otherwise, it is not
credible that the all-wise and best God would have entered upon this plan,
which would seem poorly suited to his wisdom and goodness. For if he could
have liberated human beings by a word alone, would it have suited wisdom to
accomplish so easy a thing by so great an effort and, as it were, to move
heaven and earth? Would he have done through more what he could have done
through fewer things? And who can believe that the Father, without necessity,
willed to send down from heaven his most beloved Son, in whom all his εὐδοκία,
*good pleasure*, rests, to clothe him in our flesh, and to expose him to a
thousand miseries and finally to a most dreadful death for us? Surely God’s
wisdom and goodness do not permit us to believe that this was free for him;
some ineluctable necessity must have intervened here.

XVIII. Nor should it be thought that by maintaining this we wish to set
limits upon God’s omnipotence or to define what he can do to a creature by
his supreme right. We only show from Scripture what God can or cannot do
according to his ordained power and his right tempered by his virtues. The
other matters pertaining to this will be treated in the following questions
when the necessity of satisfaction is discussed.

[Marginal heading: The third proposition. The work of salvation required a
θεάνθρωπον, God-man.]

XIX. The third proposition is: *The work of redemption could not have been
accomplished except through a θεάνθρωπον, God-man, who through the incarnation
would join human nature to the divine nature by an indissoluble bond.* Since
two things were especially required for our redemption—the acquisition of
salvation and its application, the endurance of death for satisfaction and
victory over death for the enjoyment of life—our Mediator had to be
θεάνθρωπος, *God-man*, in order to execute them. He had to be man to suffer
and God to conquer, man to bear the penalties owed by us and God to endure
and exhaust them, man to acquire salvation for us by dying and God to apply
it to us by conquering, man to become ours through the assumption of flesh
and God to make us like himself through the bestowal of the Spirit. Neither a
mere man nor God alone could do this. For God alone could not experience
death, nor could man alone overcome it. Man alone could die for human beings;
God alone could conquer death. Therefore both natures had to be joined so
that in each conjointly humanity’s utmost weakness might exercise itself for
suffering and divinity’s supreme power and Majesty for victory.

XX. 1. He had to be man by reason of *God’s justice*, which required that sin
be punished in the same nature in which it had been committed, according to
the oracle in Ezek. 18:4: *the soul that has sinned shall die*. But he also
had to be God, who would add infinite value to his sufferings. 2. By reason
of *Christ’s office*, he had to perform the function of Mediator in order to
reconcile human beings to God. He therefore had to stand between both and,
like Jacob’s ladder, join heaven and earth by participation in each nature.
As Prophet, he had to be taken from his brothers as man so that he might
associate familiarly with human beings and we might freely approach him. But
as God he had to send the Spirit into our hearts and inscribe the law upon
our souls so that he might make us

[Printed p. 268 | Internet Archive PDF 296 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

θεοδιδάκτους, *taught by God*. As Priest, he had to be man, because every high
priest is taken from among men (Heb. 5:1), so that he who sanctifies and those
who are sanctified might all be from one (Heb. 2:11). But he also had to be
God so that he might reconcile man to God, satisfy divine justice, abolish
sin, and bring in everlasting righteousness, which no mortal could do. The
victim to be offered also could not be angelic, because an angel could not
die, nor brute, but had to be rational and human—indeed, more than human and
heavenly—who would offer himself through the eternal Spirit and add infinite
weight and merit to the reality of his sufferings. As King, he had to assume
humanity from us by which he would be united to us, but it had to be united
to divinity by which he would exercise dominion not only over bodies but also
over souls, not for a time but eternally, and not in only one nation but
throughout the whole world.

XXI. 3. By reason of *us*, he had to be man so that by the *right of
proximity*, as our brother and גאל, *kinsman-redeemer*, he would liberate
captives enslaved to the devil (Luke 1:71, 74), and, after freeing us from
our former husband—the law and the covenant of works—would unite us to
himself in the covenant of grace by an eternal and indissoluble bond (Rom.
7:4; Eph. 5:25). He had to be God so that by the *right of ownership and
dominion* he might redeem us and claim us for himself. We needed the
ὁμοιοπαθείᾳ, *fellow feeling*, of one who dies and the συμπαθείᾳ,
*compassion*, of one who lives. He had to be man so that as ὁμοιοπαθής, *one
subject to like suffering*, he could endure all the penalties owed by us. He
had to be God so that as a merciful Priest he could sympathize with us in our
sufferings and aid us when tempted. The evil by which we were oppressed was
such that, although none but man could bear it, none but God could liberate
us from it. And the good that had to be conferred upon us, namely
righteousness and life, was such that, although it pertained to man, none but
God could put us in possession of it.

XXII. Hence it happens that when Scripture speaks of Christ, it nearly always
joins these two σχέσεις, *relations*, and attributes to him things which teach
that he is truly θεάνθρωπον, *God-man*. If he is called the woman’s seed,
which belongs to man, the crushing of the serpent, which belongs to God, is
attributed to him. If he is called Abraham’s seed, it is added in the same
place that he is the seed in whom all nations of the earth are to be blessed.
If the Prophet had to be taken from among his brothers, nevertheless he was
such a Prophet that he had to be heard on pain of death (Deut. 18:19). He is
a child born in time as man, but also the Son who as God is the Father of
eternity (Isa. 9:6). He is the Branch of David according to the flesh, but
Jehovah Our Righteousness according to the Spirit (Jer. 23:6). He is the Son
to be born of a virgin, but his name will be Immanuel (Isa. 7:14). He is the
Angel of the covenant, sent by God for the work of salvation, but also the
Lord himself, who comes to his own temple (Mal. 3:1). He is a citizen of
Bethlehem who arises from an obscure place, but also the Ruler whose goings
forth are from eternity (Mic. 5:2). He is Priest, but forever; King, but his
kingdom will have no end. He is David’s seed according to the flesh, but the
Son of God according to the Spirit of holiness (Rom. 1:3–4).
