CHAPTER THREE

On archetypal theology.

TRUE THEOLOGY is either archetypal or ectypal.

This division is analogical, because archetypal theology is theology first
and principally and is so called, while ectypal theology is so secondarily and
by likeness to the archetype. For the same account applies to theology as to
wisdom, goodness, justice, power, and the other qualities created by God in a
rational creature. Their archetype is in God—or rather, their archetype is the
Deity—while their image and likeness is in the rational creature. Indeed, all
true virtues are

[1615 printed locator C; the witness divides menti across the line turn.]

in some manner impressed upon a created mind by the form of that eternal and
immutable substance which is God. Therefore, just as wisdom, goodness,
justice, power, and so forth are either archetypal or ectypal—the former
existing in God, the latter expressed in rational creatures—so also is
theology. Thus our theology raises our souls to God and unites them with him.
Thomas Aquinas, in the first part of the *Summa of Theology* and in his
*Exposition on Boethius’s On the Trinity*, and John Duns Scotus, in the
prologue to the *Sentences*, question 3, folio 8, column 4, and more clearly on
folio 10, column 2, as well as throughout his works, mention the theology of
God, theology in itself, the theology of the blessed, and the theology of the
way, or

[1615 printed locator D; the witness divides viatorum across the line turn.]

of wayfarers, or our theology. Pelbart of Temesvár likewise does so in the
*Golden Rosary of Theology on the Four Books of Sentences*, proemial question
7, § 25.

That theology exists in God is certain, for:

I. Wisdom concerning divine things is in God. But theology is wisdom
concerning divine things. Therefore theology is in God.

II. All perfections that are in us are also in God. But theology is a
perfection in us. Therefore theology is in God. This theology is archetypal
and is described as follows.

Archetypal theology is wisdom concerning divine things

[1615 printed locator E; the witness divides residens across the line turn.]

residing in God, essential to him and uncreated.

This is also called πρωτότυπος, *prototypal theology*. The scholastics commonly
call it the theology of God and also exemplary theology. Created theology is
expressed and conformed to it as an image is conformed to a primordial,
primary, and immutable idea and exemplar. This divine theology we adore but
do not investigate; and it is, so to speak, a part of God’s essential wisdom.

Since in every discipline one must know the use of its very terms, by which we
advance to the perception of the things themselves, here too it must be
explained what the word *archetype* means. From this it will more readily

[1615 printed locator F]

be understood what archetypal theology is. Ἀρχέτυπον is a primordial
exemplar, a primordial form, the first type, an idea, or a primitive exemplar,
after whose likeness other things have been made and, as it were, fashioned
as images. This is shown by a notable passage of Philo the Jew in his book
περὶ τοῦ τὸ χεῖρον τῷ κρείττονι φιλεῖν ἐπιτίθεσθαι—that is, *On the Worse
Being Accustomed to Plot against the Better*: “ἀρχέτυπον μὲν φύσεως λογικῆς ὁ
θεός ἐστι, μίμημα δὲ καὶ ἀπεικόνισμα ἄνθρωπος,” that is, “God is the archetype
of rational nature, while the human being is its image and representation.”

The same is declared by a passage of Clement of Alexandria, who, in book 5 of
the *Stromata*, p. 239, says of God the creator of the human being: “ὃς
ἐτεχνίτασεν αὐτὸν ἀρχετύπῳ χρώμενος ἑαυτῷ,”

[1615 printed locator G]

that is, “who fashioned him, using himself as archetype.” He cites this from
Eurysus the Pythagorean and explains it a little earlier in this way: “Τὸν
δημιουργὸν αὐτῷ χρώμενον παραδείγματι, ποιῆσαι τὸν ἄνθρωπον,” that is, “The
craftsman, using himself as an exemplar, made the human being.”

The same is shown by a passage of Basil the Great in book 2 *Against Eunomius*,
p. 306 of the edition of the five *Dialogues of Athanasius on the Trinity* and
the four books of Basil *Against Eunomius*, printed in octavo by Henri Estienne
in 1570: “νοεῖν δὲ εἰκόνα τοῦ θεοῦ τοῦ ἀοράτου, οὐ κατὰ τὰς τεχνητὰς ταύτας
ὕστερον ἀπεργασθείσας πρὸς τὸ ἀρχέτυπον, ἀλλὰ συνυπάρχουσαν καὶ παρυφεστῶσαν
τῷ πρωτοτύπῳ ὑποστήσαντι, τοῦ εἶναι τὸ ἀρχέτυπον, οὖσαν· οὐκ

[1615 printed locator H]

ἐκτυπωθεῖσαν διὰ μιμήσεως, ἀλλὰ ὥσπερ ἐν σφραγῖδί τινι τῆς ὅλης φύσεως τοῦ
Πατρὸς ἐναποσημανθείσης τῷ Υἱῷ.” That is, one must conceive the image of the
invisible God not as one which, like those artificial images, is afterward
fashioned according to the norm of an exemplar, but as one which came into
existence as soon as the exemplar subsisted, subsisted together with it, and
was there as soon as the archetype was. It is an image, I say, not formed by
imitation, but with the whole nature of the Father impressed upon the Son as
upon a kind of seal.

Clement of Alexandria, in the *Protrepticus to the Greeks*, calls the Son of
God “φωτὸς ἀρχέτυπον φῶς,” “archetypal light of light.” Theodoret, in discourse
2 *On the Cure of Greek Affections*, p. 20, says: “φωνὴν ἀκήκοας, εἶδος δὲ οὐχ

[1615 printed locator I; the witness divides ἑώρακας across the line turn.]

ἑώρακας· μηδένα οὖν τύπον κατασκευάσῃς, οὗ τὸ ἀρχέτυπον οὐκ ἐπίστασαι,” that
is, “You have heard a voice, but you have seen no form. Therefore fashion no
type whose archetype you do not know.” In discourse 12 he calls them
“ἀρχέτυπα τῆς ἀρετῆς,” “exemplars of virtue,” in imitation of which εἰκόνες,
images, are depicted. On Colossians 3 he says: “The discourse turns from the
type to the archetype”—that is, from the figure to the exemplar. For in
baptism he teaches that there is the figure of the new human being whom we put
on, and in Christ the archetype.

Theology, therefore, is most properly that knowledge which is in the divine
mind concerning divine things. In this manner God alone is called a
theologian, and

[1615 printed locator K]

therefore God is understood to be the first, best, and most perfect
theologian.

This theology is formal wisdom, absolute or most perfect,

[1615 BSB witness | digital canvas 27 | printed p. 3]

[1615 printed locator A; the witness divides infinita across the page turn.]

infinite, possessed all at once, incommunicable, and communicating only a
certain image of itself to rational creatures.

It is formal because it is essential and is the form of God, or of the Deity,
which is the purest form. For God alone is naturally known to himself alone.
See John Duns Scotus, prologue to the *Sentences*, question 3, folio 8, column
B.

It is most perfect because it is not only about all things but is also all the
knowledge that God can have concerning them.
