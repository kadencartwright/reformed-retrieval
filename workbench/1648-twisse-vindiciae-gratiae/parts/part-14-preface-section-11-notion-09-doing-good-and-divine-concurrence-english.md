SECTION ELEVEN NOTION IX

PERKINS

A human being does not do the good, even that which by grace he is able to do, unless God effects in him that he do it, just as God effects that he is able to do it if he wills.

ARMINIUS

This is true: to be able, to will, and to do good are gifts and effects of God in human beings. But it must be added that God gives no one the power to act well without also being prepared to give willing and doing in addition, that is, to concur with the person by the further assistance of grace for willing and actually doing that good for which he has received sufficient strength, unless the person places or has placed an obstacle on his own part, as the Schoolmen say. “For to one who has, it will be given, and from one who does not have, even what he has will be taken away.” Otherwise the power will have been given in vain. But the wise God does nothing in vain. Thus he gave Adam the capacity to perform the law which he had laid down, and he was prepared to give what was required beyond the capacity for actual obedience—namely, willing and doing—unless Adam of his own accord and by a voluntary motion had turned himself away from God and his grace. Here I see a labyrinth which I shall not now enter, because departure from it would not be possible except with the thread and guidance of an exact explanation concerning the mode of God’s concurrence with a human being for performing some good work. Such an explanation does not belong to this place and, to confess the truth, is beyond my powers.

RESPONSE 1

“To be able, to will, and to do good are gifts and effects of God in human beings.” Indeed, these and statements of this kind are gold paint and coatings for their dogmas, quite specious and very familiar to the Arminians. But all who have the mysteries of Arminian theology adequately known and thoroughly examined know with how much candor these things are said. But let them still enjoy their masks; let us see what had to be added: “namely, that God is prepared to concur with a person for willing unless he places an obstacle.” But when, I ask, will a person be said not to place an obstacle? Namely, when he does not resist? But what is it not to resist? Namely, not to dissent but to consent, for this is how Grevinchovius explains it elsewhere. But this is to will. If, therefore, God requires beforehand that the person will, it will follow that God is prepared to concur with a person for willing, provided that he wills.

2. “To one who has,” he says, “it will be given”; but the act of willing is a gift of God. Therefore God gives this act to one who has. But I ask, one who has what? Does he mean one who has the power? Indeed, this is false, for God does not give the act of willing good to everyone who has the power of willing good. But if he means to one who has the act, see whether these things are not uttered beyond every semblance of reason: namely, that to one who has the act of willing good, God will give the act of willing good; that is, he will give a human being the act of willing, provided that the same person has already had the same act. Likewise, “from the one who does not have, what he has will be taken away.” Here again I ask, does he mean from the one who does not have the power of willing good, or from the one who does not have the act? For the one who does not have the power of doing what is good, there is nothing that presents itself to be taken away. Meanwhile it is true that to us, who do not have by nature the power of doing what is good, God is accustomed to grant it according to his good pleasure and from grace. But will what he has be taken away from the one who does not have the act of willing good? Yet he has nothing besides the power; therefore the power will be taken away. But this is not what is presently at issue, for the issue is God’s concurrence either supplied or denied toward an act, and indeed that act cannot with any appearance of reason be said to be taken away before it existed.

3. As for what he says, that otherwise the power was given in vain: unless a position so grossly absurd, so trifling, and so contradictory is admitted, God’s wisdom will evidently be imperiled. The power was given for acting freely and freely ceasing from action. Therefore, whether God concurs with the person for willing this, willing that, acting, or suspending action, the power will always exercise that for which it was naturally suited by divine constitution, and this whether God has determined the will to its own act, as Arminius holds. For this natural power of acting freely is prepared for both, and equally so.

4. What he says—that God was prepared to give Adam willing and doing unless Adam of his own accord and by a voluntary motion had turned himself away from God and his grace—contains a manifest absurdity. For it is just as if he had said that Adam would have obeyed God unless he had refused to obey God; or, to explain it in this way: unless Adam of his own accord and by a voluntary motion had turned himself away from God and his grace, he certainly would not of his own accord and by a voluntary motion have turned himself away from God and his grace. For God’s having been prepared to give him both willing and doing is nothing other than God’s having been prepared to ensure that Adam did not of his own accord and by a voluntary motion turn himself away from God and his grace. To such an extent, indeed, are the things customarily uttered most splendidly by these Doctors foul and corrupt: namely, they advance reasonings woven together with no thread of even tolerable consequence. Certainly, whenever they think themselves to philosophize very plausibly about the mode of our conversion to God, they cast themselves into labyrinths of this kind, from which no explanation, however exact, can discover an exit. But Arminius will also have a place to attempt and try this explanation, into which we have also gathered our studies on this matter; these, learned reader, we gladly submit to your judgment.
