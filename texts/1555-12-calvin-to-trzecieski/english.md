# Calvin to Andrzej Trzecieski, Letter 2372

Geneva, late December 1555 (editorial placement)

This is a fresh translation of the complete Latin letter at CO XV 910–912.
Calvin urges the Polish humanist Andrzej Trzecieski to undertake a Polish
translation of Scripture.

## Translation

### [CO XV, p. 910]

To the noble Master Andrzej Trzecieski, Polish knight, a man outstanding in
godliness and learning.

### [CO XV, p. 911]

Greetings. You may perhaps wonder, most distinguished man, what consideration
led me to decide to write to you before some other approach to your friendship
had been opened to me. But that which is foreign to common custom, and still
more to my own personal disposition, the excellent Master Francis Lismanino
forced from me: that I should deal with you as familiarly as if you had long
been known to me. For when he spoke of your consummate intellectual skill and
the excellence of your learning, he added that by the wishes and entreaties of
many you are being pressed to undertake a splendid work, worthy of the highest
praise—the translation of the sacred books into the Polish language—but that
by declining modestly you are drawing out the time, so that this delay is
troubling to very many good and learned men.

Although the difficulty of the work deservedly keeps you hesitant and
anxious, nevertheless, in the very great need that you see, no just reason
seems available for you to defer your nation's desires any longer, nor does
it seem that an honorable excuse for inaction can be offered. You know the
old saying, “Noble things are difficult.” It not only restrains the rashness
of those who dare attempt anything beyond their powers; it also corrects the
sluggishness of timid people whom diffidence obstructs and delays.

The magnitude of the work deters you. If it does so only to this extent—that,
distrusting yourself, you implore aid from heaven—this fear deserves praise.
Meanwhile, however, you must take care not to deprive your nation of the
service that it rightfully requires from you. If the ability to perform it
were shared by many others with you, you might, not without a plausible
pretext, summon them into partnership in the labor. But now that you possess
a singular gift, consider yourself the one divinely chosen upon whose
shoulders this burden rests. If you do not take up the burden laid upon you,
take care lest you cast off God's yoke.

Furthermore, just as I judged that I should deal with you personally about
this matter, so I shall not hesitate to exhort you freely to consecrate every
kind of service to Christ. Your kindness, I hope, will grant me that freedom.
You know better from the situation before you how ardently the ungodly exert
themselves to retain their tyranny. We should be ashamed of our sloth if at
least their so obstinate struggle does not kindle us to godly emulation, so
that we fight no less vigorously to vindicate God's glory, restore his pure
worship, and establish Christ's kingdom.

But perhaps I say these things to you out of season. Yet if you are already
sufficiently motivated of your own accord, I do not fear that my spurs will
cause you any annoyance. For in this part too our efforts are always slower
than is right, so there is no room for resentment; and the more eagerly
anyone runs,

### [CO XV, p. 912]

the more gladly, conscious of his weakness, he allows himself to be urged.
Farewell, most excellent man, worthy of my heartfelt esteem. May the Lord
enrich you daily with the gifts of his Spirit, strengthen you with power, and
protect you by his defense. Geneva.

## Translator's notes

1. CO's heading identifies the recipient as Andrzej Trzecieski (*Andreas
   Tricesius*), son of the Polish humanist and patron also named Andrzej. The
   edition's biographical note describes the younger Trzecieski as learned in
   Hebrew, Greek, and Latin and as the owner of a distinguished Kraków
   library; that editorial note is not part of Calvin's letter.
2. Francis Lismanino, a Franciscan provincial who embraced the Reformation,
   was then preparing to return to Poland. In this cluster of letters he
   supplied Calvin with information and carried exhortations to Polish
   nobles and reformers.
3. The Greek maxim *δύσκολα εἶναι τὰ καλά* means literally “noble things are
   difficult.” Calvin applies it in both directions: it checks overconfidence
   but also rebukes the paralysis of a capable person made hesitant by
   modesty.
4. The “sacred books” are the Scriptures. Calvin is asking Trzecieski to take
   responsibility for a Polish Bible translation, not merely a devotional
   selection. CO says no Polish scriptural version was extant that could be
   attributed to him; later scholarship nevertheless includes Trzecieski
   among the contributors to the collaborative Brest Bible of 1563.
5. *In re praesenti* may carry the legal-humanist sense of knowing the matter
   from the situation itself or on the ground. The English “from the
   situation before you” preserves that contrast with Calvin's distant
   report.
6. The letter has no surviving day or month. Its body closes only with
   “Geneva.” CO places it with the coordinated letters sent around
   Lismanino's departure at the end of December 1555; the displayed date is
   therefore editorial, not an authorial dateline.
