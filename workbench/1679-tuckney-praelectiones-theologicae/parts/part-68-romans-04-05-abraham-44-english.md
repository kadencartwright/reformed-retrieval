2. Second, we turn to what Castellio says against the claim that God is
unwilling for a person freed from sins to become proud. Castellio says this
“is just as if you said that God is unwilling for him to be freed from sin
lest he sin, which is no less absurd than saying that Christ was unwilling
to heal the sick man lest the sick man become sick, as though God either did
not wish or were unable to cleanse him from pride as well as from the other
sins.”

I answer as follows.

1. The word “unable” is maliciously introduced, as though we had ever even
thought that God was not as able to subdue pride as the remaining sins.

2. Nor is the word “unwilling” much more candid. For God wills pride to be
completely removed even in the saints. Indeed, he wills this especially so
as to serve his glory, against which pride rises more than the other sins.
For this reason God resists it most strongly and wills it removed above all.

But pride was the first sin. In our now fallen nature we are especially
liable to it and cling to it most tenaciously. Almost all other sins have
their place in evil deeds, whereas pride must be feared in the best works.
It therefore puts down deeper roots and can be torn out or healed only with
the greatest difficulty. Just as it was the first cause of human ruin, so
it is the final vice against which we must fight. God therefore wills that
special precautions be taken against it.

And since pride can easily arise from the best works, as I have said, and
from total deliverance from the remnants of sins—just as Lucifer's pride
arose from considering his own brightness and dignity—God in his supreme
wisdom cures poison with poison in the ordering of his grace, the stronger
with the weaker. This is not so novel that Castellio, the greatest of
innovators, should be the one to deny it. It is plainly established from
Holy Scripture and has always prevailed in the church by the common
judgment of fathers and recent writers, Reformed and Roman alike. There is
no need to heap up testimonies here. Let one passage from Gregory the Great
suffice, *Morals*, book 19, chapters 4–5, on the words, “He made a weight for
the winds and weighed the waters by measure”: “The saints themselves, who
are carried to the heights by the Holy Spirit's support, are swept up by
certain temptations as long as they are in this life, lest they become
proud through any exaltation. Thus they are by no means able to advance as
far as they wish; rather, lest they be lifted up in pride, their virtues are
measured out in them.” Gregory shows this more fully in Elijah and Paul. On
the words “My grace is sufficient for you,” he says that God's express
voice shows that weakness is the guardian of virtue. “For we are well
guarded inwardly when, by God's ordering, we are tolerably tempted outwardly,
sometimes by afflictions and sometimes by vices,” as he shows from Paul in
Romans 7. To conclude everything: I do not say with Castellio that God is
unwilling for the believer to be wholly freed from sin here lest he sin,
but lest he sin more grievously; nor that he is unwilling to heal the sick
man lest he be sick, but lest he be fatally sick. Physicians commonly leave
a lesser disease in a mildly sick person untreated so that, as often
happens, they may oppose one more savage and deadly. They draw out the
material of fevers not only through the wounds of fontanelles, but treat a
lethargy with the fever itself.

This does not mean that God wills the existence of any sins or their
remnants in themselves by his simple will of complacency, as though he
simply delighted in them. Rather, for the reasons already given, he wills
them by a will of permission and ordering toward his own best ends, which
serve his glory and the greatest benefit and salvation of the elect. Though
the thorn in the flesh must be avoided in itself, insofar as it is a way to
virtue and the exercise of virtue, it is to be desired—though we do not
admit that it is simply to be desired. Aquinas speaks somewhat more harshly
in this way, and Estius after him. Calvin speaks more gently and, as is his
custom, more elegantly: “But what does it mean that Satan, who was a
murderer from the beginning, becomes Paul's physician?”

He answers: “In accordance with his nature and custom, Satan devised
nothing but a plan to kill and destroy; but this was the Lord's singular
kindness, to turn what was naturally deadly into medicine.” To this end the
wisest and likewise most holy God allows the remnants of sins to remain in
even the holiest people while they sojourn here: for the exercise and growth
of faith, repentance, humility, patience, and the other virtues belonging
to our pilgrimage. This continues until at last in death, when both the
body itself and the body of sin are put off, we may say with Christ as the
soul breathes its last, “It is finished.”

Thus at last—after making a rather long excursion into the two questions
just discussed—we have, I think, sufficiently answered Bellarmine's second
and third arguments against justification from the imputation of Christ's
righteousness.
