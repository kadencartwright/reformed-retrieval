## Part L: Postscript—The Lord’s Supper and the Person of Christ

[Part II | PDF page 239 | logical source scan 238 | printed page 237]

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to All Dr. Andreae’s Preceding Observations

Since these observations contain nothing except the emptiest insults, I will not allow myself to appear to abuse the readers’ patience any longer. I should instead ask their pardon because, both earlier while pressing my adversary’s footsteps, I repeated the same points so often, and now will again set down replies to matters I have already answered so often in both prefaces and in the disputation itself. For although that man—not now as a colloquy participant but again either seizing the person of the most illustrious prince or eagerly accepting it as imposed upon him—had put an end, as he himself, evidently the weightiest censor, says on page 549 of his edition of the Acts, “to Beza’s absurd, horrendous, and manifestly impious statements,” he nevertheless wished to declaim for four further pages, adding, as he put in the margin on page 553, “a brief repetition of Beza’s doctrine and glosses.” Therefore I too am compelled, very much against my will, to answer these points, some incompletely proposed—which is one of the chief forms of falsifying truth—some invented, and some twisted slanderously.

### Summary of Beza’s GLOSSES in the Controversy about the Lord’s Supper, as Charged by Dr. Andreae

1. “This is my body,” that is, “signifies my body,” or, “is the sign and figure of my body, which is absent from the earth and exists only in heaven.”

#### Theodore Beza’s Reply

A twofold slander confronts us at the very threshold. One is that the word SIGNIFIES is charged against me as though it had been put nakedly and simply, as if I had not diligently and carefully distinguished sacramental signification—in which the true presentation of the thing signified is always conjoined—from common and ordinary signification. The other is that these things are said against me in such a way that they can also be referred to the time of the institution of the Supper. It is as though I were insane enough to teach that, when Christ spoke these words, his body was absent from the earth and existed only in heaven, although at that time he was both present on the earth, from which he had not yet departed, and absent from the heavens, above which he had not yet ascended. Yet his sacramental presence then was no different from what it is now. I wished to note this here advisedly, because there is nothing so absurd that these people do not dare fasten it upon us. The entire matter stands thus. It is beyond controversy that the Eucharist consists of two things: one

[Part II | PDF page 240 | logical source scan 239 | printed page 238]

earthly, namely bread as a sign that strikes the very senses; the other heavenly, namely that true body of the Lord given for us. Although it is a true body taken from the earth, it is called heavenly partly because, on account of the glory that did not remove the nature of a body from it, it was raised even above the angels, and partly because in this mystery it is presented to be contemplated not by the bodily senses but by the eyes of the mind, as represented by the outward sign. Both are indeed proposed for this common end: that the bread should not merely be looked upon with the eyes of the body but also received by the hand and eaten by the mouth, and that the Lord’s body should not merely be recalled to memory but should also truly become for us the food of eternal life, the Holy Spirit effecting this by his power and the instrument of faith apprehending it. Thus far, then, there is agreement between us, as I judge. If everyone weighed this attentively enough, the remaining controversies in this business also—namely concerning the sacramental conjunction and the manner of receiving the thing signified—would long ago have been settled among lovers of truth. But these matters have already been debated more than enough. I now say only that two things diverse in number and nature, proposed in this mystery for distinct particular ends, to distinct objects—one, namely, to the bodily senses and the other to the mind—and in distinct modes—one bodily and the other spiritual—cannot be one and the same thing. It therefore necessarily follows that neither the body can be predicated of the bread itself, nor the bread of the body, *hypostatically* [ὑπαρκτικῶς]. “But,” you say, “Christ’s statement must be true.” Entirely so. Yet since truth itself, as we have just said, forbids the literal wording [τὸ ῥητόν] to be retained in that predication, and the analogy of our faith in no way permits it, all who are not contentious must confess that “body” is said of the bread itself not because the bread hypostatically is the body itself, but because, so that the force and use of this sacramental signification might be declared more expressly, the designation of the thing signified is transferred to the sign. This form of speech, as our writers have shown so often, is not only so familiar to Holy Scripture that it scarcely speaks otherwise of sacred signs, but is also confirmed by ordinary usage—so much so that even empty pictures are customarily called by the names of the things depicted. What lust for wrangling is this, then, to curse the word SIGNIFIES, and the terms SIGN and FIGURE, and very many similar words that undeniably occur everywhere in all the ancient orthodox Greek and Latin fathers, as though they were profane and wicked? Yet the sacraments cannot be defined otherwise. And although sacramental signification is not always joined with reception, because of the fault of those approaching, nevertheless, as regards God who promises, we rightly always join it with the true presentation of the thing signified. My adversaries would evidently have me add that the bread signifies the body present by its essence on earth with the bread and presented to the mouth itself. Neither the truth of Christ’s body nor his

[Part II | PDF page 241 | logical source scan 240 | printed page 239]

true ascension above the heavens and return only on the last day, nor the very nature and spiritual end of the sacraments permit this. But even if—God forbid—we granted it, would this not still remain firm: that the bread is truly called Christ’s own body not by essence but only by sacramental signification? Certainly, if we examine more deeply the very dogma of transubstantiation, by which bread is transformed into what they call the species of bread, or accidents without a subject, truth possesses such force that the dogma cannot be defended without finally resorting to that sacramental metonymy. For the species of bread can no more hypostatically be Christ’s body itself than can the very essence of bread. This is enough concerning the GLOSS charged against us.

2. “The one who eats and drinks unworthily is guilty of the Lord’s blood,” that is, “the impious become guilty of Christ’s body and blood not by eating but by neglecting and rejecting Christ’s body.”

#### Theodore Beza’s Reply

It is evidently an error, and a grave one, to say with Paul that just as we are grafted into Christ by faith alone, so Christ has nothing in common with Belial, nor light with darkness—much less that Belial should receive Christ into himself, or darkness the light itself, or that light could be received without illuminating the darkness—and, with the same apostle, to deny that anyone can be a participant of both the Lord’s table and the table of demons. But let Dr. Andreae further say with what conscience he deleted the words “of that bread” and “of that cup”—whether it was to mock the reader, as though Paul said that Christ’s body and blood were eaten and drunk unworthily. For Paul did not simply say, “Whoever eats or drinks unworthily is held guilty of the Lord’s body and blood,” but, “Whoever eats THIS BREAD or DRINKS THIS CUP of the Lord unworthily,” because contempt for the sacred symbol evidently passes over to the very thing signified. Is this acting in good faith?

3. “Not discerning the Lord’s body,” that is, “not distinguishing from ordinary bread this bread of the Lord’s Supper, which has the name and designation of the Lord’s body.”

#### Theodore Beza’s Reply

Dr. Andreae wrote this not only incompletely but also with the sense corrupted. For I did not say the unworthiness that the apostle deservedly censures consisted solely in unworthy communicants’ failure to distinguish the sacred

[Part II | PDF page 242 | logical source scan 241 | printed page 240]

bread from any ordinary bread, as though this injury to Christ stopped with contempt for the bread alone, but that it passes over to Christ’s own body, of which that sacred bread is the sacrament. These are my words in my larger Annotations: “One is said to discern the Lord’s body who takes account of its dignity, distinguishes it from other things, and consequently approaches this bread and cup with the highest reverence—not as common foods but, trembling, as mysteries of a most precious thing.” Is what that man does really stating the truth simply, or is it slandering?

### Dr. Andreae’s GLOSSES concerning the Lord’s Supper

Now let us hear what sort of man he proves himself here to be, he who so fiercely rebukes us as corrupters of the Lord’s testament, and whether he does not himself utterly destroy the entire institution of the Lord’s Supper rather than merely contaminate it. First, in his reply to Sperling and in the theses against the Heidelbergers, he denies that the cause of the sacramental conjunction and presence is to be sought from the words of institution, or that the presence with the bread and wine of the Lord’s sacred Supper is different from the presence with any other creature. From this it necessarily follows that with precisely the same reasoning it can be said of anything whatever, “This is Christ’s body, given for us.” “For the words of the Lord’s Supper,” he says, “are only words of the distribution of Christ’s body and blood, already communicated before and from another cause—namely, of that body and blood which, through the union of a property, is essentially present in bread and wine as in any other creature.” Is this not to tear the institution of the Lord’s Supper up by its foundations, so that in its place a merely Eutychian definition of the hypostatic union may be established?

Moreover, I say and affirm that there is no single word in the statement “This is my body” that he has not removed by his interpretation. I intend the same to be said of the other part of the institution, which concerns the blood. Unless I prove this openly, I do not refuse to be regarded as a filthy sycophant. First, in the Maulbronn colloquy, he contends that the pronoun THIS demonstrated the bread Christ took in his hand, broke, and offered to his disciples, so that THIS has the same force as THIS BREAD. Luther himself everywhere holds this view, and the earlier Brenz—that is, before he openly became a ubiquitarian—explains it by synecdoche. Those who renew the old controversies do not indeed dare deny the synecdoche altogether, but they wish it to be so ordinary that on its account no trope is introduced into Christ’s statement, because, as they do not fear to write with the papists, only simple expressions are suitable for testaments. Is Dr. Andreae, then, now sharper-sighted than both Luther and his own Brenz? He wishes Christ to have spoken just as if

[Part II | PDF page 243 | logical source scan 242 | printed page 241]

some merchant, pointing to pepper contained in a sack without any regard for the sack, said, “This is pepper,” or as if someone, offering a drink to his companion and presenting wine contained in a cup, said, “This is Rhine wine” or “Alsatian wine.” But this interpretation of the phrase is sufficiently refuted by the fact that its author later reestablishes this interpretation—indeed, most stubbornly maintains it: “This is my body,” that is, “My body is in, under, or with this bread.” This certainly cannot stand in any way unless he himself presupposes that the bread is demonstrated by that pronoun.

Let us now come to the verb or copula IS. Although he so stubbornly defends its proper and hypostatic [ὑπαρκτικήν] signification that, because we take refuge in the meaning [διάνοιαν], he curses us as the most pernicious of all heretics, he nevertheless manifestly removes it in reality, wishing “This is my body” to be the same as “In, with, and under this bread is my body.” By this explanation not only is what was the subject in Christ’s statement turned into the predicate, and conversely, but—what is the most manifest falsification—the question by which one says what a thing is is transformed into the question where a thing is. In place of sacramental and spiritual conjunction, upon which the use of the sacramental institution depends, it degenerates into the utterly false inclusion of one essence in another, or its concealment under another, or its comprehension with another. And this is evidently “preserving the literal wording” [τὸ ῥητόν].

Finally, if we believe the ubiquitarians, what sort of thing will this body of Christ be? Finite, visible, local, remaining in itself, and yet, from the very moment of the hypostatic union, present everywhere by its essence infinitely, invisibly, and without location; and for a particular end most intensely present in, under, or with that bread in however many places the Lord’s Supper is celebrated according to his word—not indeed to be touched by the communicants’ hand, for some reverence, or rather this remnant of ancient superstition, remains here too, but to be thrust into the mouth and really eaten even by the mouth of the most unworthy, with his own bread distributed to each communicant. Is the reason perhaps a fear that if the breaking of bread were preserved, the substance of Christ’s body impanated in it would itself be torn apart? What, then, remains intact in the institution of the sacred Supper on this account?

### GLOSSES Charged by Dr. Andreae against Theodore Beza in the Article concerning Christ’s Person

1. “The Word became flesh”; likewise, “You are the Christ, Son of the living God,” that is, “The man united with Deity is a man who is borne and sustained by the Son of God.”

#### Theodore Beza’s Reply

May God restrain the spirit of falsehood. Have I really interpreted this

[Part II | PDF page 244 | logical source scan 243 | printed page 242]

statement, “The Word became flesh,” so that, inverting the terms of the statement, I substituted the person according to both natures for the Word as subject? Or have I ineptly explained both propositions in which the natures are distinctly considered? This corruption certainly shows that the man either has utterly rubbed away his forehead or understands neither what we say nor what he says against whom. The matter stands thus. In the former statement the subject declares Christ’s deity as the second person in the Trinity, while the predicate declares the human nature, and that as singular and in the concrete. The sense therefore is that God the Word became man by assuming that human nature, that is, assuming it in himself to such a point as to confer upon it his own subsistence. Thus John’s words describe the union [ἕνωσις] of the person of the Word with that assumed human nature, from which afterward arises the communication of properties [ἰδιωμάτων κοινωνία]. Nor in the latter statement, in which Christ is called Son of the living God, is any property named. Rather, by a statement proper in every respect, the designation “Son” is attributed to Christ—that is, to the person—whether distinctly according to the divine nature, as if, speaking of Peter, I said Peter was endowed with a soul, or according to both natures, as if I said Peter was a human being. For although Christ is not Son of the living God according to the flesh, as Eutyches blasphemed, nevertheless even insofar as he is man he is truly Son of the living God, since this man does not subsist by himself, as Nestorius imagined.

2. “This man is God,” that is, “The Son of God united with humanity is God.”

#### Theodore Beza’s Reply

The same falsehood appears here again. We do indeed teach that this man is God for no other reason than that this humanity was assumed by the Son of God into the unity of the person, and that the statement is true in no other way. But the statement *that it is so* [τὸ ὅτι] must not on that account be confused with the statement *why it is so* [τὸ διότι]. And although the designations God and man include properties of the natures, they declare not the properties themselves but existing realities.

3. “That man is omnipotent,” that is, “The deity united to humanity is omnipotent.” “God suffered,” that is, “The flesh united to deity suffered.”

#### Theodore Beza’s Reply

These are pure slanders, although the sense of these interpretations is perfectly true. But it is the ubiquitarians’ practice, not ours, to explain concretes by abstracts and abstracts by concretes, and with a doubled error: “man” is explained by “deity united to humanity,” and “God” by “flesh united to deity,” which are utterly crude expressions. From the usage of Holy Scripture and the writings of all the

[Part II | PDF page 245 | logical source scan 244 | printed page 243]

orthodox fathers against Nestorius and Eutyches, we teach that the whole person, because it is one, is often denominated from only one nature—that is, called Man or God—but only in the concrete; and hence it happens that through reciprocal exchange [ἀντίδοσις] the properties [ἰδιώματα] of one nature are attributed to the other: either divine properties to the man, as when “the Man is omnipotent” is said, or human properties to God, as when “God suffered” is said. Unless these are prudently distinguished so that divine properties are attributed to the man *according to another respect* [κατ’ ἄλλο], and human properties likewise to God *according to another respect*, one must fall into the Charybdis of Eutyches, as Cyril especially warns in countless places. Both the divine and the human are nevertheless attributed to the whole person according to itself [κατ’ αὐτό], but always with the properties of each nature distinct, just as the hypostatic definition requires, no less than the natures themselves. Therefore those people are also seriously mistaken who suppose that, through the communication of properties [κοινωνία ἰδιωμάτων], divine things are attributed according to itself [καθ’ αὑτό] to the person denominated in the concrete from either nature no less than when “Christ is omnipotent” or “Christ suffered” is said. “Otherwise,” they say, “we divide the person with Nestorius.” But I, on the contrary, say this lest we confuse the natures with Eutyches. If Dr. Andreae either does not understand these things or pretends not to understand them, am I therefore a heretic, or do I corrupt the Lord’s word with some GLOSSES?

### Dr. Andreae’s GLOSSES in His Treatment of Christ’s Person

1. Concerning John’s words “The Word became flesh,” behold what sort of God Christ is for Dr. Brenz and Dr. Andreae. “From this statement of the Gospel, ‘The Word became flesh,’ which is said of Christ, not Peter, the distinction of Christ and Peter must be established” (says Brenz on page 40 of his *Recognition*), “because although the Son of God fills Peter as well as the man Christ by his essence, he nevertheless does not communicate all his properties to Peter, but only some.” Dr. Andreae, however, says in his *Apology to the Ingolstadt Theses*: “The man Christ is called God not because God is in him (for he is in all creatures, not only by nature but also according to substance), but because in the assumption of flesh he gave this man all power in heaven and on earth. Likewise, the deity of the man Christ is not that eternal essence, common with the Father and the Spirit, but one communicated by the second hypostasis.”

Concerning Christ’s humanity before and after the ascension into heaven, Brenz writes as follows on page 273 of the *Recognition*: “Christ (evidently when dead) was not in the tomb as the little women sought him, nor according to external appearance. Yet he was not only in the tomb but also in heaven and on earth according to the majesty of his deity.” And elsewhere in the same writing: “Christ according to his humanity, or by his humanity, was at the same instant both in the manger at Bethlehem and in heaven at the Father’s right hand.” And Dr. Andreae, in those theses full of Eutychian blasphemies, says, “Christ’s glorious body is no longer physical,”
