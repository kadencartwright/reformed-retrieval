ON

GABRIEL PENOTTUS’S

*Bulwark of Human Liberty*

BOOK III, CHAPTER XX.

In which he asks whether future contingents can be known with certain and infallible knowledge from
the inspection of secondary causes alone.

There is plainly no need for us to linger longer over this question. For the resolution of this
question readily inclines to the negative side. If these secondary causes are so disposed that they act
contingently and indifferently toward opposites, then to know the effect determinately from inspecting
them is plainly the same as to infer the determination of the effect from the indetermination of the
cause, as though someone argued thus: “This or that cause is no more determined toward this effect than
toward its opposite; therefore it will certainly and infallibly produce this effect and not its
opposite.” But if secondary causes are so disposed that they act necessarily, yet are capable of being
impeded and resisted in their operation, it cannot come about that those effects toward whose necessary
production they are disposed—so far as concerns the causes themselves—are certainly and infallibly
known to be going to be produced. Nevertheless, all who undertake to assert the doctrine of middle
knowledge must either support in this way the certain and infallible knowledge of future contingents
which they attribute to God or support it with no prop at all. Penottus both acknowledges that Molina
regularly did this and faults him for it, as may be seen in the preceding chapter, number 3. But since
Penottus cannot embrace this root of certainty, he rests upon none; instead, he introduces a certainty
of divine knowledge weighed by no weight and resting upon no foundation. “To assign,” he says, “the
reason why God, from eternity forestalling and anticipating

[Book I, printed p. 116; BSB canvas 00142]

the differences of times, sees the things which are future before they come to be, and how by his
intuitive cognition he reaches the very being of actual existence which a thing will have when it is in
the world, before it is really in the world—this is the summit of the difficulty in this matter, this is
the immense sea of divine wisdom, which cannot be satisfied by the things Molina has. And Thomas’s
argument does indeed demonstrate the fact, namely that matters stand in such a way that from eternity,
without succession, God sees whatever will successively be future in every difference of time. But it
does not appear sufficiently to demonstrate the mode of the fact—that is, how this comes about.
Therefore, to speak sincerely, perhaps this knot cannot be manifestly untied in the present life, and
perhaps in this sense that opinion related in Chapter 11 from Gabriel Ockham and Marsilius is true.”

These are Penottus’s words, but beyond all candor—though he makes a show of sincerity—he passes over the
very summit of the difficulty. If it were candidly proposed, it would make a way for removing the whole
difficulty from our midst. For the difficulty does not lie in how God understands from eternity the
things which are future. Beyond all dispute, the manner of this is set before us in the case of certain
future things, namely those depending upon God’s choice alone. Rather, the summit of the present
difficulty is this: how even those things which depend upon the creature’s will were made future out of
things merely possible, and that from eternity. For once it is posited that they are future, they must
be understood by God, since otherwise the divine intellect would by no means be infinite. But since no
account of this migration can be given apart from the determination of the divine will, it is manifest
from this that the account of how God understands is just as readily at hand in this case as is the
account of how he understands the future things whose production depends upon God’s will alone.

Yet I am also pleased to examine how Penottus conducts himself in treating this controversy. First he
proposes a distinction among future things which is not very exact, namely between things future simply
and things future in a qualified respect. He describes things future in a qualified respect in this
manner: “They have possible being in their causes tending toward the being of actual existence in such
a way that, if the causes were not impeded, they would in fact attain that being; but merely because of
a supervening impediment in the secondary causes they will in fact never attain the very being of
actual existence. These, therefore, are called future in a qualified respect—that is, according to the
disposition and order of their causes, but not simply and absolutely.”

But I am exceedingly displeased with this description, and that on two counts. First, since a future
thing of this sort is something that will at some time be outside its secondary causes, a person would
distinguish most absurdly if he said, “The thing will be outside its causes either simply or in a
qualified respect,” if by “in a qualified respect” something is signified from which it comes about that
it never goes forth outside its causes. An Ethiopian is said to be white in a qualified respect insofar
as he is white with respect to his teeth; therefore he has whiteness, although not so widely diffused
through the greater part that he could be called white simply. But something which will never be
outside its causes even in part is most absurdly called future in a qualified respect.

But you will say: “A physician who sees a bad temperament of the humors certainly predicts that the
sick person’s death will be future even if it is prevented by the application of medicines, and he
predicts truly, because for his statement to be true it suffices to see such a temperament of the
humors that death would infallibly follow from it if it were not impeded by medicines.” But, I say, it
is as easy for us to erase as it is for you to invent or paint. A physician is not accustomed to say
absolutely that death will be future, but with a condition of this sort interposed: in the absence of a
refusal to employ medicine or of divine providence. And if perhaps he does say it as you imagine, it
must be understood only as spoken interpretatively, the entire meaning being that the disease is
signified to be lethal by its nature. Astrological predictions have the same account, though far less
certainty in kind: “There will be hailstorms”—that is, in their judgment the heavenly bodies are or will
be so disposed as to produce hailstorms.

Again you will say: “But the Scriptures too speak in this manner about future things. Thus Isaiah says
to Hezekiah, ‘Set your house in order, because you will die,’ 2 Kings 20. ‘Yet forty days, and Nineveh
will be overthrown,’ Jonah 3. ‘If Ahab has died in the city, the dogs will eat him; but if he has died in
the field, the birds of heaven will eat him,’ 1 Kings 21 and 23.”

I answer that Isaiah’s statement to Hezekiah signifies only that the disease from which the king was
suffering was incurable by the powers of nature. Nor did the king doubt that he could be healed by
divine power; hence he undertook to entreat God with prayers to grant him a longer life. And this would
be called future in a qualified respect only absurdly, since it would not be future absolutely in any
part. Whatever things are called such in a qualified respect are such absolutely, not conditionally.

The account of Jonah’s prophecy to the Ninevites is different, for that declaration was by no means
absolute but conditional, although the form of the condition is not expressed at all in Jonah’s
prophecy. Nor did the Ninevites themselves understand that declaration otherwise. “At one moment I
speak,” says the Lord in Jeremiah 18:7, “against a nation or against a kingdom, that I will uproot,
demolish, and destroy it. But when that nation against which I have spoken turns from its wickedness, I
will repent of the evil which I had thought to do to it.”

But the third passage proposed rests upon a false translation by the Vulgate interpreter. The Hebrew
text has המרת לאהב, “he who has died belonging to Ahab,” that is, whoever from Ahab’s offspring or
household has died. Nor do the Seventy Interpreters depart at all from this sense: τὸν τεθνηκότα τῷ Αχαάβ
ἐν τῇ πόλει φάγονται αἱ κύνες, “the one belonging to Ahab who has died in the city, the dogs
will eat,” and so forth. I acknowledge every prophecy of threatening to be true, but not in such a way
that what God threatens would be future in a qualified respect, which cannot be said without absurdity;
rather, unless they repent, it will come to pass in this manner. In my judgment the disposition of
natural causes which act naturally and necessarily is also not rightly confused with the disposition
of moral causes, which do not act altogether efficiently to call down the wrath of God from heaven upon
their own heads but only meritoriously. Yet I am not unaware that Aquinas confuses these dispositions,
2a2ae, q. 171, art. 6, reply 2.

So much for the first objection to this distinction; the second follows. A description of future
things in this manner can be accommodated only to those things which are future from natural causes,
which are indeed determined toward one thing and would in fact attain that toward which they are
determined if they were not impeded. But it cannot be accommodated to things which arise only from free
causes. For since free causes, in whatever order they are placed, are

[Book I, printed p. 117; BSB canvas 00143]

indifferent to acting and are not determined toward one thing, it cannot likewise be said of them that
they are so disposed toward some one thing that they would in fact attain it if they were not impeded.
For they are not so disposed toward anything that they are not indifferent, according to choice,
either to acting or not acting, or to this or that. Nor can they be said to be impeded. For just as the
will cannot be compelled to will something, so it cannot be prevented from not willing. The account of
each is the same. But I readily believe that by this distinction Penottus wished to prepare his reader
to imbibe his doctrine of middle knowledge somewhat more easily.

From here Penottus proceeds to inquire whether conjectural knowledge can be attributed to God, namely
from the contemplation of secondary causes, whose indetermination can evidently generate only
conjectural cognition; and by a certain distinction he settles the controversy, saying: “It can happen
in two ways that these future contingents are known in secondary causes: in one way, by knowing only
the order and disposition of the causes themselves toward such effects; in the other way, by knowing
not only the disposition and order of the causes but also the outcome of the order and disposition.
The first knowledge is of itself imperfect and conjectural. The second, although not perfect in that
respect in which it is only knowledge of the disposition and order, is nevertheless perfect in that it
is knowledge of the outcome of the order itself. But God knows these future contingents not in the
first but in the second way. Therefore no imperfection is posited in divine knowledge, because although
such knowledge is not perfect in every respect merely in that it is knowledge of the order, it is
nevertheless perfect in that it is knowledge of the outcome of the order itself.”

Scarcely anything solid or sound occurs in these things. The whole distinction, and especially the
accommodation of the distinction, is better fitted to obscure the truth than to illustrate it. For one
thing is knowledge of the order and disposition of the causes themselves, and another is knowledge of
the future event from the knowledge of this order and disposition of the causes. Knowledge of the order
is perfect in its own kind just as knowledge of the outcome of the order is, and the one knowledge is
attributed to God just as properly as the other. But knowledge of the future event from knowledge of
the order and disposition of the causes is merely conjectural and therefore imperfect, and it does not
fall within God. For its imperfection is not removed by the perfection of another knowledge. In sum,
God knows the order and disposition of secondary causes thoroughly, and he also knows thoroughly that
every cognition of a future event proceeding from knowledge of the disposition of those causes is
merely conjectural. That knowledge of God is infallible and not at all conjectural.

From here he advances to the principal question: “Whether, namely, contingents which are simply and
absolutely future can be known certainly and infallibly in contingent secondary causes alone; or
whether, by inspecting only the order of causes toward contingent effects and prescinding from
knowledge of the outcome of that order, God can certainly know the contingent effects themselves.”
Concerning this doubt he reports two opinions of the Doctors. Some of them say: “God cannot know this
in indeterminate secondary causes, but he can indeed know them in this way in the same causes when
perfectly determined toward producing the effects themselves. So Cajetan holds in the cited q. 14,
art. 13, § ‘But in reply to the objections against the application’; Báñez there in conclusion 3;
Nazarius in controversy 2, conclusion 4; Ripa, disputation 1, chapter 4; and Alvarez, disputation 12.
Durandus, 1, distinction 38, q. 3, no. 2; Argentina, distinction 40, art. 3, reply 6, against conclusion
1; and Gregory of Valencia, q. 14, art. 13, point 1, appear to incline to the same opinion. The
exception is that Báñez, Nazarius, Ripa, and Alvarez say that God can know future contingents in
secondary causes determined passively, while Durandus, Argentina, and Valencia say that they are known
in secondary causes determined actively by themselves.”

Yet Valentinianus no more asserts this concerning the active determination of a secondary cause—as
Penottus calls it—than concerning the passive, namely determination through God. For he writes thus at
the same place, point 5, § 2, p. 249: “We speak of a free contingent cause not only as it is not impeded,
but also as it is freely determined by itself—either naturally or through the aid of divine grace—to
one thing rather than another.” Nor is it an objection that he says this determination of the created
will is brought about by the will itself. For even those who subject the determination of the created
will to divine determination by no means deny that the created will is determined by itself. They
intend that God determines the created will to determine itself, and indeed to determine itself
freely. And lest even so some covering be drawn over those men from Valentinianus’s statement that “a
free contingent cause is freely determined to one thing through the aid of divine grace,” Valentinianus
continues and says that a determination of this sort is equivalent to natural determination: “By the
very fact that through its perfection, whether natural or aided by grace, equivalent to some extrinsic
change through which it can be understood to be determined, it actually elicits an action concerning
one of the opposites rather than the other.”

And he draws Scotus to this point, who on the preceding page affirms that God knows future things
certainly in the determination of his own will. “And perhaps,” he says, “what Scotus affirms in 1,
distinction 39, the single question, can also be understood in this way, namely that God certainly knows
future contingents in the determination of his will, which is immutable. For this appears to be the
same in fact as God certainly knowing future contingents because he sees that his will is now
proximately determined toward them and cannot be impeded by any other cause.” Since secondary causes
are not determined in time to acting any earlier than they act, while God certainly knows both this
determination of secondary causes and the effects themselves from eternity, I certainly do not
understand how this determination of secondary causes can be set before divine cognition from eternity
except in the determination of his own will. Nor can it at all be understood how a thing merely
possible by its nature becomes future except through the will of God decreeing that the thing will be
future, as we have already explained more than once.

Nor does Durandus’s opinion exclude a determination of the indifferent cause to be made by the will of
God; indeed, it rather includes it. For he holds that the certainty of God’s cognition of future things
arises only from considering all the things which can and will determine an indifferent cause. Thus
Durandus in 1, distinction 38, q. 3, no. 9: “Likewise, when an indifferent and impedible cause is known,
if with it are known all the things which can and will determine it, and all the things which can impede
it, and which will or will not impede it, it can be infallibly known which effect will occur.”

[Book I, printed p. 118; BSB canvas 00144]

“I say nothing about Argentina, since he is not at hand. The second opinion,” he says, “is that of those
who teach that future contingents themselves can in no way be certainly known in contingent secondary
causes alone. Ferrariensis, *Contra Gentiles* 1.67, § ‘Concerning the assigned difference,’ appeared,”
he says, “to incline to this opinion. But Suárez expressly defends it in book 1, *On the Knowledge of
Future Contingents*, chapter 3. All those who wish the ground of certain knowledge of those future
contingents to be taken from the presentiality alone of future contingents must also defend it.”

But Penottus neither listed those who establish this, nor have I observed them thus far. Nor is it at
all probable that they do not consider secondary causes as indeterminate. He also cites Ferrariensis
modestly for this opinion as though he only inclined in this direction; he did not venture to affirm it
as certain, but merely says that Ferrariensis appears to incline in this direction. As for Suárez, he
acknowledges that the human will can be determined infallibly in a moral though not a physical kind. It
is clear that a determination of the latter kind obstructs human liberty, while one of the former kind
does not; book 3, *On Aids*, chapter 10, no. 5. Let the reader also see how shamefully he conducts
himself when he approaches the dissolution of the foundations of the other opinions.

[Marginalia: Lib. 1. de absoluta scientia futuror. contingent. c. 3. n. 16.]

“To the first,” he says, “it is answered that those expressions, ‘If in Tyre and Sidon,’ and so forth,
and similar ones are not grounded in cognition of future effects in their causes but in the conditional
presence of future contingents.” For that foreknowledge either has a foundation in the contemplation of
secondary causes or it has no foundation at all. Universally, their doctrine of middle knowledge either
rests upon no prop at all or upon the contemplation of secondary causes alone, just as Molina
philosophizes about the comprehension of free choice which God has, from which by the infinity of his
intellect he foreknows all its effects from eternity. But to settle the disturbances of minds at this
summit, Penottus inserts himself as an honorary arbitrator.

He therefore intends that, when a contingent cause has been perfectly determined, contingent effects
can be known in it. But, I say, a free cause is not determined at any instant of time earlier than the
effect exists. Indeed, for a free cause to be determined to willing is nothing other than for the free
cause to will vitally, either from itself alone or also from a divine operation flowing into it.
Penottus therefore intends that the two opinions agree in fact and differ only in the mode of speaking:
“Whether, namely, because the cause is prior to the effect, the effect can be said to be known in the
cause itself insofar as in that prior state it is conceived as determined toward the effect; or whether,
because this determination—although it can be conceived as prior in some instant of reason or even of
nature—exists only in the same instant of time in which the effect begins to issue from the cause, it
must be said that the effect cannot be known in the cause but only in itself. In this controversy,
which concerns only the mode of speaking, the second mode appears more proper and more in accordance
with the mind of Thomas,” and so forth.

Is this, I ask, anything other than to see the effect in itself, when it is supposed to be future—that
is, to have migrated from the nature of a thing merely possible into the condition of a future thing,
apart from every cause or account of this migration? Far better, in my judgment, is it said to be
certainly known in its causes, because in this way some foundation is laid beneath this cognition by
which the thing said to be known becomes truly knowable under the aspect of a future thing. Once this
is posited, it must by no means be doubted that it is comprehended by an infinite intellect, since
otherwise the intellect so called would not really be infinite. But because this determination of
secondary causes is itself among future contingents and is merely possible by its nature, what
secondary causes can be invented which render this determination of secondary causes future from
being possible, so that it becomes knowable under the aspect of a future thing? What secondary causes,
I ask, can be invented from the inspection of which God might be said infallibly to know the
determination of secondary causes, when it is nevertheless established that this determination must be
numbered among future contingents? If secondary causes were invented which were the causes of the
determination of secondary causes, and those too determined, would not a broad road thereby be opened
to a regress to infinity, from which all learned philosophy recoils?

I therefore judge it must be entirely denied that cognition of future contingents is to be attributed
to God from the determination of secondary causes, since this very determination of secondary causes
must be numbered among future contingents. It is therefore preferable to stop at the determination of
the divine will as that in which divine and indeed intuitive knowledge of future contingents is
grounded. Without it not even Thomas’s opinion can be recalled to sobriety. As for Penottus’s
interpretation of Aquinas, I think we have now seen how much it deserves to be valued. But let us weigh
the argument which he adds.

PENOTTUS.

“That this mode is more proper is proved because, for as long as an effect is conceived as still having
being only in a contingent cause insofar as it is such, it is always conceived as having uncertain and
indeterminate being; and it is conceived as having determinate being only when it is conceived as
issuing from the contingent cause itself. For we showed in book 1, chapter 29 and the three following,
that it belongs to the nature of a contingent cause, especially a free one, that when all the things
required to complete it in first act have been posited, insofar as it is conceived as prior to its
operation it always remains indifferent to operating or not operating.”

RESPONSE.

Although in an indeterminate contingent cause the effect is conceived as having uncertain and
indeterminate being, it is not so conceived in a determinate cause. Nor is it any objection that it is
not conceived as having determinate being except when it is conceived as issuing from the contingent
cause itself. For as soon as a free cause is conceived as determined to acting, it is conceived as
acting; and by this very fact the contingent effect must be conceived as issuing from the contingent
cause itself. Although before the instant in which a free cause acts it is conceived as indifferent to
acting, it is nevertheless not indifferent in the very instant in which it acts, or in which it is
determined to acting.

PENOTTUS.

“But that this mode of speaking is more conformable to Thomas’s doctrine is proved. For Thomas, 1, q.
14, art. 13—indeed, in all the other places which I have been able to find in which he speaks about
this matter, which places I noted above in Chapter 15—appears to assign only two modes according to
which we can conceive that some future contingent can be certainly known: in one mode in its contingent
causes; in the other

[Book I, printed p. 119; BSB canvas 00145]

mode in its presentiality. I omit cognition in the ideas, since whether it is granted or not it is of
no service at all to the present undertaking. Therefore either this knowledge of contingents in
ultimately determinate causes is comprehended under one of these two modes, or there is a third mode
distinct from them. The second should not be said, because if it were said, Thomas’s entire argument,
repeated in so many places, would be rendered insufficient, since it would proceed from an insufficient
enumeration of the modes of knowing future contingents themselves. But if we say the first, then either
this knowledge is comprehended under knowledge of the effect in a contingent cause—and this likewise
cannot be said, because Thomas expressly denies that a contingent effect can be known certainly and
infallibly in a contingent cause—or every certain and infallible knowledge of a future contingent in
Thomas’s opinion is therefore reduced to knowledge had from its presentiality.”

RESPONSE.

The answer is easy and ready at hand: this knowledge of future contingents in their presentiality is
nothing other than knowledge of future contingents before God in willed being—that is, in the
determination of his will. For according to Thomas, singulars are seen by God in the divine essence
either as it is their likeness or as it is their active principle. Thus for a thing to be seen in
itself, and for God to see a thing in himself—that is, in his essence as it is willing, namely in the
determination of his will as first cause—are altogether one and the same before Aquinas. Nor does he see
future things otherwise when they actually exist, although he sees them as being otherwise than before.
Before, he had seen that they would be future; then he sees that they are present; afterward he will see
that they are past. That is, they are otherwise and otherwise on the side of the things known, but not
on the side of the knower, since according to Augustine, on the side of the knower God does not see
things done otherwise than things to be done.

Therefore I readily concede that all the things which you gather from Thomas tend in this direction.
And I judge that there are just as many testimonies against your doctrine and that of the Jesuits,
posited for asserting middle knowledge and knowledge of futures not only absolutely but also
conditionally; likewise for confirming our opinion that cognition of future things is grounded in the
determination of the divine will. You hold the contrary, wish that this doctrine poses no obstacle to
the middle knowledge fabricated by you, and wish to assault the knowledge of future things which is
referred back to the determination of the divine will. I am therefore pleased to examine how you
conduct yourself in solving and refuting the arguments which you propose as though they ran against
your own opinion.

PENOTTUS.

“But the first argument against this doctrine is taken from certain authorities of divine Scripture,
from which we have that God knows certain future contingents which could not be known in themselves,
since they never were and are not going to be. Therefore they could not be known except in their
causes.

“It is answered that these are not numbered among things future absolutely, of which we are now
speaking, but are future only in a qualified respect, which can without impropriety be known in their
causes. For to know future things of this sort is nothing other than to know the order of their causes
toward them, if the causes are not impeded from their operation, together with this, however, that God
also sees the outcome of the causes themselves if they were not impeded.”

RESPONSE.

But where, I ask, does this distinction between things future absolutely and conditionally occur in
Aquinas? Where, finally, is this distinction between cognition of future things absolutely—not in
their causes but in themselves—and of future things conditionally—not in themselves but in their
causes? Will it not also follow from this that Thomas’s doctrine is not only insufficient but false,
since he acknowledges no cognition of future things in their causes but only in themselves? Yet the
entire force of your preceding dissertation was placed in this kind of argumentation.

II. We have already taught how absurdly you posit certain things future in a qualified respect. And
long ago Prosper complained to Augustine about the Massilians because they contended, with a novel kind
of absurdity, that things not to be done were foreknown. Augustine too, in *On the Predestination of the
Saints*, chapter 14, professes that passages of Scripture of this sort, which bear the appearance of
something such, were by no means spoken according to God’s foreknowledge: “He foreknew,” he says, “what
was going to be, not what was not going to be.”

[Marginalia: Epist. ad August.]

III. Suárez himself makes knowledge of conditional futures distinct from cognition of future
contingents in their causes, book 1, *On the Absolute Knowledge of Future Contingents*, chapter 3, no.
16. “To the first it is answered,” he says, “that those expressions, ‘If in Tyre and Sidon,’ and so forth,
and similar ones are not grounded in cognition of future contingent effects in their causes but in
conditional foreknowledge of future contingents.” In these words he expressly denies that conditional
futures are known in their causes, which Penottus nevertheless asserts here.

IV. When he says that God also sees the outcome of the causes themselves if they were not impeded, he
surpasses all sobriety by holding that something is seen by God which never was or will be. It would be
sober to say that it would come to pass that God saw the outcome of the causes themselves if they were
not impeded; yet Valentinianus denies that conditional cognition of future things of this sort is the
knowledge of vision and intends that it is only the knowledge of simple intelligence, 1, q. 14, p. 247.
So too Molina, disputation 14 at the end, as reported by you in Chapter 18. But to say that God presently
sees the outcome of the causes themselves when it will in fact never be future, and is not said to be
future except conditionally—namely, if those causes were not impeded—surpasses all sobriety.

V. If conditional futures are infallibly known in their causes, then absolute futures would certainly
be infallibly known in their causes. For if the absolute positing of causes does not suffice to ground
infallible cognition of absolute futures, certainly neither will the conditional positing of causes
suffice to ground infallible cognition of conditional futures. For the conditional positing of causes
is related in every way to conditional futures just as the absolute positing of causes is related to
absolute futures.

VI. You posit that the outcome of causes is seen by God, but without any foundation. For it cannot be
seen by God unless it is future. Yet this outcome of causes is by its nature merely possible. But whence
it has migrated out of the condition of a merely possible thing into the condition of a future thing,
you neither explain nor will be able to explain according to middle knowledge. Nor does any account of
explaining this difficulty remain at all unless recourse is had to the determination of the divine
will.

PENOTTUS.

“Second, a very recent author argues: An effect is certainly known in a cause which is necessary
simply, because

[Book I, printed p. 120; BSB canvas 00146]

such a cause has an infallible order toward such an effect, just as we know that every human being is
capable of laughter because he is rational. But a completely determined contingent cause which will
not be impeded, however free it may be and although it can fail to act, will nevertheless in fact
operate and produce its effect. Therefore its contingently future effect can also be known in such a
cause.

“I answer that, for the argument to consist of the proper form and matter, the arguer ought to have
subsumed this minor: ‘But a contingent cause, however free, when perfectly determined—at least by the
first cause—is a cause which is necessary simply.’ In fact, these minors ought to be subsumed in almost
all the arguments in which this author argues from his physical predetermination. But to conceal his
opinion he subsumes another minor, namely: ‘A completely determined and unimpeded contingent cause,
however free,’ and so forth. I have never been able to understand what proportion and connection this
minor has with the major such that the conclusion intended by the arguer follows.

“II. Moreover, if a completely determined contingent cause, even though it is free, can act and not
act, whence can it be known that it, so determined, can act and not act? Who will be able to understand
these paradoxes? For if, however determined, it is still free and can by its liberty act or not act, it
can no more be known from such determination that it will operate than that it will not operate. But if
it can be certainly known from such determination that it will operate, it no longer remains equally
indifferent to not operating as to operating.”

RESPONSE.

This very recent author is Diego Alvarez, *On Aids*, disputation 12. It is everywhere apparent how
Penottus was disposed toward him, so that from this claw alone you could recognize Penottus as a true
disciple of the Jesuits. From him Penottus is accustomed to select the things which he more conveniently
perceives to lie open to calumny and to be capable of being dragged into odium. Yet such things, as
opposed to the studies of the Jesuits, are very few. Between the Dominicans and the Jesuits, an
impartial assessor of affairs will notice without difficulty which have cultivated Scholastic theology
better and more learnedly. But to me the difference between them seems as great as that between solid
and superficial learning. As for Penottus, who conducts himself as an equal even to the Jesuits, I
certainly judge him unworthy to come under consideration on the same day as Alvarez. But let us call
Penottus’s answer to examination.

I. “The arguer,” he says, “ought to have subsumed this minor: ‘But a contingent cause, however free and
perfectly determined, is a cause necessary simply.’” Yet, I say, this proposition is true according to
Alvarez and also simply, provided it is rightly understood—namely, not absolutely but upon a
hypothesis, that is, upon the supposition of its determination. Again, Alvarez aims only to prove from
this that the effect of this cause can be infallibly known by God. But Penottus himself acknowledges
that from the determination of a contingent cause its effect is infallibly knowable. You may see him
discussing it in number 5 in this manner: “But when a contingent cause has been perfectly determined
toward some effect, that effect will then contain determinate truth and determinate being. Therefore it
will be certainly and infallibly knowable in a determinate contingent cause. And certainly no
philosopher ever denied that an effect can be known in its cause, but denies only that an effect can be
known in a cause not yet determined.”

Even Valentinianus the Jesuit explains that a determined contingent cause is equivalent in acting to a
necessary cause, 1, q. 14, point 5, § 2, p. 248. “Whatever may be true of Scotus’s mind, our assertion is
proved by an argument of this kind: Just as a necessary effect infallibly follows from a necessary
cause which cannot be impeded, so also a contingent effect infallibly and certainly follows from a
contingent cause which is indeed capable of being impeded but which is already proximately determined
to acting and is impeded by no cause at all. Therefore, just as for this reason in the first assertion,
according to the mind of all theologians, we posit that a necessary effect is certainly known by God in
a necessary cause, so it must also be conceded that a contingent effect is certainly known by him in a
contingent cause which is proximately determined and unimpeded.” It was therefore permissible for
Valentinianus to profess that a determined contingent cause is equivalent to a necessary cause—a
necessary cause which he undoubtedly acknowledged to be necessary even simply. But Alvarez will not
get away unpunished when this Novarian is his censor.

II. Second, Penottus argues by asking whence it can be known that the cause will in fact operate
infallibly if it can act and not act; or, if it will operate infallibly, whence it can be known that it,
so determined, can act and not act. But, I say, Alvarez does not say that a completely determined
contingent cause can act and not act. He does indeed say that a completely determined contingent cause
will operate infallibly, however much it can fail to act; he intimates that it can fail to act, not that
it can act and not act. For when he intimates that, notwithstanding its determination, it can fail to
act, he intimates only that the power of not acting belongs to that cause notwithstanding its
determination, which is also true. But when we say that a contingent cause is determined, we are by no
means accustomed to say that, notwithstanding its determination, it can act and not act. Indeed, when
we say that a contingent cause can act and not act, we are not accustomed to consider it as determined.

Nor, I think, would any sober person ask whence an effect can be known infallibly from a contingent
cause if it is only posited as completely determined. Everyone knows that determination to acting by no
means takes away the power of not acting. How much less was it fitting for Penottus to parade this as
contrary to reason, when he had already professed that, provided a contingent cause is determined, its
effect is certainly and infallibly known. For it is not credible that, in Penottus’s opinion, this
determination would take away that cause’s power of not acting. Why, then, would Penottus again ask, if
it will operate infallibly, whence it can be known that it, so determined, can fail to act? As though it
were a paradox that a contingent cause can be determined to acting while the power itself of not acting
remains unimpaired. You will say: “Indeed, Penottus does not ask whence it can be known that it, so
determined, can fail to act, but whence it can be known that it, so determined,

[Book I, printed p. 121; BSB canvas 00147]

can act and not act.” But, I say, if there is only so much difference between these things, with what
candor did Penottus undertake to corrupt Alvarez’s opinion and transform it from an opinion not only
true but open into an alien one, so that he might have something against which to run and exclaim,
“Who will be able to understand these paradoxes?” Alvarez’s opinion as proposed by him has nothing
paradoxical at all, but only as it has been transformed into an alien sense by Penottus himself. From
this you may see with what spirit he blazed up against Alvarez, although he lashed him without naming
him so that he might conceal his splendid bile. We have far more illustrious indications of Penottus’s
state of mind than Penottus had when, relying on them, he said that Alvarez wished to conceal his own
opinion.

PENOTTUS.

“Third, they argue: God infallibly knows future contingents, even free ones, in his absolute and
efficacious decree. Therefore he can also know them in the determination of secondary causes which will
not be impeded. The consequence is proved because in the very motion by which God determines contingent
secondary causes toward their effects there is an intention of God which cannot fail.

“I answer that many things about which theologians are in doubt are implicated in this argument. First,
the question is implicated whether God knows future contingents in his decree; second, whether
contingent causes are physically predetermined by the first cause. It is not pertinent to speak about
these things in this place.

“II. Second, I say that in the determination of free secondary causes God’s efficacious intention exists
not by way of an antecedent but by way of a concomitant. Hence it is denied that free effects, insofar
as they proceed freely from a free secondary cause, are known in the divine decree before their actual
presence. This is the express doctrine of Thomas, *On Truth*, q. 6, art. 3, reply 6, of which matter we
shall speak immediately.”

RESPONSE.

This too is Alvarez’s argument in the place cited. Although this doubt exists among theologians, your
answer is nevertheless irrelevant. For Alvarez does not rest upon it as something posited beyond
controversy but as something already confirmed by him with various arguments in the preceding
disputation. In this he does nothing alien to the common custom of all treatise writers. Again, the
resolution of the second doubt follows upon the resolution of the first. For unless contingent causes
are physically predetermined by the first cause, it cannot come about that God knows future
contingents in the decree of his will. Nevertheless, Alvarez has nothing here about the determination
of secondary causes to be made by the first cause. Nor does Penottus say that the consequence Alvarez
makes is doubtful among theologians.

II. God’s intention must be antecedent to every determination of secondary causes, because every
determination of secondary causes occurs in time, while every intention of God—since it is an immanent
act—existed from eternity. Unless, perhaps, you refer it to the determination of secondary causes by
itself as foreseen by God, as though God, foreseeing that secondary causes would determine themselves,
efficaciously intended that same determination in conformity with it. From this it follows that God’s
intention is neither antecedent nor concomitant, but rather subsequent. Again, this rests upon your
doctrine of middle knowledge, in which you suppose that some contingent thing is transmuted from the
state of a possible thing into the state of a future thing without any cause of that transmutation.

PENOTTUS.

“Fourth, they argue from certain authorities of Thomas, namely in *Contra Gentiles* 1.66, after the
sixth argument; Chapter 67, argument 3; and Chapter 68, argument 4. There he appears to say that God
knows things not only as they are in themselves but also as they are in their causes. And more clearly
in the first part, q. 14, art. 13; and still more clearly in the *Secunda Secundae*, q. 171, art. 6,
reply 2, where he says that GOD beholds future contingent effects in two ways: in one way, as they are
present in themselves in eternity; in another way, as they are in their determinate secondary causes.

“I answer: Unless the will to contradict disturbs his mind, nothing is more certain in Thomas’s doctrine
than that future contingents cannot be certainly known in their causes, however determinate. I think
this is sufficiently evident from the testimonies just adduced. And to the passages adduced to the
contrary, I say that if Thomas’s words are read, the opposite is manifestly gathered from them. For he
says that, since future contingents can be known in two ways, in themselves and in their causes, the
knowledge had through the causes alone is not certain or sufficient in itself. But I answer each
passage particularly.

“I. In *Contra Gentiles* 1.67 and 68 he speaks only of things future in a qualified respect. Hence that
passage is irrelevant.

“II. In the first part, q. 14, art. 13, both in the body and in replies 2 and 3, he so manifestly teaches
the opposite that it is astonishing that the arguer adduces that passage for his own opinion.

“III. Finally, in the *Secunda Secundae*, q. 171, art. 6, reply 2, not only does he have none of the
things which the arguer fabricates for his convenience, but he says the complete opposite. And, if I
may speak with everyone’s leave, intolerable is this license which the arguer has arrogated to himself,
not only in this argument but in many others, of altering the authorities of the Doctors by adding,
subtracting, and changing. For where, I ask, does Thomas have this clause: ‘in another way, as they are
in their determinate secondary causes’? Nor have I said this from spite, but for the sake of illustrating
the truth. For there are those who have acquired such authority in writing—whence it comes I do not
know—that they invent authorities for themselves at will, and so forth. Whether this befits even a
pagan Doctor, to say nothing of a Christian theologian, let others judge.”

[Book I, printed p. 122; BSB canvas 00148]

RESPONSE.

All these things are taken from Alvarez, against whom Penottus flies. “Nothing is more certain,” he says,
“in Thomas’s doctrine than that future contingents cannot be certainly known in their causes, however
determinate.” Yet in this very chapter, namely number 5, Penottus has professed that this disagreement of
opinions—whether cognition of future things can be had only by inspecting them in themselves, or also
in determinate causes—when well inspected, concerns words and the mode of speaking rather than the
reality itself. He added only that “the mode of speaking according to which they are said to be known in
themselves appears more proper and more in accord with Thomas’s mind.” Observe that he says “appears more
in accord with Thomas’s mind,” and he proposes this concerning the mode of speaking, which he
acknowledges to be different, but he admits no difference in reality. If this is true, it follows that
in reality Thomas by no means denies that GOD sees future contingents at least in determinate causes.
Yet here, in the same chapter, Penottus declares: “Nothing is more certain in Thomas’s doctrine than that
future contingents cannot be certainly known in their causes, however determinate.”

From these things let the reader see whether the will to contradict disturbs Penottus’s mind, if it
disturbs anyone’s mind at all. For he could equally have attacked Cajetan, Domingo Báñez, and Navarrete,
who nevertheless came forth from the school of Thomas as Scholastics of the first rank, to say nothing
of others listed by Penottus himself, including even Valentinianus the Jesuit, whom it would have been
fair for him to treat more gently. Cajetan, on the first part, q. 14, art. 13, says: “Future contingents
as future from indeterminate causes are not knowable, as the text says, either by God or by anyone,
because they are no more beings in act or in potency than their opposites. But from determinate causes
they are knowable according to the determination of the causes.” Cajetan has these words while
commenting upon Thomas himself.

Concerning Domingo Báñez, see what Navarrete reports in controversy 55: “Concerning this doubt,” he says,
“Master Báñez holds in this article that God knows future contingents through proper ideas, which are in
GOD of future contingents, and that the idea represents certainly and infallibly by way of exemplar and
efficient cause. Second, he says that God knows future contingents in determinate, complete, and
unimpeded causes. Third, he says that God knows future contingents in their particular causes insofar
as the particular causes themselves are subject to the determination and disposition of divine
knowledge and will, which is the first cause. Fourth, he says that although contingent causes, insofar
as they are subjected to the determination of the first cause, are determinate and complete for
operating in the composite sense, they nevertheless remain simply contingent, indeterminate, and
incomplete in the divided sense.” Navarrete adds from his own opinion that “this opinion of Domingo Báñez
is in every respect most true and conformable to the common doctrine of Thomas’s disciples.” But let us
inspect the passages themselves.

I. In the passages alleged from *Contra Gentiles* 1.67 and 68, Penottus says that Aquinas speaks only of
things future in a qualified respect. But he brings forward no indications from the author’s context by
which that interpretation is confirmed, so it is probable that Penottus rests content with these things
when he brings boldness and cheek to answering. Again, Suárez, in book 1 of *On the Absolute Knowledge
of Future Contingents*, chapter 1, says that Thomas speaks there of natural effects, the entire order of
natural causes which can concur to promote or impede the effect being comprehended, and teaches there
that from this comprehension of causes it is evidently known whether or not that effect is to be
impeded. But the words in Alvarez indicate that Aquinas deals not only with natural effects but also
with free effects, for Alvarez affirms that Thomas concludes: “That God can know the movements of our
will by knowing himself.” If Alvarez had corrupted Thomas’s words in this allegation, Penottus would by
no means have passed it over unchastised.

II. As for the second passage from the first part, q. 14, art. 13, Aquinas certainly teaches only that
future contingents are known by God in themselves, but not certainly and infallibly in their causes.
Yet it is nevertheless true that these causes are considered by Thomas as holding themselves toward
opposites—that is, as indeterminate toward one thing. Cajetan acknowledges that this is taught in the
text, although he adds that future things are also known by GOD in determinate causes. Perhaps he added
this from Thomas’s doctrine in *Contra Gentiles* 1.66–68, where Thomas appears to have this purpose: to
teach in how many ways future things are known by God. But in sum, in the passages cited here he shows
only how the contingency of future things can stand with God’s infallible cognition of them, which
existed from eternity.

But when Alvarez reports an opinion of this sort from Thomas—“God knows future contingents not only as
they are in themselves but also as they are in their causes”—a lapse of memory appears to have driven
him across the path. For an entirely inverted statement occurs in Thomas in these words: “But God knows
all contingents not only as they are in their causes but also as each one of them is in itself.” He uses
this gradation to establish the infallible condition of divine cognition, which in Thomas’s judgment
would not be sufficiently established if God were said to know future contingents only from their
causes. For he had already said that “whoever knows a contingent effect only in its cause has only
conjectural cognition of it.” Therefore the gloss Alvarez applies here, as though Thomas pronounced
these things concerning determinate causes, is far removed from the sense of the present passage. But I
believe Alvarez wrote these things not by inspecting the passage in Thomas but from confidence in his
memory, from which we are all, to a person, prone to error. Yet this deflection of Aquinas’s opinion in
Alvarez appears to have deceived Penottus; otherwise he would not have let Alvarez get away unpunished.

III. As for the final passage from the *Secunda Secundae*, q. 171, art. 6, Aquinas says that divine
foreknowledge looks to two things: namely, according as they are in themselves and according as they
are in their causes, insofar as he

[Book I, printed p. 123; BSB canvas 00149; upper section]

sees the order of causes toward effects. Here too Alvarez suffered something human. For the meaning of
the passage by no means tends to establish the infallibility of divine knowledge from causes insofar as
they are determinate. Indeed, the causes there are considered as indeterminate. For Thomas has it thus:
“Although future contingents, as they are in themselves, are determined toward one thing, nevertheless,
as they are in their causes, they are not so determined that they cannot turn out otherwise.” Alvarez
therefore dictated these things too without having Thomas’s text before his eyes, but trusting his
memory. Accordingly, just as with the preceding passage, so also he accommodated his own gloss to this
one, as though in his judgment Aquinas had spoken of determinate causes. For I conjecture that it
happened through a typographical error that those words, “determinate secondary causes,” were printed in
smaller type, as though they completed Thomas’s statement together with the preceding words. Yet I judge
that they were set apart in another typeface as the interpreter’s opinion.

The sum is this: I consider that the passages in Aquinas on which the Dominicans chiefly rest in this
opinion of theirs, as the most illustrious, are those taken from *Contra Gentiles* 1.66–68, and therefore
they are placed first by Alvarez. To conceal this and drive the suspicion from his reader, see, I ask,
the devices by which Penottus proceeds. After, following Alvarez, he had recalled the things Alvarez
took from *Contra Gentiles* in his writing, he proceeds to others in this manner: “And more clearly in
the first part, q. 14, art. 13. And still more clearly in the *Secunda Secundae*, q. 171, art. 6”—as
though, in Alvarez’s own judgment, those passages were weightier for proving what Alvarez intended than
those excerpted from *Contra Gentiles*. But these phrases—“and more clearly,” and so forth, “and still
more clearly,” and so forth—do not occur at all in Alvarez. He speaks more simply in this manner: “He
teaches the same in the *Secunda Secundae*, q. 171,” and so forth; and again, “Which he also teaches in
the first part, q. 14, art. 13.” Let the reader see which savor more: a mind fermented by spite, or one
zealous to illustrate the truth, as Penottus himself makes a show of doing.
