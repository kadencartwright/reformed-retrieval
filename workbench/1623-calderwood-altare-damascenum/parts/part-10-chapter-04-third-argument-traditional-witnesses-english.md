### 3. Third argument

The apostles themselves ordained bishops greater than presbyters in the
authority of ordination and jurisdiction, and entrusted churches to them.
Therefore the episcopal function is without controversy of apostolic
institution. We deny the antecedent, and affirm that the apostles appointed
no perpetual presidents—much less princely bishops superior to presbyters in
directive or corrective authority of

[1623 printed p. 138 | IA leaf 150 | PDF p. 150]

ordination and jurisdiction. They prove that bishops were ordained by the
apostles partly from tradition and partly from the Scriptures. From the
histories and monuments of the ancients they bring forward Evodius at
Antioch, and Linus at Rome, as bishops ordained by Peter and Paul; Mark at
Alexandria as, if not ordained, at least designated by Peter; Polycarp at
Smyrna as ordained by the apostle John; James the Just at Jerusalem as
appointed bishop by Peter, James, and John—or, as others say, by all the
apostles—before the dispersion and immediately after the Lord’s passion; and,
after James the Just was killed, Simeon son of Cleopas as elected to succeed
James in the episcopate by the apostles, disciples, and kinsmen of the Lord
who were still surviving and had assembled from every side. The most ancient
monuments of the ancients by which they prove these men’s episcopates are
those lacunae of Clement and of Hegesippus, a writer no better, and of Papias,
an author of tales in Eusebius’s own judgment, of whom we spoke above.

Ignatius, in the epistle to the Antiochenes, says that Evodius was the first
after the apostles to obtain the helm of that church. But Chrysostom, a
presbyter of that same Antiochene church, contradicts this pseudo-Ignatius and
openly declares that Ignatius was the first man appointed by Peter to that
see. Theodoret affirms the same, as does Pope Felix in the epistle to the
emperor Zeno. Others say that Ignatius was substituted for Evodius. Clement,
in the *Constitutions*, relates that they were created bishops at the same
time, Evodius by Peter and Ignatius by Paul. They further relate that Evodius
was appointed bishop of Antioch in the year of Christ 45. Yet after the year
45 no mention of him is made in Acts 13:1–3, where we read that Paul and
Barnabas were set apart for the work to which the Holy Spirit had called
them, even though the chief prophets and teachers are there recounted by
name. When Paul and Barnabas returned to Antioch, they gathered the church and
reported how much God had accomplished through them, Acts 14:37. They did not
report to Bishop Evodius. When certain men were sent to Jerusalem, Acts 15:2,
we do not read that Evodius or any other bishop was sent by the Antiochene
church. So too in Acts 15:22, 23, 30, 31, and 32 nothing is recorded about
Evodius or Ignatius, although the occasion presented itself.

At Rome, some relate that Linus, some that Linus together with Cletus, and
some that Clement first assumed the episcopate after Peter. Some relate that
Linus was appointed bishop before, others after, the martyrdom of Peter and
Paul. Bellarmine says that, when his passion was approaching, Peter left the
episcopal see to Clement; but that after Peter died, Clement refused to sit in
the apostolic see while Linus and Cletus lived, because they had been the
assistants of blessed Peter in the episcopal office. Therefore the first
pontiff after Peter was not Clement but Linus. *An example of obstinacy and
rebellion in Peter’s first successor. By what*

[Marginalia: Chrysostom, *On the Translation of St. Ignatius*; Theodoret, *On
the Immutable*, dialogue 1; *Controversies* 3, book 2, chapter 3, number 12.]

[1623 printed p. 139 | IA leaf 151 | PDF p. 151]

*right, with what effrontery, could this Clement refuse an office entrusted
to him by apostolic authority? Was he not demented if he considered himself
wiser than Peter himself?* asks Tilenus.

Jerome says to Evagrius that from Mark the Evangelist down to Heraclas and
Dionysius the presbyters selected one of their own, placed him in a higher
rank, and named him bishop. It is plain that the words *from Mark* must be
understood exclusively. For the presbyters did not choose or ordain Mark from
among themselves, whom they themselves say was sent or ordained by Peter.
Yet elsewhere the same Jerome writes that Mark was the first bishop of the
church of Alexandria. This is true, but improperly so, just as the ancients
said that Peter himself was the first bishop of the Roman church, though
Downham admits that he was an apostle, not a bishop. In the *Chronicle*,
Eusebius places first in the series of bishops of Alexandria not Mark, whom he
calls an evangelist and Paul’s interpreter, but Anianus: *First after Mark the
apostle and evangelist, Anianus, a devout man celebrated in every respect,
undertook the administration of the Alexandrian parish.* Eusebius calls Mark
an evangelist and apostle—that is, an evangelist properly so called. And the
histories which relate that he traveled through Egypt, Libya, Pentapolis, and
other regions testify that he performed the work of an evangelist. The things
related about Peter ordaining or designating a bishop of the Alexandrian
church are therefore tales. *Concerning the beginnings of the Alexandrian
church, nothing certain can be demonstrated from Scripture, or indeed even
from the fathers who flourished before the Council of Nicaea,* says Tilenus.

The origin of the error, it seems, came from Peter’s words misunderstood:
*The church which is in Babylon, elect together with you, greets you, and so
does Mark my son,* 1 Peter 5:13. It should be observed in passing that Jerome
says that from the time of Mark a presbyter chosen by the presbyters and
placed in a higher rank was named bishop. Bishops, therefore, were not called
apostles in the age of the apostles, as Theodoret dreamed. We spoke above
about Jerome’s testimony and his prochronism. I add that Clement of Rome says
in the *Constitutions*: *Anianus, bishop of Alexandria, whom Mark the
Evangelist first ordained, was succeeded by Abilius, ordained by Luke the
Evangelist.* He does not say that the presbyters placed in a higher rank the
man whom they named bishop. Clement continues: *Ariston was the first bishop
of Smyrna; after his death his successor was Strateas, son of Lois, whom a
third bishop, another Ariston, succeeded.* Suidas says that Bucolus was the
first and Polycarp the second bishop of the church of Smyrna.

Add what we said above about the interval between the writing of Revelation
and Polycarp’s martyrdom. The most ancient monuments of the ancients can hand
down nothing certain about the things done by the apostles, because they are
either fabulous, spurious, or interpolated. Baronius himself admits this in
the *Annals*: *Since both deeds and writings are found to have been falsely
ascribed to the apostles, and since, if anything*

[Marginalia: proem to Matthew; *History*, book 2, chapter 24; *On the
Pontiff*, book 2, chapter 24, note 8; *Constitutions*, book 7, chapter 48;
*Annals* 44, number 30.]

[1623 printed p. 140 | IA leaf 152 | PDF p. 152]

*about them was related by true and honest writers, it has by no means
remained whole and uncorrupted, they cast the mind into a kind of despair of
ever being able to attain what is true and certain.* Moreover, it is not
enough to prove from the testimonies of the ancients that Evodius, Polycarp,
and Linus were appointed bishops by the apostles, unless they also
demonstrate that these men were superior to the presbyters and their
colleagues by a peculiar authority of ordination and jurisdiction.

But let us proceed to James the Less. Jerome, they say, testifies in the
catalog of writers that James was ordained bishop by the apostles. *James was
ordained bishop of Jerusalem by the apostles immediately after the Lord’s
passion.* Clement of Alexandria likewise relates in the sixth book of the
*Hypotyposes* that after the assumption of our Savior, Peter, James, and John
chose James the Just as bishop of Jerusalem. Eusebius also relates from
Hegesippus that the episcopal see was conferred upon James, the Lord’s
brother, by the apostles.

I answer first that those witnesses are too late to deserve credit concerning
the apostles’ acts. That catalog of writers is not Jerome’s, in the judgment
of Bellarmine himself in *Ecclesiastical Writers*. *For it numbers Pope
Hilary, who succeeded St. Leo and began to flourish long after Jerome’s
death.* Junius conjectures that it should be read thus: *Whom John mentions in
his book was ordained bishop of Jerusalem by the apostles immediately after
the Lord’s passion.* He proves this to be no slight conjecture from Jerome’s
own words when he reports the words of Hegesippus: *After the apostles the
Lord’s brother, surnamed Just, undertook the church at Jerusalem.* The sense,
therefore, is that it was not immediately after the Lord’s passion, but after
the apostles—that is, after the apostles left Jerusalem to go into the world
to preach the gospel, as Junius interprets it in the same place. It is evident
from the Acts of the Apostles that the apostles remained at Jerusalem for
several years after Christ’s passion.

We do not labor greatly over the date, but over the matter itself. Eusebius
and Jerome cite Hegesippus, a writer no better than Clement of Rome, as
Scaliger says, as the first author of this history. Hegesippus’s whole
narrative is fabulous. For he says that James was holy from his mother’s
womb, drank neither wine nor strong drink, that no razor came upon his head,
and that he alone was permitted to enter the holy place, for he wore not a
woolen garment but linen; and he relates many other things which Joseph
Scaliger noted in his *Animadversions*. *When the fathers call James a bishop,
or even Peter,* says Whitaker, *they do not take the name bishop properly, but
call them bishops of those churches in which they remained for some time.* In
the same place: *And if he is speaking of a bishop properly,* he says, *it is
absurd that the apostles were bishops. For one who is properly a bishop
cannot be an apostle, because a bishop is bishop of only one church. But the
apostles were founders and overseers of many churches.* Likewise: *But they
are surely altogether mad. For it is not very*

[Marginalia: *History*, book 2, chapter 23; p. 166; *Controversies* 3, book 1,
chapter 15, number 18; 2077; Whitaker, *On the Pontiff*, question 3, chapter
15, section 2.]

[1623 printed p. 141 | IA leaf 153 | PDF p. 153]

*far from madness to say that Peter, or the other apostles, was properly a
bishop. For they possessed the highest authority of ministry; the office of
a bishop is nothing in comparison with the apostolic office.* Likewise: *The
apostles were greater than all bishops. Both offices cannot fall to one and
the same person. For a bishop has only one flock, which he feeds as his own.
But the apostles had no fixed province and no fixed churches to which they
were bound. For Christ did not entrust any particular church to any apostle,
as he certainly would have done if they had been bishops, but said to all of
them, Go into all the world; and they, mindful of their commission, did so. It
could indeed happen that they made a longer stay in one place than in another,
but not as bishops, because then they would have ceased to be apostles such as
Christ had made them. They knew that they must always follow the Holy Spirit’s
leading and proceed at once wherever he himself called them, which they could
not have done if their seats had been fixed in certain places.* *To make Peter
bishop of one place or city,* says Tilenus, *is to detract from his apostolic
dignity.* What is affirmed about Peter can be affirmed about the other
apostles.

Junius resolves the equivocation in the word *bishop* as follows: *A bishop is
spoken of absolutely or relatively, or—to speak better—commonly and
singularly. Absolutely or commonly we call a man a bishop who is furnished
with God’s gift and calling and possesses `τὸ ἀξίωμα τῆς ἱερωσύνης`, “the
dignity of the priesthood,” without consideration of a particular place; but
relatively or singularly we call a man bishop who is assigned to a definite
place and church. Now all the apostles were bishops commonly from the time
they were called. Afterward, however, for the sake of order and the churches’
convenience, certain tracts, as though lots, were distributed among them by
region through a certain agreement.*

Downham says that James differed from Peter and the other apostles in this:
Peter and the others traveled through various regions; and although, when
advanced in age, they began to remain in some one city in which they devoted
their labor, and were therefore held by the ancients to be bishops of that
place, perhaps some of them were not properly bishops. Downham thinks that
they instead exercised patriarchal authority in the places where they founded
and established churches, as John at Ephesus and others elsewhere. But he
says that from the beginning, before the dispersion, the church at Jerusalem
was assigned to James, and that James did not go from one region to another.
Yet a stay of either three or thirty years in one city does not diminish the
apostolate. He would always have remained an apostle even if he had remained
at Jerusalem for a hundred years. *The apostles could do—indeed, as apostles
they did do,* says Whitaker, *whatever bishops can or ought to do, and
something more besides.* Moreover, it is not staying but the obligation to
stay perpetually in one place that makes a bishop. But the apostles had to
follow the Holy Spirit’s leading and could not be bound to remain always in
one place. *For no apostle was enrolled in one city as though attached to its
soil, which belongs to an ordinary bishop, not an apostle,* says Tilenus.

[Marginalia: question 3, chapter 3, section 9; *On the Pontiff*, book 2,
chapter 4, note 6; *Controversies* 3, book 1, chapter 23, number 3; *Defense*,
book 4, chapter 3, section 7; *On the Pontiff* 1, question 6, chapter
15, section 2; *On the Pontiff*, book 1, chapter 25, note 7.]

[1623 printed p. 142 | IA leaf 154 | PDF p. 154]

Finally, James did not undertake the care of only the church at Jerusalem,
but of all the churches in Judea. For there were several churches of Judea,
as is clear from Galatians 1:22: *I was unknown by face to the churches of
Judea which were in Christ*; and 1 Thessalonians 2:14: *For you, brothers,
became imitators of the churches of God which are in Judea.* Downham himself
says that Judea was assigned to James. Therefore James was not bishop of one
church but of several—that is, he discharged his apostolate in several
churches of Judea. A bishop properly so called is bishop of one church. It is
unheard of in the church for a man to be called bishop of several churches.
Bishop and flock, not bishop and flocks, are correlatives.

James is called an apostle in Galatians 2:19: *But I saw no other of the
apostles except James, the Lord’s brother.* And in chapter 2:9, James, Cephas,
and John gave the right hands of fellowship to Paul and Barnabas, so that the
latter should discharge the apostolate among the Gentiles, and the former
among the uncircumcised. Therefore, as Peter and John were, so was James the
Lord’s brother: an apostle of the circumcision, not bishop of the one church
at Jerusalem.

The Jews were distinguished thus: the Hebrews of Palestine, the dispersion
of Babylon, and the dispersion of the Hellenists. The chief men of the
Hellenists were the Alexandrian Jews. They were called Hellenists because
they read the Greek Bible in the synagogues. The Babylonians were the head of
the Asian dispersion; Jerusalem, of the Palestinian Hebrews. See Scaliger in
the third book of the *Isagogical Canons*, and what we cited above from his
*Notes on the New Testament*. It therefore appears that Peter, as apostle of
the circumcision, chiefly cared for the churches of the Asian dispersion
after the dispersion of the apostles, while James cared for the churches of
the Palestinian Hebrews. Upon this distinction of the Jews, taken from
Scaliger, Tilenus constructs the same division of provinces and regions:
*When the other apostles departed into the provinces, it was equitable for
one of that company to remain at Jerusalem to watch over the Jews’ salvation.
Thus James adorned his allotted field at Jerusalem among the Jews
`Ἑβραιοζήλας`, “zealous for the Hebrews”; Mark his at Alexandria among the
Hellenists; and Peter his at Babylon in the eastern `διασπορᾷ`, “dispersion.”
But just as their episcopate came from an immediate calling and shone with
the brilliance of extraordinary gifts, so there is no reason to think that
their `πρωτεῖα`, “preeminences,” belong to subsequent bishops called by men.*

If the apostles ordained James bishop of the church at Jerusalem, did they
confer on him a new authority which he did not possess before as an apostle?
Downham answers that he previously possessed the authority of order, as
bishops without a title possess it, but that he received the authority of
jurisdiction when he was designated bishop of Jerusalem and the church of
Judea was particularly assigned to him. He should say “the churches of Judea
were assigned.” Therefore, in Downham’s opinion, the other apostles

[Marginalia: *Defense*, book 4, p. 56; *On the Pontiff*, book 2, chapter 9,
note 25.]

[1623 printed p. 143 | IA leaf 155 | PDF p. 155]

possessed only the authority of order, not of jurisdiction. For they were not
ordained bishops of particular churches, as he himself
admits. But the authority of jurisdiction was conferred on all the apostles
in that general commission: *As the Father sent me, so I send you*, Matthew
28:18; and Matthew 18:18, *Whatever you bind on earth*, and so forth; and John
20:23, after he breathed on them he said, *Receive the Holy Spirit. If you
remit the sins of any, they are remitted to them*, and so forth. In these
passages we see that James and all the apostles received the authority of
jurisdiction and order. Paul was nowhere appointed bishop of any particular
church, yet everywhere he possessed the authority of jurisdiction. If the
other apostles did not possess the authority of jurisdiction, how can bishops
succeed the apostles in the church’s external government, since the part of
external government which the bishops claim for themselves as the principal
and most particularly their own is the authority of jurisdiction?

The glossator on Distinction 66, chapter *Porro*, reasons about James’s
consecration as follows: *Were not all the apostles bishops? To this some say
that all the apostles were simple priests and yet could consecrate. For Moses
was a simple priest and yet consecrated Aaron, as in Distinction 21; nor was
there then a difference between a bishop and a priest, as in Distinction 95,
chapter “Olim.” Or say that these three consecrated him only with a visible
anointing, but he had previously been invisibly anointed by the Lord. Or say
that they did not ordain him, but only showed others the form of ordaining. Or
say that they ordained him not bishop but archbishop. Or say that they
ordained—that is, enthroned—him for the administration of a definite place,
for he had previously been a bishop without a title.* You see into how many
and how narrow straits those who wish James to have been a bishop properly so
called are forced, and whence Downham took that absurd distinction, namely,
that the apostle was previously a bishop without a title. Even if it were
granted that hands were laid on James, it would nevertheless not follow that
he was a bishop, any more than it follows that Paul and Barnabas were ordained
bishops in Acts 13, as Bellarmine wishes.

From Junius’s distinction just stated, Tilenus answers Bellarmine:
*Ordination to the apostolic episcopate is considered in two ways, absolutely
or relatively. In the former sense all the apostles were ordained bishops
immediately by God to traverse the whole world and preach to every nation. In
the latter sense it is regarded with `σχέσει`, “relation,” to the provinces
which the apostles allotted and divided among themselves for the convenience
of their function. In this way Paul, having long discharged that apostolic
episcopate of his considered absolutely, received definite limits assigned to
him within which he should labor. This assignment is improperly called an
ordination. For he was thus ordained not to an office, but to the place in
which he would discharge the office.* James, therefore, was not properly
ordained a bishop. Rather, his own province was assigned to him, as to the
other apostles, in which he would discharge the apostolate. For they themselves

[Marginalia: *On the Pontiff*, book 1, chapter 23.]

[1623 printed p. 144 | IA leaf 156 | PDF p. 156 | ends at the traditional-witness transition]

do not dare assert that he undertook the care of the one church at Jerusalem.
Downham admits it was the care of all Judea, therefore of the churches of
Judea. And since Jerusalem was the metropolis of all the Palestinian Hebrews,
it is consistent that he undertook the care of all the churches of Palestine.
For he was an apostle of the circumcision, like Peter. If it is true that he
remained at Jerusalem, he did not remain on account of a particular church,
but on account of all the Jews who came together at Jerusalem from every
side, while the Mosaic polity and the Levitical worship of the temple
continued. *You see, brother, how many thousands of Jews there are who have
believed*, Acts 21:36. But what is said about an assignment must be received
cautiously, lest we remove the leading of the Holy Spirit which the apostles
had to follow. *No apostle, much less Peter, ever bound himself fixedly to any
particular place, nor ought he to have been bound there until death,* says
Spalatensis. His death at Rome, he says, was a mere accident; and if he had
not been crowned with martyrdom there or hindered by chains and prison, he
would have gone elsewhere or could have done so. His death contributes
nothing to that see, he says, as Bellarmine admits, such that it should be
called Peter’s proper see.

What is said about enthronement is fabulous. The vainglorious terms
“episcopal throne” and “archiepiscopal throne” had not yet arisen and were not
known to the churches. Therefore, when Eusebius names James a bishop and
assigns him a throne, he speaks according to the custom of his own age. The
apostles were not anxious about thrones and enthronements, as the glossator
pretends.

Concerning Simeon son of Cleopas, Eusebius relates that after Jerusalem was
devastated, the apostles and disciples of the Lord who still survived
assembled into one from every side together with the Lord’s kinsmen according
to the flesh, and judged with one and the same opinion who was worthy to
succeed James in the see of the Jerusalem parish. “It is reported,” says
Eusebius. Ought he to have compiled tables of episcopal successions from
reports? Since in the sacred books we do not read even a `γρῦ`, “the slightest
sound,” about his episcopate, let us dismiss the reports, lest we badly spend
good hours refuting trifles of this sort. Whether he was an evangelist or not
does not concern me, since there are so many things that move me to doubt the
matter itself. If James, ordained bishop immediately after the Lord’s passion,
presided for thirty years, and Simeon did not succeed before the destruction
of Jerusalem, the see stood vacant for ten years. Was it so important to
elect a bishop of Jerusalem that the apostles too had to assemble at
Jerusalem from the remotest regions on every side? While apostles were still
living, would they substitute someone other than an apostle in an apostle’s
place? They showed favor to flesh and blood, it seems, in bestowing
episcopates, and obliquely censured the Lord’s counsel, because he had not
chosen Simeon as an apostle.

So much for the bishops whom they prove from the monuments of the ancients to
have been appointed by the apostles. Let us proceed to Timothy and Titus,
whom they contend from sacred Scripture were ordained bishops by the
apostles.
