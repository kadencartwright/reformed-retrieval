VERSE 30. Who is equal to that wise person? Or who is equal to the person who
knows the interpretation of a matter? A person’s wisdom so illuminates his
face that the firmness of his countenance is doubled.

CHAPTER VIII

VERSE 1. Observe my determination—the king’s determination—but in accordance
with the obligation of the oath of God.

VERSE 2. Do not depart from his presence in alarm; do not persist in an evil
matter, for he will do whatever he wills.

VERSE 3. Wherever the king’s word is, there is dominion; who can say to him,
“What are you doing?”

VERSE 4. The person who observes the command will not experience an evil
matter; for the mind of the wise person knows time and judgment.

VERSE 5. For every purpose has a time and judgment, but a person’s evil weighs
heavily upon him.

VERSE 6. For he does not know what will be; who can tell him how it will be?

VERSE 7. No person rules over his spirit so as to restrain his spirit; there
is no dominion in the day of death and no weapon in that battle; finally, the
conflict does not free the person who employs it.

Praising wisdom in general, and bringing forward one kind of that wisdom,
placed in the due and lawful duty owed to the magistrate, Solomon illustrates
it by its contrary: disobedience and the rash refusal of obedience.

There are two parts: the praise of wisdom in general; and one kind of wisdom,
illustrated by its contrary, in lawful obedience to the Magistrate.

Of the two things Solomon set out to investigate in chapter 7, verse 29, he
now comes to the first, wisdom, having completed his treatment of the
investigation of folly or madness. He proves the boundlessness of wisdom’s
praise from the wonder attached to it, as though it surpassed a person’s
speech and adornment. He confirms this from the person who possesses it: the
wise person in whom wisdom resides, whose equal no one can find—not a rich
person, not one eminent in power, not the person most completely instructed in
every kind of knowledge, not one attended by friends, and finally not even a
person equally heaped with all these things.

For it may truly be said of this wisdom what Solomon’s mother declares in
Proverbs 31:29 about the chaste and thrifty matron: “Many women have acted
valiantly, but you surpass them all.” Like Esther among all the virgins, a
shining crown must rightly be set upon wisdom’s head. Since this is confirmed
by the testimony of Solomon, the wisest man of all, the folly of those who
cast wisdom behind their backs must be astonishing. They regard her as some
abject and ignoble maidservant who should serve and wait upon the acquisition
of riches, power, and

honors—or rather, they misuse her mask and shadow to acquire these things,
since they cannot misuse wisdom herself. Indeed, what madness it is to
exchange her for the smallest pleasure, and one often shameful and unlawful.

There are two kinds of these wise people. One consists of those endowed with
this wisdom; the other, of those who, besides being imbued with it, know how to
teach it to others and interpret it for them. The latter surpasses the former.
For just as the Apostle distinguishes between the gift of a tongue and the
interpretation of a tongue, 1 Corinthians 12:10, so there is some difference
between the person endowed with wisdom and the person who also knows how to
teach it to others. On this chiefly depends the dignity and excellence of the
Ministry of the Word.

Next he commends this wisdom from two effects. The first is that it makes a
person’s face shine—that is, makes him honored and venerable—even when the
form and structure of his body are not very impressive. See an excellent
example of this in Job 29:1, 8–10. Above all, an exceptionally clear example
of this teaching appears in Stephen, Acts 6:15. Although something in this
radiance approaches the nature of a miracle, God nevertheless sealed this
teaching by setting it upon him as a seal.

The other effect of this heavenly wisdom is that the strength of the face is
doubled by it; that is, it causes a person to inspire awe as though he excelled
in power and strength. This is what is said in chapter 7, verse 19: this wisdom
brings more strength than ten rulers in one and the same city. Thus we read
that Herod and Saul, savage tyrants, feared John the Baptist and David, Mark 6
and 1 Samuel 18:11.

Everyone seeks honors and power, yet they do not enter the road that is
shortest and most direct to honors and power. Hence they either do not attain
what they desire and love, or, if they do attain it, they obtain the smoke of
titles and honors rather than true honor. Even after gaining control of
affairs, without this wisdom they become fearsome as tyrants rather than
venerable as fathers of their country.

Women diligently pursue beauty and attractiveness so that they may please
suitors or husbands. For this reason they carefully seek paints and cosmetics
with which to color and adorn themselves, and beg from elsewhere for the
beauty they do not possess as their own. Hence they carry one face by night
and another by day. Yet by wisdom and holiness a beauty is acquired that old
age does not deform but makes more beautiful. Men desire to make themselves
inspiring of awe to others, and therefore, when they set out for combat and
war, they blacken themselves with soot so that they may appear terrifying to
the enemy.

But through this wisdom, which is true piety, only five men will turn a
hundred to flight and a hundred will put a thousand to flight, Leviticus 26.
Indeed, one will put a thousand to flight, Deuteronomy 32:30. Thus this wisdom
makes the misshapen beautiful and the unattractive attractive; while for those
who already excel in beauty and attractiveness, it will double their grace
and attractiveness. So much for the first part.
