QUESTION XIII.

THE MATTER OF SATISFACTION

*Must Christ’s satisfaction be restricted to the sufferings and punishments he
bore for us? Or must it also extend to the active obedience by which he
perfectly fulfilled the law throughout his life? The former denied; the latter
affirmed.*

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. Theologians differ concerning the *matter and parts of satisfaction*. Some
restrict it to the sufferings or punishments Christ bore for us. Cargius, a
Lutheran minister, seems first to have asserted this, followed by Piscator, the
Reformed professor at Herborn, and their followers. Some place all righteousness
in Christ’s death; others add all his lifelong sufferings and call this
*passive righteousness*. They regard active righteousness—obedience to the
commandments—as a required qualification of the mediator for executing his
office, but not as part of the satisfaction or merit imputed to us.

II. The common doctrine received in our churches is that Christ’s satisfaction,
imputed to us as righteousness before God, embraces not only the sufferings he
bore in life and death, but also his lifelong obedience: the righteous and holy
actions by which he perfectly fulfilled the law’s commands in our place. From
these two parts arises the complete and perfect price of our redemption.

III. The question is not whether Christ perfectly fulfilled both the general
law of worshiping God and the special command to expose himself to death, nor
whether his lifelong obedience was necessary for executing salvation and
useful to us. Our opponents admit both. Rather, was it also furnished in our
place and does it enter the satisfaction he owed God for us? They deny; we
affirm.

[Printed p. 392 | Internet Archive PDF 420 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

IV. Nor is the question whether bare sufferings belong to satisfaction. Our
opponents acknowledge that no suffering can satisfy without action: to please
God it had to include active obedience, a voluntary offering embracing supreme
love and perfect righteousness and holiness; indeed, they say this one action
contained the whole law κατ᾽ ἐπιτομὴν. Rather, must his obedience in death,
bearing our punishments, be joined with his lifelong obedience by which he
fulfilled all righteousness and perfectly conformed to the law? We affirm;
they deny.

V. Nor is the question whether Christ’s original and habitual holiness was a
qualification of the mediator as man, Israelite, priest, and spotless victim.
Rather, does the righteousness of merit, belonging to the execution of his
office and imputed to us for remission and the right to life, arise only from
his punishments, or also from the holy acts by which he fully kept the law
throughout life? We distinguish what directly constitutes merit and
satisfaction—*that which merits*—from antecedent qualifications that belong to
it mediately—*that by which he merits*. We place personal holiness in the
latter class.

VI. Thus the question is whether Christ’s satisfaction in our place is limited
to his death and the sufferings preceding or accompanying it, or embraces all
he did and suffered for us from life’s beginning to its end. Learned men affirm
the former; we assert the latter.

VII. For clarity we suppose first that Christ’s satisfactory sufferings extend
to all imposed upon him, not only on the cross but in the garden and throughout
life. We cannot approve restricting them to the three hours of darkness before
his death, excluding the rest from satisfying justice. Though those last
sufferings, with which he struggled in the final νυχθημέρῳ, were unquestionably most grievous, the rest also belonged to
satisfaction. 1. Scripture nowhere restricts his sufferings to those hours but
speaks generally (Isa. 53:4–5; 1 Pet. 2:21; 3:18; Matt. 16:21; Heb. 5:7;
10:8–9), extending them to Christ’s whole κένωσιν and humiliation (Phil.
2:6–7). 2. His agony in the garden, described by ταραχὴν, ἀδημονίαν,
ἐκθάμβησιν, and περίλυπον μέχρι θανάτου under the weight of divine wrath and
curse, was a principal part of the spiritual punishments borne for us. 3. The
restriction deprives pious souls resting in all the Savior’s sufferings of
their consolation and hides countless proofs of his immense love.

VIII. Zechariah 3:9, that the whole land’s sin would be erased in one day, and
Paul’s reference of Christ’s propitiatory sacrifice to his “one offering on the
cross” (Heb. 10:10) do not prove his preceding sufferings were not
satisfactory, but that satisfaction was then

[Printed p. 393 | Internet Archive PDF 421 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

completed, with all the elect’s sins erased that day. Paul refers expiation
synecdochically to the one offering because it was the last and gravest
suffering, without which the preceding ones could not suffice, as payment of
the last farthing completes satisfaction and is immediately followed by
cancelling the bond and giving a receipt. Though publicly inaugurated into his
office only at thirty, Christ was priest, victim, and mediator before then. His
being in the Father’s favor, beloved and sometimes glorified, does not prove he
did not bear wrath, since these are not ἀσύστατα. Even in the cross’s anguish
he remained personally dearest to the Father while, as our surety, bearing
wrath and becoming a curse. Nor did his punishments always need equal intensity;
intervals of mitigation strengthened him for the appointed contest.

IX. Second, Christ’s actions and sufferings have a material aspect, considered
as entities, and a formal aspect, considered morally with respect to the right
and δικαίωμα arising from them before God. Materially they are many; formally
they have one form, δικαιώματος, a whole arising from all the actions and
sufferings together. No single act or suffering is fully meritorious or
satisfactory without perfect obedience. Thus the obedience begun at birth,
continued through life, and completed in death is one with respect to the
ἀποτέλεσμα of salvation and the δικαίωμα flowing from it.

X. Third, Christ’s obedience has *satisfactory and meritorious* power: the
former delivers from punishments incurred by sin; the latter acquires the right
to eternal life lost through sin. As sin brought loss of life and liability to
death, redemption brings deliverance from death and a right to life, exit from
hell and entrance into heaven. Scripture expresses both: propitiation of
iniquity and introduction of everlasting righteousness (Dan. 9:24); release
from the law’s curse and bestowal of blessing (Gal. 3:13); release from the law
and adoption (Gal. 4:5); salvation from wrath and gift of life (Rom. 5:10);
remission and an inheritance among the sanctified (Acts 26:18).

XI. Though these benefits are inseparably joined in the covenant of grace, they
must not be confounded. Delivering from death differs from introducing into
life; freeing from punishment from bestowing reward. Nor does the necessary
connection of deliverance and life make release from deserved death identical
with acquisition of glory.

[Printed p. 394 | Internet Archive PDF 422 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

There are degrees of life and righteousness. Release from death brings life,
but not immediately blessed glory, just as release from prison brings liberty,
not immediately crown and throne; Joseph could be freed from chains without
immediately ruling Egypt. Between death and life simply there is no middle,
but between eternal death and glorious life there is the servant’s life in the
state of the way, where duty remains.

XII. Although distinguished, we need not anxiously assign satisfaction only to
sufferings and merit only to actions. Scripture presents Christ’s obedience as
one, furnishing all the law required. His active obedience paid what we owed;
his passive obedience, proceeding from supreme love—the law’s fulfillment—also
merited, having the character of λύτρου and τιμῆς, the price acquiring life.
Both benefits depend jointly on his whole obedience: sin could not be expiated
before the law was fulfilled, nor life acquired before guilt was erased. He
merited by satisfying and satisfied by meriting, performing what was hardest,
impossible to all others, and not owed by him, in perfect obedience and love.
Satisfaction belongs chiefly to his steadfast inward will, by which we are
sanctified (Heb. 10:14), not merely to payment of λύτρου but to the whole λύτρῳ,
Christ giving and subjecting himself for us.

XIII. Socinus says satisfaction and merit are ἀσύστατα because satisfaction
pays a debt while merit is an unowed work. This applies to proper satisfaction,
not vicarious satisfaction. A surety can merit toward the debtor by freely
paying his debt and acquiring immunity, and toward the creditor especially
where a covenant promises that such payment will both satisfy sins and acquire
an unowed right (Isa. 53:10; Heb. 9:15; Col. 1:19–20).

XIV. Fourth, the law has *precepts* prescribing duty and *sanctions* assigning
reward or penalty. Fallen man can owe both obedience and punishment (Locus XI,
Q. 22), because he has a twofold σχέσιν toward God, both as creature and as guilty creature.
As the former he always owes obedience; as the latter punishment. These are
not one debt paid twice, but a penal debt from past transgression and an
obediential debt from the creature’s perpetual obligation.

[Printed p. 395 | Internet Archive PDF 423 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

XV. Fifth, subjection to law is threefold. *Natural* subjection arises from the
law as the norm of holiness and is eternal and indispensable for every
creature. *Covenantal* subjection arises from the law as the condition for
acquiring blessedness during the state of the way. *Penal* subjection belongs
to the guilty sinner. The first is *absolute and immutable*; the second
*economic and mutable*, ending at the goal; the third *necessary and
inevitable* given sin. The first rests on God’s *essential and
αὐτοκρατορικῷ right* and natural creaturely dependence; the second on his
*free right* to covenant; the third on *judicial right* and *avenging justice*.
All rational creatures have the first; innocent Adam had the second; devils
and reprobates have the third.

XVI. This threefold σχέσιν explains how Christ was made ὑπὸ νόμον. As man he
was naturally subject to the law as holiness’s norm, like angels and saints.
But he was not therefore economically subject to it to merit life for himself,
which he already possessed by the hypostatic union, still less penally, being
sinless. If he assumed both covenantal and penal burdens—fulfilling precepts
and bearing sanctions—he did so voluntarily for us by his mediatorial office
and covenant with the Father, acting and suffering as our surety what we owed.

[Marginal heading: Satisfaction is proved to consist in lifelong obedience: 1. From Romans 5:19.]

XVII. Romans 5:19 refers satisfaction to Christ’s obedience, not merely his
death: “As through one man’s disobedience many were constituted sinners, so
through one man’s obedience many are constituted righteous.” 1. It speaks
without limitation of his whole, lifelong obedience. 2. Obedience means full
conformity to law, not only its penal sanction

[Printed p. 396 | Internet Archive PDF 424 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

but chiefly its commands. 3. It is the “gift of righteousness” (v. 17), which
cannot mean suffering. 4. It answers Adam’s disobedience: as that violated the
whole law, this fulfills it. 5. It is what we owed in both penalty and duty.
Nor is it merely ἓν δικαίωμα producing εἰς δικαίωσιν ζωῆς (v. 18): δικαίωμα
denotes not one righteous act alone but the right arising from complete
obedience. Evil arises from any defect; good requires a perfect cause.

[Marginal heading: 2. From Philippians 2:8.]

XVIII. Christ’s obedience was ἕως θανάτου, “unto death” (Phil. 2:8), denoting
not only its supreme intensity but its extent and duration from life’s
beginning. It belongs to his whole κένωσιν and humiliation and is elsewhere
described as the law impressed on his heart (Ps. 40; Heb. 10:5), a race run
(Heb. 12:1–2), and a work completed (John 17:4), all requiring lifelong
constancy.

[Marginal heading: 3. From Romans 8:3–4.]

XIX. Christ came in sinful flesh’s likeness to supply the law’s ἀδυναμίαν and
fulfill its right in us (Rom. 8:3–4). The weakness was objectively in sinners,
not subjectively in the law, and concerned command-keeping, not punishment.
Thus Christ fulfilled the δικαίωμα, the right to life arising from fulfillment,
actively as well as passively. Punishment does not fulfill commands but
satisfies threats; an executed thief has not obeyed the king’s command, nor is
one praiseworthy for being tormented in hell.

[Marginal heading: 4. Because Christ fulfilled what we owed.]

XX. Satisfaction extends to everything we owed for obtaining life: obedience
of life as well as suffering of death. Both were owed and necessary for
remission and the right to life, for the law promises life to the doer: “do
this and live.” Gospel and legal justification differ not in the righteousness
required—perfect righteousness answering God’s immutable right—but in mode:
what the law demanded through our own obedience is supplied through Christ’s.

[Printed p. 397 | Internet Archive PDF 425 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

[Marginal heading: 5. From Romans 1:17; 3:21; and 5:18.]

XXI. Christ’s satisfaction is sought in his *righteousness* (Rom. 1:17; 3:21;
5:18; Phil. 3:9; Dan. 9:24), and justification is described by imputation of
righteousness (Rom. 4). Righteousness consists in action, not suffering. Christ
fulfilled “all righteousness” by doing his Father’s will (Matt. 3:15). As our
law-breaking sins were imputed to Christ, his law-fulfilling righteous acts are
imputed to us (2 Cor. 5:21).

[Marginal heading: 6. Because Christ’s righteousness is not divided.]

XXII. As Christ is not μεμέρισται (1 Cor. 1:13), his righteousness must not be
divided, but applied ὁλόκληρος and undivided. The whole Passover lamb was eaten;
the whole Christ must be received with all he did and suffered. Subtracting
part of his perfect obedience from salvation’s price diminishes his glory and
our consolation and tears his seamless robe.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XXIII. Scripture’s attribution of salvation to Christ’s blood and death does
not exclude lifelong obedience, but is synecdoche: the last and chief stage of
humiliation, crowning obedience, represents the whole. The other parts would
have been useless without it, as righteousness must endure to the last breath
and payment is incomplete until the bond is cancelled.

XXIV. Paul’s attribution of blessed remission to shed blood (Rom. 4:6–7) does
not place all righteousness in suffering. He argues not from equivalence of
imputation and remission, but from their ἀλληλουχίᾳ, inseparable covenantal
connection: whoever receives remission receives the right to life. Similarly,
Paul identifies love of neighbor with fulfilling the law because the other
parts necessarily follow (Gal. 5:14).

XXV. Though Christ’s obedience in life and death was each perfect in its kind,
neither alone could satisfy; both command-keeping and punishment-bearing were
required to acquire deliverance from death and the right to life. Positing one
does not exclude but includes the other.

XXVI. A private person cannot pay for another what he owes for himself, but a
public person can act in his own and his people’s name. One who voluntarily
makes himself debtor to free another satisfies for him when he pays

[Printed p. 398 | Internet Archive PDF 426 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

what he owes. Christ became man and fulfilled the law not for himself but for
us. Though naturally under law as creature, he was not covenantally under it
to obtain life or stand as sinners’ surety; this was voluntary. He owed nothing
such personally as θεάνθρωπος, God’s Son and law’s Lord, nor in his human
nature joined to the person of Λόγου. His assumption and legal subjection were
humiliation. Nor as man was he represented by Adam’s legal covenant, since he
did not descend naturally from Adam. He needed no obedience to acquire life,
which necessarily followed union with Λόγῳ. Thus he owed covenantal obedience
for us as our representative surety.

XXVII. Christ’s obedience for us does not free us from obedience to God. We no
longer obey covenantally to obtain life, but naturally because we live—not to
acquire a right to life, but to enter possession of the acquired right. Though
Christ died for us, we still die, not for punishment but salvation.

XXVIII. *Righteousness of innocence* means freedom from guilt;
*righteousness of perseverance* earns reward for completed duty. Remission
gives the first by removing guilt, not the second as though all duty had been
performed. It makes sin not imputed, removing guilt and punishment, not making
the sin nonexistent. To be regarded as having done no evil and omitted no good
can mean, as to *punishment*, receiving none as though innocent; remission does
this. As to *reward*, it means being judged to have done everything required
for life; remission does not do this, for pardon does not make a confessed
criminal one who fulfilled every duty.

XXIX. God does not exact two payments. Since sinners owe obedience and penalty,
Christ’s actions and sufferings form not two but one payment

[Printed p. 399 | Internet Archive PDF 427 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and one δικαίωμα acquiring deliverance and life.

XXX. The law is not fulfilled κατ᾽ ἐπιτομὴν by Christ’s voluntary passion. It
requires obedience not only κατ᾽ ἐπιτομὴν but καθ᾽ ἕκαστα, intensive in degree
and extensive in duration from life’s beginning to end, which cannot occur in
one or two acts.

XXXI. Christ’s imputed righteousness cannot be located solely in sufferings
and called active on that account. Properly righteousness lies in action, not
passion; no one is righteous merely because tortured. Sufferings do not obey
commands promising life but satisfy sanctions. Any righteousness in punishment
belongs to the punisher, not the punished.

XXXII. Calvin establishes the received view in *Institutes* 2.16.5: Christ
removed alienation and acquired righteousness “by the whole course of his
obedience,” as Romans 5 and Galatians 4 extend redemption to his whole life.
From assuming the servant’s person he began paying liberation’s price; Scripture
attributes it especially to death without excluding lifelong obedience, for
even sacrifice profits nothing unless voluntarily offered. In 3.14.12 Calvin
says the Father clothes us with Christ’s innocence; his one perfect
righteousness alone endures God’s sight and is represented for us as surety.
Clothed with it, we receive continual forgiveness, our stains covered by its
purity. He confirms this on Romans 5:19 and Galatians 4:4.

XXXIII. The French synods repeatedly decreed this doctrine inviolate, including
Privas (1612) and Tonneins (1614): man, finding no righteousness in himself
before or after calling, is justified only in Christ, who obeyed the Father
from entering the world to the shameful cross, perfectly fulfilling in life
and death the whole law and the command

[Printed p. 400 | Internet Archive PDF 428 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

to suffer and give his life as ransom for many. By that perfect obedience we
are made righteous when God graciously imputes it and gives faith to receive
it, assuring us that by its merit we have remission and are made worthy of
eternal life.
