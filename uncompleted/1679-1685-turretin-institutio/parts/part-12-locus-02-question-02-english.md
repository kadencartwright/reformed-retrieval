QUESTION II

ON THE NECESSITY OF SCRIPTURE

Was It Necessary for the Word of God to Be Committed to Writing?—Affirmative.

I. Just as we proved the necessity of the Word in the preceding question, so
this question concerns the necessity of Scripture, or of the written Word,
against the Roman Catholics. For just as they strive with great zeal to lower
the authority of Scripture so that they may more easily establish their
“unwritten” traditions (ἀγράφους) and their pontiff's supreme tribunal, so for
the same reason they are accustomed to diminish its necessity in more than
one way. They seek to prove that Scripture was indeed useful to the church
but not necessary, as Bellarmine argues in *On the Word of God* 4.4. Indeed,
Cardinal Hosius burst forth into such blasphemy that he said, “The church
would have been better provided for if no Scripture had ever existed”; and
Valencia, “It would have been more advantageous if it had not been written.”

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. To establish the state of the question, observe that Scripture can be
considered in two ways: either materially, with respect to the doctrine handed
down, or formally, with respect to its writing and the manner of handing it
down. In the former sense, as was said before, we confess that it is simply
and absolutely necessary, so that the church can never lack it. But in the
latter sense, which is now in question, we acknowledge that it is not
absolutely necessary with respect to God. For just as God had instructed his
church for two thousand years before Moses by the living voice alone, so he
could, if he had wished, have taught it in that manner afterward. It is only
hypothetically necessary because of the divine will, insofar as for weighty
reasons it seemed good to God to commit his Word to writing. Therefore, once
the divine ordinance was established, Scripture became so necessary to the
church that it belongs not only to the church's well-being but also to its
very existence, so that today the church cannot subsist without it. Thus God
is indeed not bound to Scripture, but he has bound us to Scripture.

III. The question, therefore, is not whether the writing of the Word was
absolutely and simply necessary, but whether it was necessary in a certain
respect and upon a hypothesis; not from every age, but today in the present
state of affairs; and not in relation to God's power and freedom, but to his
wisdom and economy in dealing with the human being. For just as in the natural
economy parents vary the manner of instruction as their children's age
increases, so that infants are first formed by the living voice, then are
taught by a master's voice and the reading of books, and finally, once the
hand with its rod has been withdrawn, draw teachings from books for
themselves, so the heavenly Father, who instructs his people as a father does
a son, Deuteronomy 8:5, instructed the church while it was still an infant
and lisping by the living voice, himself lisping with it, which is the
simplest manner of revelation. Soon afterward he taught the church in its
adolescence and early youth under the law both by the living voice, on account
of the remains of its infant age, and by writing, on account of the beginnings
of its stronger age, up to the time of the apostles. Finally, under the
gospel, he wished the adult church to be content with the light of the most
perfect manner of revelation, namely, the written Word. Thus Scripture became
necessary not only by a necessity of command but upon the hypothesis of the
divine economy, which God willed to be varied and “manifold” (πολυποίκιλον)
according to the church's various ages, Ephesians 3:10.

IV. Hence arose the distinction of the Word into “unwritten” (ἄγραφον) and
“written” (ἔγγραφον). It is not a division of a genus into species, as the
Roman Catholics hold, as though the unwritten Word were one thing and the
written Word another. Rather, it is a distinction of a subject in its
accidents, because it happened to the same Word that formerly it was not
written and now it is written. It is therefore called “unwritten” (ἄγραφον)
not with respect to the present time but to the past, when it seemed good to
God to teach the church by the living voice alone and not by writing.

V. Although God spoke to the fathers “at many times and in many ways”
(πολυμερῶς καὶ πολυτρόπως), Hebrews 1:1—sometimes by a clear voice and spoken
discourse (προφορικῷ), sometimes by inward inspiration as though by inward
discourse (ἐνδιαθέτῳ), at times by sending dreams, at times by presenting
visions, sometimes by assuming a human form, often by the ministry of angels,
and by other symbols of his presence—the doctrine was nevertheless always the
same. It was not changed by a change in the manner of revealing and handing
it down or by a change of times.

[Marginal heading: The necessity of Scripture is proved.]

VI. Three things chiefly confirm the necessity of Scripture: 1. the
preservation of the Word; 2. its vindication; 3. its propagation. It was
necessary for the written Word to be given to the church so that it might be
a constant and immovable rule of faith and true religion, more easily
preserved pure and entire against the weakness of memory, human perversity,
and the brevity of life; so that it might be more certainly vindicated from
the frauds and corruptions of Satan; and so that it might be propagated and
transmitted more conveniently not only to the absent and far distant but also
to posterity. For, as Vives rightly observes in *On the Causes of the
Corruption of the Arts* 1, “All the arts are preserved by letters as in a
treasury, lest they ever perish, whereas that transmission from hand to hand
is unfortunate.” As Quintilian says, “This is the divine and wonderful
benefit of letters: that they preserve voices and return them to the absent
as though a deposit.” Nor are public laws, statutes and edicts of kings, and
decrees of the people inscribed on bronze or committed to public tablets for
any other reason than that this is the surest means of preserving without
corruption and propagating through many ages the memory of those things which
it is in the public interest to know.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

VII. Although the church before Moses lacked the written Word, it does not
follow that it can also lack it now. For the condition of the church while
still an infant, when it did not yet constitute the body of a commonwealth,
was one thing, and that of the church when already enlarged and more populous
was another; the condition of the earliest times was one thing, and that of
the times which followed was another. In the earliest times the “unwritten”
Word (ἄγραφον) could more easily be preserved because of the longevity of the
patriarchs, the small number of those in covenant, and the frequency of
revelations, although it also suffered not a few corruptions of its own. But
in the later times, when human life was contracted to a shorter span, the
church no longer lay hidden within one or two families but grew into a very
numerous people, and divine oracles were granted more sparingly, there had to
be another manner of instruction, so that this sacred commonwealth would be
governed not only by the living voice but also by written laws.

VIII. Although certain particular churches could for a time lack the written
Word of God, especially when they were first being established, nevertheless
they did not lack that Word of God which is written, since it undoubtedly
sounded in their ears through human ministry; nor did the church in common
then lack Scripture.

IX. The Holy Spirit—the supply (ἐπιχορηγία) by which the faithful ought to be
“taught by God” (θεοδίδακτοι), Jeremiah 31:34; John 6:43; 1 John 2:27—does not
render Scripture less necessary. 1. He is not given to us to bring new
revelations, but to impress the written Word upon our hearts. Thus the Word
must never here be separated from the Spirit, Isaiah 59:21. The former works
objectively and the latter efficiently; the former strikes the ears outwardly
and the latter opens hearts inwardly. The Spirit is the teacher; Scripture is
the doctrine which he teaches us. 2. The words of Jeremiah 31 and 1 John 2:27
must not be understood absolutely and simply, as though believers of the New
Testament no longer needed to use Scripture, for otherwise John would write
to them in vain. They must be understood in a certain respect, because on
account of the fuller abundance of the Holy Spirit in the New Testament they
did not have to be taught so laboriously as under the Old through rude and
beggarly elements. 3. Jeremiah's promise will attain its fulfillment only in
heaven, where, because of the clear

vision of God, there will no longer be any need either for Scripture or for
the ministry of pastors, but everyone will behold God immediately, face to
face.

X. It is false that the church was preserved in the Babylonian captivity
without Scripture. For near the end of the seventy years Daniel is said to
have considered from the books the number of the years, Daniel 9:2, and Ezra
is said to have brought the book of the law, not newly composed it, Nehemiah
8:2. The passage at 4 Esdras 4:23 proves nothing, since it is apocryphal.
Although Ezra collected the sacred books into one corpus and even corrected
things corrupted through the negligence of the scribes, it does not follow
that the church had entirely lacked Scripture.

XI. Bellarmine groundlessly imagines that after the time of Moses those who
were brought to the true religion from other peoples used oral tradition
alone and lacked Scripture. For if any became proselytes, they were carefully
instructed in the doctrine of Moses and the prophets, as is evident even from
the single example of the eunuch of Queen Candace, Acts 8. Nor was Scripture
entirely unknown to the gentiles, especially after it was translated into the
Greek language in the time of Ptolemy Philadelphus.

XII. Christ is our sole teacher, Matthew 22:8, in such a way that the ministry
of Scripture is not excluded but necessarily included, because now he
addresses us only in Scripture and instructs us through it. Christ is not set
in opposition to Scripture, but to the false teachers of the Pharisees who
ambitiously sought the office of teacher which is due to Christ alone.

XIII. Although Scripture formally has no personal use with respect to the
“illiterate” (ἀναλφαβήτους), those who do not know how to read, nevertheless
materially it serves their instruction and edification, insofar as the
doctrine which resounds in the church is drawn from no other source.
