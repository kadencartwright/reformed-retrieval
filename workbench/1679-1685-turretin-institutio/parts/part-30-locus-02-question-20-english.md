QUESTION XX

ON THE SUPREME JUDGE OF CONTROVERSIES AND INTERPRETER OF SCRIPTURE

Is Scripture, or God speaking in Scripture, the supreme and infallible judge
of controversies and interpreter of Scripture? Or is the church or the Roman
pontiff? We affirm the former and deny the latter, against the Roman Catholics.

I. This question is primary and almost the sole reason all the other

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 139 | IA leaf 193 | PDF p. 193]

controversies raised about Scripture have been stirred up. For no other reason
do the Roman Catholics call the authority of Scripture into doubt, undermine
its integrity and purity, or charge it with obscurity and imperfection than to
prove that Scripture cannot be the judge of controversies and that recourse
must necessarily be had to the tribunal of the church.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. To determine the state of the question, it must be observed, first, that
the inquiry here does not concern just any judgment—whether any judgment about
controversies of faith belongs to the church or its rulers. The orthodox refute
by their own practice the charge that they deny this. The inquiry concerns only
the supreme and infallible judge by whose decision one must necessarily stand
or fall: whether this authority belongs to Scripture itself, as we maintain, or
to some person or assembly composed of human beings, as the Roman Catholics
maintain.

III. Three kinds of judge must be carefully distinguished here. The first is
supreme and sovereign (αὐτοκρατορικὸς), judging with legislative and absolute
authority in the manner of a supreme prince who enacted the law and from whom
there is no appeal. The second is subordinate or ministerial (ὑπηρετικὸς),
interpreting the laws in the manner of a public servant. The third is private
(ἰδιωτικὸς), judging both the laws and their interpretation by way of private
discretion. The first renders a judgment of ultimate and absolute decision.
The second renders a judgment of public determination, but one that is
subordinate and bound by the laws. The third renders a judgment of private
discretion, without any public authority. Here we dispute not about the
ministerial and private judge, but about the supreme and infallible judge.

IV. The question is not whether Scripture is the rule and norm of
controversies. The Roman Catholics do not deny this, or at least wish to appear
to affirm it with certainty, although they take away with one hand what they
give with the other by charging Scripture with obscurity and imperfection. The
question is whether Scripture is the total and complete rule, not a partial and
imperfect one. They maintain that it is only a partial rule, and that only
insofar as it is explained according to the mind of the catholic—that is, the
Roman—church.

V. The Roman Catholic position amounts to this. 1. They distinguish between
the norm and the judge who must render judgment from Scripture. They indeed
acknowledge Scripture to be a norm, but a partial and inadequate one, to which
“unwritten” tradition (ἄγραφος) must be added. They maintain that Scripture is
not sufficient to settle controversies unless the ruling of some visible and
infallible judge is added, clearly pronouncing which side has the better case,
because otherwise disputes would never end. They maintain that such a judge is
to be sought nowhere but in the church, where they set up four tribunals from
which there is no appeal: 1. the church, 2. councils, 3. the Fathers, and 4. the
pope. But once the accounts are correctly totaled, only the pope remains, to
whom they are accustomed to assign this supreme and infallible judgment.

VI. That this is their position is proved by the following words of Andradius,
who took part in the Council of Trent, book 2 of *Defense of the Tridentine
Faith*: “We attribute this very great authority of interpreting the Scriptures
not indiscriminately to individual bishops, but only to the Roman pontiff, who
is the head of the church, or to all prelates gathered into one assembly by his
command.” Bellarmine likewise says in *On the Word of God*, book 3, chapter 19:
“That judge cannot be Scripture, but must be an ecclesiastical ruler, either
alone or with the counsel and consent of his fellow bishops.” And Gregory of
Valencia says in *On Analysis*, book 7: “The Roman pontiff himself is the one in
whom resides that authority which exists in the church to judge absolutely all
controversies of faith.” This is not the position of them all, however. Those
who contend that the pope is above a council attribute this judicial power to
the pontiff, but those who maintain that a council is above the pope think
differently. Still others, in order to reconcile both positions, judge this
infallible judge to be the pope in a council, or a council approved by the
pope.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 140 | IA leaf 194 | PDF p. 194]

VII. For our part, we do not deny that the church has a ministerial and
subordinate judge who can publicly settle controversies of faith from the Word
of God. We also acknowledge, with respect to inward full assurance
(πληροφορίαν), that the Holy Spirit persuades us of the true interpretation of
Scripture in the manner of a principle. Nevertheless, with respect to the
external demonstration of the object, we deny that any supreme and infallible
judge is to be sought besides Scripture, much less that the pope should be
admitted to perform such an office. For we hold that Scripture alone, or God
speaking in it, suffices for this purpose.

[Marginal heading: Scripture alone is the supreme judge of controversies. 1.
From Isaiah 8:20, and so forth.]

VIII. The reasons are these. 1. In both the Old and New Testaments God refers
us absolutely and without any condition to this judge: “You shall act
according to the law which they have taught you,” Deuteronomy 17:10; “To the
law and to the testimony,” and so forth, Isaiah 8:20; and “They have Moses and
the prophets; let them hear them,” Luke 16:29. Christ does not say that they
have priests and scribes who cannot err, but that they have Moses and the
prophets—that is, their writings—in order to indicate that these are amply
sufficient for full instruction and that their authority must be accepted.
Christ's meaning is no different in Matthew 19:28, where he promises the
apostles that after his departure they will “sit upon twelve thrones, judging
the twelve tribes of Israel.” This can refer to nothing other than the judicial
power they possess in the church through the Word. Thus in Matthew 22:29 Christ
says to the Sadducees, “You err, not knowing the Scriptures.” Elsewhere he
sends the Jews to search the Scriptures, John 5:39.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the practice of Christ and the apostles.]

IX. 2. The practice of Christ and the apostles confirms this. In controversies
of faith they appeal to the Scriptures, Matthew 4 and 22; John 5 and 10; Acts
17, 18, and 26. They profess that they say nothing beyond Moses and the
prophets, Luke 24:44; Acts 26:22. Peter sets the Word before a heavenly vision
as “a more sure word” (λόγον βεβαιότερον), to which people do well to attend, 2
Peter 1:19, without any consideration of his own privilege or papal
infallibility. The Bereans are commended in Acts 17:11 for weighing the doctrine
by the norm of Scripture rather than consulting some other oracle of
infallibility. The Pharisees and Sadducees, by contrast, are condemned because
they departed from that norm, Matthew 15:3 and 22:29.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the nature of an infallible judge.]

X. 3. The supreme and infallible judge is one who never errs and cannot err in
judging, who is led by no partiality, and from whom there is no appeal. None of
these properties can belong to the church, councils, or the pontiff, for they
can err and have often erred most grievously. They are also an accused party,
having been charged as falsifiers and corrupters of Scripture, and appeal may
always be made from them to Scripture, 1 John 4:1; Isaiah 8:20; John 5:39; Acts
17:11. But God speaking in Scripture alone claims these properties as his own:
he cannot err in judging because he is truth itself; he is led by no
partiality, because he is “no respecter of persons” (ἀπροσωπολήπτης); and no
appeal can be made from him because he acknowledges no superior.

[Marginal heading: 4. Because every human being is liable to error, and God
alone is infallible.]

XI. 4. A human being cannot be the infallible interpreter of Scripture and
judge of controversies, because a human being is liable to error, and our faith
does not resolve into him but into God alone, on whom the meaning and judgment
of Scripture depend. God is the best interpreter of his own words and can best
explain the meaning of the law, as our one teacher, Matthew 23:8, 10, and our
legislator, who can save and destroy, James 4:12. Nor, if the rulers of the
church are led by the Holy Spirit, do they immediately cease to be human and
therefore fallible. Their inspiration is only ordinary and common, not the
extraordinary and special inspiration which bestows the gift of infallibility,
as it did in the prophets and apostles.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 141 | IA leaf 195 | PDF p. 195]

[Marginal heading: 5. Because Scripture acknowledges no one besides God.]

XII. 5. If such a judge as the Roman Catholics contend for were given, 1. It
would be remarkable that the Lord nowhere mentioned this so necessary
interpreter. 2. It would be remarkable that Paul in his epistles, especially
the one to the Romans, did not warn them about so great a privilege with even a
single word. 3. It would be remarkable that Peter in his catholic epistles
neither claimed this privilege for himself so that it might be transmitted to
his successors, much less exercised it. 4. It would also be remarkable that
the pontiffs, by this infallible authority, have been either unable or unwilling
to resolve the various and exceedingly grave controversies which the Roman
Church nurtures within its bosom—between Thomists and Scotists, Dominicans and
Jesuits, Jesuits and Jansenists, and so forth. Why do they not abolish disputes
and untie perplexing knots by means of their infallibility? If they cannot, how
are they infallible? If they can, why do they not free the church from scandals
of this kind?

[Marginal heading: 6. Because the church cannot be judge in its own case.]

XIII. 6. The church cannot be appointed judge of controversies because it
would be judge in its own case and the rule of itself. For the principal
controversy concerns the church's power and infallibility. In the question
whether the church is the judge, or whether the Roman Church can err, will that
same church sit as judge, and must it be believed because it proclaims itself
to be such? Is it tolerable to refuse to let Holy Scripture be the judge, when
everyone acknowledges it to be the infallible Word of God, and to wish instead
that the church or the pope sit as judge in his own case and be the infallible
judge of his own infallibility—the very point most in dispute—when it is
established that he not only can err but often has erred? The Roman Catholics
themselves are compelled to confess that not a few popes have been heretics,
or impious men devoted to magical arts. Adrian VI, *Sentences*, book 4.

[Marginal heading: 7. From the Fathers.]

XIV. 7. The ancients support us. Constantine, addressing the Nicene Fathers in
Theodoret, book 1, chapter 7, first said that the things we ought to believe
about God are clearly taught in the evangelical, apostolic, and prophetic
books. He then added, “Therefore, putting away strife that makes war, let us
take the solution of the matters in question from the divinely inspired words”
(τὴν πολεμοποιὸν οὖν ἀπελάσαντες ἔριν, ἐκ τῶν θεοπνεύστων λόγων λάβωμεν τῶν
ζητουμένων τὴν λύσιν). Optatus says in *On the Schism of the Donatists*, book
5: “You say, ‘It is permitted’; we say, ‘It is not permitted.’ Between your
‘it is permitted’ and our ‘it is not permitted,’ the souls of the people sway
and drift. Let no one believe you or us; a judge must be sought from heaven. No
judgment about this matter can be found on earth. But why do we knock at heaven
when here in the gospel we have the testament?” Augustine says in his
*Exposition of Psalm 1*, section 11: “We are brothers; why do we quarrel? Our
Father did not die intestate; he made a testament, and so forth. Open it; let us
read. Why do we contend?” And in *Marriage and Concupiscence*, book 2, chapter
33, he says: “This controversy seeks a judge. Let Christ judge; let the apostle
judge with him.” Lactantius says in *Divine Institutes*, book 3, chapter 1:
“God speaks in the divine writings as the supreme judge of all things, whose
part is not to argue but to pronounce.” Gregory of Nyssa says in *Against
Eunomius*, book 1: “The divinely inspired Scripture is a sure criterion for
every doctrine” (κριτήριον ἀσφαλὲς ἐπὶ παντὸς δόγματος ἡ θεόπνευστος γραφὴ).
So also Cyprian in his letter to Caecilius; Chrysostom, homily 23 on Acts; and
Augustine, *On Baptism*, book 2, chapter 6.

XV. 8. Just as it belongs to a prince to interpret his own law, so it belongs
to God to be the interpreter of his Scripture, which is the law of faith and
morals. The privilege belonging to other authors—that each is the interpreter
of his own words—must not be taken away from God speaking in Scripture.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XVI. When we say that Scripture is the judge of controversies, we mean nothing
other than that it is the source of divine law and the most absolute norm of
faith, by which all controversies about faith can and ought to be settled with
certainty and clarity, just as in a commonwealth the grounds of decisions and
judgments are sought from the law. Thus Scripture is called a judge broadly
and by a metonymy of an adjunct: a normative judge, not a

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 142 | IA leaf 196 | PDF p. 196]

personal one. It must therefore not be confused with the subordinate judge who
settles controversies according to the norm of the law and applies the law's
right to “the particulars” (τὰ καθ’ ἕκαστα). This is the point of the
Philosopher's words in *Politics* 4.4: “The law must rule over everything, but
the magistrates and the polity must judge the particulars” (δεῖ τὸν νόμον
ἄρχειν πάντων, τῶν δὲ καθ’ ἕκαστα τὰς ἀρχὰς καὶ τὴν πολιτείαν κρίνειν).

XVII. It is not always necessary to distinguish between the judge and the law.
The Philosopher acknowledges in *Politics* 3.6 that in prescribing universal
law, the law has the character of a judge. But in particular application, “in
the particulars” (ἐν τοῖς καθ’ ἕκαστα), the interpreter of the law performs
the office of a judge, though a ministerial and subordinate one. In this sense
we do not deny that the church is a judge, but it is a judge always bound to
Scripture. In a commonwealth the magistrate's ruling is valid only insofar as
it rests on the law and agrees with it; if it conflicts with the law, the
ruling is void and can be appealed. Likewise in the church, the judgment of
pastors can be admitted only insofar as it is found to conform to Scripture.

XVIII. Although Scripture does not hear the arguments of both sides in a
dispute and does not always render its decision so as to acquit this person and
condemn that one by name, it does not follow that Scripture is not the supreme
judge and perfect norm. These acts do not properly belong to the supreme judge
but to the ministerial judge, who is bound to render a decision according to
the law and needs an examination of witnesses and arguments and an inspection
of the laws, because the matter concerns fact, not law. But the supreme judge
is the one who, apart from every dispute, ordains under universal law what is
to be done and avoided, and subordinate judges are bound to his ruling. Nor has
it ever happened that an express condemnation of Titius or Maevius is read in
the laws. This is how the matter stands here, because in a case of faith the
question concerns law, not fact, when one asks what must or must not be
believed. The judge and the law can settle this without hearing the parties.

XIX. There is no need for the supreme judge speaking in Scripture to bring us
a new word every day because new heresies arise. Since he knew this would
happen, he pronounced his truth in the Word in such a way that faithful
ministers can recognize catholic truth from it and refute every error from it.
Thus from Scripture the Fathers most solidly refuted the heresies of Pelagius,
Arius, Macedonius, and others, although it teaches nothing explicitly about
them.

XX. There need not be a visible infallible judge besides Scripture to resolve
all controversies. 1. An end of controversies is not to be hoped for in this
life, for “there must be heresies, so that those who are approved may be made
manifest,” 1 Corinthians 11:19. Various corruptions had already crept in during
the apostles' time and were not entirely removed. 2. It is one thing to
convince an adversary in fact and stop his mouth so that he makes no reply; it
is another to convince him by right, so that there is adequate ground from
which he can be convinced unless he is obstinate. Scripture does not always do
the former because of human perversity, but it always does the latter, which is
sufficient. 3. Just as in a well-ordered commonwealth it is sufficient to have
good laws by which “the particulars” (τὰ καθ’ ἕκαστα) can be judged by
subordinate judges, so in the church it is sufficient to have the infallible
written Word, from which individual pastors may seek the norm of judgment in
individual controversies. 4. The Roman Catholics' visible judge does not
prevent innumerable controversies from arising, which he has not yet resolved
by his infallible authority.

XXI. Scripture has various and ambiguous senses not from the nature of the
thing affirmed or the intention of the one affirming, but from the ignorance
or obstinacy of the one who distorts it. Therefore whatever ambiguity and
obscurity there may be does not remove Scripture's authority, but shows the
necessity of the illuminating Spirit and of the ministry that expounds the
Scriptures.

XXII. Although the true interpretation of some passage of Scripture is at
issue, there need not on that account be some visible infallible judge outside

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 143 | IA leaf 197 | PDF p. 197]

Scripture, because Scripture interprets itself. Although a human being presents
such an interpretation, he must not be regarded as its author, because he
devises nothing of his own and attaches nothing to Scripture, but only elicits
and draws out from Scripture what lay hidden in it. Likewise, one who
legitimately deduces a conclusion from premises does not fabricate it at will,
but elicits it from the conceded premises in which it lay hidden.

XXIII. When the judge of controversies is disputed, the question properly
concerns not the principles—that is, questions about Scripture, which is
assumed here as a principle rather than proved—but the things derived from the
principles, that is, the doctrines contained in Scripture. Once Scripture's
authority is posited, we believe these doctrines can be sufficiently settled
from Scripture itself. Nevertheless, we do not deny that Scripture also proves
itself, as was demonstrated above, not only authoritatively and by way of
testimony, but also rationally and by way of argument.

XXIV. Scripture can no more be called mute and speechless for purposes of
judgment than can the church in councils and the Fathers in their writings,
whom our adversaries nevertheless contend both speak and judge. If a father
speaks in a testament and a king in edicts and charters, why should we not say
that the heavenly Father in both Testaments and the King of kings in the divine
oracles address us in the clearest voice? No one can doubt this, since so often
Scripture, or the Holy Spirit in it, is said to address, accuse, and judge human
beings. The law is said to speak to those under the law, Romans 3:19. Abraham
says to the rich man, “They have Moses and the prophets,” Luke 16:29—not Moses
and the prophets living and seeing, but neither mute nor speechless; rather,
speaking and to be heard. Thus Isaiah is said to cry out, Romans 9:27. Moses
accuses the Jews, John 5:45. The law judges, John 7:51 and 12:48. “Whoever does
not receive my words has one who judges him” (ἔχει τὸν κρίνοντα): “The word
which I have spoken will judge him on the last day.” In the same sense the Word
of God is called “discerning” (κριτικὸς), a judge of thoughts, Hebrews 4:12.

XXV. An earthly judge in an external court must be furnished with coercive
power. But the rationale of a spiritual judge in the court of conscience is
not the same, because the kingdom of God is advanced not by bodily coercion but
by the spiritual demonstration of truth, 1 Corinthians 2:4. Further, although
bodily coercion has no place here, spiritual and inward coercion is not lacking:
with respect to the godly, whom God speaking in the Scriptures draws and
constrains to obedience by a soul-bending and gentle power, John 6:44; 2
Corinthians 10:4, and with respect to the ungodly and unbelieving, whose
consciences he torments and vexes.

XXVI. The example of Moses and Aaron cannot be used to establish a supreme and
infallible judge outside Scripture. Both were ministerial judges, not sovereign
ones (αὐτοκρατορικὸς); Moses was extraordinary and Aaron ordinary. They settled
controversies not by their own authority, but from God's law and prescription:
Moses as an intermediary by referring the matter to God, Exodus 18:19, and
Aaron from the law by answering according to it, Deuteronomy 17:11: “Act
according to the law which they have taught you.” Otherwise, if they pronounced
against the law, they were not to be heard. 2. The question there does not
properly concern controversies of faith but ritual matters, judging between
blood and blood and between leprosy and leprosy. 3. It concerns not only the
high priest but the whole Levitical priesthood, whose decision had to be
accepted when it was pronounced from the prescription of the law. Otherwise,
if it had to be accepted absolutely, Jeremiah in Jeremiah 26:12–13, Christ in
John 9, and the apostles in Acts 3 and 13 would have committed a capital crime
by departing from it. 4. The inference from the high priest to the pontiff is
invalid, because in the New Testament there is no high priest besides Christ,
of whom Aaron was a type.

XXVII. The “one shepherd” of Ecclesiastes 12:11 does not signify a priest

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 144 | IA leaf 198 | PDF p. 198]

typifying the Old Testament, but the true priest of the New Testament, Christ
Jesus, who is that good shepherd of his people, Ezekiel 34:23; John 10:11. From
him all the words of the wise proceeded, because the men of God spoke as they
were moved by his Spirit, 2 Peter 1:21, as the Roman Catholic interpreters
Tirinus, Menochius, and a Lapide themselves explain.

XXVIII. In Haggai 2:11 and Malachi 2:7, not some one priest but priests
indefinitely are commanded to be questioned and, when questioned, to answer
from the law. Their infallibility is not indicated, but their office, which the
same passage teaches they did not always perform, since verse 8 immediately
adds, “But you have departed from the way,” and so forth.

XXIX. The chair of Moses in Matthew 23:2 is not succession to Moses' place and
office, or an external tribunal of a supreme judge to which the supreme right
in question is attached. No chair of Moses remained, nor was such a privilege
attributed to him. Rather, it is the promulgation of the true doctrine delivered
by Moses, as the Ordinary Gloss says on Deuteronomy 17: “Wherever the doctrine
of Moses is, there is the chair of Moses; and wherever the doctrine of Peter
sounds forth, there is the chair of Peter.” Thus those who were teachers of the
law delivered by Moses are regarded as having taught in Moses' chair, as Hilary
says. The Pharisees teaching in Moses' chair were therefore to be heard insofar
as they sincerely presented Moses' doctrine to the people without mixing in
their own leaven.

XXX. Although in Matthew 18:17 Christ sends us to the voice of the church and
whoever does not hear it is to be regarded as a heathen and a tax collector, he
does not appoint it an infallible judge in matters of faith. 1. The passage
does not concern controversies of faith but private offenses and fraternal
correction. If private correction does not succeed, the matter must be brought
to the public censure of the church, which is pronounced not by one infallible
prelate to all churches, but by individual pastors to their own flocks. 2. The
passage alludes to the custom of Jewish discipline, which excommunicated the
obstinate. This pertains no more to the Roman church than to any particular
churches within their own bounds. 3. If the argument is drawn from similarity,
the church is commanded to be heard as long as it hears Christ and speaks the
words of Christ. But if it departs from Christ and speaks things contrary to
him, anathema must be pronounced against it, Galatians 1:8.

XXXI. Councils sometimes sought from absent popes not an authoritative
confirmation, but fraternal assent. Otherwise they could not have claimed for
themselves the right to depose popes and to examine and annul their acts, as
they did. The Fathers and particular churches could consult them in more
difficult ecclesiastical affairs, not as infallible judges to whose decrees
they were bound to submit their consciences, but as honored and prudent
arbitrators. Before they were swollen with the poison of pride, superstition,
and tyranny, they were held in great esteem among the churches, especially
because of the city's primacy (πρωτεῖα).

XXXII. Although in the external court of the church every private person is
bound to submit to synodical decisions unless he wishes to be excommunicated,
and although such judgment ought to have force for the preservation of order,
peace, and orthodoxy so that the undertakings of innovators may be restrained,
it does not follow that the judgment is supreme and infallible. There is always
an appeal from it with respect to the internal court of conscience, and it
binds no one in that court any further than he is persuaded that it agrees with
the Scriptures.

XXXIII. Although we maintain that the judgment of private discretion belongs
to individual believers, because “the spiritual person judges all things,” 1
Corinthians 2:15, and the apostle commands them to “test all things,” 1
Thessalonians 5:21, we do not thereby assert against Peter a “private
interpretation” (ἰδία ἐπίλυσις) of Scripture, 2 Peter 1:21. For “interpretation”
(ἐπίλυσις) here denotes not so much the interpretation of Scripture as the

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 145 | IA leaf 199 | PDF p. 199]

origin of the prophetic oracles: they are said to have been written not from
each person's own private impulse and instinct, as is said of those who run
without being sent by God, Jeremiah 23:21, but from the dictation of the Holy
Spirit by whom they were moved. Thus “interpretation” (ἐπίλυσις) here pertains
not to the office of the interpreter, hearer, or reader of Scripture, but to
the power or impulse of prophesying, or to that motion by which the prophet was
impelled to speak or write. The comparison with the preceding and following
verses favors this. They do not inquire who has the right to interpret
prophecies, but by whose impulse and motion the prophets wrote, what place we
ought to assign to prophecies, what reverence is owed to them, and why they
should be believed as God's undoubted oracles—namely, because they did not flow
from the motion and will of human beings themselves, as though they were a
human invention or devised by human choice, but from the impulse and
inspiration of the Holy Spirit, by whom holy men of God were moved to speak. In
this sense “interpretation” (ἐπίλυσις) will signify the sending of the men of
God to prophesy, by which God opens for them, as it were, the starting gates
for running. The allusion is to runners in a stadium who, once the barriers are
released, burst from the starting gates upon the course which they then begin.
But if the word here is explained of interpretation, as various interpreters
do from the force of the word “interpretation” (ἐπίλυσις), which signifies to
expound and explain, Mark 4:34; Acts 19:39, the denial will be that prophecy is
“of private interpretation” (ἰδίας ἐπιλύσεως) with respect to its principle and
origin—that is, that it comes from one's own mind—not with respect to its
subject. Thus private interpretation here is opposed not to common or public
interpretation, but to the adventitious gift of the Holy Spirit.

XXXIV. From this judgment of private discretion attributed to each believer,
some wrongly conclude that human reason is the judge of controversies and the
interpreter of Scripture. The Socinians would have this, but it was refuted by
us above when we disputed about the use of reason in theology, Topic 1,
Question 8, because the believer here is moved not only by the light of reason,
but chiefly by the direction of the Spirit. Although an interpreter may
examine the meaning of Scripture he has conceived in relation to natural
reason, he may not oppose the dictate of reason to Holy Scripture or refuse to
believe Scripture because of some preconceived opinion of reason that perhaps
conflicts with it. It is more certain that human reason, which is deceptive and
unstable, departs from the truth of the matter than that Holy Scripture, which
is the Word of Truth and truth itself, does so. Here reason must be brought
captive under faith, 2 Corinthians 10:5, not lifted above it.

XXXV. The uncertainty of human judgment cannot make God speaking in the
Scriptures an unsuitable judge in our case on the ground that neither who has
the Holy Spirit nor whether the Spirit is actually possessed can be known. It
is unnecessary in this matter to know immediately and *a priori* who has the
Spirit, but only who speaks according to Scripture. Once that has become known
from comparison with Scripture, it can easily be judged *a posteriori* who
presents the Spirit's words and speaks from him. Thus the Bereans did not
inquire *a priori* whether Paul, who preached to them, was moved by the Holy
Spirit, since this was known only to God, “the knower of hearts”
(καρδιογνώστῃ), but whether Paul spoke according to the Scriptures. From his
conformity with Scripture they concluded that he spoke not from himself but
from the Holy Spirit. We conclude with Basil's golden words in letter 80 to
Eustathius the Physician: “Therefore let the divinely inspired Scripture
arbitrate for us, and let the divine verdict of truth come entirely upon those
among whom doctrines are found to agree with the divine words” (οὐκ οὖν ἡ
θεόπνευστος ἡμῖν διαιτησάτω γραφὴ, καὶ παρ’ οἷς ἂν εὑρεθῇ τὰ δόγματα
συνᾴδοντα τοῖς θείοις λόγοις, ἐπὶ τούτοις ἥξει πάντως ἡ θεία τῆς ἀληθείας
ψῆφος). That is: “Let divinely inspired Scripture judge for us, and let the
verdict of truth belong entirely to those among whom doctrines are found that
agree with Scripture.”
