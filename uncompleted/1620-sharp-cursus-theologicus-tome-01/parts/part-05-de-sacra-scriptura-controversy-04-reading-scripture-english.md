Fourth Controversy: The Reading of Scripture

Bellarmine says in book 2, chapter 15 of *On the Word of God* that the reading
of Holy Scripture is indeed permitted, but only to those who have obtained
authorization from the ordinary: “It is prohibited, however,” he says, “that
permission to read be granted indiscriminately to everyone without
distinction.” But other Roman Catholics simply deny that laypeople should be
permitted to read Holy Scripture. One is Johannes Molanus, the Louvain doctor
of theology, in book 3, chapter 27, conclusion 2 of his treatise *On Practical
Theology*: “We deny that study of the Scriptures is required of laypeople.
Indeed, it is said with salutary effect that they are kept from reading the
Scriptures, and that it is enough for them to regulate the course of their
lives according to the direction of their pastors and teachers.” Hosius says
the same in his book *On Reading Sacred Things in the Vernacular*, as do
others.

We, on the contrary, say that it is the duty of all Christians to devote
themselves to Scripture, not only publicly, when it is read aloud or
explained, but also privately, by reading and searching it. The state of the
controversy, therefore, is whether the reading of Holy Scripture ought to be
permitted and commended to everyone, including laypeople. They deny it; we
affirm it, relying on these arguments.

1. Argument. What has been commanded to everyone ought to be done by everyone.
But reading and

[1620 Tome I | col. 42 | BSB frame 00035]

searching the Holy Scriptures has been commanded indiscriminately to everyone
(Deut. 6:6–9; 11:18; 17:19; Josh. 1:8; Ps. 1:2; John 5:39, and so forth).
Therefore.

2. Argument. What the apostles praise in laypeople is permissible for them,
and they ought to do it. But the reading of Scripture is praised in laypeople
(Acts 17:11; 2 Pet. 1:19). Therefore.

3. Argument. God commands everyone to hear him speaking in the word. But he
speaks to us not only from the chair through doctors and pastors, but also in
the sacred writings themselves, for in them he teaches, commands, threatens,
and promises. Therefore, it belongs to everyone to hear God speaking there as
well, which happens only by reading.

4. Argument. If individual Christians ought to be ready to give an account of
their faith to anyone who asks, then they ought to read the Holy Scriptures
continually, for the account of our faith and hope is to be sought nowhere
else than from the Scriptures. But the former is true (1 Pet. 3:15).
Therefore.

5. Argument. God commands that the people be instructed and full of wisdom and
knowledge, and he often complains of their ignorance. Hence he commands them
to be exercised in the word so that they may acquire understanding for
themselves from it (Deut. 31:11–12; 2 Cor. 8:7; Col. 1:9; 2:2; 3:16). Indeed,
unless they do this, he threatens to execute his judgments upon them (Jeremiah
9). Therefore, since this cannot happen without the reading of Scripture, the
people ought to read Scripture.

6. Argument. Moses wishes in Numbers 11:29 that the whole people might
prophesy, as does Paul in 1 Corinthians 14:5. But to prohibit the reading of
Scripture is nothing other than to wish to hinder this very thing. Therefore,
it ought not to be prohibited.

7. Argument. Individual believers ought to instruct, admonish, and teach one
another (Col. 3:16; 1 Thess. 5:11; 2 Tim. 2; Heb. 3:13). But they cannot do
this except from Scripture and according to Scrip—

[1620 Tome I | col. 43 | BSB frame 00036]

—ture. Therefore, and so forth.

8. Argument. The apostle commands that those women who wish to advance in the
knowledge of sacred doctrine ask their husbands at home. Therefore, laypeople
may also exercise themselves privately in reading Holy Scripture, so that they
can answer those who question them about the Scriptures, since this cannot be
done without reading. But the former is true (1 Cor. 14:35).

9. Argument. Private households are called churches (Rom. 16:5). But this name
was evidently thought suitable to be given to a private household because its
members privately formulated prayers, read Holy Scripture, and meditated upon
it. Therefore, and so forth.

10. Argument. The eunuch mentioned in Acts 8 was a layman. But he read
Scripture, and this reading was beneficial to him and approved by God.
Therefore, laypeople are permitted to read Scripture.

Testimonies

Jerome says in his commentary on Colossians 3:16: “From this we perceive that
laypeople ought to possess knowledge of the Scriptures not only sufficiently,
but also abundantly, and to teach one another.” See the same author on Psalm
86. Chrysostom says in homily 9 on the Epistle to the Colossians: “Pay
attention, all of you who are laypeople and who preside over wives and
children, to how the apostle especially instructs you to read the Scriptures,
and to do so not casually or in passing, but with great diligence.” See also
homily 3 on Lazarus and tome 1, homily 35 on Genesis.

Augustine says in book 2, chapter 5 of *On Christian Doctrine*: “From this it
came about that divine Scripture—by which relief is brought to such great
diseases of human wills—proceeded from one language, in which it could
conveniently be disseminated throughout the world, and was spread far and wide
through the various languages of interpreters, so that it might become known
to the nations for salvation. Those who read it seek nothing other than the
thoughts and the wi—

[1620 Tome I | col. 44 | BSB frame 00036]

—ll of those by whom it was written, and through them the will of God, in
accordance with which we believe such men spoke.”

Objections

Objection 1. Moses enclosed the tablets of the law in the ark so that they
would be preserved there and not seen by everyone, and he gave them to the
priests and Levites for safekeeping. Therefore, he willed that only the learned
should read the Scriptures, not laypeople.

Response.

This is the fallacy of non-cause as cause, because Moses did this not so that
the law would not be read by the common people—indeed, this was commanded to
each and every one of them in Deuteronomy 6—but for the following reasons.
First, so that the very tablets received from God might be preserved as a
memorial and as a testimony against Israel. 2. So that by this God might teach
that the law is to be preserved in each person’s heart as though in an ark. 3.
So that it might also signify that Christ, the true priest, would preserve and
fulfill the law for us. 4. The tablets were given to the priests so that they
might know that by their own example they ought to go before others in
observing the law. 5. The ark was a type of Christ, and the law a pedagogue
leading to Christ. God therefore willed to teach that all who were to be under
the law were enclosed under faith in Christ, which was at last to be revealed.
Finally, it does not follow that because the tablets of the law were in the
ark and were guarded by the priests, therefore copies of them at least were
not elsewhere and the people did not read the law.

Objection 2.

God commands the law to be read before the people every seventh year. But if
the law was in each person’s hand so that it could be read privately, God gave
this command in vain. Therefore.

Response.

This is the fallacy of non-cause as cause, because God did not command this
for this rea—

[1620 Tome I | col. 45 | BSB frame 00037]

—son, that the people should not read privately; for elsewhere this was
specifically commanded to the people in Deuteronomy 6. Rather, he commanded it
so that he might stir the whole people, solemnly assembled in public, to read
the law, and so that he might render them inexcusable if they did not observe
it. By similar reasoning one could argue in this way: on the Lord’s Days the
word is to be read and preached publicly; therefore it is not to be read or
preached on any other day or in private. How exceedingly absurd this is is
quite evident.

Objection 3.

In Josiah’s time only one copy of the law had been hidden. But if the people
had then read the law and possessed books, there would undoubtedly have been
several. Hence, since there were not many copies of the law then, it follows
that it is not now necessary for the people to read at home copies of the
sacred writings translated into the vernacular language.

Response.

The assumption is denied, because the people did indeed possess many copies
of the law, but they had perished during times of persecution.

2. Nor does this follow: the people did not then possess what they nonetheless
ought to have possessed by God’s command; therefore, neither do we need those
things. For examples are to be imitated only insofar as they agree with the
word of God.

Objection 4.

Christ says that it was given to the apostles to know the mysteries of the
kingdom of heaven, but not likewise to others. Therefore, they are to be
communicated only to bishops and priests, not to the common people.

Response. The inference is denied, because Christ is speaking there only of
the scribes and Pharisees who resisted him, as even the Roman Catholics
interpret it. Therefore, nothing can be concluded from this against the
reading of laypeople, but rather against the reading of the learned, because
the Pharisees were teachers.

2. It does not follow, because when the Jewish people

[1620 Tome I | col. 46 | BSB frame 00037]

were rejected, it was not given to them to know the mysteries, and so forth;
therefore, neither will it be given to the elect Christian people.

Objection 5. Nehemiah, Ezra, and the Levites read and interpreted the book of
the law to the people because the people understood nothing of what was being
read. For the Hebrew language had then ceased to be vernacular, yet it was
retained in the synagogues and is retained by the Jews to this day. Therefore,
Scripture is neither to be translated nor read by the people.

Response. The text itself teaches that the people understood what was read by
the priest (Neh. 8:3–4), for to what end would they have read it if the people
had not understood? Interpretation was indeed added to the reading, but it was
not, as Bellarmine maintains, a bare translation of words from an unknown
language into a known one. Instead, it was an interpretation of the true
sense, as is taught there in verse 9. For this reason Ezra is called skilled
in the law in the following verse. 2. It is false that the Jews did not then
understand the Hebrew language. For although their language was not pure
Hebrew but was mixed from Hebrew and Chaldean, they were nevertheless not
entirely ignorant of Hebrew; during the seventy years of the Babylonian
captivity, they could not have completely forgotten it. Furthermore, if they
had not understood Hebrew, the prophets after the captivity would not have
set forth their sermons in Hebrew. Finally, these passages make clear that in
the time of Christ himself or of the apostles, the Hebrew text was commonly
read and understood by the people: Luke 4:18; John 19:20; Acts 15:21; 13:15;
21:40; 22:1.

Counterinstance 1. Christ says, speaking of the Jews, “This crowd does not know
the law.” Therefore, the Hebrew language was not vernacular to them.

Response 1. We deny the inference, because he is speaking not about the
language, words, or letters, but about the meaning and true sense of the law,
which those people did not know.

[1620 Tome I | col. 47 | BSB frame 00038]

Counterinstance 2. When Christ cried out on the cross, “Eloi, Eloi, lamma
sabacthani?” the people did not understand, and therefore some said, “He is
calling Elijah.” Therefore.

Response. Jerome says on that passage that those who said, “He is calling
Elijah,” were soldiers. But these men were Romans and foreigners. It is
therefore not surprising if they did not understand. But if they were Jews,
they were seizing their customary opportunity to mock Christ, as though he
were imploring Elijah’s help because he could not free himself.

Objection 6.

It is dangerous if the people read Scripture, because heresies have arisen
from this. Furthermore, if someone were to read what is said in the Song of
Songs about the love of Christ and the church and what is said elsewhere about
the incest, adultery, and drunkenness of the saints, he would be provoked to
imitate such things or would despise the holy fathers. And since there are
many apparent contradictions in Scripture, if he could not resolve them,
there is a danger that in the end he would believe nothing. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because if Scripture ought not now to be
read by the people on account of these dangers, why did God formerly command
the reading of Scripture, when the same things could have happened, and will
it to exist in the church’s vernacular language? Furthermore, it does not
follow that because certain profane people can abuse Scripture, therefore the
godly ought not to use it rightly. This is just as if you were to conclude:
many abuse food and drink; therefore, no one ought to eat or drink. For in
this way many learned men have abused Scripture and, through their abuse, have
become authors of heresies. Hence, if that reasoning were valid, not even the
learned would read the Scriptures.

3. No stumbling block justly arises from Scripture. But if there is one, it
is taken, not given, because the words of God are pure (Ps. 12:7). 4. It is
false that from reading histories in which

[1620 Tome I | col. 48 | BSB frame 00038]

the sins of the saints are recounted there arises either contempt for them or
an impulse to imitate such sins. On the contrary, just as the saints’ sins are
recounted, so too are their repentance and the punishments inflicted for
those sins. Finally, just as heresies have arisen from Scriptures either
poorly understood or not understood at all, so all heresies are refuted from
them when they are rightly understood. Therefore, so that heresies may be
vanquished by means of rightly understood Scriptures, the Scriptures ought for
that reason to be read.

Objection 7.

If the Scriptures were to be read by the people, then new translations would
have to be made in every age, because letters change in every century. But
this could not be done, because suitable men for translating would often be
lacking, and it would be absurd for translations to be altered so often.
Therefore.

Response.

It is false that all languages can be changed, for Hebrew, Greek, Latin, and
others remain intact.

2. In Christian churches there have been, and certainly are and always ought
to be, learned men who can translate the Scriptures.

3. It is not absurd that obsolete translations, when they are less readily
understood, ought to be altered and corrected.
