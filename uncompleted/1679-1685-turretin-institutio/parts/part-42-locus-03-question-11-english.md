QUESTION XI

ON THE IMMUTABILITY OF GOD

Is God immutable both with respect to essence and with respect to will?
Affirmative.

I. Immutability is an incommunicable attribute of God by which not only every
change but also the possibility of change is denied of God, both with respect
to existence and with respect to will.

II. The adversaries also assail this immutability of God, especially that which
concerns acts of the will, so that God could now be unwilling to do what he
previously willed and now will what he previously did not will. See Crellius,
book 1 of *On Religion*, chapter 21, and Vorstius, *Treatise on God*. We, with
the orthodox, hold that immutability of every kind must be attributed to him,
both with respect to nature and with respect to will.

[Marginal heading: That God is immutable is proved.]

III. 1. Because Scripture expressly attributes it to God. Malachi 3:6: “I am
Jehovah, and I do not change.” Psalm 102:26: “The heavens will perish, but you
remain,” and so forth. James 1:17: “With God there is no variation or shadow of
turning.” Here not only change but even the shadow of change is denied of him,
so that he is thus set in contrast to the sun, the source of bodily light,
which is subject to turnings and overshadowings by which its light is
intercepted. But God, the Father of lights, acknowledges no turnings, nor can
he be obscured by any shadow, since there is nothing that can intercept his
power. More particularly, the immutability of the divine will and counsel is
asserted repeatedly. Numbers 23:19: “The mighty God is not a man, that he
should lie, or a son of man, that he should repent. Has he himself said, and
will he not do it? Has he spoken, and will he not perform it?” Isaiah 46:10:
“My counsel will stand, and I will accomplish all my good pleasure.” Likewise
Psalm 33:11 and 110:4; 1 Samuel 15:29; Hebrews 6:17.

IV. Reason confirms it, because he is Jehovah and thus a necessary and
independent being who can be changed by no one. All created things are
changeable—“everything created is changeable” (πᾶν κτιστόν τρεπτόν), says
Damascene—because they receive their being from elsewhere. But God is
immutable because he is from himself and acknowledges no cause above himself.
2. Because he cannot change for the better, since he is the best, nor for the
worse, since he would cease to be the most perfect. Augustine, tractate 15 on
John: “Whatever is changed from better to worse and from worse to better is
not God, because supreme excellence cannot advance to something better, nor
can true eternity decline to something worse.” 3. Because all causes of change
are removed from God: dependence upon something prior, passive potency, error
of mind, and inconstancy of will.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

V. Creation brought change not to God but to creatures—not physical change
properly so called, which presupposes its changeable subject, but hyperphysical
change, through which the creature passes from nonbeing to being. An agent is
said to be changed when it is disposed differently in itself from how it was
disposed before. But what is disposed differently, not in itself but only
relatively and in relation to another, cannot be said to be changed. Thus when
God becomes Creator, he is not changed in himself, for nothing newly accrues
to him. From eternity he had the efficacious will to create the world in time,
but only

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 186 | IA leaf 240 | PDF p. 240]

in relation to the creature did a new relation accrue to him. And as for the
act of creating, since it is transitive rather than immanent, it is not so much
in God as from God.

VI. God was not changed through the incarnation, because “the Word (λόγος)
became flesh,” not by a conversion of the Word (τοῦ λόγου) into flesh, but by
the assumption of flesh into the hypostasis of the Word (λόγου).

VII. It is one thing to change the will and another to will the change of some
thing. God can will the change of various things, such as the institution and
abrogation of Levitical worship, while his will remains unchanged, because
from eternity he had already decreed such a change. Thus from eternity he made
a decree concerning the world—creating it and preserving it for a definite
time, but afterward destroying it by the flood. We must reason in the same way
about knowledge. God's knowledge is not changed along with the thing known,
because God, who knows it, not only knew that this change would occur but also
had decreed it.

VIII. It is one thing to be indifferent toward various objects and another to
be changeable. The cause of indifference is not mutability but freedom. God's
will could be indifferent before the decree, but after the decree it cannot be
changeable.

IX. The power to vary his acts is not a principle of mutability in himself,
but only in his objects, unless this is understood of variation in his own
internal acts. A perfect will does not vary those acts; only an imperfect will
does. For no one varies his decree unless he detects something unsuitable in
it, and that cannot befall God.

X. It is one thing to ask whether God could have determined himself toward
objects other than those he decreed before he had resolved anything about
them, and another to ask whether, once the decree was established, he could
have annulled it. We deny the latter but affirm the former. Nor is any
mutability thereby ascribed to God, because someone is finally said to change
when he begins to will what he did not will or to be unwilling to do what he
willed. This has no place in God.

XI. Repentance is attributed to God anthropopathically (ἀνθρωποπαθῶς), but
must be understood in a manner befitting God (θεοπρεπῶς): not with respect to
his counsel but to the event; not to the will itself but to the thing willed;
not to affection and inward grief but to effect and outward work, because he
does what a repentant man is accustomed to do. If in Genesis 6:6, 8 repentance
over the creation of man, which could not be made undone, is attributed to
God, it must be understood not passively (παθητικῶς) but actively
(ἐνεργητικῶς). For although what he had done could not be undone by noncreation,
it could nevertheless be changed by abolition.

XII. Promises and threats which are not fulfilled do not imply a change of
will, because they were conditional, not absolute, as is clear from Jeremiah
18:7. Although the condition is not always expressed, it is tacit and implicit
and must be understood.

XIII. When Hezekiah's death is foretold, this does not indicate what was going
to happen according to the will of God, but what would have happened according
to the course of secondary causes if God had not prevented it.

XIV. The necessity of immutability which we attribute to God does not imply
Stoic fate, because it is only an extrinsic necessity and a necessity upon the
hypothesis of the divine will, while the freedom and contingency of things
remain intact, as will be proved later when the decrees are treated.
