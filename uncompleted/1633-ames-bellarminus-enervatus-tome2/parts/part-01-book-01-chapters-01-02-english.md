TOME II. BOOK ONE.

ON COUNCILS.

CHAPTER I. On the convocation of a council.

Although this method is plainly Babylonian—or, as the Apostle speaks in
Ephesians 6:11, “a wile of the devil”—to treat first of the Pontiff as the head
of the Church, and of the councils in which churches are gathered, before
anything is taught about the Church, nevertheless the design of our work
requires us to follow Bellarmine’s footsteps, though he proceeds in a
disordered manner.

The first question is whether it properly belongs to the Roman Pontiff to
summon a general council, so that unless his authority is added, it is not a
council but a conventicle. Bellarmine affirms this in book 1, chapter 12; we
deny it.

Bellarmine’s arguments.

THESIS I.

Bellarmine 1. *A council of the Church is not legitimate unless it is gathered
in the name of Christ. This, however, appears to be nothing other than to be
gathered by Christ’s authority, that is, by the person who has from Christ the
authority to gather it.*

Protestants. I. For something to be done in Christ’s name, Christ’s authority
is certainly required as its efficient cause. But it is also required at the
same time that the matter, form, and end of the action agree with Christ and
his will, so that the entire nature of the action is such as Christ approves
and acknowledges as his own. The explanation by that name alone is therefore
imperfect and deceptive. Cardinal Cajetan himself, on Matthew 18, makes these
two observations against Bellarmine’s gloss: 1. *Christ did not say, “if all,”
lest it be understood only of the universal Church. He did not say, “if many,”
lest the statement appear restricted to the favor of large churches. But he
said, “if two,” so that it might be shown how great the power is even of the
smallest number of believers.* 2. *He does not say, “where two or three are
gathered,” or are of one mind, but adds, “in my name,” to distinguish this from
worldly agreement or even human friendship, which, although praiseworthy, does
not enjoy this privilege of the divine presence. For those whose life is
something other than Christ are not of one mind in the name of Jesus Christ.*
To be gathered in Christ’s name does not refer to the summoning of people to a
certain place. For people who blaspheme Christ’s name could in this way be
gathered in Christ’s name. II. What is added—that a gathering in Christ’s name
always depends—

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n221 | printed p. 4]

—on the authority of this or that person under Christ—is false. For two or
three, by mutual consent, are granted the ability to gather themselves in
this manner without the authority of any other person, Matthew 18:19–20. III.
There is no one person who has received ordinary authority from Christ to
gather people in his name from every part of the world into a single assembly.

2. Bellarmine. *When Christ ascended into heaven, he did not entrust Tiberius
with governing the Church, and consequently with gathering it, but Peter.*

Protestants. I. It was demonstrated in the preceding tome that the Church was
no more entrusted to Peter than to the other Apostles. II. The Church was
entrusted to Tiberius just as much as to the man who was Pontiff at Rome at
that time. III. Although the Church was not immediately entrusted to the faith
of Tiberius so that the government of the Church depended on him while he
remained an unbeliever, nevertheless God commanded him to have regard for the
faith and to apply his government in accordance with faith to assist the
Church. IV. If Tiberius had caused nearly all the bishops to assemble in one
place, whatever his intention had been, those gathered there could have shared
counsels among themselves and determined what served the common good, in the
manner of a general council, and done so in Christ’s name—just as Paul and
Silas were gathered in Christ’s name in prison to pray, Acts 16:25. V. Nothing
prevented Tiberius from being able to do in convening a Christian council what
Cyrus did in arranging the council and return of the Jews, Nehemiah 1:6. As
regards execution, Christian emperors and princes are deservedly granted more
power in ecclesiastical affairs than can or should be granted to Tiberius or
other unbelievers.

3. Bellarmine. *The Pope is the shepherd of emperors and kings. It does not
belong to a sheep to gather the shepherds, but to the shepherd to gather the
sheep.*

Protestants. I. The present-day Pope is an idol of a shepherd, not the
spiritual shepherd of anyone, much less of all kings and emperors. II. One who
is spiritually a sheep can temporally be the shepherd of the one whose sheep
he is, and govern him with others outwardly or politically, even in matters
pertaining to sacred things. David, Solomon, Hezekiah, and Josiah formerly
gathered their shepherds, the priests. What reason can be given why Christian
princes may not do the same with their own shepherds?

4. Bellarmine 2. *A general council must be summoned by the authority of the
one who can compel everyone. But the Pope alone, not the emperor, has everyone
so subject to him that he can compel them.*

Protestants. I. It is not always necessary for a council to be gathered—

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n222 | printed p. 5]

—by coercive authority. II. Where authority of this kind is required, it need
not belong to one person. III. By what means can the Pope compel those who
live in the dominion of unbelieving tyrants and are excluded from Christian
councils, as is the condition of the Greeks today? IV. Experience itself
might have taught Bellarmine that not even the subjects of Christian princes
can be compelled by the Pope at his discretion, as in the assembly of Trent
itself, which the French did not attend until it was already drawing to its
close.

5. Bellarmine 3. *Provincial councils are summoned by a metropolitan, national
councils by a primate or patriarch; therefore general councils are to be
summoned by the Pope.*

Protestants. I. “Metropolitan,” “primate,” and “patriarch” are names of human
creation, drawing somewhat upon the pattern of the world. They contain nothing
of divine right in themselves and can confer nothing upon the Pope. But the
question here concerns divine right and what is necessary for holding a
gathering *in the name of the Lord.* II. Although, for the sake of order, the
ordinary convocation of councils of this kind was formerly entrusted by other
bishops to the bishops of the chief cities, no one in those times was so
deluded as to judge that a legitimate council *in the name of the Lord* could
not be held in any other way.

6. Bellarmine 4. *By a most ancient canon it is established that councils
cannot be celebrated without the consent of the Roman Pontiff.*

Protestants. I. No canon of this kind is found in the canonical Scriptures.
II. Nor is anything of the sort found among the genuine witnesses of earliest
antiquity. III. The words of the canon itself contain nothing about convening
councils, but concern framing canons. IV. Whatever was granted in that matter
to the Roman bishops beyond others at that time was granted not by divine
right but by human counsel, not because of their power but because of the
constancy of that see when many others were wavering. It therefore does
absolutely nothing for degenerate popes, who claim this prerogative for
themselves as the summit of power by divine right.

7. Bellarmine 5. *In the Council of Chalcedon, action 1, Dioscorus is condemned
because he dared to hold a synod without the authority of the Apostolic See.
And for this reason the council he held at Constantinople was judged invalid
at the seventh general council.*

Protestants. I. Dioscorus was condemned for various very grave crimes, to which
this accusation was added by the legates of the Roman Pontiff, not by the
synod. II. “To hold a synod” does not there mean to convene a synod, if the
accusation is true. For the Council of Ephesus under discussion there was not
convened by

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n223 | printed p. 6]

Dioscorus, nor without the authority of the Roman bishop. The emperor Theodosius
summoned it at the urgent entreaty of Leo, bishop of Rome, as appears from his
Epistles 9 and 10. The “seventh council,” as it is called, is of no authority,
for it was the mother of idolatry. Yet nothing is said there about convening
the Council of Constantinople, but about cooperation which the council lacked
after it had been convened.

8. Bellarmine 6–7. *The same is proved from the Roman council, from the
testimony of ancient pontiffs, and so forth.*

Protestants. The testimonies are drawn from his own bosom; they concern
particular councils; they come from flattering or ambitious people.
Bellarmine could wrest nothing distinctive from the sacred writings to this
question except that one phrase about assembling *in the name of the Lord,*
which has already been shown to be irrelevant. He could produce nothing from
Augustine, Chrysostom, Jerome, Ambrose, Gregory, or any other ancient and
classic author. Thus this poverty of proofs sufficiently demonstrates that in
this matter the Pope dishonestly claims for himself a power which he cannot
show by any charter to have been entrusted to him.

9. Protestants. I. Emperors summoned the first general councils: Constantine
summoned Nicaea; Theodosius, Constantinople; the younger Theodosius, Ephesus;
Valentinian and Marcian, Chalcedon; and so afterward.

Bellarmine 1. *This argument is drawn from mere tradition and ecclesiastical
practice, without any testimony of Scripture. The adversaries deny that such
arguments are effective.*

Protestants. I. Although arguments drawn from tradition are ineffective in
themselves, they are most effective against the patrons of traditions. It is
certainly far too ridiculous for Bellarmine, who fights in this very question
almost solely with traditions, to condemn this kind of argument in the same
breath. II. This argument is drawn not from mere tradition but from known
custom and certain history. III. Although an argument drawn from custom and
practice has no force in a question of faith, it nevertheless has its force in
a question of practice. IV. There are sufficiently many testimonies and
examples in Scripture about the greatest assemblies of the Old Testament,
which were their councils, because they were customarily summoned by godly
kings, such as David, Solomon, Hezekiah, and Josiah.

10. Bellarmine 2. *Another custom, by which popes summoned various councils,
can be set against this custom.*

Protestants. I. The corrupt custom of later times, which arose from the
fragmentation and diminution of the Empire and from the ambition of the Pope,
cannot be set against primitive practice.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n224 | printed p. 7]

II. No truly ecumenical councils, or councils such as the first four, were
summoned by the Pope in the way in which the emperors summoned those first
councils.

11. Bellarmine 3. *There has been no general catholic council summoned by the
emperor alone, that is, without the consent and authority of the Roman
Pontiff.*

Protestants. I. We may much more truly affirm—and Bellarmine himself does not
deny it—that no general council, whether catholic or a “robber council,” such
as the Council of Trent was, has ever been summoned by the Pope alone,
that is, without the consent and authority either of emperors or of other
kings and princes. II. Consent and authority must not be confused here. It is
false that the Pope’s coercive authority intervened in convening the first
councils. But we do not deny that his consent, like that of the other chief
bishops, was joined to the authority of the emperors.

12. Bellarmine 4. *We read in the sixth synod, action 18, that Constantine and
Sylvester gathered the Council of Nicaea.*

Protestants. I. That sixth synod was held three hundred and more years after
Nicaea, and thus it should not be heard if it testified anything about the
history of the Council of Nicaea contrary to Eusebius, Athanasius, and the
acts of the council themselves, which together with all the historians of
that event consistently attribute its convocation to Constantine alone. II.
The sixth synod itself, in that same action 18 as elsewhere, acknowledges that
it was *gathered by an imperial decree.* And Pope Leo, in an epistle to the
emperor Constantine, says that that synod was *convened, by God’s direction,
through the emperor’s clemency.* It is therefore improbable that the synod
thought or pronounced anything against the emperor along with Bellarmine. III.
If such words were spoken in that action, they must be understood of
Sylvester’s consent, not his command.

13. Bellarmine 5. *Rufinus, book 10, chapter 1, says that Constantine summoned
that council according to the judgment of the priests.*

Protestants. I. That same Constantine also undertook various campaigns
according to the judgment of his soldiers, yet by his own authority, not
theirs. II. Those according to whose judgment he did this were priests, not
one high priest. III. The priests were in Egypt and lived near Constantine,
not at Rome. There is nothing papal here.

14. Bellarmine 6. *Constantinople I was convened by letters of Damasus sent by
the emperor to the bishops. Theodoret, book 5, chapter 9.*

Protestants. Whoever consults the passage as it reads in Greek will see the
contrary.

15. Bellarmine 7. *From Prosper and Evagrius it is evident that the Synod of
Ephesus was not assembled without the Pontiff’s consent.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n225 | printed p. 8]

Protestants. We readily grant this, although nothing of the sort about consent
preceding the summons can be proved from Prosper or Evagrius.

16. Bellarmine 8. *The point is evident concerning Chalcedon from the epistle
of Marcian and from those of the bishops of Moesia Secunda and of Gelasius.*

Protestants. I. Nothing appears from them that diminishes the emperor’s power
in convening that synod. II. In action 1 of the synod itself, *the synod* is
said to have been *held by the decree of the most pious emperors Valentinian
and Marcian.*

17. Bellarmine 9. *Councils at that time were summoned by emperors according to
the judgment and consent of the pontiffs, not by the Pontiff alone. 1. Because
frequent assemblies of people had not yet been prohibited without the
emperor’s authority. 2. Because a council could not be held except in an
imperial city. 3. Because councils were held at public expense. 4. Because at
that time the Pope subjected himself to the emperors in temporal matters.
Therefore, although he should only have sought the emperor’s assistance in
convening the synod, or asked him to permit the synod to be convened, he
nevertheless entreated him to command that the synod be convened.*

Protestants. I. In these final words Bellarmine, overcome by the evidence of
the truth, concedes to us the whole argument about which he has so far
contended in vain: *The Pontiff entreated the emperor to command that a synod
be convened.* And on that account he virtually accuses the Roman bishops of
excessive modesty: *he should only have requested, not entreated; that the
emperor permit, not command, the council to be convened.* II. Bellarmine here
incidentally concedes that the Roman bishops were subject to emperors in
temporal matters for six hundred years, even in relation to the spiritual good
which councils customarily regarded. This completely overturns what he
previously disputed concerning the Pope’s temporal power. III. Yet Bellarmine
speaks too timidly and sparingly when he says *they subjected themselves to
emperors,* since it is plain that they were subjected by God, Romans 13:1. IV.
He should have told us by what right the Roman bishops have now exempted
themselves from this rule, and have advanced to such pride that, conversely,
emperors and kings must now entreat the Pope to command that a council be
convened, and finally receive as a great favor such a little larva of a council
as the one at Trent. V. Those four fictitious reasons which Bellarmine gives
for the emperor’s authority having been invoked at that time are ridiculous.
1. They would have applied in provincial or national councils, which were
nevertheless in use even before any emperor was a Christian. II. As far as
divine right is concerned, they apply just as much now as they did formerly,
for no change has been made in it. Godly bishops would not have neglected for
so many centuries a right granted them by God, nor would godly emperors have
claimed something of this sort for themselves as though by hereditary

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n226 | printed p. 9]

right. III. The ancient Fathers or those Roman bishops nowhere transmitted
those reasons, but Bellarmine has at last invented them for their benefit. 4.
The true reason why universal councils were summoned by the emperor’s
authority was that the emperor alone, not the Pope, possessed universal power.
And this is the whole thing for which we contend in this question. We do not
say that by divine right this power of convening councils belongs to the
emperor alone, but to him or to those princes who have power throughout all
the provinces—power such as the emperor alone possessed at the time of the
first councils in those parts of the world where God’s churches especially
flourished.

Certain difficulties.

18. 1. What will happen if a council is necessary for the Church, and yet the
Pope is unwilling to summon it?

Bellarmine 1. *A case of this sort is scarcely possible, for it is not credible
that any Pontiff will be so evil. If he were, God would either change his mind
or remove him from the midst.*

Protestants. I. It is now not so much credible as palpable that for several
centuries a free council has been no less hateful and terrifying to the Roman
Pontiff than the tribunal of a severe judge is to criminals. The common
sentiment and judgment of all the popes is what Clement VII used to say: *A
council is useful so long as the Pontiff’s authority is the subject; but when
that authority is called into question, nothing is more dangerous. Thus the
only protection is to avoid and evade councils.* *History of the Council of
Trent,* book 1. Who will guarantee to us that *in such a case God will either
change the Pope’s mind or remove him from the midst?* On that condition, let
this power to convene a council belong to the mufti, the Mohammedans’ pope;
there will be no danger.

19. 2. What will happen if the Pope is a heretic, schismatic, captive, dead,
insane, or has resigned?

Bellarmine. *In that case an imperfect council may be gathered to provide the
Church with a head, either by the cardinals or by the bishops themselves
assembling in one place.*

Protestants. I. It is credible, then, that the Pope can become a heretic. Why
not also an enemy of a necessary council? II. Therefore, not only for heresy,
but also for schism—indeed, for captivity or insanity (and why not also for
crimes?)—the Pope can be deposed and another elected in his place. III. This
council must not be called imperfect if it must judge the Pope, that is, the
very perfection of the Roman Church. IV. It is tacitly conceded here that a
council can be gathered in the name of the Lord without the Pope’s authority,
and thus the only sport of an argument that Bellarmine could bring from
Scripture in this question is taken—

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n227 | printed p. 10]

—away. V. If bishops can assemble at a council by themselves, why can they not
also—and why should they not—do this by command of kings and emperors?

CHAPTER II. On those who vote in a general council.

The second question is whether in a general council only the greater
prelates—that is, bishops—ordinarily possess the right of a deciding vote,
while by privilege and custom cardinals, abbots, and generals of orders also
possess it, even though they are not bishops. Bellarmine affirms this; we deny
it.

Bellarmine’s arguments.

THESIS I.

Bellarmine. *To define in councils what must be believed or done is the proper
duty of pastors, for this is properly to feed: it is to teach, and to teach in
such a way that others are bound to believe. But bishops alone are pastors.*

Protestants. I. To define what must be believed and done with judicial
authority which of itself binds consciences is proper to God, the supreme
pastor of the Church. But to define by discerning and testifying concerning
that will of God belongs to pastors by their ordinary office, yet is not so
proper to them that on a just occasion it cannot also be done by others, not
only privately but publicly. II. Defining in general councils cannot be part
of the duty of pastors, because then no pastor of the primitive Church and
only a few in subsequent centuries could have fulfilled the pastoral duty.
*For in the first three hundred years there was no general assembly, and
afterward scarcely one in every hundred years.* These are Bellarmine’s words
in *On the Roman Pontiff,* book 1, chapter 8. III. Even if this were the proper
duty of pastors, Bellarmine teaches us that it can nevertheless be entrusted
to others and performed by them. In *On the Roman Pontiff,* book 3, chapter
24, he contends that it is sufficient if the Pope and bishops preach and
administer the sacraments through others. IV. Even if it were proper to
pastors and bishops, nothing nevertheless prevents it from being shared with
other theologians, especially ministers instituted by God, by the same right
by which Bellarmine wishes to extend it to cardinals, abbots, generals, and
such novel human creatures. V. If teaching is properly feeding, then at least
most popes do not properly feed the Church, because they do not teach. VI.
Diocesan bishops as such are not pastors instituted by Christ, much less the
only pastors. VII. Among the more learned papists this monopoly of

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n228 | printed p. 11]

bishop-satraps was formerly so disapproved that in the very definition of a
council they wished to guard especially against it. A synod, says Cardinal
Cusanus in *Catholic Concordance,* book 2, chapter 1, *is an assembly of elders
or presbyters tending along one way.*

2. Bellarmine. *The ancients testify everywhere that councils belong to
bishops.*

Protestants. That is, they belong chiefly to presbyters, and especially to
those who were eminent among them and first in rank.

3. Bellarmine. *The decrees of all councils celebrated thus far were made by
bishops alone.*

Protestants. I. Even if this were true, nothing would prevent similar decrees
from having been, and from being, made by others. II. Nothing could be
concluded from this concerning divine right. III. Yet what is affirmed is
false.

4. Bellarmine. *Bishops alone are ecclesiastical and public persons.*

Protestants. All presbyters are ecclesiastical and public persons. Moreover,
so are any who are delegated in the Church’s name to some public business.

5. Bellarmine. *All learned people cannot be admitted.*

Protestants. Between bishops alone and all learned people there is a third
possibility, namely those learned people—and especially presbyters—who are
delegated by churches.

6. Bellarmine. *If this were so, then one prince in whose territory the council
is held could easily gather all the presbyters and learned men of his province
and define whatever he wished.*

Protestants. I. We do not say that this is so, namely that everyone should be
admitted indiscriminately. Nor do we say that all who are admitted possess the
right to vote according to the number of individuals, but rather according to
the number of churches by which they are delegated. II. This very thing is the
Pope’s device for confirming the bishops’ monopoly, as Louis, cardinal of
Arles, observed long ago at the Council of Basel, according to Aeneas Sylvius,
book 1: *If bishops alone have the (deciding) vote, that will finally be done
which pleases the Italian nation, which by itself either surpasses or equals
all other nations in the number of bishops.* This was the stratagem by which
alone the Pope obtained many things among the Tridentine fathers themselves.
He sent there as many bishops as he wished, and the ones he wished—even from
among those whom he customarily creates *titular* bishops—so as to outnumber
those who wished in any measure to bring his unbridled license into order.
Paolo Sarpi, *History of the Council of Trent,* book 6, session 18.

7. Bellarmine. *There is no commonwealth which does not have some order in its
assemblies, so that not just anyone from the people but only princes and the
heads of the rest have a place and vote.*

Protestants. I. Bellarmine is beating the air here, for there can be some order
in councils although others besides bishops are present. Nor is the position
attacked here ours, namely that *anyone at all from the people should have a
vote.* II. Everyone knows—

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n229 | printed p. 12]

—that in the assemblies of most kingdoms and commonwealths, not only great
lords but any citizens delegated by cities and provinces have a place and
vote.

Our arguments.

8. Protestants. I. In that celebrated council in Acts 15, although the Apostles
themselves were present, the presbyters nevertheless had a place and vote with
others. *For Paul and Barnabas went up to the Apostles and presbyters, verse 2;
they were received by the Church and by the Apostles and presbyters, verse 4;
the Apostles and presbyters assembled to discern this matter, verse 6; the
Apostles, presbyters, and brothers wrote their common judgment and sent chosen
men to carry it to Antioch, verses 22 and 23.*

Bellarmine 1. *The Apostles participated as judges to define the matter; the
presbyters, to deliberate. The people were present without having been
summoned and consented, not by defining or disputing but only by hearing and
not protesting.*

Protestants. I. In matters of faith no judgment belongs to human beings except
one of inquiry, discernment, and deliberation. Therefore that entire
distinction between *those who define and those who deliberate* is futile. II.
The Apostles did not need the presbyters’ deliberation. If, therefore, they had
wished to define the matter by themselves, they would also have deliberated by
themselves alone. III. To deliberate well is a matter of greater virtue than
to define well from the counsel of others, although the latter belongs to
greater power. On this account the more perfect activity is assigned to the
presbyters, and the less perfect to the Apostles. IV. Bellarmine unjustly
accuses the Jerusalem believers of an irregular gathering, as though they had
intruded without a summons into a public and sacred assembly. V. His claim
that the text gives the people nothing beyond hearing and not protesting
plainly contradicts the text: *it seemed good to the Apostles and presbyters,
with the whole Church, to write and send—not to impose any burden.*

Bellarmine 2. *The custom of the Church in subsequent councils proves that
these things were so.*

Protestants. I. Custom should be proved from Scripture rather than Scripture
from custom. As Cyprian once said, *custom without truth is the antiquity of
error.* II. That custom must first be proved before it can prove anything
else.

Bellarmine 3. *Only the Apostles and presbyters were summoned, verse 6.*

Protestants. I. Nothing can be elicited from this against the presbyters having
deciding votes. II. It is possible that the Apostles first assembled with the
presbyters for the sake of preparation, or the entire assembly may,
synecdochically through them,

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n230 | printed p. 13]

be denoted there. It is certain that those in whose name the judgment was
delivered and the letter
written had been summoned.

Bellarmine 4. *Only Peter, Paul, Barnabas, and James are introduced as
speaking. None of the others expressed judgments.*

Protestants. I. By this argument, not only the people and presbyters but also
nine Apostles are deprived of the right to vote. II. A vote is cast not only
by speaking but also by consenting, or, as it were, by going over on foot to
another’s judgment.

9. Protestants 2. A general council represents the universal Church;
therefore people of every kind ought to be present.

Bellarmine. *The best manner of representation is that in which peoples are
represented through princes.*

Protestants. I. By this device, then, the Pope alone, who calls himself prince
of the whole Church, can represent everyone and define everything without a
council—and in the best manner. II. Bishops are not princes or lords of the
churches by any other right than that by which they occupy Christ’s place.
III. The best manner of representing a free society is one which arises from
freedom of election.

10. Protestants 3. A common cause must be assisted by common votes, and the
cause of faith is common.

Bellarmine. *The cause of faith belongs to prelates as teachers, and to the
rest as disciples.*

Protestants. I. Papal bishops are not the only teachers, nor are they chiefly
teachers. II. Disciples who are also furnished with suitable gifts can teach
as occasion arises. 1 Corinthians 14:31: *You can all prophesy one by one.*

11. Protestants 4. The judgment of one person, even a layman, which is better
supported by the authority of Scripture, ought to be preferred—and has often
been preferred—to the judgment of a whole council. Panormitanus and Gerson are
authorities for this. Many laypeople are also more learned than bishops.

Bellarmine 1. *In discussion, the judgment of a private person can be
preferred, because learning, not the person’s authority, holds first place
there; but by no means after the definition of a legitimate council.*

Protestants. I. The judgment which must be preferred in discussion must also
be preferred in the definition and after the definition. For just as the
conclusion follows from the premises, so the definition follows from the
discussion. Truth is also one and of one and the same value, whether it is
regarded after or before human decisions. II. A judgment supported by
Scripture has the same authority as Scripture itself. III. Bellarmine here
reveals that all papal councils are merely human when he affirms that human
persons hold first place in their definitions.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n231 | printed p. 14]

Bellarmine 2. *The statement of Panormitanus and Gerson is conditional; it is
not permissible to infer from it that councils can err.*

Protestants. On the contrary, this was assumed absolutely at that time: not
only that councils could err, but also that they had in fact erred. Thus
Cardinal Cusanus, *Catholic Concordance,* book 2, chapter 5: *It must be noted
as true by the experience of affairs that a plenary universal council can
fail, just as there have been various such councils which erred in judging.*

Bellarmine 3. *Bishops possess the authority to judge, not because they are
learned but because they are public persons.*

Protestants. I. The faculty or power of judging comes from learning; public
authority is added by public delegation. Therefore judgment, as regards both
faculty and authority, can belong to learned men who are not bishops. II. But
bishops who are not learned lack the faculty and ability to judge. Authority
is therefore communicated to them in vain. Learning without public authority
has far more value for judgment than such authority without learning.

12. Protestants 5. In the primitive Church many causes of faith were debated
before the people as judge.

Bellarmine. *A private judgment was given to the people, not a public one, and
this because it could not be done otherwise.*

Protestants. I. Nor can it ever happen otherwise that the people should assent
in true faith to some judgment without any judgment of their own. II. The
judgment of the people collectively is what some call *popular,* and therefore
public, not private.
