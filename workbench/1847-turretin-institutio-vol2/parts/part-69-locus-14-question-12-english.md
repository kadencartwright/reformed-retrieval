QUESTION XII.

THE PERFECTION OF SATISFACTION

*Was Christ’s satisfaction so perfect that after it there can be no place
either for human satisfactions in this life or for purgatory after this life?
Affirmative, against the Roman Catholics.*

I. Just as in the preceding question our dispute was with the Socinians, so in
this one we must deal with the Roman Catholics. Although they verbally assert
the unity and perfection of Christ’s satisfaction and loudly complain that a
grave injury is done to them when they are charged with saying that “something
was lacking in Christ’s sufferings, by which he expiated our sins, so that his
satisfaction could not be full and perfect,” as Bellarmine speaks in *On
Indulgences*, book 2, chapter 14, nevertheless in fact they undermine and
overthrow it in more than one way. They maintain that it must be restricted to
prebaptismal sins and to the guilt of fault but not punishment, or to eternal
but not temporal punishment, as will now be said.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. It must be observed in advance that one satisfaction is *furnished to
God*, another *to men*. The latter is either *private*, called *reparative*,
and owed to injured and offended neighbors, or *public*, called *canonical*,
because it was contained in certain canons and rules of the ancient church and
was very frequently imposed in the external forum for correcting sinners and
repairing scandal. The former is properly said of Christ’s λύτρῳ, by which he
as our surety satisfied for us, but abusively attributed to satisfactory works
by which men are imagined to satisfy for their own or others’ sins. Here we do
not treat the latter, but the satisfaction to be furnished to God for
satisfying justice and expiating sin. For the question is not whether Christ’s
satisfaction excludes all human satisfactions, whether private or public and

[Printed p. 386 | Internet Archive PDF 414 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

canonical, which are imposed upon sinners for their own correction and the
repair of scandal given to the church. We judge that this was rightly practiced
in the ancient church and can laudably be observed. Rather, given Christ’s
satisfaction, can or should other satisfactions to be furnished to God for
sins be imposed upon believers? We deny this; the adversaries affirm it.

III. The question is not whether Christ’s satisfaction excludes believers’
chastisements, which are called *medicinal punishments* and improper, such as
the discipline of children in their father’s house. We readily grant this.
Rather, does it exclude their satisfactions, or *penal sufferings*, which are
not directed merely to healing or a trial of piety, but to the satisfaction of
avenging justice, and which are imposed by a judgment of God that is not
*domestic* and paternal, exercised by the head of a household toward his
children according to the rules of love and wisdom, but *forensic*, decreed by
a judge against criminals according to the prescription of laws? This is the
adversaries’ doctrine which we oppose. They maintain that although Christ’s
satisfaction was infinite, it was not so full and sufficient that various
satisfactions are not still required from believers—if not for fault or
eternal punishment, which they acknowledge Christ removed, at least for
temporal punishment. The Council of Trent, Session 14, chapter 8, canon 13,
says: “If anyone says that, as to temporal punishment, satisfaction is by no
means made to God for sins through Christ’s merit by punishments inflicted by
him and patiently borne, or enjoined by a priest, nor yet by those voluntarily
undertaken, such as prayers, fasts, alms, or other exercises of piety, and so
that the best repentance is only a new life, let him be anathema.”

IV. The Remonstrants also undermine the perfection of this satisfaction to no
small degree. Although they have not yet reached the boldness of wholly
rejecting satisfaction with the Socinians, they nevertheless seem with all
their strength to diminish and depreciate its force and fullness. They
maintain that satisfaction was admitted by God not because of its inherent
worth but by acceptilation, so that it was not true satisfaction but only an
imaginary payment. Their meaning is that God acquiesced after Christ died, not
because he had actually been satisfied, but because it pleased God to be
gracious and to admit Christ’s satisfaction as full.

V. We, however, judge Christ’s satisfaction to be so perfect and sufficient
that by the one offering of himself he most fully satisfied for all our sins,
not only for fault but also for both temporal and eternal punishment. Thus no
propitiatory sacrifice remains afterward to be offered again, nor any
satisfactions to be paid either in this life or after it, although believers
are often exposed to God’s chastisements to promote their repentance and
holiness.

VI. This perfection must be regarded as consisting in its adequacy to God’s
justice revealed in the Word and to the law’s demand, and in its corresponding
to the demerits and necessities of those for whom it was given—not by the
recipient’s gracious acceptilation, for then the legal victims could also have
expiated sins contrary to Hebrews 10:4, but by the sufficiency of its merit and
the worthiness of the work. 1. With respect to its *parts*, because he most
fully fulfilled everything owed by us to the law: both as to obedience of life
and the suffering of death; as to *satisfactory power*, by which through
bearing the punishments owed by us he delivered us from liability to death and
the curse; and as to *meritorious power*, by which he reconciled the Father to
us and acquired the right to life for us. 2. With respect to its *degrees*,
because he not only did and suffered everything the law demanded, but did so
fully and perfectly as to mode and degree that nothing could be desired in it,
because of the dignity of the person making satisfaction and the severity of
the punishment endured. Hence follows the third σχέσις of this perfection,
with respect to its *effects*

[Printed p. 387 | Internet Archive PDF 415 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

which are regarded: with respect to *God*, in perfect *reconciliation* with
God (Rom. 5:10; 2 Cor. 5:18); with respect to *sin*, in perfect *expiation and
remission* (Eph. 1:7; Heb. 1:3; 9:26); and with respect to the *faithful*, in
their full *completion and redemption* (Heb. 9:12; 10:14), both as to
deliverance from death and acquisition of the right to life.

[Marginal heading: The perfection of satisfaction is proved: 1. From the dignity of the satisfaction;]

VII. Many things prove that this satisfaction possesses such perfection.
First, *the dignity of the person making satisfaction*. Since that person was
not only holy and most pure, but also truly divine, in whom all fullness
dwelled (Col. 1:19)—fullness of deity, fullness of office, fullness of merit
and graces—it cannot be doubted that the satisfaction furnished by him was of
infinite value and price, and thus altogether sufficient and full, since
nothing can be added to the infinite. For although the human nature, which
acts in the manner of the principle by which, is finite, the satisfaction
itself does not cease to be infinite in relation to the person, who is here the
principle which acts and to whom it must be attributed, because actions and
sufferings belong to supposits.

[Marginal heading: 2. From its unity;]

VIII. Second, this is confirmed by *the unity of the offering made by Christ*.
For why would Paul so often assert in Hebrews 7, 9, and 10 that Christ offered
himself once for us and that by that one offering those who are sanctified are
perfected forever? Why would he set before us Christ’s one obedience by which
we are constituted righteous (Rom. 5), unless it was full and perfect? For as
repetition supposes imperfection, so unity on the contrary necessarily implies
the thing’s fullness and perfection.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the judge’s approval;]

IX. Third, *the approval of God the judge* establishes the same. For if God has
testified by some proof that he was most fully satisfied, no one can deny that
the satisfaction was most perfect, since only this is required: that it be
approved and accepted by the supreme judge who demands it. That it was
approved and accepted by God is evident not only from Christ’s appointment to
the mediatorial office, to whom the Father more than once bore witness from
heaven that he was his beloved Son in whom he fully acquiesced, but especially
from his *resurrection* from the dead. This was not only an undoubted argument
of his deity (Rom. 1:4), but also the surest pledge and proof of his most
perfect satisfaction. Is it credible that God the judge and his inexorable
justice, which demands full payment, would have delivered him and raised him
to that supreme glory to which he was exalted as the reward of his perfect
obedience (Phil. 2:9)? As a creditor does not release a surety from prison
unless full payment has been furnished, so Christ could not have been released
unless he had satisfied in every respect. Therefore, since he rose so
gloriously and was raised by the Father himself, no further room remains to
doubt the perfection of the satisfaction and the payment of the fullest
λύτρου, for which the Father gave us so authentic a receipt.

[Marginal heading: 4. From the effects.]

X. Fourth, the *effects following satisfaction* proclaim its sufficiency. For
why would reconciliation of God with us and appeasement of his wrath,
expiation and remission of sins—not partial but full and entire—or the
redemption and perfection of believers themselves be attributed to Christ’s
death and obedience, as is often done in Scripture (Col. 1:20; 2 Cor. 5:21; 1
John 1:7; Rom. 3:24; 5:10; Heb. 1:3; 9:14; 10:14), if his satisfaction was not
full and perfect? A perfect effect necessarily supposes a perfect cause.

XI. Once this perfect satisfaction is established, whatever the Roman
Catholics build upon it collapses of itself, whether through the sacrifice of
the Mass, through human merits and satisfactions to be furnished in this life,
or through the punishments of purgatory to be borne after this life. Such
things cannot be drafted as reinforcements for Christ’s merit and satisfaction
without either accusing the latter of insufficiency

[Printed p. 388 | Internet Archive PDF 416 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

or God of injustice for wishing to exact a double price for the same thing and
a double punishment for the same fault. Hence whatever the Roman Catholics
may say about the infinity and sufficiency of Christ’s satisfaction is in fact
overthrown by this invention of theirs.

XII. The adversaries reply in vain: 1. “Our satisfactions do not detract from
the infinity and satisfaction of Christ; indeed, greater glory accrues to him
from them, since they borrow their force and efficacy from nowhere but
Christ’s satisfaction, who not only satisfies for us but also gives us power
to satisfy. Thus they are not associated causes of the same order but
subalternate and subordinate.” For what had to be proved is assumed without
proof: that Christ gives us strength to satisfy, when this is unheard of in
Scripture and contrary to the nature of the thing. It is one thing to satisfy,
another to give strength for satisfying. Indeed, these are ἀσύστατα, for if he
fully satisfied for us, why are other satisfactions sought? No association or
subordination of causes can be admitted here, where the primary cause stands
alone and admits no others. So far is this from promoting Christ’s greater
glory that associates or subalternate causes are added to him, not without
signal insult, in an office belonging uniquely to him, that he may be our
perfect Redeemer. Nor, if believers are συνεργοὶ of God in the application of
salvation, to which they must contribute something, can they therefore
cooperate with him in its acquisition, to which they can contribute nothing.

XIII. 2. They cannot escape any better by distinguishing “the guilt of fault
from punishment, and that Christ fully satisfied for fault but not immediately
for punishment, or for eternal punishment but not temporal punishment, which
remains for believers to pay either in this life or the next.” For besides the
fact that Scripture attributes the full and entire remission of all sins to
Christ’s satisfaction, through which there is no longer any condemnation for
them (Rom. 8:1) and they are fully perfected, the distinction is ἀσύστατος.
Punishment and fault are so joined that when fault as the cause is removed,
punishment as fault’s effect must necessarily also be removed. Nor is remission
of fault anything other than immunity from all punishment, because punishment
could only be unjustly inflicted where no fault any longer occurs, just as it
would be unjust to exact payment of a debt already paid or remitted.

XIV. 3. They maintain that “Christ also satisfied for temporal punishment,
not immediately but mediately, insofar as he satisfies in and through us.” But
1. These things are rashly said without any scriptural authority. 2. They are
said dangerously, because thus men are associated with Christ in satisfaction,
so that he is no longer entirely our Redeemer, since redeeming and satisfying
are equivalent, as he redeems only by satisfying. 3. They are said falsely and
contrary to Scripture, which asserts that Christ satisfied once through
himself, not that he still satisfies daily through others.

XV. Further, this perfection of satisfaction which we assert also destroys
the invention of acceptilation devised by the Remonstrants. For where perfect
payment is given, there can be no place for acceptilation, because in payment
grace is not regarded, but fullness of satisfaction. This arises not only from
the dignity of the payer but also from the equality and value of the price
paid. This is further confirmed because Christ is said to have come to fulfill
the δικαίωμα, or *right of the law* (Rom. 8:3–4), namely, by doing what we
ourselves could not furnish because of the law’s impotence. Now if through
Christ’s satisfaction the right of the law was fulfilled in us, it had to be
adequate to the law’s demand. For if by gracious acceptilation it had only been
accepted by God, not the right of the law but the right of grace would have
been fulfilled in us.

XVI. Further, acceptilation cannot be given because Christ received upon
himself every punishment owed by us (Isa. 53:6–8), including the greatest

[Printed p. 389 | Internet Archive PDF 417 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and most grievous of all, the law’s own κατάραν (Gal. 3:13). Finally, if God
could have accepted any satisfaction whatever, there would have been no need
for God’s Son to present himself as surety and be subjected to such torments
and so dreadful a death, since satisfaction could have been made to God
through any other man.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XVII. It is one thing *to suffer for the church* to redeem it by the price of
satisfaction, another to edify, console, and confirm it by an example of
constancy and obedience. Paul says that he suffers for the church, or for
Christ’s body (Col. 1:24), not in the first sense, in which elsewhere he denies
that anyone but Christ alone was crucified for us (1 Cor. 1:13), but in the
second, as he himself interprets it in 2 Corinthians 1:6, ὑπὲρ τῆς ὑμῶν
παρακλήσεως. And in 2 Timothy 2:10 he says that he endures everything *for the
sake of the elect*, not to redeem them from temporal punishments, but so that,
confirmed by his example, they may obtain salvation through Christ. Thomas
rightly observes this in Part 3, question 48, article 5, reply 1: “The
sufferings of the saints profit the church, not by way of redemption but by
way of example and exhortation.”

XVIII. The *sufferings of Christ* whose remainder Paul had to ἀνταναπληροῦν,
*fill up in turn* (Col. 1:24), are not the personal sufferings of the natural
Christ, but the sufferings of the *mystical Christ*, that is, of his body or
the church. They are imposed after Christ’s pattern to be borne by each member
for his own part, and those assigned particularly to Paul by God he therefore
says that he in turn, or for his own part, fills up, cheerfully bearing the
part of the cross which Christ had imposed upon him to bear. Thus *Christ* is
often put for his body (Acts 9:4; 1 Cor. 12:12), and the *sufferings of Christ*
denote believers’ afflictions (2 Cor. 1:5; Heb. 11:26): not only by reason of
the *principle*, because Christ as supreme director of the contest imposes
them and calls us to them (Acts 9:16; 1 Pet. 2:21; Phil. 1:29), nor only by
reason of the *object*, because they are imposed on account of Christ and his
gospel, but also by reason of the *communion* we have with Christ. For as we
become one with Christ, so our good and evil things are common to us: Christ
is affected by our evils no less than by his own, and we are called to
participate in his sufferings, that we may be conformed to him in the cross
before we are conformed in glory (Rom. 8:18).

XIX. It is one thing to redeem one’s sins by true satisfaction, another to
break off their course by works of repentance and love. Daniel urges the
latter upon Nebuchadnezzar in chapter 4:27, not the former. The τὸ פרק in the
first place does not mean *to redeem*, but *to break off, draw away*, and hence
*to deliver*. Thus he counsels the king to change his life and repent, that by
beneficence he may repair the injuries and oppressions he inflicted and break
off the course of sins, so that it may go better for him and he may obtain
exemption from the punishments threatening him for his crimes and the
continuance of his peace: “if perhaps there will be a prolonging of your
tranquility.” Here must be referred the various passages in which remission of
sins is promised to *repentance*, not as a meritorious cause or satisfactory
punishment, but as an *annexed condition*.

XX. It is one thing to speak of punishments properly so called and
satisfactory, inflicted by a judge to satisfy justice, another of improper,
chastening, or medicinal punishments. We do not deny that these punishments are
often imposed upon believers—not by God the judge, but by God the Father; not
from hatred, but from love; not for vengeance, but for healing; not for
destruction, but for correction. Hence Cyprian says, “The Lord rebukes in order
to amend; he amends in order to preserve.” Thomas says in Part 3, question 69,
“Before remission are the torments of sinners; after remission, the contests
and exercises of saints.” And Augustine best explains the distinction between
the punishments of the wicked and chastisements of the pious in *City of God*,
book 1, chapter 8: “When good and evil alike are afflicted, they are not
therefore not distinct because what they have both suffered is not distinct.
There remains

[Printed p. 390 | Internet Archive PDF 418 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

a dissimilarity of the sufferers even in the similarity of sufferings, and
although under the same torment, virtue and vice are not the same. Under one
fire gold glows and straw smokes; under the same threshing sledge straw is
broken and grain cleansed; dregs are not confounded with oil because they are
pressed out by the same weight of the press. So one and the same force assails
the good to test, purify, and refine them, but the evil to devastate, condemn,
and destroy them. Hence in the same affliction the evil curse and blaspheme
God, but the good pray and praise. What matters is not what one suffers, but
what sort of person suffers.”

XXI. Such chastisements sometimes retain the name “punishments,” but
improperly. 1. Because by their nature punishments were established by the
supreme judge for wretched mortals on account of the violated law. Thus even
when the believers’ state is changed, the old name is retained, because though
they are not formally the same, they are materially. 2. Because there is a
manifold similarity between them and punishments properly so called. First,
they are no less grievous and troublesome to the flesh which must be destroyed
than proper punishments. Second, they are sent upon believers not rashly, but
by God’s definite good will and dispensation, just as punishments are inflicted
at a judge’s discretion. Third, they are inflicted on the occasion of sin.
Fourth, they are so called from believers’ sense, since they often apprehend
God as an angry judge. Fifth, both kinds are set forth as an example. But the
distinction always remains: in proper punishments, satisfaction of justice is
intended as the end by God the judge; in chastisements, the sinner’s correction
by God the Father.

XXII. The death of David’s little son inflicted upon him after his sin was
remitted (2 Sam. 12:14) was a paternal and medicinal punishment, not a
satisfactory and judicial one, because once the sin was remitted, no
punishment could remain to be borne. Nor is the latter gathered from the fact
that it is said to have been sent by God because through his sin David caused
the enemies to blaspheme the Lord’s name. For this denotes the occasion and
impelling cause, but not the final cause; or if a final cause is implied, it is
not the satisfaction of justice, but the repair of scandal given, because it
was fitting that the discipline of the divine house, violated by so shameful a
crime, be confirmed by some example. Nor is it gathered from the fact that he
deprecates it with great earnestness and tears. For such is the disposition of
our nature that it flees whatever is hard and troublesome and tries to remove
it, as a sick person deprecates biting powders, the discomfort of cautery, and
the bitterness of medicine, although they are anything but punishments.

XXIII. Although death cannot be inflicted upon believers as a precaution for
the future, or for amendment, it does not follow that it is punishment
properly so called, imposed for satisfaction. There are several other causes:
that the remnants of sin in us may be abolished and thus we may pass from the
animal and earthly state to the heavenly and spiritual; that the exercise of
piety and the manifestation of Christian virtues may thus shine forth; and
that the necessity of dying may be the most powerful incitement for everyone
to amend his life.

XXIV. The *judgment* which Peter says *begins at the house of God* (1 Pet.
4:17) is not the legal judgment of vengeance proceeding from an angry judge,
but the paternal and evangelical judgment of chastisement. It is not for
punishment and destruction, but for example and correction, so that when we
are thus corrected by the Lord, we may not be condemned with the world, as
Paul speaks (1 Cor. 11:32). The *vengeance* of which Paul speaks (2 Cor. 7:11)
is not punishment properly so called from God the judge, but either denotes
the canonical punishment of excommunication, imposed by the church for the
repair of scandal and for an example, or rather denotes the exercise of
repentance and contrition, by which the sinful man is angry with himself and
takes vengeance upon himself for the crime committed.

XXV. Although those whose sins had been remitted in the Old Testament still
had to offer sacrifices for them, it does not follow that satisfaction can
have place after the remission of sin. For the sacrifices were not
satisfaction properly

[Printed p. 391 | Internet Archive PDF 419 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

so called, but types of another’s future satisfaction through Christ, by whose
power that remission was given.

XXVI. When it is said in Proverbs 16:6 that “by mercy and truth sin is redeemed
or expiated,” this does not favor human satisfactions, whether it is understood
of *God’s mercy and truth*, as some wish, because this does not remove but
establishes Christ’s satisfaction, or of *man’s mercy and truth*, as others
wish, because this denotes the condition to which remission of sin is given,
not the cause for which it is given. For as judgment without mercy must be
exercised upon the unmerciful, so the merciful will obtain mercy (Matt. 5:7).
Nor does כפר here denote expiation properly so called, but either mere covering
and remission, which God bestows upon true, merciful believers, or removal and
taking away, as elsewhere in Isaiah 28:18, because the exercise of love and
sincere piety removes and takes away the contrary vices. The following words
confirm this when they add, “in the fear of God is departure from evil.”

XXVII. Although nothing defiled can enter the kingdom of heaven, satisfaction
after Christ in this life or purgatory in the next to wash away stains is not
therefore needed, because at the moment of the soul’s separation from the body,
believers are perfectly purified from the remnants of sin by Christ’s Spirit.

The remaining arguments commonly brought forward either for human
satisfactions or for purgatory will be examined in their proper place.
