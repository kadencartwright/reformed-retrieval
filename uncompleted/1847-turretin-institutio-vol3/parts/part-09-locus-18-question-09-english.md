QUESTION NINE

ON THE SPLENDOR OF THE CHURCH

Must the church exist in perpetual splendor and eminence? Or can it sometimes
be so obscured and diminished that no assembly of it publicly appears on
earth? We deny the former and affirm the latter against the papists.

I. As the preceding question asserted the church’s perpetuity and
indefectibility, so this one will explain the manner of that perpetuity, which
is not always joined with splendor and eminence but often with obscurity.
Although this controversy properly belongs to the outward state of the church,
nevertheless, because it has a close affinity with the preceding question, we
treat it here.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 42 | IA leaf 76 | PDF p. 76]

[Marginalia: State of the question.]

II. For the state of the question, note, 1. that the subject is not the
invisible catholic church, which is the whole body of believers dispersed
throughout the entire world, but any visible particular church in which there
are pastors and sheep and, as Bellarmine says, “prelates and subjects.” Nor is
the subject determinately this or that church, but specifically every
particular church without any exception, whether each is considered
separately or all are considered together.

III. 2. The question does not concern inward splendor, which consists in
faith, hope, love, and the inward gifts of the Holy Spirit. We acknowledge
this to be essential to the church and perpetual. Rather, it concerns outward
splendor, which consists in a great number of people, tranquility from
persecutions, the clarity and purity of doctrine, and a distinguished and
uncorrupted ministry of the word. Since this splendor is accidental, we judge
that it can be absent and is not rarely wanting in the church.

IV. 3. The question is not whether this splendor has often prevailed in the
church and can still prevail. We readily grant that, and it is demonstrated by
various examples in both the Old and the New Testaments. Rather, it is whether
that splendor must be perpetual and constant, so that the church is always
established in such splendor that it can be hidden from no one but is exposed
to everyone’s eyes, or whether it is sometimes so obscured and corrupted that
it is withdrawn from human eyes and does not appear. We maintain the latter,
but our opponents deny it.

4. The question does not concern the church’s essential invisibility, which
flows from its inward form and cannot be separated from it, as was seen in
Question VII. It concerns accidental invisibility, which regards the outward
form: must that form always be so conspicuous that it is visible to everyone,
or is it sometimes so covered and corrupted that it cannot be observed?

V. The papists maintain that the church not only cannot fail so as to exist no
longer, but also cannot be obscured or diminished. They say there always
remains an assembled multitude in which there are prelates and subjects,
especially the Roman pontiff, who stands eminent in majesty as the head of the
catholic church, as Bellarmine says in book 3, chapter 13. Thus they wish not
only that the church always exists in the world, but also insist that it is
visible and conspicuous, perpetually recognized and seen no less than other
assemblies and societies of people and the empires of the world. They maintain
that this is none other than the Roman church, to whose see they claim that
God has affixed the true church.

VI. We, however, hold as follows. As was seen above, the church can never fail
utterly on earth, but it is not always conspicuous and illustrious. It is often
so obscured and overwhelmed, whether through persecutions or errors, that no
illustrious assembly of it appears in the world, but it remains obscure and is
withdrawn from human eyes.

Just as Christ, the head of the church, experienced a twofold state—one of
emptying, in which his majesty lay hidden beneath the veil of flesh and the
weight of the cross, and another of exaltation, in which his glory shines with
full light—so the church, which is his mystical body, passes through different
states. At one time it is in a state of humiliation and obscurity, when it is
oppressed by the violence of persecutors, the cunning of heretics, or the
frequency of scandals and is compelled to hide, like “a dove dwelling in the
clefts of the rock and in the hiding place of the precipice” (Song of Songs
2:14). At another time it is in a state of splendor and clarity, when it enjoys
the tranquil administration of sacred things and the splendor of an
uncorrupted ministry, and flashes and shines through the public exercise of
divine worship.

In this the church best resembles the moon. For just as the moon undergoes
various phases, increases, and decreases, at times shining with full light and
at others suffering waning and eclipses, so the church experiences various
vicissitudes and has different times of light and obscurity, according as it
is more or less illuminated by its sun and according to the adversity or
prosperity of the times. At one time it shines through the public and free
exercise of divine worship and the ministry. At another it is obscured through
the darkness of prevailing heresies and through the severity of persecutions,
by which it is scattered and dispersed and ceases to assemble and to be seen.
Yet even then it does not cease to utter groaning and lamentation from the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 43 | IA leaf 77 | PDF p. 77]

clefts of the rocks, or to strive to make itself conspicuous in the world by
its blood and martyrdoms. To this belong these words of Ambrose, book 4,
chapter 8 of the *Hexaemeron*: “Like the moon, the church has frequent wanings
and risings, but it has grown by its wanings and merited increase by them; it
is diminished by persecutions and crowned by the confessions of martyrs.”

[Marginalia: It is proved that the church is often obscured. 1. From the
condition of the church of the Old Testament.]

VII. Our position is confirmed by various arguments. The first is drawn from
the condition of the church under the Old Testament, under which it is evident
that it was not rarely obscured and deprived of all splendor. This can easily
be demonstrated through its various periods.

Before the flood, who could deny that it was without splendor, when all flesh
had corrupted its way (Genesis 6), and during the flood, when it was reduced
to eight souls and enclosed in the ark? In Abraham’s time before his calling
from Ur of the Chaldeans, it lay hidden in his father’s family, which was given
to idolatry (Joshua 24:1–2). What splendor did the church possess in Egypt,
where it was captive for so long without any form either of commonwealth or of
sacred ministry? What splendor did it possess under the judges, when after
Joshua’s death the Israelites forsook the God of their fathers and went after
other gods (Judges 2:7; 3:8, 12)? Speaking of those times, Azariah says that
“many days passed in which the Israelites were without the true God, without
a teaching priest, and without the law” (2 Chronicles 15:3).

What appearance and what splendor did the church possess in Elijah’s time (1
Kings 19), when he thought himself left alone to worship God, while God
comforted him by the fact that he had preserved seven thousand faithful
people, known to himself alone, who had not bowed the knee to Baal? What
splendor did the Jewish church possess under Ahaz, Manasseh, Amon, and other
impious kings? Under them the sacrifice was interrupted by law, the doors of
the temple were closed, an altar patterned after the one in Damascus was built
by Uriah the chief priest, and idolatry was introduced everywhere, as is
related in 2 Kings 16:11–12, 14, etc., and 2 Chronicles 28:3–4, 24–25.

And if we descend to the Babylonian captivity, where was the church’s
splendor after the city was leveled to the ground, the temple polluted, the
sacred vessels carried away, the sacrifices abolished, the worship of God
interrupted—which could be performed only at Jerusalem—and the people reduced
to the most grievous slavery? Hence the godly lament most bitterly that their
prophets and all signs had been taken from them (Psalm 74:9).

Finally, what appearance and eminence could the church possess under the most
terrible persecutions of Antiochus and his successors, which are mentioned in
the books of the Maccabees and in Josephus, book 1 of *The Jewish War*? Of
that time Paul says, “The faithful were stoned, slain by the sword, destitute,
afflicted, and wandered in sheepskins and goatskins, in deserts and mountains
and caves of the earth” (Hebrews 2:37–38).

VIII. Our opponents object here in vain, 1. that “the synagogue and the church
differ; the synagogue was to cease, but the church has the promise of its
firmness and stability, which the synagogue did not have. The synagogue was
not the catholic church as the Christian church is, and outside it there were
various faithful people, such as Melchizedek, Job, the centurion, Cornelius,
etc.”

For although there is a manifold difference between the church of the Old and
New Testaments with respect to the manner of administration, none appears with
respect to the thing itself. In each there was a true church of God, which had
the same faith and the same hope and had to agree in essentials. Therefore, if
splendor is an essential property of the church that belongs to it always and
everywhere, it had to prevail no less in the Old Testament than in the New.
Hence elsewhere the Holy Spirit presents the ancient church as a type of the
Christian church (Romans 11; 1 Corinthians 10).

2. The synagogue was indeed to cease after Christ, but according to the sacred
oracles it had to endure until his coming and therefore to retain its splendor,
if that is the lot of the true church. The preceding examples demonstrate more
clearly than the light of midday that this did not happen.

3. The Jewish church was not catholic in precisely the same sense as the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 44 | IA leaf 78 | PDF p. 78]

Christian church diffused throughout the entire world. Nevertheless, it can
rightly be called catholic because in its totality it contained the whole body
of called believers, and outside it there was no other visible assembly of the
faithful (Psalm 147:20). Nowhere else was there a temple, priesthood, or public
exercise of divine worship. If any among the nations were converted to God,
they were members of that church, if not by place and outward society, as
happened with proselytes, then by the desire of the heart and inward
communion. As for Job and Melchizedek, since it is evident that they lived
before the constitution of the Israelite church, they do not belong here.

IX. They answer the example of Elijah by saying, “Elijah does not speak of the
Jewish church, in which the excellent kings Asa and Jehoshaphat reigned in his
time, but of the Israelite church, which was subject to the king of Samaria.”

But, 1. whatever may be true of the Jewish church, it is certain from this
example that in Israel—that is, among those Jews who lived under the rule of
Ahab and Jezebel—there were seven thousand people who constituted the true
church, but one so invisible and hidden that not even Elijah knew it. Thus the
true church is not always illustrious and conspicuous, but can lie hidden and
be preserved where we least suppose, so that not only most people but even the
godly themselves fail to recognize it. Elijah therefore complains that he
alone was left, not that he alone was a prophet, as some would have it, but
that he alone was a true worshiper of God, as is evident from God’s answer: “I
have left for myself seven thousand people”—not prophets, but faithful
people—“who have not bowed the knee to Baal.”

2. Paul extends this passage to all Israel in general (Romans 11:3). He calls
himself an Israelite, although he was from the tribe of Benjamin and therefore
from the Jewish church (verse 1), and from these words depicts the condition
of the whole church. His purpose is to prove that, just as in Elijah’s time no
church appeared to have been left among the Jews when Elijah complained that
he alone remained, yet at that time there were many thousands of true
believers whom God had preserved for himself, so not even now has the whole
Jewish church failed, although the greatest part has fallen away. A remnant
according to the election of grace always remains.

3. Nor are reasons lacking from which it can be gathered that the subject is
the entire Jewish church. Elijah speaks of altars that had been overthrown,
though none were lawful except at Jerusalem. He withdrew into the wilderness,
not into Judea, where he could otherwise have gone and found godly worshipers
of God. He was affected with such grief that in his sorrow he wished for death.
These things sufficiently demonstrate the corruption of the whole church.
Hence, in the description of the church’s state in those times, “the kingdom
of Judah” is said to have “walked in the errors of Israel and not kept the
commandments of the Lord” (2 Kings 17:19).

X. That this happened not once but often is evident from other passages that
describe the most corrupt state of the Jewish church. Thus under Rehoboam, the
first king of Judah after the division of the kingdom: “Judah did evil before
the Lord, and they provoked him to jealousy beyond all that their fathers had
done in the sins that they committed. For they also built high places,
statues, and groves upon every high hill and beneath every leafy tree” (1 Kings
14:22–23).

The same is said to have happened under various other succeeding kings, and
especially in Manasseh’s time, of whom the history speaks as follows: “He did
evil in the sight of the Lord according to the idols of the nations whom God
had destroyed before the children of Israel. For he rebuilt the high places
that his father had destroyed, erected altars to Baal, and made a grove, as
Ahab king of Israel had done. He worshiped and served the entire host of
heaven. Moreover, he built altars for the whole host of heaven in the courts
of the Lord’s house, made his son pass through the fire, etc., and devised
enormous evils in the eyes of the Lord in order to provoke him to anger” (2
Kings 21:2). And lest anyone think that this concerned the king alone while
the people were free from these crimes, it is added: “Manasseh seduced the
Israelites so that they did worse than the nations whom the Lord destroyed
before the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 45 | IA leaf 79 | PDF p. 79]

children of Israel.”

To this belong the various complaints of the prophets, who speak of the most
corrupt state of the church in their time (Isaiah 1:2–5, 21–22, 29; 5:4;
Jeremiah 2:8, 11, 13, 26–27; 6:13; 7:28; Ezekiel 7:26; 22:26, 28, 30; Hosea
4:1, 6, 9, 12; Micah 7:1). Although it cannot be gathered from this that there
were no longer any godly worshipers of God, it is rightly inferred that there
were very few. They lay hidden in obscurity and did not constitute any visible
and illustrious assembly, since the public ministry and the whole body of the
visible church had been foully corrupted.

[Marginalia: 2. From the state of the church of the New Testament.]

XI. Second, from the state of the New Testament church in the time of Christ.
The thing itself declares that it was obscure and without splendor, since it
was contained in Christ and in a few obscure people: Joseph, the blessed
Virgin, Simeon, Anna, Zechariah, Elizabeth, fishermen, tax collectors, and
others from the lowest common people. Meanwhile the chief priests, leaders of
the people, priests, and Pharisees, who excelled in dignity, authority, and
power and who gloried in the name of the church—indeed, arrogated this
privilege to themselves alone—were nothing of the kind in reality and were
rejected by Christ as spurious and alien.

Nor should Stapleton be heard when he is not ashamed to assert that such
people were the true church. As though those who were Christ’s open enemies,
who persecuted him with deadly hatred, rejected his doctrine and miracles with
obstinate unbelief and rebellion, and finally delivered him to the most cruel
death deserved the name of the church, rather than those who followed Christ,
believed in him, and were his genuine disciples. Likewise, it is evident that
at the time of the passion the church was in great obscurity and reduced to
very few, when the apostles fled and deserted Christ and some even denied him.
Where then was the church’s splendor and glory?

XII. It is true that after his resurrection and ascension the church obtained
remarkable splendor through the outpouring of the Holy Spirit and the calling
of the Gentiles. Not long afterward, however, it is astonishing how greatly it
was obscured by the bloody and terrible persecutions through which it was
afflicted and dispersed for three hundred years, both by the Jews and by the
pagan emperors. In these persecutions nothing was left undone to destroy it
utterly.

What eminence and splendor could the church possess when its condition was
most grievous, sacred buildings were overthrown, pastors were slaughtered,
flocks were dispersed, and the faithful were tortured in various ways—either
killed by the cruelest kinds of punishments, wretchedly driven into exile, or
compelled to hide in crypts and caves to escape the persecutors’ savagery? Not
only the pagans but afterward the Arians also raged cruelly against the
faithful, when that pestilential plague invaded the church like a deluge and
finally enveloped it in thick darkness. Where then was the church’s splendor,
when the Arians occupied the episcopal sees, the emperors stood on their side,
Athanasius and a few other confessors were driven into exile and did not have
the free exercise of religion, and “the whole world groaned and marveled that
it was Arian,” as Hiero? says.

XIII. Perron tries in vain to evade the force of this argument when he says
that “the church is illustrious not only with lilies but also with roses; that
is, it is conspicuous and evident not only in tranquil and peaceful exercises
held for hearing the word, worshiping, and participating in the mysteries of
the sacraments, but also in military exercises, which are martyrdoms and
sufferings endured in defense of the faith.”

For whatever the glory of the martyrs may be, it cannot confer upon the church
the splendor the papists require, so that it is a visible and illustrious
assembly of pastors and sheep. On the contrary, persecutions of the kind in
which martyrs are crowned disperse assemblies and obscure the church’s
appearance. Since, therefore, cross and persecution are the church’s
inseparable companion (Psalm 129:1; Matthew 24:20; John 15:20), everyone sees
that the splendor and clarity they attribute to it cannot belong to it.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 46 | IA leaf 80 | PDF p. 80]

[Marginalia: 3. From Revelation 12:6, 14.]

XIV. Third, from Revelation 12:6, where “the woman who gave birth to the male
child is compelled to flee into the wilderness, where she has a place prepared
by God so that she may be nourished there for one thousand two hundred and
sixty days.” For if the church is designated by that woman who gave birth to
the male child—that is, to the mystical Christ in his members—and is compelled
to flee into the wilderness, that is, into a solitary and obscure place out of
the world’s sight, so that she may lie hidden there more safely and escape the
fury and savagery of the dragon, that is, of the devil who persecutes her, how
can her state always be illustrious and visible?

Nor are the wings of an eagle said to have been given to her without reason,
not only to signify the speed of her flight but also its imperceptibility,
since, as the wise man says, the eagle’s tracks are unknown. Nor can it be
objected that, if the wilderness is referred to invisibility, Babylon must be
called invisible, because John is said to have been “carried away into the
wilderness to see the woman sitting upon the beast” (Revelation 17:3). For
there is an enormous difference. Babylon is not said to have been carried into
the wilderness and received by God. Rather, John is said to have been carried
away in spirit into the wilderness, so that this is a description of ecstasy
and the location of the vision. He was indeed carried outside himself and away
from cities and human assemblies into a secret place where, with no other
objects intervening, his mind could be directed more freely to this one thing.

[Marginalia: 4. From the revelation of the Antichrist.]

XV. Fourth, from the revelation of the Antichrist, who must “sit in the temple
of God,” that is, the church, and exalt himself above “everything that is
called God, etc.” (2 Thessalonians 2:4). But if he must sit—that is, exercise
rule over the church—what splendor could it then possess? Indeed, what darkness
and obscurity must not occupy its heaven?

The papists themselves do not deny it. Bellarmine, book 3, chapter 7, *On the
Roman Pontiff*: “It is certain that the persecution of the Antichrist will be
most grievous and most conspicuous, so that all public ceremonies and
sacrifices of religion will cease.” And afterward: “The Antichrist will
prohibit all divine worship that is practiced in the church of Christians.”
Stapleton, book 13, principle of doctrine, chapter 2: “The church certainly can
be driven into the wilderness when the Antichrist reigns, and at that moment
the church may appropriately be sought in the wilderness, that is, in hidden
places, in caves and hiding places to which the saints withdraw.”

The Rhemists on 2 Thessalonians, section 10: “Shortly before the end of the
world the outward state of the Roman church will cease, as will the public
fellowship of the faithful with it, and apostasy will then prevail everywhere.
Nevertheless, the godly will then communicate in heart and mind with the pope
and the Roman church.” And on Revelation 12 they acknowledge “that the church
can at some time lack outward splendor, a public state and government, and the
free exercise of sacred functions.” Gregory of Valencia in his *Analysis*
acknowledges from Hilary and Jerome: “The church is not always to be sought in
buildings and outward pomp, but rather in prisons and caves.” Why, then, do
they contend for its perpetual splendor?

[Marginalia: 5. From the church’s fallibility.]

XIV. [sic] Fifth, from the church’s fallibility, because any visible
particular church can err ruinously both in faith and in morals, as will be
proved afterward. Therefore, it can also be obscured and fail, for such errors
are the proximate causes that corrupt and destroy the church. Otherwise there
would have been no need for Christ to warn the faithful so carefully to guard
themselves against false Christs and false prophets, as he does (Matthew
24:24). Nor would John have had to command Christians with such earnestness to
test the spirits (1 John 4:1–2), if the true church had to be illustrious and
conspicuous in every age. It would have been enough to warn them in one word
never to depart from communion with such a church.

[Marginalia: 6. From the testimony of the fathers.]

XVII. Sixth, from the testimony of the fathers, who agree with us here.
Athanasius in the *Letter to Those Living the Solitary Life*: “What church now
worships Christ freely, since if it is godly, it is exposed to danger? For if
there are anywhere godly people and those devoted to Christ, they likewise,
like the great

[1847 vol. III printed p. 47 | IA leaf 81 | PDF p. 81]

prophet Elijah, are hidden and conceal themselves in caves and caverns of the
earth, or dwell as wanderers in solitude.”

Hilary, *Against Auxentius*, near the end: “I warn you of one thing: Beware the
Antichrist. An evil love of walls has seized you; evilly do you venerate the
church of God in roofs and buildings; evilly do you introduce the name of
peace beneath them. Is there any doubt that the Antichrist will sit in them?
Mountains, forests, lakes, prisons, and chasms are safer for me. For in these
the prophets, either remaining or submerged, prophesied by the Spirit of God.”

Augustine, Letter 80: “The church is the sun, moon, and stars, as has been
said, beautiful as the moon and choice as the sun. When the sun is darkened,
the moon does not give its light, the stars fall from heaven, and the powers
of the heavens are shaken, the church will not appear, when the ungodly
persecutors then rage beyond measure, etc.” And on Psalm 119: “The church,
still established in this mortality of the flesh, is signified in Scripture by
the name of the moon on account of this very mutability.” And Letter 48 to
Vincentius: “This is the church that is sometimes obscured and, as it were,
overcast by the multitude of scandals.”

Jerome on Psalm 132: “The church does not consist in walls but in the truth of
doctrines. The church is where the true faith is. Moreover, fifteen or twenty
years ago, heretical churchmen possessed all the walls here; for twenty years
ago heretics possessed all the churches here. But the church was where the
true faith was.” Ambrose, book 4, chapter 2 of the *Hexaemeron*: “The church
appears to wane like the moon, but it does not fail. It can be overshadowed;
it cannot fail.”

XVIII. The various passages of the prophets that promise splendor and eminence
to the church do not favor the papists. 1. By Jerusalem and Zion they do not
mean a mixed society of good and evil people or some visible assembly affixed
to a particular see, but the society of true believers, to which alone God’s
promises are directed and belong.

2. They speak not so much of outward glory and splendor as properly of
spiritual and inward glory, in accordance with the style of the Old Testament,
by which heavenly and spiritual things are represented under earthly and
bodily things, as was said above. Isaiah 60 must be explained in this way.
There God, who will raise up the prostrate church, shows the power of the word,
of which he spoke in the preceding chapter, and symbolically depicts the
glorious state of the church under the Messiah by the glory of earthly
kingdoms and by all the most precious and excellent things. So far is this
from having to be understood literally of sensible and worldly glory that the
Holy Spirit attributes such glory to Babylon, which is presented as clothed in
purple and adorned with gems and gold (Revelation 17), while the heavenly
Jerusalem, the Lamb’s bride, is said to be adorned “with pure and shining
linen, which is the righteousness of the saints” (Revelation 19:8).

3. If some splendor and conspicuousness are to be understood, they must not be
extended to every period of the church. Rather, this especially regards four
periods in which the church had to be more conspicuous. The first was at the
beginning of Christianity through the calling of the Gentiles, when the
Christian church had to be illustrious and conspicuous throughout the entire
world as the apostles founded Christ’s kingdom by the preaching of the gospel
and gloriously triumphed over idolatry and the errors of paganism. To this
belong the oracles in Psalm 87:3–4, 6; 110:2–3; and Isaiah 2:1–2, because then
the law is said to have gone out from Zion and the scepter of power to have
been sent from it into the world. Likewise Isaiah 60:3–5.

The second is in the progress of that same Christianity, when it was
wonderfully increased and made illustrious, whether through the conversion of
emperors and princes, under whom the church could enjoy some peace and
tranquility—when kings and princes became its foster fathers and their queens
its nurses (Isaiah 49:23), whose breasts it was to suck (Isaiah 60:16)—or
through its remarkable reformations accomplished at various times.

The third is in the general conversion of the Jews near the end of the world,
after the fullness of the Gentiles has come in. The prophets, and Paul after
them, promise a remarkable glory of the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 48 | IA leaf 82 | PDF p. 82]

church at that time, which will be like resurrection from the dead (Romans
11:15). Paul testifies that Isaiah 60 looked to this when he refers to it the
prophecy immediately preceding in Isaiah 59:19–20 (Romans 11:26).

The fourth is at the end of the ages through the glorious resurrection and the
consummation of eternal felicity. The words reported in Isaiah 60:18–20 prove
that the Holy Spirit regarded this in the foregoing oracle, and the same Holy
Spirit, the best interpreter of himself, transfers those words to the heavenly
Jerusalem (Revelation 21:23–25). Although, therefore, the Christian church had
to obtain some splendor, it does not follow that it will always possess the
same, because God wills it to experience various states.

XIX. It is one thing for the gospel ministry to be the ordinary means for the
gathering and preservation of the church, which will endure perpetually until
the consummation of the age, as Paul asserts (Ephesians 4:11–12, etc.) and we
concede. It is another for this ministry to be perpetuated in an uninterrupted
series of succession in some one see, namely Rome, and always with the same
splendor of both uncorrupted doctrine and inviolate order and of approved
dignity. We deny this after Scripture, which more than once describes the
varying condition and vicissitude of the ministry in this world (Acts 20:29; 2
Thessalonians 2:3; 1 Timothy 4:1–3).

XX. The eternal foundation of the church, of which Psalm 48:8 treats, does
indeed entail its perpetuity and constant duration, but its perpetual
conspicuousness and eminence cannot be inferred. For an argument cannot
rightly be drawn from the removal of a separable accident, such as the
church’s outward splendor, to the removal of its essence. Nor can the church
immediately be said to have failed when it is deprived of this splendor.

XXI. It is said in Isaiah 61:8–9, “I will make an everlasting covenant with
them, so that their offspring may be known among the nations and their
descendants among the peoples. All who see them will recognize them, because
they are the offspring whom the Lord has blessed.” This indeed shows that the
church of the New Testament is to be propagated among the Gentiles and that
the signs and effects of divine grace in it will be so great and splendid that
all who see them will be carried into wonder and compelled to confess that
these are the children of God, the offspring whom the Lord has blessed. The
same was to be said of Abraham (Genesis 12:3) and of the Jewish church brought
back from captivity (Psalm 126:2).

It does not follow, however, that its state will always be such. Nor is it
necessary for the fulfillment of the oracle that this testimony be given by
everyone and at all times. It is enough that this happen at certain intervals
until perfection comes on the final day of judgment, when God himself will
confess his people before heaven and earth and everyone will acknowledge the
felicity of the faithful.

XXII. It is one thing for Christ always to be present with his church to teach,
sanctify, console, and defend it against the enemies by whom it is attacked,
lest it ever fail or the gates of hell prevail against it, as Christ promises
(Matthew 16:18; 28:20). It is another for Christ to exercise this power of his
in preserving the church by sensible effects and temporal blessings that make
its state peaceful and desirable. Although this sometimes occurs, it is not
perpetual. Nor is it promised otherwise in the New Testament than under this
condition: insofar as it conduces to promoting and perfecting God’s glory and
the salvation of the elect. Therefore, although that state is sometimes, and
indeed very often, disturbed, it must not for that reason be thought that God
has failed to keep his promises.

XXIII. Things that concern the state and office of the apostles and the
faithful servants of God must not be transferred to the church’s perpetual
splendor. Nor, if

[1847 vol. III printed p. 49 | IA leaf 83 | PDF p. 83]

they are the light of the world and a city set upon a mountain that cannot be
hidden (Matthew 5:14) wherever they exercise their ministry and while they
exercise it, does it follow that this ministry is never to be obscured or
taken away and that the church will always enjoy this light, so that it is
never covered by clouds or placed beneath a basket.

XXIV. It is one thing to speak of the perpetual duration of Christ’s kingdom
and of its inward glory and splendor, and another to speak of its outward
splendor and eminence. The former is meant in Psalm 89:29, when God promises
Christ that he will establish his offspring forever: “Establishing his
offspring forever and his throne like the days of heaven,” and in verse 36:
“His throne is like the sun before me and like the perfect moon forever.” The
latter is not meant, however, for Christ’s kingdom can endure without having
outward glory.

Nor is the nature of Christ’s kingdom the same as that of worldly kingdoms.
Worldly kingdoms cannot consist of a few people who are downcast and hidden,
but require a multitude and visible splendor. Yet since Christ’s kingdom is
not of this world, he can reign by the Spirit among a few who are dispersed,
who are therefore rightly called his heavenly kingdom.

XXV. Although a visible particular church is not always conspicuous and
eminent, it does not follow that it is impossible to join oneself to the
church for salvation, on the ground that no one can resort to a hidden church.
1. For the church that we are bound to join and outside which there is no
salvation is not a visible particular church, as though it were affixed to one
nation or place, but the catholic and invisible church. Even those who cannot
have communion with a visible church can adhere to it.

2. Although outward communion with a visible church is also necessary
whenever and insofar as opportunity for it is given and is not prevented by
the adversity of the times or of people, since the invisible church is
contained in the visible, nevertheless mere deprivation of it does not
condemn. Only contempt does, when we are able to have it. Thus the seven
thousand people in Elijah’s time had no outward communion with the church,
since they were unknown to Elijah himself, yet they were no less to be saved.
Entrance into the church by faith occurs even when no church appears (Hebrews
12:22), while entrance through profession can occur even when the church is
less illustrious and escapes the eyes of the world.

XXVI. Although confession of the mouth is joined with faith of the heart by
the apostle (Romans 10:10), because the one who created and redeemed body and
soul wills to be glorified by both, nevertheless the necessity of faith and
of confession differs. Faith is always and everywhere required, but confession
is not. It is required only when God’s glory and our calling demand it: when
it is asked of us (1 Peter 3:15), or when our faith must be proved and our
neighbor’s salvation promoted by it.

Further, one confession is public and another private; one is made before
anyone, even enemies of the truth, and another before the faithful; one is
before God and another before people. Confession before God is always
necessary, but not confession before people. Although it is never lawful to
deny the Lord, we are not always bound to perform the positive act of public
and open confession, unless our calling and the glory of God require it.
Therefore, although many people cannot openly profess the faith publicly
before others while the church is hidden, it is enough that they approve it
before God and his godly worshipers.

The dilemma with which our opponents press us does not strike us. They say,
“Either those hidden people who constitute the invisible church openly
profess the faith and abstain from idolatry, or they do not. If they profess
it, the church is certainly visible. If they do not profess it, ‘there is no
true church, whose property it is to believe with the heart and confess with
the mouth.’” Their enumeration is inadequate and lacks a third member, which,
when added, unties the knot: either they openly profess the faith, or they do
not, or they secretly preserve the faith and abstain from idolatry, as was
done by the hundred

[1847 vol. III printed p. 50 | IA leaf 84 | PDF p. 84]

prophets whom Obadiah hid in a cave in Jezebel’s time (1 Kings 18:3).

Nor can Bellarmine object, when he says in book 3, chapter 2, *On the Church*:
“Some are in the church in body only, some in soul only, and others in soul
and body together.” The same argument can be turned against the seven thousand
people hidden in Elijah’s time. Either such people confessed publicly with
their mouth, or they did not. If the former, they were not hidden. If the
latter, the question returns concerning them: how could they be faithful and
not confessors? The same can be said of all who escape their persecutors by
flight and withdraw into caves or crypts. Although they cannot openly profess
the faith, no one would therefore expunge them from the number of the
faithful.

XXVII. Although the church, by the office entrusted to it by Christ, ought to
“be the pillar and foundation of the truth” in order to promulgate and defend
it, it does not follow either that the church always discharges this office or
that it discharges it conspicuously and eminently. The failure and destruction
of the Ephesian church itself, which is the particular subject of that
passage, sufficiently testifies to this.
