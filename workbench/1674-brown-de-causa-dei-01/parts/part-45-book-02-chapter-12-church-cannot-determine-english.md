CHAPTER XII

The Church Is Not Permitted to Determine This Principal and Solemn Time

Although the truth of this assertion has been abundantly confirmed by what
was said in the preceding chapter, nevertheless, because the adversaries speak
more particularly about the power of the church and rely on this foundation
alone when they defend some sort of observance of the Sabbath, it is worth
examining this foundation a little more closely: namely, whether the church
possesses any such power

[1674 logical printed p. 288 | IA leaf 306 | PDF p. 306]

to determine and institute this necessary, sufficient, principal, and solemn
time.

By *the church* we here understand the rulers of the church, to whom Christ
has given the power to govern the church, to propose and explain Christ's laws
ministerially, and to determine certain things that are indifferent when
considered in their own nature and specifically, in view of circumstances and
according to Christ's general rules, and to enjoin them as necessary here and
now.

That this power does not belong to them can be confirmed by several kinds of
arguments.

The first kind consists of arguments drawn from the nature and properties of
this time explained above, namely:

1. This time is *necessary for religion*, as has been said. But the church does
not possess the power to make by its determination some time necessary which
was not previously necessary for religion. I say *to make it necessary*,
because if the time is necessary for religion in itself, or before the
determination made by the church, the church's determination is only a
ministerial declaration, not an authoritative constitution. Consequently, it
is properly no determination at all, but supposes a determination made by God
and signified either through nature or through Scripture. That the church does
not possess this power to make by its determination a time necessary which was
previously unnecessary is clear from the following. 1. The church has no power
over human consciences, as our theologians prove against the papists. But the
church would possess such authority over consciences if it had the power to
determine necessary time, because consciences would necessarily be bound to
observe this necessary time. Every Christian is bound in conscience to observe
what is necessary for religion. Therefore, if the church could make unnecessary
time necessary, it would bind and tightly constrain consciences that were
previously free. 2. Then every time determined by the church would be necessary
by the very fact that it was determined by the church, because its force and
power are always the same, and the inference from a thing *insofar as it is
such* to every instance is valid. But this is absurd. For if the church
determined that two or three whole days together should be spent on religion,
or that a whole day should be spent religiously three or four times every week,
that time would certainly not be necessary, but an intolerable burden. 3. The
power of the church by which it properly constitutes something extends at most
only to indifferent matters in which it is exercised. For as regards necessary
matters, it has only the power to declare. Indeed, strictly speaking, the
church cannot enjoin an indifferent thing *as such*. It must first, by reason
of the circumstances, be either necessary or at least expedient before the
church's determination is made, because all the church's power has been granted
for edification. To enjoin an indifferent thing as such is not to edify, but
to take childish delight in a frivolous exercise. Therefore, if the church
cannot enjoin an indifferent thing unless it is first in some way necessary or
expedient, much less can it make something unnecessary necessary.

2. This time, as has been said, must be *ordinarily sufficient*. Therefore, if
the church possesses the power to determine this solemn time, it will possess
the power to constitute as ordinarily sufficient a time that was not formerly
ordinarily sufficient. But this is false. Therefore, and so forth. The
connection in the major premise is clear from what has now been said, for this

[1674 logical printed p. 289 | IA leaf 307 | PDF p. 307]

solemn time about which the question is raised ought to be ordinarily
sufficient both for promoting God's honor and for promoting human salvation.
It ought to be such as would be acceptable to God and could satisfy consciences
that raised a doubt about the matter. It must be made such by determination,
because if it were such before determination, there would be no need for a
constitutive determination of the church, but only for one that explains and
expounds. We are not speaking of the latter here. It is therefore necessary
that by this determination the time which was not such before become ordinarily
sufficient. Consequently, if this power belongs to the church, it possesses
the power to make this time such by determining it.

That this is absurd and false is evident. The church's power, as has been said,
does not reach necessary matters except by ministerially declaring what is
necessary. 2. At most it deals with indifferent matters, but this time is not
to be numbered among indifferent matters. 3. Then whatever time the church
determined would be ordinarily sufficient, because the inference from a thing
*as such* to every instance follows perfectly. But this is absurd. For if the
church determined only one hour every week or every year, would that time be
ordinarily sufficient for celebrating divine worship? The absurdity of this is
obvious to everyone. 4. To make time sufficient for promoting God's glory,
divine approval and acceptance are necessarily required, and the church is not
their lord. Nowhere do we read that God wills to approve every determination
of the church in this matter, and to judge and accept as sufficient whatever
the church sees fit to determine and grant. 5. God's blessing is also required,
but we do not read that God has promised to attach his blessing to all the
church's determinations. 6. It is required that the time be sufficient to
promote human salvation. But the church certainly cannot always perceive and
determine without error what is sufficient to promote human salvation. 7. It
is required that human consciences be satisfied. But before a conscience could
be certain that what the church determines is acceptable and pleasing to God
and will be blessed by him, it must be evident that the church neither errs nor
can err in making its determination. We do not read that God has granted the
church this privilege. We know that infallibility was granted to the apostles,
but our theologians disputing against the papists deny that it was given to
others.

The adversaries cannot weaken this and the preceding argument by saying that
they do not maintain such absolute power in the church, but grant that the
church ought not to determine time that is excessively long with respect to
duration or excessively frequent with respect to recurrence, nor, conversely,
time that is excessively short or infrequent. For they must grant either that
some rule is given according to which the determination ought to be made, or
that no rule is given. If they say the latter, they must acknowledge—if such
power belongs to the church—that the church does not err whatever time it
determines, however long or however short, frequent or infrequent, because
where there is no law, there is no transgression. But if they say the former,
they must acknowledge that through that rule the time has already been divinely
determined, and that the church does not determine it. They must also
acknowledge that this rule is contained in the fourth precept of the Decalogue,
which determines this time, since no other command can be assigned. Thus this
precept, which determines one day in the weekly cycle

[1674 logical printed p. 290 | IA leaf 308 | PDF p. 308]

and enjoins it, is moral—something which they have so far been unwilling to
assert, but oppose as much as they can.

3. It is required, as has been said, that the continuous duration of this time
be sufficient not only for exercising public worship but also for performing
domestic and solitary worship, both before and after public worship. But the
church cannot determine a time that lasts so long. For 1. These religious
exercises—domestic and solitary worship—take place within private walls, where
affirmative human precepts have no place. Much less can the positive precepts
of the church reach such time, of which every head of a household is rather to
be regarded as lord. Hence no laws made by either church or magistrate exist
concerning positive duties to be performed within private walls. 2. The church
cannot determine anything concerning solitary worship or the time to be spent
on that worship, because it cannot judge whether the command given is obeyed
or not. Why, therefore, should anything be instituted where it cannot be judged
whether it was done? 3. Those solitary duties are immediate duties of
conscience and are highly spiritual. Therefore, ecclesiastical power does not
reach either them or the time determined for them, because it does not
immediately touch the conscience and the inward person. This power which
immediately commands the conscience is proper to God alone.

It is objected in vain that the church can prescribe a day of solemn
humiliation to be spent in fasting and in both public and private prayers. The
church here prescribes nothing about the duration of time. Scripture indicates
to us that a whole day has been determined by God for this business. The church
only indicates that the day is to be so spent, or specifies the opportunity
designated by divine providence. It names the duties particularly for this
business but does not determine them, nor does it assign the time for performing
them. It only indicates ministerially that at this present juncture God requires
that a whole day be spent in fasting and prayers. Christian consciences are
bound to perform these duties of piety, not by reason of the church's
appointment, but by reason of the divine command.

4. The determination of this time implies that all for whom the time is
determined are bound to public exercises of worship and constantly to spend
that time in the exercise of public worship. For the determination makes the
time necessary for religion and therefore to be constantly observed. And
because they maintain that the fourth precept is moral only in requiring public
worship and, consequently, that this public worship have time determined for
it, they must grant that this time is to be observed perpetually. Finally, the
determination would be made in vain unless those for whom the determinate time
is prescribed were bound to observe it. But the church does not possess the
power to bind everyone to observe the time appointed for public worship, as the
adversaries themselves acknowledge when they exempt painters, cooks, and
servants of that kind and say that the fourth precept was not given to servants.
If a master demands that a servant remain at home during public worship, will
they say that the servant is bound to refuse obedience? If they acknowledge
this, why are they so hostile to us for urging the same thing on account of the
divine determination? If they deny it, it is sufficiently evident by their own
admission that the church

[1674 logical printed p. 291 | IA leaf 309 | PDF p. 309]

cannot bind everyone who is also a member of the church to observe this time,
for they will not deny that servants too can be members of the church. But
where, then, is the morality of the fourth precept? Where is public worship?
Where is the time determined for public worship? All of it then vanishes.
Furthermore, let us suppose that the church determines ten or twelve hours to
be spent continuously in public divine worship, and this two, three, or four
times every week. Can it bind all the members of the church to observe time
that recurs so often in this manner? The adversaries will not say this.
Therefore, our argument remains firm.

5. This determination of *necessary and sufficient time* must be perpetual,
because that solemn time ought not to be changed either by subtraction, since
it is necessary, or by addition, since it is sufficient. But the church does
not possess the power to make such a perpetual determination. The adversaries
themselves assert that the church can change at pleasure a determination of
solemn time already made. And all the orthodox grant that all ecclesiastical
determinations, as such, are in themselves mutable. Therefore, the rulers of
the church do not possess the power to make this determination, which ought to
be immutable.

Second, another kind of argument can be drawn from doubts that may be raised
about the church itself and its determination. They include the following:

1. It can be doubted, and is doubted by some godly and upright men, whether by
*the church* which possesses this power we ought to understand, if not the
entire congregation or assembly of all members, both men and women, at least
the rulers together with certain members, especially the leading ones, so that
nothing can be concluded unless at least all these consent. Some wish *the
church* which possesses this power, if the power is granted to the church, to
mean the rulers of particular congregations determining independently. Others
perhaps wish it to belong to a classis or assembly of ecclesiastical rulers,
or to a provincial or national synod where one can be held. Others perhaps will
claim all this power for prelates alone—for archbishops or primates. There may
be some who wish this power to be ascribed only to a general council composed
of delegates from all the churches. I say nothing of those who prefer one pope
to all others and assert that he is the sole subject of this power. With these
disputes about the church continuing, it is evident that no church exists whose
determination could satisfy all Christians. Yet it is most fitting that all
Christians observe the same solemn time. It is also certain that the
determination of some who wish to be regarded as the church has no authority
among many and therefore is empty. Thus, if the church possesses this power,
each faction will have a distinct day determined for itself, and there will
perhaps be as many different solemn times as there are different opinions about
the church, which would be absurd.

2. Let us suppose that everyone is in agreement here and that this authority
belongs by right to this or that definite assembly. Nevertheless, it is not yet
agreed among everyone which members of that assembly possess the right to vote.
For some wish this right to belong to all men; others only to all senior members
and rulers, including deacons. Some grant it to absolutely all pastors and
rulers called to the synod;

[1674 logical printed p. 292 | IA leaf 310 | PDF p. 310]

others only to delegates. Some admit ruling elders in all cases, while others
reject them in matters concerning doctrine. Some say that this right belongs
only to prelates. Others admit presbyters, either all or at least some. From
this it is clear how doubtful the authority of this determination would be and
how others would readily reject the determinate day which these admitted.
Thus, it is not yet probable that the same solemn time will be determined.

3. Let there be unanimity here as well. Nevertheless, there will be a
controversy whether the church's judgment and determination is valid when it
is not approved by civil magistrates, who have an interest in it. They can
claim that the matter greatly concerns them, because once a determination is
made, the people are prohibited from secular business. A doubt will therefore
remain about the force of the determination. And many will judge it more than
probable that God would have wished to settle this controversy clearly in
Scripture if he had granted human beings the power to determine this time,
which is a matter of such importance.

4. Let us suppose that everyone agrees about the persons who possess this
power. Who will assure everyone, or anyone at all, that those persons are
infallible or have determined infallibly? We know that human beings are
fallible and have been mistaken in deciding controversies even where Scripture
had proposed God's judgment clearly enough. And according to the adversaries,
who reject the fourth precept, it is more probable that human beings can err in
determining this time, since Scripture is silent about it. Otherwise we must
say that whatever time they determine will be principal, solemn, necessary,
and sufficient.

5. Furthermore, it may be doubted whether in a matter of such importance the
unanimity of the entire assembly or council would be necessary, or whether the
consent of the majority would suffice, so that the question would be decided
by the greater number of votes. And when the question had been decided by the
majority, exceeding the other side by only one or two votes, is it probable
that the determination would have less force when so many stood on the
opposite side and a schism would thus arise—especially since this question has
not yet been decided: whether the votes of the majority must be followed, or
only the unanimous consent of all?

6. Let it be granted that the judgment of the majority must be followed.
Nevertheless, it will remain uncertain whether this majority gave their votes
freely—neither compelled nor enticed by gifts or promises, and moved neither by
fear nor by the favor of the great. Where, then, is the certain foundation on
which human conscience can rest in such a matter of great importance that
recurs so often?

Third, we can argue further against the adversaries by asking to which church
they wish this power to belong, namely:

1. To each *particular church*? If they deny this, then 1. This power does not
belong to the church *as such*, because a particular church is a church, and
the inference from a thing as such to every instance is valid. 2. What will a
particular church do when no other church has determined this time? 3. What
will a particular church far removed from all others and not yet informed of
a determination made by them do? 4. The religious observance of this time
concerns particular churches and even individual persons. 5. When the greater
churches err in a determination already made, or when the determination made
is not accepted because of the difficulties mentioned above, what

[1674 logical printed p. 293 | IA leaf 311 | PDF p. 311]

will a particular church do? 6. Long before any general synod existed, a
certain solemn time was observed by particular churches, as Scripture testifies
in Acts 20:7 and 1 Corinthians 16:2, and as the practice of the primitive church
in its first three centuries proves.

But if they grant that this power also belongs to particular churches, 1. Let
them say whether this power is granted to all the members of that particular
church, both men and women, as the Anabaptists wish; only to the men, as some
Independents wish; to the consistory; or only to pastors and teachers, as
others wish. If they entirely exclude the people as distinct from the
consistory, how will the people be satisfied if the determination is excessively
strict and burdensome and less than necessary, or, conversely, excessively lax
and less than sufficient? 2. In whatever subject this power is placed, it is
certain that in a particular church there are many liable to corruptions and
not a few who, although otherwise by no means evil, are nevertheless wholly
unfit to make such a determination. Who does not perceive how dangerous it is
to entrust a matter so necessary for promoting both God's glory and a person's
eternal salvation to the determination of such people? 3. It is not probable
that all particular churches will determine one and the same time, whether
with respect to the continuous duration of time, its frequent recurrence, its
beginning, or the occasion itself—much less with respect to all these aspects.
And if there are as many different determinate times as there are distinct
particular churches, or at least a very great many, what confusion will follow?
Will the members of one church not impede the members of others? Will contention
and discord not arise from it, these charging those with profanity and those
in turn charging these with excessive strictness, and so forth?

2. Or does it belong to the *national church*? If they deny this, then, as
above, 1. This power does not belong to the church as such, because the
inference from a thing as such to every instance is valid. 2. What will a
national church do when the ecumenical church does not determine the matter?
Does the religious observance of this time not also concern it? 3. What if the
ecumenical church errs in its determination?

If they affirm this, then 1. Let them say whether the power belongs to all the
members of the national church, only to all its governors, only to all its
pastors, or finally only to delegates from all the provincial synods or all
the classes or presbyteries. 2. If the people are excluded, the same doubt
raised above recurs. 3. It would contribute more to declaring God's glory if
all churches, including national churches, appointed the same time for his
public worship. But it is not probable that they will determine one and the
same time with respect to *how long* and *how often*. 4. What national church
determined this time for several centuries after the apostles? It cannot be
denied that some solemn time was observed, but by what national church had it
been determined? 5. The Galatian churches were equivalent to a national church.
But it is not credible that they wished to determine any such time without at
least consulting some apostle, since the primitive churches instituted nothing
without the apostles leading or consenting.

3. Does it belong to the *catholic church assembled in a general council*? If
they grant this, then 1. Let them say who, by divine right, is to cast votes in
these councils. 2. How many are to be elected from each national church? 3. How
are votes to be cast—by heads or by nations, as in the Council of Basel? 4. What

[1674 logical printed p. 294 | IA leaf 312 | PDF p. 312]

if the determination made is either excessively lax or strict? Must it be
accepted in every case? Or can the several national churches remedy the evil
for themselves? And what if one national church judges lax a determination
which seems strict to others? 5. How has it happened that to this very day this
catholic church has not determined this time with respect to the particular
day, much less with respect to frequency or duration? And if it has determined
it, why do the adversaries still speak of change? Why do they say that the
present church can change the day from the first to the fourth, from one in
seven to one in ten, and so forth? But since we read of no such thing determined
by them, have not these councils declared sufficiently by their silence that
this principal and solemn time had already been declared and received as such
by all Christians? Provincial councils also testify to and confirm this when
they enjoin and urge the sanctification of that day. And indeed, it is worthy
of observation that no general synod confirmed this solemn time by its own
sanction, much less determined it. Divine providence arranged it so that their
confirmation might not perhaps be regarded as a determination or institution.

4. Does it finally belong to the *primitive or apostolic church*? If they deny
this, no church can claim this authority for itself by any right, not only
because that church was a true church, but because it was granted far greater
power than any other church, as everyone knows. If they grant it, they will say
either that the apostolic church actually determined this time, or that it did
not. If they say the former, their case falls, for it is not ours to change
what the apostles constituted, but perpetually to observe it. Yet the
adversaries roundly assert that the apostles did not determine this time. But
if they assert the latter—namely, that the apostles did not determine this
principal and solemn time, ordinarily sufficient and necessary—it is evident
that they never received from Christ the power to determine such time. The
reason is that we must not say they received from Christ a power which they
never put into action, and consequently did not discharge their office
faithfully. And if it did not possess this power, should it be said that any
other church received it?

Fourth, we can argue as follows and press further what we have now said. If the
determination of necessary and sufficient time has been granted to the church,
then it was granted to the apostolic church and to the apostles themselves.
But such power was not granted to them. Therefore, and so forth. The major
premise is certain. I confirm the minor as follows. The apostles did not receive
from Christ a power which they did not put into practice. But they did not put
into practice this power of determining such time. Therefore, and so forth.
The major premise is certain unless we wish to declare them guilty of violating
their office and of unfaithful administration. The minor is proved as follows.
Whatever the apostles did in matters of the greatest importance for religion,
they clearly handed down to posterity. But we see nothing concerning the
determination of solemn time handed down to memory. Therefore, and so forth.
Whoever denies the major will accuse the apostles of the greatest negligence,
because although they handed down many things in writing, they said nothing
about the determination of solemn time necessary and ordinarily sufficient for
the worship of God, in order to inform Christians in future ages of a necessary
duty. The adversaries who are unwilling to grant that the apostles observed
the Lord's Day will not deny the minor. And they will much more strongly affirm
that the apostles did not determine precisely the first day

[1674 logical printed p. 295 | IA leaf 313 | PDF p. 313]

of the week, or even one day in the weekly cycle, much less one day with respect
to duration—about which the greatest controversy is raised—although they most
especially ought to have determined this, since it is of the greatest
importance. It consequently appears that they did not determine this solemn
time with respect either to *how long* or to *how often*. That is, they did not
institute one day in the weekly cycle to be spent holily, but observed what had
been determined by God and clearly explained and commanded in the fourth
precept. And after precisely the seventh day had been abrogated, they spent in
pious exercises the first day, which, by virtue of the prior determination of
one day in the weekly cycle, had succeeded to the place of the seventh day. But
more about this later in Book V.

Fourth, other arguments can be drawn from the difficulty of making this
determination of principal and solemn, necessary and sufficient time. Namely:

1. Even ecclesiastical rulers lack the wisdom and knowledge required to discern
and determine a sufficient and necessary continuation of time. This is
abundantly evident from the controversies already raised about it. They
likewise lack what is needed to designate the frequency of the time, as the
various opinions of our adversaries about it also confirm; to determine its
beginning, occasion, or opportunity in such a manner as satisfies every
conscience; to designate the complete portion of time—for some think a tenth,
twelfth, or twentieth part of time can suffice, while others will perhaps
require a greater portion and judge it necessary—and finally, rightly and
wisely to distribute this total portion between its duration and frequency, as
is clear from what has been said.

2. Many are, moreover, carried away by their own depraved affections and appear
excessively carnal and worldly. They therefore wish to determine an excessively
scant portion of time, whether with respect to *how long*, *how often*, or both.
Others, conversely, labor under empty superstition and, while making a pretense
of love for heavenly things and neglecting other duties toward themselves and
others, may wish to make an excessively rigid determination.

3. According to the adversaries' hypotheses, there is no certain and firm rule
according to which the determination ought to be made. This further confirms
the two preceding difficulties. But where there is no rule by which the rulers
of the church can judge certainly and consistently both the total portion of
time and the geometrically equal distribution of this portion between duration
and frequency, it is absolutely certain that the determination cannot be made
with sufficient wisdom. Thus, those who wish to make this determination are
oppressed by difficulties. Since the adversaries reject the determination made
in the moral law, a rule must be sought elsewhere. This rule is either:

1. The nature of *God himself*, who is the sole object of all religious worship
and the primary end of religious time. But his nature is infinite and cannot
have a proportionate definite time. Therefore, if it is asked what time is
sufficient or necessary for the honor of God considered in itself, no other
answer can be given than that all time is necessary and anything less is not
sufficient. Nothing therefore appears here which will teach us to assign some
portion of time to worldly matters, much less which can teach us what
geometrically equal proportion we can safely give to our earthly cares and
labors. I do not say that human beings are not permitted to spend any time on
worldly business;

[1674 logical printed p. 296 | IA leaf 314 | PDF p. 314]

but I do say that the nature, majesty, glory, and honor of God, who is our Lord
and the Lord of all our time, considered in itself, does not teach that any
time is to be spent otherwise than in worshiping him, much less what or how
great and how frequently recurring a time may rightly be given to other matters.
Therefore, God's will alone must teach us here, for it is only by his good
pleasure that any time may be spent on other matters and that any portion less
than the whole may be regarded as sufficient. This does not arise from God's
nature.

2. Or the rule is the nature of *our soul*, which is the primary subject of
worship and the secondary end of religious time. But since our soul is eternal
with respect to the future and is destined through certain means to eternal
happiness or misery, and since the means by which it participates in
happiness and avoids misery are almost infinite, how can it be judged from
this that any definite time is sufficient for promoting the soul's good, and
not rather that all time is necessary? I do not say that sufficient time may
not be assigned to our bodily necessities, but that no proportion can certainly
be determined from this consideration. Indeed, from it one could rather
determine that absolutely all time is necessary and that no determinate time
less than the whole can be sufficient for promoting our salvation, since there
are many things very necessary to know which must still be learned, or which
have been consigned to oblivion and must again be recalled to memory; since
our strength and affections flag daily, while our corruptions gain strength
daily; since we are continually pressed by both internal and external
temptations; and thus are impeded along the way by many evils and serious
obstacles. That judgment will be still more confirmed whenever the soul burns
with love for God and also senses God's love diffused within it. Therefore, the
nature of our soul does not prescribe how much time, how frequently recurring
a time, or what time is to be withdrawn from the soul's affairs and given to
other, bodily affairs.

3. Or the rule is the *nature of divine worship itself*. But since all the
duties of divine worship, except the sacraments, are indefinite—such as hearing,
reading, and meditating on the Word, prayer, and so forth—they cannot direct us
to determine any definite time as necessary and sufficient. For these duties,
considered in themselves, can always be performed, and all our time can be
spent in them. Conversely, each of them can be performed quickly, in the space
of one hour or less. We must therefore have recourse to the divine will in
order to know what principal time ought to be spent in these exercises of
worship and how often it ought to recur, for nothing else can declare this to
us.

4. Or the rule is the *nature of our body and worldly affairs*. It is certain
that these affairs hinder and prevent us from spending all our time on divine
exercises, and claim at least some time for themselves. But from this we cannot
draw any certain conclusion about the distribution of time between divine
exercises and these worldly cares. For 1. Many who are wholly occupied with
these cares would assign excessively much time to them and less than is right
to divine exercises, as daily experience testifies. Even those who do not deny
that by force of the fourth precept a whole day every week ought to be dedicated
to sacred things rarely spend the whole day as they ought. Much less will those
who assert that the precept does not bind anyone to spend one day in the weekly
cycle on sacred things set apart a whole day. And the matter will fare still
worse when the determination is to be made according to this rule. 2. Worldly

[1674 logical printed p. 297 | IA leaf 315 | PDF p. 315]

affairs are various, uncertain, and continually recurring, and sometimes
require entire days. Human beings therefore cannot promise themselves immunity
from them or predetermine such a time to be spent on sacred matters because
they will have nothing else to do during it. This is especially true if we
speak of human beings in general. 3. These worldly affairs are inferior and of
less importance. There is therefore no reason why we should learn from them
how much time we ought to spend on divine matters. 4. It can also be doubted
which worldly affairs are understood here: everything lawful in itself, or
only necessary matters. If everything, then certainly very little time would
have to be assigned to God's worship. If only necessary matters, then more time
could indeed be assigned to religion. But concerning these necessary matters,
it can further be asked whether only those things without which nature cannot
be preserved should be regarded as necessary, or also other things which, as
matters now stand, cannot be omitted. If only the former are understood here,
one could perhaps conclude from this that more time ought to be given to God
and less spent on these earthly matters than divine indulgence has now granted
us, because nature in itself is content with little and does not require a long
time to provide necessities.

5. Or perhaps the rule is that proportion long ago designated by God—namely,
one day in the weekly cycle. But 1. This could not have been a rule for Adam,
the patriarchs, and others who lived before the time of Moses, because the
adversaries maintain that the Sabbath was not instituted before Moses. 2. The
adversaries themselves assert that this Sabbath has now been abrogated, both
with respect to a whole determinate day and with respect to one day in the
weekly cycle. That is, they assert that this determinate time, both with
respect to *how long* and to *how often*, no longer obliges us but has been
abolished. How, then, can the church determine one day in the weekly cycle and
a whole day any more than it can determine the seventh day, since they assert
that the fourth command has been equally abrogated in all these respects? I
hope they will not say that the church possesses the power to restore precisely
the seventh day from banishment. But, I ask, how can it any more restore a
whole day and one day each week, since all of these, according to the
adversaries, have been equally abrogated? Can it satisfy tender consciences in
the church by any such determination? Not a few think, and not without reason,
that the church does not possess the power to restore from banishment anything
which God once saw fit to abrogate. How, then, can that determination, now
entirely abolished, be a rule for the church according to which it can determine
principal and solemn, necessary and ordinarily sufficient time, when it cannot
determine one whole day in the weekly cycle? For if it determines one whole day
in a cycle of four or six days, or in a cycle of ten or twelve days, it does
not observe that rule; nor does it if it determines a period of four, six, or
ten hours every day, week, or month. But more about this in its place.

Sixth, we can argue as follows. If this power to determine principal and solemn
time has been granted to the church, it has been granted either as a privilege
or as a burden. But neither can be said. Therefore, and so forth. The major
premise cannot be denied because there is no third alternative. We prove the
minor in parts, and first that this power has not been given as a burden. 1.
The adversaries themselves will not say this. 2. Indeed, because of our
Christian liberty, they wish us to be free from

[1674 logical printed p. 298 | IA leaf 316 | PDF p. 316]

every divine determination formerly made. 3. The state of the Christian
church, which is a state of liberty and in this respect far preferable to the
state of the ancient church, sufficiently confirms it.

Second, it is clear as follows that this power has not been given as a
privilege. 1. The adversaries themselves sometimes assert that the Christian
church can now institute more days than the Jewish church celebrated. On this
account, therefore, the liberty of the Christian church would be less. 2. And
if this power has been granted to the church, I do not see why it cannot
constitute and determine many more days. What kind of privilege will this then
be? 3. But let it be granted that the church cannot constitute more days than
God formerly determined. Where is the privilege? Where is the liberty they
proclaim, if the church can institute the same number? Is it greater liberty
to be bound by human determinations than by God's? 4. If they say that the
church was free either to restore or not to restore the same time which had
formerly been determined, and that the church's liberty appears in this, I
answer that this contributes absolutely nothing to the members of the church,
who are no less bound now than formerly to observe the same determinate time.
The succeeding rulers of the church will also be bound by this same
determination unless they are granted power to change the time determined by
their predecessors. 5. If they say that the privilege consists in the fact
that the determination is now less strict, I answer that the determination is
then of no use if one is permitted, notwithstanding it, either to observe or
not to observe the determinate time. And if the determinate time must
necessarily be observed by reason of the determination, where is the liberty
or privilege? 6. If they say that the liberty or privilege consists in the
fact that a whole day is not now determined, I answer that a whole day can be
determined. They will not say that it is unlawful for the church to determine
a whole day. A whole day can also be lawfully observed. A whole day is the
best distribution of time and contributes more to promoting religion than if
its quantity were distributed in some other way, as was shown above in Chapter
VII of this book. Furthermore, what if only one hour were subtracted from a
whole day? Would this be great liberty? 7. But what kind of liberty is it to
have a time appropriated to celebrating God's praise and promoting our own
salvation that is much shorter and less frequent than the Jews had? Did Christ
die to acquire this privilege for us? The godly would rather regard it as
bondage. 8. Let us suppose that a godly servant suffers many things from his
severe and very harsh lord or master on account of the holy observance of the
determinate time. Can he endure these evils with spiritual joy because of a
mere determination of the church? And can this be said to belong to his
Christian liberty, when it lacks a more solid foundation?

Seventh, if the church possesses this power, it possesses it either only with
respect to the members of the church or also with respect to all other human
beings. But neither can be said. Therefore, and so forth. The major premise is
certain. The minor is proved in parts. First, in the preceding chapter we
proved the latter false. As for the former: if this power belongs to the rulers
of the church only with respect to members of the church, all others who are
outside will either have determinate time or not. The latter cannot be said
because of what was said above concerning the necessity of this determination
or of solemn time. Nor can the former be said, because that time will be
determined for them either by their own pagan magistrates—which we proved false
above—or by

[1674 logical printed p. 299 | IA leaf 317 | PDF p. 317]

God. If by God, then this was done either through the law of nature, which the
adversaries will not grant, or through Scripture. If through Scripture, will
these Scriptures not also bind the members of the church? Who will deny this
if he recalls that the Scriptures were given especially to the church?

Eighth, if the church possesses the power to determine principal and solemn,
necessary and sufficient time, then the church can determine either that one
whole day be observed, that several days be observed together, or that some
period shorter than a day be holily observed. But none of these can be said.
Therefore, and so forth. The major premise is certain because the enumeration
is complete. We prove the minor in parts. First, the church cannot determine
one whole day, because the adversaries maintain that a part of the Jewish
burden, from which Christ redeemed us by his blood, consists in this. Because
we urge the observance of a whole day, they attack us rather bitterly. And
indeed, if the duration of a whole day was abolished together with the seventh
day, who gave the church the audacity to resume and reestablish this now
abrogated determination? Second, the church cannot prescribe and determine
some longer period to be spent together, because the adversaries themselves
say that one day is by no means necessary. Therefore, a period of two days in
some cycle will be much less necessary and is not to be determined by the
church. Third, neither can it determine a period shorter than a day. For let us
suppose that the church determines a whole day except for two or three hours.
How will it be proved that those two or three hours are not just as necessary
as the remaining time of the day? Or how will it be proved that the remainder
is sufficient? Will they say that the time spent in public worship is
sufficient? Then a period of two or three hours will be sufficient. And if it
is sufficient, is it also necessary? Some will affirm that those few hours are
by no means necessary. And how will the church demonstrate either this or that?
Furthermore, we showed above that time is not sufficient in which one is not
permitted to worship God not only publicly, but also in private families and
alone in one's chamber, and to perform the duties belonging to these parts or
kinds of worship.

Ninth, if this power has been granted to the church, then it can determine one
day in a cycle of seven days, in a cycle of more days, or in a cycle of fewer
days. But none of these can be said. Therefore, and so forth. The major premise
is certain because there is no fourth alternative. We prove the minor in parts
as follows. It cannot determine one day in a cycle of seven because the
adversaries maintain that this was abolished together with the seventh day.
Therefore, the church can no more determine one out of seven than the seventh.
Will it be in the church's power to restore a number rejected and abrogated by
God? What is it to resist God's authority and contradict his wisdom, if this
is not? According to our adversaries, do Romans 14, Galatians 4, and Colossians
2 not condemn one out of seven just as much as the seventh? And if the church
can restore these ceremonial and typical things—for the adversaries speak of
them this way—and recall them from banishment, why not also all the other
elements of the world? Where, then, is our Christian liberty? Would this not
be to give offense both to those who still wish the seventh day to be observed
and to us who maintain that the observance of one day in the weekly cycle is
moral? It cannot determine one day in the recurrence of more days, because
then it could prescribe that only one day every year, every two or four years,
or every century be observed. But how then can it

[1674 logical printed p. 300 | IA leaf 318 | PDF p. 318]

be demonstrated that such time is sufficient? And why has this not been done
so that our liberty under the gospel might be greater? It cannot determine a
day in a smaller cycle—that is, one in a recurrence of three, four, or five
days—because our Christian liberty would thus be diminished even more,
according to the adversaries themselves. Nor can it ever be demonstrated that
such time is necessary. And where is the zeal of the church, which to this very
day has not determined such a time?

Tenth, if the church has been granted the power to dedicate whatever time it
wishes to God and determine it for his worship, then all our time is our own.
But this is absurd. Therefore. The major premise is certain, because if all
time has not been given to human beings, how could they be free to consecrate
this or that, as they please, to God and his worship? How, unless on the
supposition of this dominion, could the church determine that a day or hour in
the weekly cycle, in a year, or in a century be spent on divine things? The
minor is confirmed as follows. 1. Then in practice absolutely no time would
belong to God except by human indulgence and free grant. What could be more
absurd? 2. It is certain that all time is originally from God and that God has
absolute power over our time. It is not ours to deprive God of his privilege.
And if God has granted human beings or the church power over all time, this
must be distinctly revealed. But we have no revelation about the matter. 3.
No domestic servant is the absolute lord of his time so that he can say he is
not bound to serve his lord except as long, as often, and when he himself
wishes. What? Is the dominion of a lord or master over his servants greater
than God's dominion over us? 4. Then the human being will have been created
more for himself as his ultimate end than for God, and more for caring for his
body than his soul—more for caring for his skin than for promoting God's glory
or his soul's eternal salvation—if all time has been given to the human being
and to caring for things belonging to the body, while nothing has been reserved
for or is to be given to God and the soul except by the mere indulgence of
human beings, who usually savor earthly things more than spiritual things. 5.
Then God showed greater care for the eternal salvation of the Jews than for
ours under the gospel, because he prescribed to the Jews time for promoting
the soul's eternal good and did not leave it to their judgment to determine.
But he determined no time for us and, although we are quite corrupt, left us
free to decide whether by mere indulgence we wish to assign any time to our
own souls. 6. The following divine precepts intimate and indeed demonstrate
something very different: *Love God with all your heart, all your soul, and all
your strength*, and so forth. *Pray without ceasing.* *Pray with every prayer
and supplication at every time, and keep watch for this very thing with all
perseverance* (Ephesians 6:18). *Seek first the kingdom of God and his
righteousness* (Matthew 6:33). *Therefore do not be anxious about your soul,
what you will eat or drink, nor about your body, with what you will be clothed.
Look at the birds of heaven, for they neither sow nor reap nor gather into
barns*, and so forth (Matthew 6:25–26, 33). *Be anxious about nothing, but in
everything by prayers and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be
made known before God* (Philippians 5:6). *Set your mind on things above, not
on earthly things* (Colossians 3:2). *Their end is destruction, their god is
their belly, and they mind earthly things. But we conduct ourselves as citizens
of heaven* (Philippians 3:19–20). *Those who are according to the flesh savor
the things of the flesh—for what the flesh savors is death* (Romans 8:5–7).
*We are debtors*

[1674 logical printed p. 301 | IA leaf 319 | PDF p. 319]

*not to the flesh, to live according to the flesh* (ibid., verse 12), *for if
you live according to the flesh, you will die* (verse 13). *He who sows to his
flesh reaps destruction from the flesh* (Galatians 6:8). *Do not lay up
treasures on earth—but lay up treasures for yourselves in heaven*, and so forth
(Matthew 6:19–20). *Do not love the world or the things that are in the world;
if anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him* (1 John 2:15).
There are many similar passages which teach that heavenly things are to be
preferred far above earthly ones and eternal things above worldly ones, and
therefore that more time ought to be spent on the former than on the latter,
unless divine indulgence had determined otherwise.

Eleventh, the church is not permitted to institute any annual feast day
dedicated to Christ, angels, or saints, and to enjoin that it be spent holily.
Much less will it be permitted to institute this principal and solemn,
necessary and sufficient time. The consequence is certain, for one who is not
permitted to do what is less is much less permitted to do what is greater.
But it is far greater to institute principal and solemn time recurring every
week than to institute a day recurring only once each year. The antecedent
will be proved in the following chapter.
