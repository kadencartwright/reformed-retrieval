### CHAPTER III

#### On Editions of Holy Scripture, Book 2

##### 1. On the Hebrew Edition, Chapter 2

###### THESIS I

Bell.: *The heretics of this time, from hatred of the Vulgate edition,
attribute too much to the Hebrew edition. For they wish absolutely everything
to be examined and emended according to the Hebrew text.*

Prot.: 1. That we do not do this from hatred of the Vulgate edition is apparent
from the fact that we contend that all other editions of the Old Testament
must be emended according to the Hebrew text just as much as the Vulgate. 2. No
one of sound mind ever thought that it was excessive to examine a transcribed
copy according to the first exemplar—the copy according to the archetype. 3.
The Vulgate edition must be examined either according to the Hebrew text, or
according to some other edition, or according to none. The Roman Catholics
will not provide another; to establish none, however, is to make the Latin the
first and to rave without any reason.

2. Bell.: *The Hebrew edition is corrupt in Isaiah 9:6, where “he will call” is
read for “he will be called”; also in Jeremiah 23:6, “he will call” for “they
will call”; Psalm 22:17, “like a lion” for “they pierced”; Psalm 19:5,
“delineation” for “sound,” as it is cited in Romans 10. After verse 22 in
Exodus 2, a verse is missing.*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 28 | printed p. 18 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Prot.: In Isaiah 9 the meaning is the same whether “he will call” or “he will
be called” is read. Nothing is also more customary among the Hebrews than to
use the third person of a verb impersonally, so that it can sometimes be
rendered actively and sometimes passively. The reasoning is similar for
Jeremiah 23. In Psalm 22:17 there is no corruption, but a variant reading in
certain copies through an error of scribes, as Bellarmine later admits.
Concerning Psalm 19, Genebrard answers on that passage that the apostle
expressed the sense of the word rather than its genuine and proper meaning.
That verse in Exodus 2 was inserted into Latin editions by some error from
Exodus 18:4. Since, therefore, nothing of the kind is found in these five
passages which Bellarmine selected to demonstrate the corruption of the Hebrew
text, it must be regarded as admitted that the Hebrew fountain remains pure
and uncorrupted.

3. Bell.: *There are others who, from zeal that is indeed good, but I do not
know whether it is according to knowledge, contend that the Jews deliberately
corrupted many passages of Scripture.*

Prot.: How perverse Bellarmine is here! Previously he said that the purity of
the Hebrew text was defended from evil hatred, but he wishes its rash
impugnment to proceed from good zeal. No Christian denies that the authentic
Hebrew text is—or at least was—the Word of God. What good zeal, however, can
there be which contends against the honor of God's Word? We read in Scripture
of people kindled with good zeal for the law of God, but good zeal against the
law is peculiar here to the Roman Catholics.

4. Bell.: *The Hebrew Scriptures have not been universally corrupted by the
work or malice of the Jews. But they have certain errors of their own, which
partly crept in through the negligence or ignorance of copyists. It can also
happen that, from a depraved disposition and hatred of Christ, the reading
which favored Christians less was sometimes more approved by them. Yet in the
things which pertain to faith and good morals they are intact.*

Prot.: 1. Bellarmine does not act sincerely when he denies only that the
Scriptures have been corrupted universally; for in this way he hands over the
whole case to the adversaries who maintain that they are corrupted not
universally, but in many passages. 2. Errors which have crept into certain
copies do not render

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 29 | printed p. 19 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

the edition in every copy corrupt, especially since those very errors can be
discerned by skilled persons. 3. Care of Scripture has been entrusted to the
Church. How, then, is it that the Roman Church has not preserved it pure from
every error? Why also does it not undertake the work of restoring it, since it
cannot err in this? 4. His statement that it can happen that the Scriptures
have somewhere been corrupted by the Jews from hatred of Christ will be
sufficiently refuted by Bellarmine himself in the second opinion, with other
arguments and especially the argument from the incredible reverence of the
Jews for the sacred books. Concerning the ancients Philo writes *that over a
period of two thousand years not even one word was ever changed in the law;
and that any Jew would die a hundred times rather than allow the law to be
changed in anything; but recent Jews almost adore the law as a deity.* He also
refutes this in the present passage when he concedes that the Hebrew Scripture
is intact in the things which pertain to faith. For if the Jews corrupted
anything from hatred of Christ, it undoubtedly pertained to Christ, and thus
to faith. This is Bellarmine's own earlier argument: *If the Jews had wished
to falsify the divine Scriptures from hatred of Christians, they would
undoubtedly have removed the chief prophecies. But they certainly did not do
this; indeed, the Hebrew codices often trouble the Jews more than the Greek or
Latin ones do.*

##### 2. On the Version of the Seventy Interpreters, Chapter 6

5. Bell.: *It is most certain that the seventy interpreters translated
excellently and had the Holy Spirit assisting them in a special manner, lest
they err in any matter.*

Prot.: What the perfection and authority of this version once was does not
greatly concern us, since Bellarmine himself admits that it no longer exists
except so corrupted and vitiated that it seems to be an entirely different
one.

##### 3. On the Greek Edition of the New Testament

6. Bell.: *The Greek codices are not the purest fountains, such that whatever
disagrees with them must necessarily be corrected, as Calvin falsely thinks,
and so forth.*

Prot.: 1. If neither the Hebrew codices of the Old Testament nor the Greek
codices of the New Testament are pure fountains, then there is no pure
Scripture in the Church of God. 2. This is affirmed with great disgrace to the
Roman

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 30 | printed p. 20 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Church, which says that it is the sole depositary of this treasure and yet did
not preserve it in its purity. 3. If something is now lacking in this edition
which formerly existed, it must at least be numbered among the apostolic
traditions which the Roman Church has in ready possession, and thus it can and
must easily restore it for us. For *Holy Scripture exists chiefly in the heart
of the Church and secondarily in books*, Bannes on Thomas, part 1, question 1,
article 8.

7. Bell.: *In 1 Corinthians 15:47 the Greek reads, “the second man, the Lord
from heaven,” from Marcion's corruption, as is evident from Tertullian's book
5 Against Marcion. It ought to read, “the second man from heaven, heavenly.”*

Prot.: 1. The meaning is the same whether “Lord” or “heavenly” is read, because
the Lord is designated as heavenly: “the Lord.” 2. The antithesis is stronger
and the meaning more effective if “Lord” is read. 3. This reading is approved
both by other most ancient Fathers and by Tertullian himself, whom Bellarmine
falsely cites to the contrary in that same book 5, chapter 10. He says: *“The
first man is from the earth, earthly; the second is the Lord from heaven.” Is
the first also Lord, if the second is?* There he defeats Marcion from this
reading.

8. Bell.: *In 1 Corinthians 7:33, μεμέρισται, which is read “is divided” in
the Latin version, is joined in the Greek to what follows: “the wife and the
virgin are distinct.”*

Prot.: 1. The Syriac and Arabic have the same reading. 2. The sense is more
fitting through this reading, and a redundancy is avoided which must
necessarily be admitted through the other reading, either of “the unmarried
woman” or of “the virgin.”

9. Bell.: *In Romans 12:11 the Greeks do not have κυρίῳ, “serving the Lord,”
but καιρῷ, “the time.”*

Prot.: This is plainly false if it is affirmed of all Greek copies, and empty
if only of some.

10. Bell.: *In very many Greek codices, many true parts of Scripture are
absent, such as the story of the adulteress in John 8, the last chapter of
Mark, and the seventh verse of 1 John 5.*

Prot.: 1. If it is granted that these are absent from very many codices, it
does not follow from this that all Greek copies are corrupt. 2. They are not
absent from those Greek codices which we regard as authentic.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 31 | printed p. 21 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

11. Bell.: *In Matthew 6:13, those words “for yours is the kingdom, and the
power, and the glory, forever” are not from the text but were added by the
Greeks. 1. Various Latin Fathers who understood Greek make no mention of them
in their exposition of the Lord's Prayer. 2. In the Greek liturgy these words
are not continued with the Lord's Prayer.*

Prot.: 1. These words were not added to the Greek text but removed from the
Latin version, as Valla observes. 2. Without these words, there is no model of
praising God in the Lord's Prayer. 3. They fit the Prayer itself so sacredly
that they savor of something greater than human ingenuity. 4. The authority of
Greek speakers, especially concerning the Greek text, is greater than that of
Latin speakers who say nothing. As for the Greek liturgy, let more recent
Greeks see to it.

##### 4. On the Latin Vulgate Edition

12. Bell.: *Although the Vulgate edition is for the most part Jerome's, it is
not, however, that pure version which he himself translated from the Hebrew,
but is mixed in some manner, with not a few things added from the version of
the Seventy which are not found in the Hebrew, chapter 9.*

Prot.: How large a part of it is Jerome's does not greatly matter. We accept
what Bellarmine gives and infer from it: 1. If the whole is not Jerome's, then
the Roman Catholics must not misuse Jerome's authority, as they are
accustomed, to confirm the authority of this whole edition. 2. If the whole is
not Jerome's, then those parts which belong to others were either translated
more accurately than Jerome translated them, or more corruptly. If more
accurately, then Jerome did not translate in the best way; if more corruptly,
then this whole edition is not of the highest grade among Latin versions. 3.
When we contend that many mixtures and corruptions of doctrine and rites
gradually crept into the Church, the Roman Catholics press us to demonstrate
precisely when, by whom, and amid whose opposition these things were done. Let
them therefore say when this mixture of the Latin version was introduced, by
whom, and with whom resisting.

13. Bell.: *We prove by these arguments that the Latin Vulgate edition must be
regarded as authentic according to the decree of the Council of Trent,
chapter 10.*

Prot.: But reason requires that it first be explained to us

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 32 | printed p. 22 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

what the Roman Catholics mean by an authentic edition, and how far they
ascribe this to the Latin Vulgate. In civil law, an authentic instrument is
properly that very thing which is commonly called the original—hence an
authentic record in law 4, *On the Family*. But they will by no means venture
to affirm this about the Latin edition. Yet they will say that it is an
authentic copy because it was transcribed from the original without error.
Here it must first be considered whether those men of Trent were public
notaries appointed by God, so that they could give certain credibility to
exemplars or copies, as they call them, especially to translations of
exemplars. Next, whether they did this from certain knowledge, with a suitable
examination instituted and a comparison made with the authentic and original
exemplar—something not even the Roman Catholics themselves will affirm. This
also needs explanation: whether they maintain that absolutely no error was
admitted into it through the translator's ignorance or fault, or instead no
error against faith and good morals. For the Roman Catholics differ about
these things and fight among themselves. Bellarmine, however, without
explaining the sense in which he takes an authentic edition, rather absurdly
sets about proving that the Vulgate is nevertheless authentic. Let us see what
sort of proofs he offers.

14. 1. Bell.: *This edition was approved by the long use of centuries.*

Prot.: 1. The use of a thing proves nothing other than that it is, or appears
to be, useful, which is not denied about this edition. 2. For some centuries
the Church used other Latin editions which Jerome shows to have been corrupt,
and the Roman Catholics do not deny it. But use, utility, and blind custom held
the chief place in directing the decrees of Trent. Thus the canonical books
are approved *as they have customarily been read in the Catholic Church*, and
that edition is pronounced authentic *which has been approved by the long use
of so many centuries in the Church*, Council of Trent, session 4. 3. The fact
that it was not regarded as authentic until after long use proves from this
very fact that it is not now authentic. For if it had been of such perfection
and authority, the more ancient Church, Damasus the Roman bishop who favored
Jerome's endeavors, and his immediate successors would have declared it such
at that time, when it was more uncorrupted,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 33 | printed p. 23 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

but it is certain that this was not done.

15. 2. Bell.: *It has the testimony of the ancients.*

Prot.: Not at all of the most ancient, nor the testimony of the ancients that
it is equally authentic with the Hebrew and Greek.

16. 3. Bell.: *The Hebrews had authentic Scripture in their language, and the
Greeks in Greek; therefore the Latins also have their own in Latin.*

Prot.: 1. By this reasoning it will follow that Germans, Frenchmen, and
Englishmen also have authentic Scripture in their own languages. 2. The Greek
version of the Old Testament was never so authentic that it did not need to be
emended according to the Hebrew text. These are Jerome's words: *The seventy
interpreters must be granted pardon as men, according to the statement of
James, who says, “We all sin in many things,”* to Pammachius. *It would take
long to unfold how many things the Seventy added of their own and how many they
omitted.* 3. From this it would also follow that all the other Latin editions
which the Latin Church used before Jerome's age were authentic.

17. 4. Bell.: *The Church would be badly provided for if in serious matters
it could not trust the Latin edition, because in general councils very few, or
sometimes none, are found who are skilled in the Hebrew language.*

Prot.: 1. If the matter stands so, God's providence must not be accused for
this, but human negligence. 2. Yet the Church has been provided for in such a
way that it has always had versions sound in the more serious matters, to which
credence could be given in their own manner and degree. With diligence also
employed, the Church could always consult the fountains in some way. 3. If
councils are of such a sort, then they are not fit to discern and define
authentic editions.

18. 5. Bell.: *The heretics forge editions so diverse and discordant among
themselves that almost nothing certain can be obtained from their editions.*

Prot.: 1. Does it therefore follow that the Vulgate edition is authentic? 2.
Nothing new has happened in this matter. Augustine testifies in book 2, chapter
11 of *On Christian Doctrine* that before Jerome's time the Latin editions
were almost innumerable. His words are cited by Bellarmine in chapter 8. Yet
Augustine did not infer from this, as Bellarmine does, that nothing certain
could be obtained, much less that

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 34 | printed p. 24 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

the Vulgate was authentic, but said in these words: *This circumstance indeed
aided understanding more than it hindered it.*

We see the wretched arguments by which the Vulgate edition is proved
authentic. The answers by which it is defended from the opposing arguments
are plainly of a piece with them.

19. Prot.: Jerome himself censures many things which are in the Vulgate
edition.

Bell., chapter 9: *There are four causes why certain things which Jerome
censures are in our text: an error of copyists; the varied meaning of words;
that Jerome changed his opinion in certain things; and that the Church judged
that certain things which Jerome wished to be changed should not be changed.*

Prot.: The whole of what was objected is therefore conceded—namely, that
Jerome censures many things which are in the Vulgate edition. 1. Let the Roman
Catholics show us which Church pronounced against Jerome and in which council.
It is not enough to say with Bellarmine, *It certainly does not seem to have
been done by chance, but by the judgment of posterity, or rather of the Church
itself*, since no such judgment can be shown.

20. Prot.: The Latin edition is by various translators who could err.

Bell.: *The translators could err, for Jerome acknowledges that he erred; and
Theodotion, to whom part of this edition is owed, was a heretic. But they did
not err in that version which the Church approved, chapter 11.*

Prot.: 1. Bellarmine relates remarkable things here. Jerome could err and
acknowledges that he erred, but nevertheless he did not err. Theodotion was a
heretic, but the Church approved his version; therefore, he did not err in
that version. 2. The Council of Trent's approval could not cause those
translators, who lived a thousand years before that council was held, not to
have erred. If they merely declared that they did not err, then the translators
themselves must nevertheless have completed this work through some special
direction and impulse of God which corrected human weakness, and this fact
must have been known to the men of Trent, although it was unknown to the
translators themselves—a great paradox indeed. But it is mitigated in some
manner by Bellarmine when he shows how far the men of Trent approved this
version.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 35 | printed p. 25 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

21. Bell.: *The Church did not canonize these authors, but only approved this
version. Yet it did not approve it in such a way as to assert that no copyists'
errors are found in it, but wished to render us certain that in this version
there are no translators' errors, especially in those things which pertain to
faith and morals.*

Prot.: 1. The present passage does not concern canonizing these authors in
their persons, but the question is whether their version was canonized,
something which Bellarmine dares neither to deny nor affirm. 2. There is no
question about copyists' errors, but about translators' errors. 3. Bellarmine
speaks far too ambiguously when he says that the Church established that they
did not err, *especially in those things which pertain to faith and morals.*
For the Roman Catholics are divided into two parties concerning the meaning of
the Tridentine decree, a disagreement which also existed among those who made
the decree. Some understand the decree's words so as to think that the Vulgate
edition was pronounced accurate and authentic in everything, even the smallest
matters. But others contend that it was decreed to be such only in matters
pertaining to faith and morals. Bannes on Thomas, part 1, question 1, article
8. The simpler people, and those who are most superstitious toward the Church's
decisions, adhere to the former opinion; most of the more learned favor the
latter. This question was agitated with such heat and commotion of minds, and
such implacable partisan hatred, especially in Spain, that from the reproaches
and insults with which they mutually defiled one another it came to tribunals;
many who thought more freely were compelled to plead their case in chains and
finally to cross into the other camp. Mariana, *For the Vulgate Edition*,
chapter 1. Bellarmine therefore acts more cautiously than chastely when he does
indeed intimate which party he belongs to, but meanwhile provides an escape
for himself in the word “especially.” For his words can be taken as though
these translators were indeed free from error in all the parts and particles
of their version, but were placed even farther beyond the danger of error in
the things which concern faith and morals. 4. The men of Trent speak without
any limitation: *In public readings, disputations, preaching, and expositions
let it be regarded as authentic, so that no one may dare or presume to reject
it on any pretext.* Although they could easily have added

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 36 | printed p. 26 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

a limitation of this kind by the word “especially,” no distinction should be
made where the law does not distinguish. 5. Who will judge what all those
things are which pertain to faith and morals? 6. If he means nothing other than
that no heresy is taught in this version, then Bellarmine himself will not deny
that other versions, both Latin and vernacular, especially those which Roman
Catholic men published, are authentic.

22. Prot.: The Fathers everywhere teach that the Latin editions must be
emended according to the Hebrew and Greek fountains.

Bell.: *There are four occasions for recurring to those fountains: 1. If there
is an error of copyists. 2. When the Latin codices vary. 3. When the words or
the meaning in the Latin are ambiguous. 4. For understanding the force and
proper meaning of words.*

Prot.: 1. In the Roman Catholics' opinion, these same occasions exist for
recurring from the Hebrew and Greek codices to the Latin ones. They therefore
contain nothing peculiar to the fountains. 2. The question concerns these
occasions for consulting the fountains critically. But when something is
stated or signified in the version which is not stated in the fountain, and
when something is found in the fountain which is not found in the version,
which edition must then be preferred? Here the Fathers pronounce for the
fountains, as all reason advises.

23. To linger over individual passages in order to show that this Latin
edition has corrupted many passages of Scripture would be to accumulate
unnecessary proofs in a matter not in doubt. The more learned Roman Catholics
who possess any sense of shame by no means deny this: Cajetan, Isidore Clarius,
Mariana, Johannes Benedictus, the Louvain theologians, Arias Montanus, and so
forth. To them can be added Clement VIII, the Roman Pontiff himself, who from
the Louvain edition corrected the Vulgate version according to the fountains
in a thousand passages, even against the reading confirmed by Sixtus V. Common
experience also teaches that all Greek authors who survive in Greek—Homer,
Plato, Aristotle, Hippocrates, Galen, to say nothing of the Greek Fathers—are
preserved far more purely and accurately in Greek editions than in Latin
versions. *Is God concerned for oxen?* Certainly a person must be utterly
senseless who will not assign

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 37 | printed p. 27 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

to the original text of Scripture, above all versions, at least the same place
and rank which profane monuments of human beings possess.

##### On Vernacular Editions

24. Bell.: *The Catholic Church does not prohibit vernacular translations
altogether, as is clear from rule 4 of the index of prohibited books published
by Pius IV. Yet it is prohibited that reading of this kind be granted
indiscriminately to everyone everywhere, and that in the public and common use
of the Church the Scriptures be read or sung in vernacular languages.*

Prot.: 1. That vernacular editions are unwelcome to the Roman Church is clear
from the fact that in those places where its decrees most prevail, as in
Spain, Roman Catholic men do not procure such editions, and they persecute
with fire and sword those procured by others. 2. Concerning the index of Pius
IV and the mind of the Roman Church about this business, we have clear
testimony in the Jesuit Azor's *Moral Institutes*, book 8, chapter 26. In the
index recently published by order of Clement VIII this observation was added:
*It must be observed concerning the above-written fourth rule of Pius IV that
by this printing and edition no power is newly assigned to bishops or others
to grant permission to buy, read, or retain Bibles published in the vernacular
language, since until now the power to grant permissions of this sort has been
removed from them by the command and practice of the holy Roman and universal
Inquisition.* Azor later adds: *At first sight, in the aforesaid fourth rule
the only prohibition is that sacred Bibles in the vernacular language not be
granted indiscriminately everywhere*—Bellarmine wished to deceive the unwary
reader under the fallacy of this first appearance. *In Spain, however, Bibles
in the vernacular language are prohibited with all their parts.* In Clement
VIII's index, and then: *The Gospels and Epistles which are publicly read and
chanted throughout the course of the year during the solemnities of Masses are
not permitted in the Spanish index to be printed separately and apart in the
vernacular language. Books of hourly prayers composed in the vernacular
language, which contain Psalms and Canticles excerpted from the Bibles, are
also prohibited; and besides these, summaries and even historical abridgments
of the same Bibles or books of Holy Scripture composed in any vernacular idiom
whatsoever.*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 38 | printed p. 28 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

25. Bell.: *We are content with those three languages which the Lord honored
by the inscription of his cross, John 19, which surpass all the others. In
these the divine books themselves were originally written by their authors:
Hebrew, Greek, and Latin.*

Prot.: 1. The Lord did not honor these languages by the inscription of the
cross, but Pilate rather contaminated them by this abuse. 2. These languages
were employed in the inscription of the cross so that everyone present would
understand Pilate's meaning. By this reasoning, therefore, all the languages
which are most widely understood by people ought to be used before others. 3.
These languages possess no such excellence that they are not surpassed by
other vernacular languages in fitness for the understanding and use of those
who understand the other languages and not these. We leave other excellences
for grammarians to consider. A golden sword is more excellent than an iron
one, but in battle the soldier demands an iron sword. 4. The divine books were
not written in the Latin language by their authors, and other languages were
also sanctified by those same authors for sacred uses, Acts 2:6.

26. Bell.: *From the time of Ezra, the Hebrew language ceased to be
vernacular. For the people understood nothing from the law without
interpretation, Nehemiah 8. In the time of Christ the Jews also spoke Syriac,
as is apparent from the Gospel, and yet in the temples and synagogues they
publicly read or sang Scripture only in Hebrew, just as the Jews also do in
synagogues today. John 7:49 also pertains to this.*

Prot.: 1. What is said about the time of Ezra—that the people did not then
speak or understand Hebrew—is plainly false. For Haggai, Zechariah, and
Malachi preached to them in Hebrew after the return from captivity, as their
books testify. Nehemiah complains that certain children of the Jews born from
Ashdodite women did not know how to speak Jewish, that is, Hebrew, chapter 13,
verse 24. But the interpretation mentioned in chapter 8 was not a translation,
but a sermonic exposition. 2. As to later ages, although the Jews did not speak
pure Hebrew, but used a mixed idiom, it was nevertheless Hebrew to so great an
extent that it is designated simply by that name, John 19:20; Acts 21:40;
22:1; and 26:14. Yet they understood the Scriptures

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 39 | printed p. 29 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

written in Hebrew, just as today the Scots, although they do not speak pure
English, nevertheless have only an English version of Scripture and understand
it. 3. In that passage, John 7:49, Bellarmine very plainly plays the sophist.
The common people are said there not to have known the law, not because they
were ignorant of its letters and syllables, but because the Pharisees
arrogantly declared that its sense was hidden from them. For a comparison is
made there between the Pharisees and the crowd of the people. The boast in
which the Pharisees gloried was not that they could read the words of the law,
but that they knew the mind of the law. The people are therefore not charged
or reproached by them for not knowing how to read Hebrew, but for not knowing
the sense of the law, which they could have grasped even without reading from
the Pharisees' instruction, at least in the Pharisees' judgment.

27. Bell.: *The apostles preached the Gospel throughout the whole world, and
yet did not write in the languages of the nations to which they preached.*

Prot.: 1. It cannot be certainly demonstrated that the apostles wrote nothing
in vernacular languages; they were debtors to barbarians just as much as to
Greeks. 2. If they themselves did not do this, it does not follow that they
were unwilling for others to do it. Indeed, they in some manner commanded it
when they wished all the gifts of Christians to be applied to the greatest
edification of the Church. 3. If certain churches lacked vernacular versions
for some period, they ought not therefore always to lack them. The condition
of the churches of God would then be extremely unequal in the things which
conduce to salvation. 4. At least in the age immediately after the apostles,
Scripture was poured into various vernacular languages—Syriac, Arabic,
Ethiopic, and so forth. Since this was done by holy men, they certainly thought
that they followed the apostles' mind in this matter. 5. It was done by God's
supreme providence that the apostles' own writings did not survive in any
language other than one, just as the prophetic books of the Old Testament
survive in one language, because otherwise disputes would have arisen among
the churches of various nations concerning the authority of their copies.

28. Bell.: *The whole Church always used the Hebrew, Greek, and Latin*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 40 | printed p. 30 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*divine language in the common and public use of the Scriptures.*

Prot.: That this is most certainly false is apparent even from the fact that
the Scriptures were translated into so many languages. For why would they
publicly translate them if they did not publicly use those versions? Nor can
any appearance of probability be applied to the contrary opinion by any other
coloring except the fact that the most distinguished churches among the
primitive ones were either Greek or Latin, while few monuments of the rest
survive. Yet these are not so few that they do not refute and testify against
the falsity of that opinion.

29. Bell.: *For preserving the unity of the Church, it is entirely fitting
that the public use of the Scriptures be in some one very widely shared
language. Unless this is so, communion among the churches will be taken away;
no one will attend churches except in his own country; and general councils
will not be possible. Since, therefore, no language except Latin is now common
to the whole West, the divine Scriptures must certainly be read in it.*

Prot.: 1. The unity of the Church was preserved when converted Jews used their
own language in public sacred rites, while the Greeks used Greek and the
Latins Latin. 2. Communion among churches in various languages understood by
some can be exercised with greater edification than the communion of all the
members of one church in one language unknown to them all. 3. If no one would
attend a church except in his own country because of the variety of languages,
then Roman Catholic commoners certainly have no reason to attend Mass in their
own country because of the foreignness of the language used there. 4. Nearly
all general councils were held despite the variety of languages, even in
public sacred rites, between the Greeks and Latins.

30. Bell.: *If the Scriptures were read in the vernacular language, the people
would nevertheless not understand.*

Prot.: 1. If he means that the people would understand nothing from the
reading of Scripture in the vernacular language, then he contradicts common
experience, condemns the most pious and ancient practice of the faithful, and
charges God's own command with futility—namely, Deuteronomy 31:11: *Read this
law before all Israel, that they may hear and learn and fear Jehovah.* But if
he denies that

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 41 | printed p. 31 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

the people understand everything, then what he says is true, but does nothing
for his case. Bell.: *Indeed, the people would receive more harm than benefit
from the Scriptures, because they would very easily take occasion to err.*
Prot.: 1. The people also took occasion to err and blaspheme from Christ's
humiliation. 2. By this reasoning, not even Latin Bibles would be permitted to
be sold publicly, because many who understand Latin take occasion to err from
reading them. 3. Greater regard must be had for God's elect, who are edified by
reading Scripture, than for those who distort it. 4. On this ground Peter
rouses the faithful to read Scripture with greater reverence, 2 Peter
3:14–17. 5. This reasoning has equal force against the Hebrew, Greek, and Latin
languages where they are or have been vernacular, unless he should deny that
those peoples can take occasion to err.

31. Bell.: *Vernacular languages change greatly in each century, and thus the
translations would have to be changed.*

Prot.: 1. Languages do not customarily change so much that writings which are
in public and perpetual use are not easily understood, if they sound in the
ears of the people without interruption. 2. There is no danger or disadvantage
if certain obsolete words are exchanged for familiar ones.

32. Bell.: *The majesty of divine offices seems to require a language more
solemn than the vernacular languages, especially since many things ought to be
secret in sacred mysteries. The Latin language, however, is more solemn and
more reverend because it is not vernacular.*

Prot.: 1. By similar reasoning, someone could conclude that Bibles and
liturgies ought not be printed with common type or on common paper. 2. So great
is the majesty of sacred things that they affect any language with a certain
reverend solemnity. 3. Although many things in the Roman Missal are shameful,
and on that account must be kept secret, nothing of the sort is found in sacred
offices agreeable to Scripture. 4. If the Latin language is more solemn and
reverend because it is not vernacular, then in Italy the English language will
be more solemn than Italian.

##### Our Arguments

33. Prot.: 1. From 1 Corinthians 14, these arguments offer

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 42 | printed p. 32 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

themselves to the reader: 1. Public sacred rites must not be celebrated in a
language which no one hears. But no one hears an unknown language, verse 2. 2.
They must also be celebrated in a language by which they can benefit and edify
their hearers. But in an unknown language they do not edify their hearers,
verse 4, nor benefit them, verse 6. 3. They must not be celebrated in such a
way that they give an uncertain sound, are recognized by no one, and are
scattered into the air. But this is how matters stand in an unknown language,
verses 7–9. 4. They must not be celebrated in such a way that the person
celebrating is a barbarian to the hearers and the hearers are also barbarians
to him. But this is so in an unknown language, verse 11. 5. They must not be
celebrated in such a way that they brand Christian hearers with the mark of
unbelief, but an unknown language does this, verse 22. 6. Public prayers must
not be uttered in such a way that the people cannot say Amen to them, but in an
unknown language they cannot, verse 16. 7. If it was not lawful in the Church
to use without interpretation even that unknown language which proceeded from
a miraculous gift of the Spirit and thus brought singular honor to God, it is
certainly much less lawful to use an unknown language formed by human effort.

Bell.: *In a large part of this chapter, the subject is not the reading of
Scripture or divine offices, but spiritual exhortation or conference.*

Prot.: The apostle's reasons are general and pertain equally to every public
discourse held in the Church.

Bell. 2: *In verses 14–16, the apostle treats spiritual songs made to praise
God and at the same time for consolation and edification. He therefore wishes
them to be made in a language which is understood.*

Prot.: 1. In verse 15 the apostle expressly says that he treats not only songs
but also prayers. 2. If the apostle wishes songs to be had in a known language
because they conduce to consolation and edification, then he also wishes
everything publicly done for consolation and edification to be done in a
known language—otherwise, there would be no consequence in his reasoning. But
all public offices are done for consolation and edification, verse 26.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 43 | printed p. 33 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Bell. 3: *At that time divine offices were conducted in the Greek language,
which many of the people understood, and this was sufficient. For the apostle
did not wish everyone to be able to respond, Amen.*

Prot.: 1. The apostle expressly wishes every unlearned person to understand,
verse 16, according to that statement of the law, *all the people shall say,
Amen*, Deuteronomy 27:2. Among the Roman Catholics sacred rites are performed
in such a language that often no one among the people can say Amen with
understanding.

Bell. 4: *At that time, because they were few, everyone sang psalms and
responded; afterward the offices were divided.*

Prot.: 1. If everyone responded at that time, then Bellarmine's recent
affirmation that the apostle did not wish everyone to respond is false. 2. The
small number of Christians was not the cause of this practice. For long before
this time, three thousand souls had been added to the Church in one day, Acts
2:41, and they thus praised God, verse 47. 3. No one is ignorant that this
division was afterward made; but no one has yet shown by what right apostolic
practice and divine institution were taken away.

Bell. 5: *The chief end of those songs was the instruction and consolation of
the people, an end which would have perished unless they had been made in a
known language or with interpretation. But the chief end of divine offices is
not the instruction or consolation of the people, but the worship of God; and
the things which the people ought to know from divine offices are explained by
the pastors.*

Prot.: 1. Bellarmine himself said before that those spiritual songs were made
to praise God and give him thanks, and at the same time for consolation, and
so forth. Their chief end therefore was the worship of God, just as much as
that of the other offices. 2. He absurdly separates those ends—the worship of
God and the consolation of the people—which God joined. 3. It must not be
allowed in the Church that even a less principal end of sacred offices should
perish. 4. Roman Catholic pastors are not accustomed to explain anything to
the people in the celebration of Mass; most of them cannot explain anything,
much less the things which the people ought to know.

Bell. 6: *Prayer benefits those by whom it is not understood, even those by
whom it is not heard, and indeed those who do not wish prayer to be made for
them; for God understands it.*

Prot.: 1. Public prayer benefits its hear-

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 44 | printed p. 34 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

ers in two respects. First, as a means of direction and arousal, by which they
are helped both to pray together in the present assembly and also to conceive
similar petitions at other times. This benefit belongs to every prayer made
with others. Second, as a means of obtaining, by which God's blessings are
obtained. This benefit belongs only to petition and does not properly regard a
petition as made with others, but also fits solitary petition. Now we affirm
with the apostle in 1 Corinthians 14 that the former benefit perishes;
Bellarmine denies that the latter is excluded. He therefore acts as a sophist,
not a theologian. 2. By this reasoning, prayer benefits those present no more
than those absent.

Bell. 7: *The Jews did not worship God in vain with ceremonies which they did
not understand.*

Prot.: 1. All the pious among the Jews understood that those ceremonies were
commanded by God. 2. They also understood how most of them were signs either
of confession of sins or of thanksgiving. 3. They understood in some measure
that the ceremonies looked toward the coming Messiah. 4. Greater understanding
is now required in Christian worship than formerly among the Jews. 5. The
Jews' understanding of words was also greater than their understanding of
ceremonies.

Bell. 8: *Many things occur while we sing psalms which we do not understand.*

Prot.: 1. We understand all the words. 2. Through the words we strive toward
knowledge of the things. 3. We understand the things themselves in part. 4. By
meditating while singing psalms, we seek fuller understanding.

Bell. 9: *In that passage in 1 Corinthians 14, prayer which is not understood
is not censured, but the prayer which is understood is preferred to it.*

Prot.: 1. We proved above that it is plainly censured. It is also apparent
that it is expressly prohibited, verse 28. 2. If prayer which is understood is
preferred, then the Roman Catholics adhere to the worse part, with great harm
to the Christian people.

Bell. 10: *Those who do not understand make progress in affection, even if not
in mind.*

Prot.: 1. They can make progress with a blind, confused,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 45 | printed p. 35 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

uncertain affection, which is often to regress. But they cannot assume the
affections which the occasion demands unless they understand the matter
proposed in some measure. 2. By “spirit” and “mind” in verses 14–15 are not
understood the speaker's affection and mind, as Bellarmine wishes, but the
spirit's inward operation in a person by itself, and also the communication of
that operation to others, for comparing their understanding with one another.

Bell. 11: *The fruit of the mind which is absent from the unlearned is
compensated by the many advantages of the Latin language.*

Prot.: The fruit of the mind which is absent is edification, consolation,
exhortation, the glorification of God, and finally salvation and eternal life.
If the use of the Latin language can compensate for the absence of these
fruits, it can certainly do more than the tongues of angels, 1 Corinthians
13:1.

Bell. 12: *Instruction, consolation, and edification are not the chief ends,
but the worship of God is.*

Prot.: 1. Things which God joined are separated. 2. The chief end of reading
Scripture consists in these things, for Scripture is read to human beings, not
to God.

Bell. 13: *Those who are not utterly unlearned know Latin and are instructed;
the rest would not be instructed even if everything were conducted in the
vernacular language. For instruction is not in the words but in the sense.*

Prot.: 1. Do all who are not stupid therefore know Latin? 2. Do all who know
Latin perceive the sense of Scripture? 3. Are there not many people ignorant
of the Latin language who would understand Scripture in the vernacular
language just as well as many people who know Latin understand it in the Latin
language?

Bell. 14: *The Scriptures are a consolation to the unlearned when they are
explained by preachers.*

Prot.: When Paul sent his epistles to the churches to be read before everyone,
he intended the consolation of everyone to be perceived even from the reading
itself, 1 Thessalonians 4:18. It would be remarkable if the apostle, who became
all things to all people and wrote to the less mature as to infants, 1
Corinthians 3:1, could write nothing to the unlearned from whose reading they
would perceive some consolation.

34. Prot.: The written Word of God must also exist in the

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 46 | printed p. 36 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

same language in which it is preached; but it is preached in vernacular
languages. Therefore.

Bell.: *The consequence is denied. 1. Because the apostles did not write in
the same languages in which they preached. 2. Because preaching contains an
explanation which everyone easily grasps, but in writing the Word is bare.
There the bread is cut up and pre-chewed; here it is whole and solid.*

Prot.: The apostles were discussed above. As to the matter itself: 1. It
follows from this that the majesty of the Word of God can coexist with
vernacular languages. It follows that the mighty works of God must be
expounded to the people in vernacular languages, Acts 2:11. It follows that
Scripture itself, with explanations of the sort which the apostles used in
their preaching, is usefully proposed in the vernacular language. But what
evil is there in writing more than in speech? 2. It is false that the whole
Word of God in the Scriptures is bare, without any explanation. 3. In the
Scriptures not only solid food but also milk is set forth. 4. Why could simple
people not profit from reading Scripture in other languages just as much as
the Jews formerly did in Hebrew and the Greeks in Greek?

35. Prot.: In the time of the Old Testament Church, Holy Scripture was poured
into many vernacular languages.

Bell. 1: *We do not deny that the Scriptures can be translated into vernacular
languages, but we deny that they ought to be read publicly in the vernacular
language or that everyone everywhere should be permitted to read them.*

Prot.: 1. We showed the contrary above from Azor. 2. If they ought not
everywhere to be read by the common people, then those who translate them into
vernacular languages so that they can be read everywhere place a stumbling
block before the blind. 3. Nowhere from Scripture does it appear that one
edition of Scripture was destined for public use and another for private use.

Bell. 2: *If Jerome and others translated Scripture into vernacular languages,
they did this for the consolation of those people who could use those versions
without danger, just as among us at this time it is granted to certain
people.*

Prot.: 1. Jerome expressly says that he gave the translation to people in
their own language, to Sophronius; and that the Scriptures were read by all
peoples so that all might understand them. On

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 47 | printed p. 37 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Psalm 86. Theodoret affirms that the Scriptures were translated into all
languages and that the nations used them, *On the Affections of the Greeks*,
book 5. Those words of Basil in *Ascetical Works*, question 95, are plainly
divine: *It is fitting and necessary for each person to learn from inspired
Scripture whatever is useful to him, for full assurance of piety and so as not
to become further accustomed to human traditions.* 2. God wills and commands
everyone who is able to read the Scriptures, or hear them read. The Roman
Catholics grant and permit this to certain people. Do they not deserve well of
God when they permit certain people to observe his command? 3. What do these
words “permit” and “grant” mean? Those words sound as though reading the
Scriptures were an evil thing, yet permitted to certain people for particular
reasons. Let the Roman Catholics show us either such a thing or such a phrase
in any ancient writer—namely, that by human permission and indulgence certain
Christians, in preference to others, read the Scriptures.

Bell. 3: *The Chaldean edition is not so much a version as a paraphrase.*

Prot.: If such a paraphrase in the vernacular language is to be admitted, why
not a version? But there were also Syriac and Arabic versions.

Bell. 4: *The Scriptures were translated into the Greek and Latin languages,
not because these languages were vernacular but because they were the most
widely shared.*

Prot.: If the most widely shared languages are sought in editions and versions
of Scripture, this is done for common use; but such use is introduced in no
way more easily than through vernacular languages. Furthermore, if a Greek
edition and its reading were granted to Ptolemy, a heathen man, those who do
not allow Christians to use the same liberty are criminally unjust to them.

35. Prot.: Chrysostom very frequently exhorts the common people to read the
Scriptures.

Bell. 1: *He perpetually exhorted everyone to read Scripture*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 48 | printed p. 38 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*because at that time people were devoted to theaters, spectacles, and other
trifles.*

Prot.: 1. Are people not now, at Rome and elsewhere, devoted to those same
trifles, and even to brothels—which are publicly permitted by the Roman
Pontiff where the use of Scripture in a known language is not permitted—just
as much as formerly? Why, then, are people not now perpetually roused to read
Scripture? 2. This, therefore, is the great honor which the Roman Catholics
give Scripture: that for passing the time people should occupy themselves in
reading it rather than in theaters, spectacles, and other trifles, just as
they permit brothels rather than more shameful lusts. 3. That invention by
which he evades Chrysostom's pious exhortations is utterly shameless. For
Chrysostom draws his arguments not from the amusements of theaters but from
the most serious benefits. In homily 9 on the Epistle to the Colossians: *If
sorrow, loss, death, or the loss of family members should occur, take from here
consolation for the evil.* In homily 3 *On Lazarus*: *You will render your
mind better instructed for hearing the things which will afterward be
discussed; from here take armor against every evil.* But when he raises for
himself the impediments by which his hearers were kept from reading Scripture,
he says nothing about theaters, but presents more serious things, which he
nevertheless calls cold words: *I am bound to legal cases; I conduct public
business; I practice a trade; I have a wife; I support children; I take care
of a household,* and so forth.

Bell. 2: *Chrysostom did not wish absolutely everyone, even the most
uneducated, to read Scripture, but at least those who could do so with
benefit.*

Prot.: Chrysostom himself answers in homily 3 *On Lazarus*: *The Holy Spirit
so arranged and tempered them that publicans, fishermen, tentmakers,
shepherds, apostles, uneducated and illiterate people, might be saved through
these books; so that no uneducated person could take refuge in this excuse of
difficulty; so that the things said might appear easy to everyone; and so that
the artisan, the servant, the widow, and the most unlearned of all people might
gain some profit and benefit from hearing the reading.*
