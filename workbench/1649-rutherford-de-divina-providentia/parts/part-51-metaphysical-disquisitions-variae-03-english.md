[Various Questions 18–20; printed page 592; IA PDF page 666; BSB canvas 642]

Question 18. Could a creature operate without the first cause concurring? I
answer negatively, because that action performed by a creature apart from God
would be an action conserved without God, since it was produced without him.
Thus, if one creature were conserved for an hour without God and did not fall
back into the original pure nonbeing, so also could all creatures be; and if
all beings could stand without God, they could have been created without him.

[Marginalia: An action of a second cause without the action of the first cause
is impossible.]

Second, this action which occurs without God would occur contrary to his will
and by chance. This action would be present, yet from eternity it had not been
future, because it was future by no decree of God, and no other cause of a
future thing is possible. Thus it would be present and yet never have been
future. Further, a present action would arise either with God knowing it or
without his knowing it. It would not arise with God knowing it, because God
would begin to know an action which he had not known before—or which he did not
know, which is the same thing. If it occurred without God knowing it, God would
not be God, because a creature would be about to do what God neither wills nor
knew. And so it would be no absurdity if several other worlds were created
against God’s will and without his knowledge. Indeed, if one action can occur
without God’s concurrence or providence, so can all of them: the Son of God
could have been crucified, the world could subsist, and everything that occurs
in heaven and on earth could occur without GOD’s providence and knowledge.

But given that things external to God have been created by him, God must by
necessity of nature exercise acts of knowledge, will, and active providence
toward them, because if GOD’s sustaining activity ceased, all things would be
reduced to nothing. Further, the efficient element which emanates from God
alone must be efficiently present in every action of a creature. This cannot
flow from a second cause unless the second cause were to take the place of the
first, which is impossible.

[Marginalia: God must premove the second cause to act, and it is impious here
to invent two simultaneous, collateral, and independent influxes, one from God
and one from creatures.]

Question 19. Do the Jesuits rightly teach that God moves the second cause to
act by a concurrence which is not prior but simultaneous? I answer: like the
wicked Davus, the Jesuits throw everything into confusion. For God

[Various Questions 18–20; printed page 593; IA PDF page 667; BSB canvas 643]

is the cause of the action of fire and of the habit of faith, both insofar as
that action is from God and insofar as it is from the second cause, because God
causes us to act (Ezekiel 36:27). The second cause, however, is the cause of the
action insofar as it is from itself, but it is not the cause of the second
cause’s action insofar as that action is from GOD.

First, we do not cause God to concur as he causes us to act. Second, there is
in God and in his concurrence something belonging to the infinite, principal,
and absolute causation of the first cause, which is proper to the Creator
alone. It is impossible that even the smallest part of GOD’s causation should
be taken away from him and ascribed to the creature, unless the creature
really acted upon the concurring God to produce his concurrence—which is
monstrous. Third, when two men carry the same stone by simultaneous operations,
neither premoves the other to act or to perform the act of carrying. But God
causes the sun to rise, the sea to ebb and flow, the eagle to fly, and the
saints to walk in GOD’s commandments, as Scripture testifies: Deuteronomy
30:6; Ezekiel 36:26; Jeremiah 31:31–34; 32:39–40; Job 37–38.

Question 20. The Jesuits say that God grants that a second cause should operate
without premoving it or placing anything real in the second cause. Is that not
sufficient? I answer: the Jesuits corrupt the youth throughout the Christian
world with wicked philosophy. Nor is this sufficient.

First, God cannot be the second cause’s efficacious ground for acting unless he
actually premoves the second cause to act. For to be operative habitually and
potentially is something real imparted to the second cause by the first,
whether when creating it or when newly cocreating with it. Therefore, much more
will its being an actual principle of operation derive from the first cause and
be something real placed in it by the first.

Second, on this account the action of the second cause will derive from God
only remotely, by virtue of simultaneous cooperation: God has imparted habitual
power to the second cause, while the second cause actualizes itself collaterally
and by a simultaneous motion and breaks forth into second act. It would be just
as when two people hurry to run at the same moment, neither one determining the
other to run, and neither rider driving the other’s horse with his spurs. How
little does what the Jesuits suggest here differ from Durandus’s position?
