## Chapter VIII

### Whether, once God’s permission has been posited, sin follows necessarily,
at least by necessity of consequence, though not by a causal nexus

#### I

This is the third question. The Jesuits teach that, once permission has been
posited, sin necessarily follows—not by the force of permission, but because of
the foreknowledge annexed to permission. Thus teach Fonseca, Lessius, Fasolus,
and Ruiz. The Remonstrant Arminians, Episcopius, Arnoldus Corvinus, and Jacob
Arminius state the matter differently. Corvinus says that “it is absurd for
God to establish a law according to that foreknowledge by which he knows that
a person will transgress it, because in this way the will is determined to one
thing.” And Arminius says that “God does not always propose an end to himself
on the basis of foreknowledge.” But on that account sin follows from permission
with no necessity.

Vorstius says that “a decree of permitting can exist even though sins never
occur”; therefore he says that “permission does not causally precede the
perpetration of sin, but follows it only as an adjunct.” But it is remarkable
that a person should sin before God permits him to sin, for a subject is prior
to its adjunct and an adjunct is subsequent to its subject. Therefore this
inference from Arminius is invalid: “This person has been permitted to sin;
therefore this person sins.”

*[Marginal references: Fonseca, Metaphysics, book 6, chapter 2, question 5,
section 7; Lessius, On the Divine Perfections, book 11, chapter 6; Fasolus, in
vol. 2, part 2, question 14, article 13, doubt 11, figure 143; Ruiz, On
Predestination, tractate 2, disputation 11, section 2, figure 3; Remonstrant
Confession, chapter 6, thesis 3; Episcopius, disputation 7, On Providence,
thesis 13; Corvinus, Against Molina, chapter 6, question 6; Arminius,
Anti-Perkins, p. 667, Bertius edition.]*

2. The most learned and distinguished Dr William Twisse censures Perkins and
Piscator because they taught that sin follows inferentially from permission.
Piscator argued in this way: “Permission and the thing permitted are
correlatives; but when one of two correlatives is posited, the other must also
be posited.” The most distinguished Dr Twisse retorts in the same manner:
“God willed the world from eternity”—since he decreed from eternity to create
the world—therefore it follows that “the world was willed from eternity.” But
this does not follow: “Therefore the world existed from eternity.” In the same
way this does not follow: “There is permission; therefore there is permitted
sin.”

2. The distinguished Twisse argues acutely, in his customary manner:
“Permitting is denying efficacious grace by which sin would be avoided. God
always denies the reprobate efficacious grace

*[Marginal references: Vorstius, Duplicate Friendly Reply to Piscator, pp.
211–212; Twisse, Vindication of Grace against the Arminian Anti-Perkins, book
2, digression 3, section 2, charge 3, figure 4; Perkins, On Predestination, p.
29; Piscator, To Vorstius’s Friendly Reply, p. 231.]*

[PDF p. 133; printed p. 81]

which, if given, would enable them to avoid fornication, murder, and other
crimes of that kind. But the conclusion does not follow: therefore the
reprobate always fornicate and always kill.” It may perhaps be easy to settle
such great disputes among such great men. Dr Twisse takes permission as the
denial of gracious efficiency, so that to permit is for God not to bring it
about that someone graciously abstains from sin. In this sense what Dr Twisse
presses is valid: “God always permits the reprobate to kill, but they do not
always kill.” For an affirmation does not follow from just any negation. A
puppy does not see before its ninth day, but it does not therefore follow that
the puppy is blind before its ninth day, because sight does not belong to it
by nature until after the ninth day.

But permission, as Perkins and Piscator take the word, is more than the bare
denial of gracious efficiency, for in this sense it adds something to that
denial. Nor is being permitted in this sense an eternal and immanent act in
God, like understanding and volition; rather, it connotes something more than
either the will to deny or the denial of gracious efficiency itself. Thus
permission, taken indefinitely and generically insofar as it is opposed to
efficiency, can perhaps be defined in that way. It cannot be so defined,
however, insofar as it is opposed both to efficiency and to prevention. In
that sense permission is a positive act of divine providence not to prevent
sin, together with the denial of gracious efficiency required here and now
for avoiding sin when an affirmative command binds here and now.

Hence the following inference is invalid: “God denies Peter, bound in deep
sleep, the efficacious grace by which he would pray; therefore Peter culpably
does not pray.” Neither the existence of sin in general nor a sin of a
particular kind follows from the mere denial of gracious efficiency. More is
required for the permission of fornication than the denial of gracious
efficiency. It requires the denial here and now of the gracious efficiency
required here and now, while temptation solicits and the command to abstain
from fornication binds here and now a person who is properly disposed,
watchful, healthy, and not ill.

*[Marginal note: Permission is not rightly defined as God’s bare
non-co-efficiency toward an act.]*

The denial of efficiency in general is perhaps more correctly called
desertion. In this desertion, as the most distinguished and learned Leiden
professors teach, “the sinner is permitted to sin rather than sin being
permitted to the sinner.” For if a particular individual efficiency of grace
necessarily concurs in individuating such a good act, then a particular denial
of the determinate efficiency required to avoid this sin here and now must
likewise concur in individuating such a non-act, while the command binds, for
a certain end which by God’s constitution is

*[Marginal reference: The Leiden professors in their Censure of the
Remonstrant Confession, chapter 6, section 3.]*

[PDF p. 134; printed p. 82]

appointed.

Thus Perkins, Piscator, the most learned and distinguished Dr Antonius Walaeus,
and all our theologians teach that, once permission has been posited, sin
always follows inferentially and is posited in the nature of things. But I
admit that sin does not always follow from God’s gracious non-co-efficiency.
For God denies to saints who are asleep, or perhaps listening to God’s Word,
the gracious concurrence by which they might pray if awake and not intent on
hearing God’s Word. Yet it does not follow that, while sleeping or hearing the
sermon, they culpably fail to pray.

*[Marginal reference: Walaeus against Corvinus, chapter 3, On Providence, p.
113.]*

Indeed, I assert that permission, as theologians commonly take it, is badly
defined as the denial of gracious efficiency. That denial frequently occurs
when affirmative commands do not bind—for they do not bind at all times—in
which case neither the holy nor the ungodly are formally permitted to sin,
because permission formally presupposes obligation. But when David is formally
permitted, he always and necessarily commits adultery by necessity of the
consequence, or inferentially, not by necessity of the consequent and by a
causal necessity. And whenever he is formally not permitted to sin, he always
and necessarily does not sin. Hence the following rules concerning the logical
connection between permission and the sin permitted.

*[Marginal note: Five rules necessary to know for discerning when the
inference from permission to the permitted sin is valid.]*

1. Rule. “Once permission has been posited in a capable subject, the inference
from it to habitual sin is always valid.” For example: “God permits”—that is,
he does not cure—“concupiscence in the regenerate; therefore concupiscence is
always present in the regenerate.” The inference is valid.

2. Rule. “Once permission has been posited in any moral act—that is, when God
denies the efficacious grace required for a more fervent intention in a good
act—there is necessarily a culpable defect in the good act.”

3. Rule. “If God punishes someone by judicial permission, the inference is
then valid.” Pharaoh was judicially permitted to harden his heart. Therefore
Pharaoh hardens his heart. This inference is as firm as it is certain that a
sinner cannot escape the punishment to be inflicted by the supreme Judge.

4. Rule. “Once permission has been posited insofar as it is opposed to
efficiency, the inference to the entailment of sin is invalid.” As Dr Twisse’s
arguments rightly prove: “A person is permitted to kill; therefore he kills.”
That does not follow.

5. Rule. “Once permission properly so called has been posited in a capable
subject, with a command binding here and now—that is, when for such an act God
denies his gracious influence from his special intention of directing the act
or the omission of the act toward an end preappointed by himself—sin always
follows as a necessary consequent from its antecedent.” Thus: “David was
permitted to commit adultery; therefore David commits adultery.” This follows
perfectly and necessarily. In this sense, the arguments

[PDF p. 135; printed p. 83]

of Dr Twisse against Perkins and Piscator do not establish their conclusion,
nor did the most distinguished Dr Twisse intend that they should. For the
following is no valid inference: “The world was willed by God from eternity;
therefore the world existed from eternity.” The reason is that the real
existence of the act that terminates God’s act does not necessarily follow from
God’s immanent act as such.

But the matter is otherwise with permission as theologians and Scripture
commonly take it. Although this permission connotes an immanent and eternal
act of God, it is nevertheless, in my judgment, a transient and temporal act,
indeed one contemporaneous with the thing permitted. In this it differs from
God’s volition and his other immanent acts. Permission is not eternal, nor
does God permit human beings to sin from eternity. Scripture instead restricts
permission to a definite time. Acts 14:16: “God in past ages permitted all the
nations to walk in their own ways.” See also Genesis 20:6; Deuteronomy 18:14;
Leviticus 19:17; Psalm 105:14; Isaiah 63:17; and Ezekiel 14:4, 9, 10.
Permission is always designated a temporal act of God, while volition is never
restricted to a time.

*[Marginal note: Permission is a temporary and transient act, not an immanent
and eternal one.]*

2. Permission is the execution of God’s eternal decree concerning the
permission of sin, but the execution of an eternal decree is temporal. The
antecedent is proved as follows. In time God permits human beings to sin so
that there may be a place for the Savior, so that the creature’s own frailty
may become evident, and so that human beings may be humbled before God.

3. If human beings pray in time that God not permit them to sin, permission is
an evil that befalls the creature in time. But the former is true: “Lead us not
into temptation.”

4. Permission is often the punishment of sin. But everything that is penal,
or can be penal, is something temporal, just as every sin is, for punishment
and sin are correlatives. Hence, once permission has been posited, sin
necessarily follows by necessity of consequence. Therefore the distinguished
Dr Twisse—whom I esteem and venerate as much as anyone—does not justly censure
the weighty theologians William Perkins and John Piscator.

3. I prove it in this way. Scripture infers sin from permission by a good
consequence, and infers non-sin, or abstinence from sin, from non-permission.
Therefore sin follows from permission by the force of consequence. The
antecedent is proved: God permitted Pharaoh to detain God’s people and Sihon to
deny his people passage through his land. Therefore the former detained God’s
people, while the latter denied

*[Marginal note: Scripture argues from permission to sin and from
non-permission to abstinence from sin.]*

[PDF p. 136; printed p. 84]

God’s people passage. Exodus 7:3–4; Joshua 11:19. Likewise, God did not permit
his people to perpetrate the abominations of the nations; therefore they did
not perpetrate them. Deuteronomy 18:14. Forms of argument like these are found
throughout Scripture.

*[Marginal references: Genesis 12:14; Genesis 31:29; 1 Samuel 10:7; Acts
17:30; Judges 10:7; 2 Samuel 24:1; Psalm 106:41; Daniel 1:1, 2, 5; 2
Thessalonians 2:11; Jacob Arminius, public disputation 10, thesis 8.]*

2. If one who is permitted to sin can refrain from sinning, then, because
permission is the execution of the divine decree concerning the salvation of
human beings through Christ and is often the punishment of sin, it could come
about that God’s decree was rescinded. God would punish someone by judicial
permission, yet the person permitted would not sin. The stroke of divine
judgment would then be avoidable by the creature: God would punish the sinner,
yet the sinner would not be punished by God, which is absurd. The reason for
the inference is that permission, if sin does not follow, is not a punishment
but, as Jacob Arminius rightly says, “material for praise.”

3. Either a form or a privation must be present in a capable subject at the
due time. But permission is privatively opposed to God’s gracious efficiency,
by the granting of which sin would be avoided here and now and a person would
holily abstain from it. Therefore, if such permission is posited here and now
for such an end, it is necessary that such holy abstinence not be posited.

4. To posit permission without positing the sin permitted utterly uproots the
fear of God, trust, fervor in prayer, humility, and gratitude. I devoutly fear
in vain that God may permit me to sin or hand me over to a reprobate mind if,
once permission has been posited, I can refrain from sinning. I regard it as
of no consequence and do not fear God’s permission. I trust in God in vain; I
humble myself under his mighty hand in vain; I pray in vain and to no purpose
that he avert apostasy; and I thank God in vain for sin avoided and ascribe
glory to him, if God’s permission neither advances apostasy nor unfailingly
entails it. Indeed, if I can refrain from sinning and earn greater praise even
when every permission of God has been posited, I am the maker and master of my
own obedience.

I pray in vain that God not lead me into temptation by permitting it, if after
permission has been posited I can refrain from sinning. For then all fear,
trust, prayers, gratitude, and humility would ultimately be resolved into free
choice, which, once permission has been posited, can stand or fall.

*[Marginal note: If the permission of sin leaves the person permitted able and
indifferent with respect to not sinning, all religion, fear, trust, prayer,
thanksgiving, humility, and so forth are taken away.]*

5. If the person permitted can refrain from sinning, then when the grace
necessary to avoid sin against the Holy Spirit has been removed, someone could
refrain from blaspheming. That grace would therefore not have been necessary
for avoiding blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, which is plainly Pelagian. For
logic teaches that a

[PDF p. 137; printed p. 85]

cause without which an effect can be posited is not simply necessary for
producing
that effect. The consequent is evident, because one who is permitted to
blaspheme would not blaspheme.

6. Finally, Navarrete, Cabrera, Báñez, the College of Salamanca, John de Rada,
and our own John Cameron prove the inference from permission to the thing
permitted. I admit that the same thing is taught by the Jesuits, but in bad
faith. Suárez, Fasolus, Fonseca, and Lessius teach that sin
follows from permission, but with middle knowledge presupposed. This merely
makes sport of the reader, because middle knowledge presupposes the futurition
of the permitted sin. If sin is future and is known by God through middle
knowledge to be future, it follows very well that the sin will necessarily
occur. But the sophists’ inference would then be: “Sin is future; therefore sin
is future.”

*[Marginal references: Navarrete, controversy 55, question 1, proof 3;
Cabrera, vol. 3, on part 3, question 62, article 1, distinction 12, chapter
12, figure 258; Báñez, part 1, question 23, article 3, letter C.]*

*[Marginal references: College of Salamanca, tractate 5, On Predestination,
sole doubt, chapter 4, §28; John de Rada, part 1, controversy 2; Cameron, To
the Letter of a Learned Man, pp. 280–281; Suárez, short work On Grace, book 3,
chapter 3, figure 4; Fasolus, vol. 4, part 2, question 14, article 13, doubt

4. The Arminians and Gabriel Pennottus have a principal argument against us:
“If, from the denial of God’s efficiency—that is, from permission—sin cannot
fail to follow, it follows that God is a cause of sin, at least a deficient
cause, since he does not exert an effective influx. Yet nothing else can be
said about a human being except that he is a deficient cause. This is
confirmed as follows: in necessary things an efficient cause includes a
deficient cause under itself. Therefore someone who permits a glass vessel
held in his hand to fall upon a stone is the cause of the vessel’s breaking.”

*[Marginal note: A physically deficient cause, insofar as it is a cause to
which the effect is morally imputable, and insofar as it is not.]*

I answer: a deficient cause is taken in two ways. 1. Physically and improperly:
namely, as a cause that is the physical cause of an act but does not act,
because it does not will to act when it is not obligated to act. In this way
God is the deficient cause of the underlying act. But it does not follow that
God is a deficient cause in the same manner as a human being is a deficient
cause; nor does an efficient cause include a deficient cause under itself in
this sense.

*[Marginal references: Remonstrants, Apology, chapter 6, folio 68; Pennottus,
Defense of Human Liberty, book 7, chapter 15, figures 3–4.]*

2. A cause is properly deficient when it does not act although it is bound to
act because it is subject to a superior’s law. In this sense God is not a
deficient cause of sin. Consequently, although the one who permits a glass
vessel to fall upon a stone is a cause of its breaking—namely, a physical
cause, because the fall is entirely physical—it does not follow in the class
of moral effects that God is a cause of sin in the same way through the denial
of efficiency. Franciscus de Herrera indicates the reason: the human will as
such is not a moral cause of moral acts;

*[Marginal reference: Franciscus de Herrera, on book 2 of Scotus’s Sentences,
disputation 34, sole question, note 1.]*

[PDF p. 138; printed p. 86]

rather, it is the will insofar as it is regulable by the law of a superior
that is a moral cause. The human will as such is only a physical cause of
moral acts. If we suppose a person not to be subject to law, then even if he
did not pray, sanctify the Sabbath, or worship God according to the
prescription of the Word, in that case he would be a physical cause—or a
physically deficient cause—of the non-acts of praying, sanctifying the Sabbath,
and the like. He would not be a moral cause to whom the effect was imputable.
The reason is that all imputability of an evil effect is founded upon an
obligation to which a person is subject insofar as he is subject to a
superior’s command.

It may be objected: If imputability is founded upon obligation, then our
obedience, which is effected by God through efficacious grace, would not be
imputable to God, and its praise would not be due to God, because God effects
obedience in us from no obligation but from his mere grace.

I answer: imputability to demerit is founded upon legal obligation, so that a
moral effect is imputable to no one for demerit unless he is obligated to obey
the law. But God in this matter is subject to no law and bound to no superior.
The principle of imputability to merit, however, is far different: the freer
someone is, and the more exempt from an obligation to do good, the more a
moral effect is imputable to him. Therefore the fact that, though not bound,
God communicates good to creatures redounds to God’s greater glory and to more
eminent and excellent merit. Thus imputability in evil things formally
consists in the obligation not to do them; in good things it consists in the
gratuitous liberality of doing or not doing them.

*[Marginal note: The imputability by which an effect is attributed to God is
far different from that by which an effect is imputed to us.]*
