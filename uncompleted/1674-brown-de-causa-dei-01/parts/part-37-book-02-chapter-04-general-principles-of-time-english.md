CHAPTER IV

Some General Principles Concerning Time Are Set Forth

Having premised these things concerning solemn worship, some general points
about time must next be set out so that what follows may become clearer. For it
is highly useful to us, as we are about to dispute concerning the Sabbath, to
explain the nature of time correctly. This is all the more necessary because
the adversaries commonly say—although wrongly—that place and time have the
same character, namely, that each is a mere circumstance of actions. We must
therefore see whether time accompanies any action, whether religious or
ordinary, in a different way, and has a different influence upon the action it
accompanies, than place does.

*Time* in general is a natural adjunct and circumstance of a human action. Yet
it is such an adjunct and circumstance that it contributes substantially to
the action itself of which it is the adjunct—provided the action is not one
completed in an instant—and affects it greatly by its own force and influence,
as will be clear from what is said below. From this it will become clear that
there is a very great difference between place and time, although each is in
general an adjunct of an action.

*Time* may be considered with respect to *natural* division, when it is
divided into *years, months, days*, and even *weeks*, which appears no less
exact a division of time than any of the preceding ones; or with respect to
*artificial* division, when it is divided into *hours and minutes*, which are
means devised by human beings for measuring and dividing time. Finally, it may
be considered with respect to some notable *action* which is said to occur or
to have occurred in it, as when we say that this was done *when*, that is, at
the same time as that event; that a person was here *as often as* such a thing
is done; or that he remained here *so long as* such a thing was being done,
and so forth.

However we consider or measure time, three questions are commonly asked about
it, or about an action performed in it and related to it. The first concerns

[1674 logical printed p. 251 | IA leaf 269 | PDF p. 269]

the *length of time*, or the *duration* of an action in time, as when it is
asked *how long*: that is, how much time is spent continuously in performing
this or that business—whether a whole hour, a whole day, half a day or hour,
and so forth. The second concerns the *number* or *frequency* of time, or the
*repetition* of an action in time, as when it is asked *how often*: that is, at
how many intervening intervals the same time, or period of time, recurs in
which something must be done again—whether this or that must be renewed and
attempted afresh twice, three times, or four times in a day, week, month, and
so forth. The third, finally, concerns *order*, or the beginning of an action,
as when it is asked *when* such a thing was done or must be done: that is, what
precisely is the season in which such an action must occur—whether at the
third, fourth, or sixth hour of the day, on the first or last day of the week,
and so forth.

As for the Sabbath, which is now under consideration, these aspects of time
which have just been mentioned and explained must be carefully observed and
distinctly conceived, and must by no means be confused, if the Sabbath is to
be rightly understood, as will become clear later.

Moreover, these three aspects of time are either *determinate* or
*indeterminate*. Determinate aspects are conceived in two ways. When *how
long* is asked, the duration which is determined is either the *minimum* or
the *maximum*, that is, the *shortest* or the *longest* which such an action
requires. When *how often* is asked, the determined recurrence of time by
which we answer this question is either the *least frequent* or the *most
frequent*. Finally, when *when* is asked, the season which answers that
question for the person acting is either the *latest* or the *earliest*; that
is, the action must be done either as late or as early as it can be. In all
these things there is a range, because they admit of degrees. They are called
*indeterminate* when it is uncertain how long an action already begun will
last, or how much time will be spent before it is finished; when it is
uncertain how often an action already completed must be repeated, or after how
many intervening intervals it is to be begun again within a given period—that
is, how often this or that must be done in a day or week; and, finally, when it
is uncertain when an action not yet begun must begin, or at what hour of the
day or on what day of the week, first or last, such a thing must be begun or
advanced.

Determination—about which we will first set forth what must be said—can occur
in three ways. *First*, it may come from the nature of the action itself, when
the action is such that it cannot be completed except within a certain amount
of time. For example, a large book cannot be read through except in some
proportionately longer time. Thus time is determined with respect to *how
long*. Similarly, time is determined by the action itself with respect to *how
often* when the action is such that it cannot be completed on one occasion,
but the same act must necessarily be repeated several times; for example, no
one can prepare a pen for writing with a single cut. Finally, time is
determined by the action itself with respect to *when* whenever the action is
of such a nature that it cannot be completed except in its proper season.
*Second*, it may be determined by superiors who possess authority to make this
determination, whether with respect to *how long*, *how often*, or *when*,
together or separately. Finally, it may be determined by a person himself,
when he resolves within himself to undertake such an action precisely at this
time, to repeat it *so many times*, and, finally, to continue in doing it *so
long* on each occasion. These last two modes of determination chiefly come
under consideration, and the second is especially relevant to our subject.

[1674 logical printed p. 252 | IA leaf 270 | PDF p. 270]

This determination made by a superior or by the person himself is twofold, or
is considered in two ways: either insofar as it concerns the three aspects of
time named above, or insofar as it concerns the mode of determination itself.

In the former way determination is manifold. A superior or the person himself
may, *first*, determine duration alone—*how long*, or the *length* of time—as
when it is determined that a whole hour or a whole day is to be spent on
studies or other labors, while it is not determined at what hour or on what day
the work is to begin, nor whether the same thing must be done again after an
interval of several hours or days. And if an hour or day must be spent on that
labor several times, *how often* is not determined. *Second*, *frequency*
alone, or *how often*, may be determined, as when it is determined that some
time is to be spent on studies every day, but neither the hour at which one
must begin nor how long one must persist in that labor each day is determined.
*Third*, only the *season* or occasion, or the hour or time for beginning and
undertaking the work, may be determined—for example, that study is to begin at
six in the morning—while neither how often this must be done nor how long one
must remain at study, whether for one or two continuous hours, is determined.
*Fourth*, both *how long* and *how often* may be determined, as when it is said
that a whole hour must be spent on study every day without designating the
hour itself. *Fifth*, *how long* and *when*, but not *how often*, may be
determined, as when we say that study must begin at six and continue for six
whole hours, while it is not determined whether this must be done repeatedly,
or how often, or only once. *Sixth*, *how often* and *when*, but not *how long*,
may be determined, as when it is said that one must go to study every day at
six in the morning, while it is not determined how long one must remain at
study each day. Finally, time may be determined with respect to all three
aspects, as when it is said that one must study every day from six in the
morning until twelve. Here *how long* is determined, namely, a period of six
hours; *how often*, namely, every day; and *when*, namely, beginning at six.

Determination with respect to its mode is twofold, either *loose* or *strict*.
It is loose with respect to *how long* when it is determined that one must
study for very many or very few hours continuously, but the precise number of
hours is not determined; with respect to *how often* when it is determined
that one must return to study *more often* or *more rarely*, but precisely how
often is not determined; and with respect to *when* when it is determined that
one must begin late or early, but precisely when or at what hour is not
determined.

*Strict determination* occurs when time is determined so precisely with
respect to these aspects that no one can doubt how long, how often, or when
labor must be given to study. It is threefold: *initial*, *conclusive*, or
*exclusive*.

*Strict initial determination* occurs when the beginning threshold of that
aspect of time affected by the determination is fixed. For example, when *how
long* is determined, it is determined that the business be performed for that
whole time and not for any shorter time. When *how often* is determined, it is
determined that the work be undertaken that often and not more rarely.
Finally, when *when* is determined, it is determined that one must begin the
work that early and no later. At the same time, however, it is indicated

[1674 logical printed p. 253 | IA leaf 271 | PDF p. 271]

that something more may be required in some or all of these aspects by some
other determination, though not by this one.

*Strict conclusive determination* is one which, although it does not prohibit
a greater portion of time than is expressly determined, whether with respect
to *how long* or *how often*, nor prohibit a beginning earlier than is
expressly determined, nevertheless strictly commands no more, whether with
respect to *how long*, *how often*, or *when*. It commands only this much: that
the action last so long, occur so frequently, and begin so early; and it
prohibits spending less time, making the recurrence less frequent, and making
the beginning later.

*Strict exclusive determination* is one which, both affirmatively and
negatively and by way of prohibition, decisively prescribes limits on both
sides so that nothing is permitted beyond what is precisely prescribed. That
is, the duration is to be so long and neither longer nor shorter; the
repetition so frequent and neither more nor less frequent; and the beginning
at precisely that time, neither later nor earlier. For example, a youth may be
commanded to devote six whole hours to study every day, beginning at six in
the morning, without any variation, increase, or decrease.

It must be noted, however, that a superior who has the authority to determine
time with respect to any of the foregoing aspects may admit some reservation
or dispensation in certain things, either when the determination is made or
later, as he pleases. Such a reservation may in some respect qualify the
determination already made to that extent, but it by no means removes the
force of the determination with respect to the other things, to all of which
it firmly obliges notwithstanding that reservation. For example, a teacher
may determine that a pupil devote himself to study throughout an entire week,
every day from six in the morning until ten in the evening, and nevertheless
permit some interruption to refresh the body with food and drink and to
perform other necessary things. Notwithstanding that interruption, the
determination remains firm with respect to everything else, as is clear.

Moreover, once the action is finished, it makes no difference with respect to
some natural consideration whether the time which was the adjunct of that
action was antecedently determined or indeterminate. But before the action is
begun, it makes a great difference. For the difference which appears here is
no less than that between *being able to be done and being required to be
done*. When time is determined, the action, provided it is rightly done, must
be begun and completed according to the determination of time already made,
whether with respect to *how long*, *how often*, *when*, all these aspects, or
only some of them. But when time is indeterminate, it follows only that the
action can be done in that manner with respect to those aspects.

Although indeterminate time is for the most part a mere accident and
circumstance of an action—necessary indeed with respect to the action’s
physical and natural character, though not with respect to its moral character
or insofar as it is considered in the sphere of morals—nevertheless, in some
actions, especially those advanced gradually and admitting of degrees, it is
a circumstance of the greatest importance, whether for advancing or impeding
the action. Reason and nature

[1674 logical printed p. 254 | IA leaf 272 | PDF p. 272]

bear witness to this, if, for example, the subject is the *how long* and *how
often* of time, that is, its *duration and frequency*.

For in acquiring the learning or knowledge which a student intends—so that we
may illustrate the point by this example—and for which various labors and
studies are required, both in public and private exercises, in consulting many
books and reflecting upon what has been read, as well as in serious and
frequent meditation and so forth, it matters greatly to advancing and completing those studies whether
he continues in these exercises for some long or short period at a time, and
whether he applies himself to them rarely or more frequently. For, other
things being equal, one who devotes himself continuously to study for a longer
time makes more progress than one who does not remain at it so long. And one
who applies himself to study more frequently also makes more progress, other
things being equal, than one who applies himself to this business more rarely.

Hence, moreover, we can know with certainty which of two otherwise equal
students has made greater progress in study and should be called more learned,
and can pronounce with certainty that the one who has given himself to study
more often and persisted in it longer has carried off the prize from the other
and should be preferred by far. Conversely, which of the two has applied
himself more frequently to study and prolonged the time in study on each
occasion can be known from the fruit, or from the greater progress in study of
one than of the other, provided we suppose other things equal.

Indeed, from the length and frequency of time, even though indeterminate, it
can be clearly demonstrated, where no obstacle intervenes, which of the two
has the greater inclination and affection for study, something which can
scarcely be shown otherwise. And what is more, these two aspects are of such
importance in all weighty actions that, from their proper use and application,
a wise person is distinguished from a fool. For we scarcely regard as
sufficiently in possession of himself one who undertakes something new every
hour and thus neither spends sufficient time to complete any work nor returns
as often as necessary to complete the work undertaken.

But we must speak very differently about the third aspect of time, namely,
*when*. For none of these things can be said about it considered in itself. It
is all the same to a student—except perhaps accidentally, with respect to his
capacity or something similar—when he begins his studies, whether late or
early. I admit that it matters greatly accidentally when he begins, namely,
when some advantage or disadvantage arises from that moment of time and that
opportunity. For example, he may begin when he is more or less suited to study
or capable of instruction; when he can expect more or less help from others;
or when by beginning earlier or later he can spend a longer or shorter period
continuously pursuing his studies and in turn repeat the work of study more or
less often. But these and similar things are mere accidents which do not
continually accompany or attend that season or opportunity. Once those
accidents are therefore set aside, it appears that this aspect of time is not
as important as the other two. For every *when*, or moment of beginning, is
equal and, in itself, advances study equally.

Hence no one can infer from this aspect of time which of two students has made
greater progress. For example, suppose two students equal in every respect
spend twelve hours in study each day, and one

[1674 logical printed p. 255 | IA leaf 273 | PDF p. 273]

applies himself to study at six in the morning while the other does so at nine
or ten. Who can conclude from this different beginning of their work,
considered purely in itself, that the one surpasses the other? Nor can anyone
conclude from this which of the two will make more progress in study or which
pursues study with greater and which with lesser love. Finally, we cannot
guess from their progress and success *when*, that is, at what hour, the one
and the other first applied himself to study. Thus these aspects—*how long*
and *how often*—differ greatly from *when*. This must be seriously and
carefully noted in this controversy.

In all these points, and very many that follow, two most learned and celebrated
Englishmen, Daniel Cawdrey and Herbert Palmer, showed us the way in their most
exact treatise on the Sabbath, part 1, chapters 7–8 and following. Fairness
requires us to acknowledge that we received these things from them.
