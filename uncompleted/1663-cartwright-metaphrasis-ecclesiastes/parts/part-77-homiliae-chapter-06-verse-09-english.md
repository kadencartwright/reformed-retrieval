VERSE 9. You will say that the sight of the eyes is better than the wandering
of desire. This too is vanity and affliction of spirit.

This is an anticipation. Its objection is drawn from a comparison in which
one thing is greater: namely, that possessing riches is better than constantly
longing for them when they are absent and desired. Everyone does this,
including prudent people who are bare and destitute of them. The condition

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA247 | printed pp. 247–248 | begins with complete igitur after catchword igitur on p. 246; ends on p. 247 immediately before repeated Vers. 10]

of the possessor is therefore preferable. A reply is attached to this
objection: first, this condition is vanity and distress of spirit. When use is
excluded, the right of possession is plainly empty and useless. For what
difference is there between someone destitute of resources and someone who
possesses resources but receives no fruit from them? Or what difference is
there between a naked, unarmed person and one girded with a sword so long as
the sword has no use either in defending himself or striking enemies? The
goodness of all things is discerned by their use.

Next, besides vanity and emptiness, mere possession also involves the anxiety
undertaken so that the things we possess may be available to us when the time
requires. Hence, when weapons have no use, the condition of an unarmed person
is more convenient than that of someone equipped with weapons, because he is
less encumbered for a journey and other duties and knows how to turn himself
more easily in every direction. Meanwhile, from this objection it is easy to
detect people's error: by attributing more to the mere possession of riches
than reason requires, they are swept away by a foolish love for them. Let us
therefore apply our mind to the pursuit of virtue and piety, from the mere
possession of which great benefit and immense profit return to us.
