# CHAPTER XXVII

## The arguments of Dr. Walaeus and Dr. Strangius for the contrary opinion are examined

In the preceding chapter we saw how the most illustrious Dr. *Walaeus* refuted
certain arguments for the true opinion. It remains now for us to see what he
says for his own opinion in *chapter 2 of his Dissertation, page 13 and
following*.

He thinks that we err no less in one extreme than the *Anabaptists* err in the
other, for he speaks as follows: [Just as this opinion of the Anabaptists errs
in one extreme, so the opinion of certain other more recent men falls into the
other extreme, for they contend that this whole precept is merely moral. I
acknowledge that among them are learned and godly men, and that in this
opinion of theirs they set a good end before themselves, namely, that the
profanity which everywhere appears in the violation of this precept may be
brought into order all the better. Yet because this opinion is both novel and
quite contrary to Christian liberty, and because we must not seek supports
for promoting piety which have no certain foundation in the Word of God, lest
we relapse into superstition, we shall bring arguments from the immovable
rules of Scripture itself by which that opinion is plainly refuted.]

We certainly do not fall into the other extreme. Rather, those men do who,
with the Jews, think that the Sabbath on the seventh day in sequence is still
in force. We deny this, asserting that that Sabbath does not belong to the
substance of the fourth precept, and that the fourth precept, according to the
letter and substance of the command, commands the observance of one day
determined by God in the weekly cycle. Nor indeed do we see how the profanity
which everywhere appears in the violation of this precept can otherwise be
remedied or removed. It has not yet been established for me that this opinion
of ours is so novel. But I cannot sufficiently wonder that this learned man
says our opinion is quite contrary to Christian liberty. This is objected to
us by those who deny what he affirms with us, namely, that by the force of the
fourth precept we are bound to observe one day in the weekly cycle and
consecrate it to God. But we regard the liberty for which those men pant as
license. In what respect, I ask, does our opinion appear contrary to true
Christian liberty? He will not say it is because we observe one day out of
seven by the force of this precept, since he says the same thing with us. Is
it, then, in the manner of observing it? But it will be evident in its proper
place that we do not err in this either. We certainly concede that we must not
seek from our own brains supports for promoting piety. But we further add
that neither must we reject those supports which God himself supplies for the
promotion of piety and which have the surest foundations in the Word of God.
By God's grace we abominate and reject every superstition. Arguments drawn
from the immovable rules of Scripture itself can certainly move us readily.
But it remains now for us to see whether the arguments which this celebrated
man brings for his opinion are of that kind.

[Printed p. 288 | IA PDF 294 | IA leaf 294 | IA decausadei02brow]

In Chapter 2, after proposing certain things to refute the opinion of the
*Anabaptists and Socinians*, who hold that this precept is merely Ceremonial,
he finally attacks those on *page 15* who say that this precept is merely Moral,
in this manner: [The first argument is taken from the very change of this
seventh day. For although the ancient Sabbatarians contended that observance
of the seventh day must necessarily be retained, they were nevertheless
condemned in this matter by the universal Christian Church for error, indeed
even heresy, because they would restore the shadows of the Old Testament. Nor
do even the very men who follow the opinion proposed above dare to defend the
Sabbatarians' opinion. From this it is evidently inferred that there is
something mutable and Ceremonial in this precept.]

*Response 1.* The opinion of those Sabbatarians, who hold that the last day of
the week must constantly be observed, is deservedly condemned by the
Christian Church. But it has not yet been established for me that those
Sabbatarians are to be condemned because by this means they would restore the
shadows of the Old Testament, unless this reason is properly explained. For
it has not yet been established for me that the observance of the last day of
the week, considered in itself, was a shadow of the Old Testament. The most
illustrious *Walaeus* will also say this with me, since he holds that the
observance of this day was enjoined even upon Adam in the state of innocence.
But the shadows of the Old Testament had no place in that state. I said
*considered in itself*, because some character of a shadow could afterward be
attached to this observance. For in that typical and Mosaic state of the
Church, many things not ceremonial in themselves assumed some ceremonial
character; indeed, the moral law had a pedagogical character attached to it.
Yet no one can infer from this that the moral law is partly Ceremonial or
mutable because this attached character was ceremonial or mutable. Thus
neither can we properly say that the observance of the seventh day in
sequence, because of the attached character of a shadow, was in itself partly
Moral and partly Ceremonial. 2. Whatever may be said about this, the argument
accomplishes nothing against the fourth precept. For, as we have said and will
prove afterward, the substance of the fourth precept concerns the observance
not of the seventh day in sequence, but of a seventh day by number—that is,
not the observance of the last day of the week, but the observance of one day
in the weekly cycle. It is therefore evident that the change of the Seventh
day does not imply that the substance of the fourth precept is Ceremonial or
Mutable. By the force of the fourth precept the Jews were indeed bound also
to observe the Seventh day in sequence, not primarily but consequently: not
because the substance of the fourth precept demanded it, but because the
seventh day in sequence was the day of the week which God then willed to be
observed. In the same way, by the force of the second precept, which commands
that God be worshiped by means prescribed by him, they were bound to worship
God by sacrifices and things of that kind, not primarily but consequently:
not because the substance of the second precept demanded this, but because
those were the means of worship then instituted by God. Therefore, although
those means were mutable and ceremonial, it does not follow that the very
substance of the second precept was partly ceremonial. So the matter stands
here as well. At most it follows only that something mutable was consequently
commanded by the second and fourth precepts.

The second argument [is taken (*he says*) from various passages of Scripture
where it is affirmed that the Sabbath had a sacramental character in the Old
Testament, while all sacramental things in the Old Testament were beyond all
controversy ceremonial and typical.

[Printed p. 289 | IA PDF 295 | IA leaf 295 | IA decausadei02brow]

The first passage is *Exodus 31:13*. A similar passage is found in *Ezekiel
20:12, 20*. In these passages the Sabbaths are said to have been instituted by
God also for this end, that they might be signs of God's grace toward the
people of God and of the sanctification by which God sanctifies his people;
and this is the very definition of sacraments.] See also Dr. *Strangius, at
the place cited above, page 590*.

*Response 1.* The Adversaries use this argument to prove that the precept
concerning the Sabbath is altogether Ceremonial, and it was sufficiently
answered above. I shall say only these things here. 1. All of this concerns
the Sabbath on the Seventh day in sequence, which the Jews observed, not the
fourth precept or the weekly Sabbath prescribed in the fourth precept. It
therefore does not follow from this that the Fourth precept is partly
ceremonial. 2. Neither does it follow that the observance itself of the
Seventh day in sequence, considered in itself and in its first institution,
was Ceremonial. For he himself admits that this observance was instituted in
paradise, in the state of innocence. And at that time there was undoubtedly
no need of sacraments of the kind that were ceremonial and typical. Therefore
this ceremonial or sacramental character which is alleged to have been
attached to the observance of the Sabbath after the fall does not make the
observance of precisely the seventh day in itself typical, much less affect
the nature of the fourth precept, which enjoins the observance of this seventh
day only consequently. 3. If the very definition of sacraments applies to the
Jewish Sabbath, then the Jews had more than two ordinary sacraments. For it
cannot be called extraordinary, since it was far older than the others and
repeated much more frequently. 4. Neither of those definitions of a Sacrament
offered by his most celebrated colleague *Rivet in the Synopsis, Disputation
43, Theses 5 and 6*, fits the Jewish Sabbath sufficiently well. He formulates
the first definition thus: *It is a visible sign, divinely instituted for the
Church, by the use of which Christ with his benefits is signified by a certain
suitable analogy and sealed to the souls of believers.* The other is: *It is
a sacred action divinely instituted in which God, by a certain suitable
analogy between the signs and the things signified, according to his promise
declared in the accompanying word of institution, offers and seals saving
grace in Christ to our souls; and in which, testifying in turn our faith and
piety toward him, we believe our separation from the world and our holy
mutual fellowship with one another.* We now ask, what is that *visible sign*
by whose use Christ was signified and sealed? What was that *sacred action* in
which such a thing was offered and sealed? Every sacrament, as we are taught
in the same place, has some substantial and visible element. But what is that
element in the Sabbath? Is it the day? But a day does not seem to be a
substance but rather an accident, nor does a day have a fitting analogy with
the thing signified here any more than an hour does. Is it the seventh day?
But that day has no more fitting analogy with the thing signified and sealed
than the first, second, or third day, or the tenth or twentieth. Is it instead
the observance of the day? But what then was the sacred action or sacred use
of the visible sign? Further, that observance consisted in worship offered on
that day and in cessation from daily works. Was that worship, then, the sacred
action, or the use of that sign? But that worship was common to the other days.
Was it instead the rest? But rest is not an action and cannot be called the
use of a visible sign. Moreover, we ask how Christ with his benefits, or
saving grace in Christ, is signified, offered, or sealed by this sign.

[Printed p. 290 | IA PDF 296 | IA leaf 296 | IA decausadei02brow]

The Sabbaths are called a sign for recognizing that God is Jehovah who
sanctifies them, and that Jehovah sanctifies them; but it is not said that
Christ with his benefits is signified, offered, and sealed. Finally, we ask
where the promise declared in the accompanying word of institution appears.
For in the first institution of the Sabbath no such promise was attached, nor
was there any need for it to be attached. Indeed, as is admitted, no sacrament
of that kind could have had a place. It is therefore better to say that the
word *sign* is not taken here for a type or a sign sealing future grace, but
only for a simple proof, or an indicative sign of present grace. Thus in these
passages the Sabbath is called a sign because in observing the Sabbath there
is a common and public profession of that communion which exists between God
and us, just as every solemn profession is a sign of the thing professed.

But this response does not satisfy the celebrated man [because (*he says on
page 17*) this manner of speaking in the Scripture of the Old Testament
always assumes a sacramental character and is used only of a sealing sign, as
may be seen in *Genesis 9:11: I have set my bow in the cloud, that it may be
a sign of the covenant between me and the earth.* Likewise *chapter 17:11:
You shall circumcise the flesh of your foreskin, that it may be a sign of the
covenant between me and you.* This is expressed thus by the Apostle Paul in
*Romans 4:11: And he received the sign of circumcision εἰς σφραγῖδα, as a seal
of the righteousness of faith which he had in uncircumcision.* He likewise
speaks in this way concerning the Passover lamb in *Exodus 12:13: And that
blood shall be a sign for you upon those houses where you are, so that when I
see that blood I shall pass over you.* A similar manner of speaking occurs
concerning the eating of unleavened bread and the redemption of the
firstborn, *Exodus 13:6, 19*, and frequently elsewhere.]

*Response.* It is one thing to be a sign of the covenant between God and us,
but another to be a sign for knowing that God Jehovah sanctifies us. The
*former* is always a sealing sign, but the *latter* can be indicative, as is
the mutual love among Christ's Disciples in *John 13:35: By this everyone will
know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another.* But we said
more about this matter in *Book III*.

Nevertheless he continues: [Furthermore, the very words added to the word
sign in these passages cannot be understood of a bare indicative sign. Thus
*Exodus 31:13* says: *It is a sign between me and you by which it may be known
that I am Jehovah who sanctifies you.* For a sign by which it is known that
God sanctifies us is a sign sealing on God's part that he sanctifies us. In
the same way, *Ezekiel 20:20* says: *that they may be signs between me and you
that I am Jehovah your God*. This is the very formula of the covenant which
God made with his chosen people, as may be seen in *Genesis 17:7; Jeremiah
31:33; and Hebrews 8:10*, not some bare profession that God is our God or
sanctifies us. Yet that profession also has a place in the sacraments
themselves, since the sacraments seal both the saving grace by which God binds
himself to us and our gratitude by which we bind ourselves to God, just as
this is beyond controversy among all the Reformed Churches.]

*Response 1.* The words added to the word *sign* in *Exodus 31:13* can very
well be understood of an indicative sign. For a sign by which it is known that
God sanctifies us is not always a sign sealing on God's part that he
sanctifies us. All the effects of the sanctifying Spirit in us are real signs
by which it is known that God sanctifies us, yet they are not instituted signs
on

[Printed p. 291 | IA PDF 297 | IA leaf 297 | IA decausadei02brow]

God's part as the one sealing. 2. Nor does *Ezekiel 20:20* prove anything. For
the words of the text should not be rendered, *that they may be signs between
me and you that I am Jehovah your God*, but thus: *that it may be known* (לדעת)
*that I am Jehovah your God*. This expression therefore differs from the one
used in *Genesis 17, Jeremiah 31, and Hebrews 8*. Nothing else is denoted here
but a public indicative sign by which they testified before the whole world
that they regarded the true Jehovah, who is holy and sanctifies, as their God,
and acknowledged no other. This was their highest privilege. And Dr.
*Strangius* is mistaken when he thinks Scripture speaks about the sign of the
Sabbath in no other way than it speaks about the other types of the Old
Testament, such as circumcision in *Genesis 17:11 and Romans 4:11*, and the
Passover lamb in *Exodus 12:13*.

Finally, he adds: [But if only a bare indicative sign were at issue here,
nothing peculiar to the Sabbath would be attributed in these passages which
could not be attributed to any external worship at any other time. A
profession of this kind had a place in all external worship offered to God on
working days, even outside that time, yet it is never said of such a
profession in the Scriptures that it *was a sign between God and his people*.]

*Response.* The reason the Sabbath could be designated in this way is that
all external worship was comprehended in the Sabbath, for we know that the
Sabbath is frequently used for the whole worship of God. Another reason is
that the use of sacrifices was retained among the other Nations, whereas the
observance of the Sabbath had fallen into disuse. Accordingly, this
observance of the Sabbath was a sign which convinced the foreign Nations that
the God of the Jews was their sanctifier more than did the use of sacrifices
and similar things, which were to some extent practiced among those Nations.
Thus the response of *Strangius* accomplishes nothing when he says that the
Sabbath is not called a sign because they worshiped God, but because God was
Jehovah sanctifying them and was their God; for these come to the same thing.

The third argument follows on *page 19*. [It is taken from certain passages
where the thing signified by the Sabbath is clearly expressed. A passage of
this kind is found in *Isaiah 66:23*, where the Prophet, speaking of the time
of the New Testament, says: *And it shall be from one new moon to another,
and from one Sabbath to another, that all flesh may come to bow down before
me, says Jehovah.* In this passage the perpetuity of the spiritual Sabbath is
established as the thing signified by the external Sabbath, which recurred at
intervals.] So also *Strangius, page 591*.

*Response.* How does this prove that the weekly Sabbath was a type of the
spiritual rest found under the New Testament any more than it proves this of
the new moon? And if the New Moon also was a type of spiritual rest of this
kind, what difference is there between these types? Will the precept
concerning the Sabbath be Ceremonial in every respect, just as the precept
concerning the New Moon was? And now that the thing typified is present, will
it be more lawful to institute Sabbaths than New Moons? Yet he himself
acknowledges that the fourth precept binds us to celebrate one day in the
weekly cycle. Will he say this of the precept concerning the New Moon? I do
not believe so. 2. I certainly do not see how the perpetuity of the spiritual
Sabbath is established here as the thing signified by the weekly Sabbath.
Indeed, this cannot even prove the perpetuity of New Testament worship without
intermission, as though under the New Testament we were to have a perpetual
Sabbath, as has been shown elsewhere. See the *English Annotations on the
passage*, where the most learned *Gataker* proves the contrary invincibly from
similar phrases in *1 Samuel 7:16; 2 Chronicles 24:5; Zechariah 14:15; and
Ephesians 3:7*.

[Printed p. 292 | IA PDF 298 | IA leaf 298 | IA decausadei02brow]

He shows that nothing else is denoted here but the perseverance and constancy
of Christians under the New Testament in holding assemblies and observing
solemn occasions. Following the Prophets' customary manner, the Prophet uses
the terms of the Old Testament to signify this so that he may be understood
more readily.

[The other passage (*he says on page 20*) is *Hebrews 4:3 and following*,
where the Apostle examines and applies to his purpose the words of God found
in *Psalm 95: Therefore I swore in my wrath*, and so forth. He teaches that
the rest into which unbelieving and disobedient people do not enter must be
understood not only of the type itself, namely, rest in the land of Canaan,
but chiefly of heavenly rest as the thing signified by it. To this assertion
he immediately joins another type, from whose signified reality the
unbelieving and disobedient are likewise excluded, namely, the rest of the
Sabbath day, when he says in verse 4, *For he spoke thus*, and so forth. And in
order to show that besides that rest of the land of Canaan and the rest of the
Sabbath there is a third kind of rest signified by each of them, he concludes
from Psalm 95 in verse 9: *Therefore a certain Sabbath-rest remains for the
people of God*, into which, namely, the faithful and obedient will enter. That
this conclusion is inferred not only from the type of the land of Canaan but
also from the type of the Sabbath—contrary to what some object here—is
invincibly demonstrated by the word *σαββατισμοῦ*, which the Apostle uses in
the designation of this third, spiritual and eternal rest, and furthermore by
the manifest application of this rest of the seventh day to the spiritual and
eternal rest which, he asserts, remains for us, when he concludes in verse 10:
*For the one who has entered*, and so forth.]

*Response.* We showed above how the Adversaries misuse this passage, and it is
not necessary for us to say much here. We ask only: 1. How is it proved that
the rest of the Sabbath day was a type? The word *Sabbath-rest* can denote
some similarity and analogy, but I do not see that it demonstrates invincibly
that the Sabbath was a type. *Σαββατίζω* does not mean *I celebrate a
Sabbath*, but *I do something similar*, and *σαββατισμός* does not denote the
antitype of the Sabbath but rather the rest which the Sabbath foreshadowed by
a certain similarity and analogy. 2. Let us grant that the Sabbath was as it
were a type of spiritual and eternal rest in heaven. How will it be proved
from this that the Sabbath was a type properly so called and that the precept
commanding it was Ceremonial? To celebrate God here with praises and hymns is
a kind of representation of heavenly and eternal joy in heaven. Shall we
therefore say that the precept concerning celebrating God with praises and
hymns is partly Ceremonial? Heavenly life is compared to a feast in *Isaiah
25:6* and to a wedding feast in *Matthew 25:10 and Revelation 19:7–8*. Shall
we therefore say that these things are partly Ceremonial? When Christ promises
eternal life to the victors, he says he will give them *a white stone, and a
new name written on the stone, Revelation 2:27*; and *authority over the
Nations, and the morning star, verses 26–28*; and *white garments, Revelation
3:5*; and that *he will make them pillars in the temple of God and will write
upon them the name of his God, the name of the City of his God, and his new
name, verse 12*; and that he will grant them to *sit on the throne, verse 21*.
What, then? Must it therefore be said that these and similar things are types
or ceremonial matters? Further, he himself will not say that the Sabbath was
such a type in its first institution, for he concedes that it was instituted
from the beginning. But before the fall there was no need of types, all of
which looked to Christ. Finally, even if we granted that this character of a
type was afterward attached to the Sabbath, it still does not follow from
this that the precept itself was Ceremonial.

The fourth argument now follows, which he proposes in this way: [Our fourth

[Printed p. 293 | IA PDF 299 | IA leaf 299 | IA decausadei02brow]

argument, and one of the greatest force, is taken from the fact that when
Christ discusses the Sabbath in the Gospels, he plainly also ranks it among
ceremonies and joins it with ceremonies and types. This is evident from
*Matthew 12*, where he joins and compares it with the bread of the Presence,
with the priests' occupations in the temple, and finally with the sacrifices
themselves, down through verse 7. These comparisons and arguments of Christ
would have had no force if the Sabbath were not also to be ranked in a certain
respect among ceremonies and types.]

*Response 1.* He himself is also bound to resolve this argument, for the
Adversaries use it in an attempt to prove that the Sabbath is altogether
Ceremonial. Therefore, if this argument proves anything, it will prove too
much. 2. The Sabbath is joined here with ceremonies, but no comparison in all
respects is instituted between the Sabbath and the bread of the Presence, or
the priests' occupations in the Temple, or the sacrifices. Christ only shows
that his disciples did not sin when, passing through the fields on the
Sabbath and being hungry, they plucked and ate the ears of grain any more than
David sinned when, being hungry, he ate the bread of the Presence, which it
was not lawful for him but only the Priests to eat; or than the Priests sinned
by profaning the Sabbath in the temple. Thus he shows that, whatever those
Pharisees judged, the law condemns neither one nor the other in that case. 3.
It no more follows from this that the Sabbath is to be ranked among types and
sacrifices than it follows that the act of plucking and eating ears of grain
is to be ranked among acts of divine worship because it is here compared with
the slaughter of sacrifices and other things pertaining to that ceremonial
worship. Yet the Sabbath is no less compared with the bread of the Presence.
I wish to append here the response found in the opinion of Dr. *Amesius, page
48*: [That inference lacks a foundation. For even the very advocates of this
opinion will not dare to say here that the Sabbath was to be equated with that
bread in all circumstances. It is therefore only in the one matter under
discussion here that our Savior compared it with the said ceremonies: namely,
that just as that bread, although it was not lawful for everyone but only the
Priests to eat it, was nevertheless taken by David to relieve his hunger, so
the sanctification of the Sabbath, with respect to its external expression,
can be interrupted without sin because of pressing necessities of this kind.
Nor can it be proved from this that the command concerning the Sabbath is
inferior to the other commands of the first table, since those commands too,
when matters are so urgent, yield in their external expression to love toward
our neighbor and ourselves. For if we are occupied with prayers, hearing the
Word, and duties of the first table of this kind, it is not wrong temporarily
to interrupt those godly exercises for the salvation of a soul, or even to
extinguish a house engulfed in fire, or for similar occasions. For since the
efficacy of our love toward our neighbor consists chiefly in its external
expression, and his need is not relieved by a wish and words unless work and
effort are also added, therefore, as James has it, *it profits nothing if I
wish to say, Go in peace, be warmed and filled, but do not give him the things
suited to the body.* But in divine exercises the principle is far different.
Thus God looks to what is inward and does not always require us, with respect
to external worship, to prefer his worship to our neighbor's safety. Hence
comes that saying of *Josephus*, ὑπὲρ μὲν τοῦ σώματος ἀμύνονται καὶ τοῖς
Σάββασι, *for the sake of the body they defend themselves even on Sabbaths*.
Secondly, there was also a reason why our Savior should adduce this
example of *David* in particular: not because the Sabbath agreed with that
bread in every respect,

[Printed p. 294 | IA PDF 300 | IA leaf 300 | IA decausadei02brow]

but rather because *it is likely* (as *Tertullian observes in book 4 of his
work Against Marcion*) *that David entered the Temple on a Sabbath and
prepared that food by eagerly breaking the bread of the Presence.* If this is
granted, the rationale of that argument seems far better than if we admit the
alternative. For the resemblance fitted exceedingly well: My disciples
rubbed the plucked ears of grain in their hands on the Sabbath day, and
David and his companions did no less previously on the same day. They not
only prepared food on that day but also ate the bread of the Presence, which
only the Priests were permitted to do.]

He takes the fifth argument [from that famous passage, *Colossians 2:16*,
where the Apostle commands, *Let no one judge you in food, or in drink, ἢ ἐν
μέρει ἑορτῆς, in respect of a feast day, or a new moon, ἢ σαββάτων, or
Sabbaths, which are* (*he adds*) *a shadow of things to come, but the body is Christ's*; that
is, the thing signified is Christ, or is in Christ, as everyone here
acknowledges. To this argument, which presses very strongly, others are
accustomed to respond that by *Sabbaths* here is not meant the Sabbath of the
fourth precept, or of the seventh day, but other Sabbaths which are typical.
Yet this response begs the question, since the Apostle speaks generally and
not a shadow of that restriction appears in the text. Furthermore, they
cannot state what those typical Sabbaths are. For these Sabbaths cannot be
understood of the Jews' other major feasts, since these Sabbaths are here
expressly distinguished from those feasts and also from new moons. Nor can it
be taken of the Sabbath of seven years or the Jubilee, because they occurred
very rarely. But the Apostle deals here with things that recur frequently,
and hence also lists Sabbaths in the plural, because they were repeated every
week. Nor was there any need to warn the Gentile Christians to whom the
Apostle writes here very anxiously about those years of rest occurring every
seven or fifty years, since they pertained only to the land of Canaan and the
Israelite commonwealth. They could not be observed by other Christians,
because Gentile Emperors or Magistrates would never have permitted Christians
either to leave their land idle, reclaim houses and fields once sold by them,
or be compelled to manumit their slaves and servants. Nor can it be proved
from any indication in the New Testament or any author of the primitive
Church that there were ever heretics who wished to impose these things upon
the Churches. Indeed, not even present-day Jews urge their observance, because
they pertained only to the land of Canaan and the Israelite commonwealth.]
See also *Strangius, page 592*.

*Response.* All these things were answered abundantly above. See *Book III,
Chapters XXI and XXII*. I shall therefore add here only the response of
*Eaton*, from the opinion of Dr. *Amesius, page 52*: [But 1. (*he says*) the
question is begged here, for it is presupposed that something in the Sabbath
is Ceremonial, which is the question at issue. 2. If these words must be
understood of every distinction of days, it will follow (as Dr. *R.* urges)
that it is superstitious to observe any day, say the Lord's Day, or one out of
seven. But the men with whom we are dealing deny this. For the things which
the Apostle wrote absolutely must not be restricted by some *insofar as*. The
Apostle did not say, *Let no one judge you in food and drink insofar as they
are ceremonial*, but absolutely, *Let no one judge you*. Thus we see that
another interpretation must be sought, of which
different men offer different versions. Some wish all the preceding feasts
and new moons to be included here in the word Sabbaths, since they were called
by the common name Sabbaths,

[Printed p. 295 | IA PDF 301 | IA leaf 301 | IA decausadei02brow]

in keeping with that Horatian jibe at the Jews' New Moons, *the thirtieth-day
Sabbaths*, and so forth. Thus the word Sabbath would comprehend both new moons
and each of the other feasts. Some think the word *feast*, placed at the
beginning of the verse, is subsequently distributed into New Moons and
Sabbaths, so that the division is between Sabbaths and New Moons. Sabbath
would then contain the first and last day of the Feast of Unleavened Bread,
the first and last day of Tabernacles, the first day of Weeks, and the first
day of the seventh month. These days were special Sabbaths for the Jews, while
to mark the difference they called the remaining feast days common and
working days. Likewise, some understand by Sabbaths in this passage only the
first and last days of the major feasts, which were commanded to be like the
Sabbath. *Leviticus 39: On the first of these days there is cessation, and
likewise on the eighth there is cessation (or a Sabbath).* This last opinion,
although the others too are not absurd, seems to agree best with the sense of
the passage. For here the Apostle deals with those days on which the
distinction of foods was observed, which was especially the case during
Unleavened Bread and New Moons, but not on Sabbath days. 2. The Apostle
himself teaches that observance of the Lord's Day had at that time become
established in all the Churches, *1 Corinthians 16:1*. But it is unlikely that
he wished to dissuade the Colossians from the things he had prescribed for
others. Yet this must be admitted if we concede that the text is to be
interpreted indifferently of all days. Now, with the sense of that passage
thus established, there is no reason for us to seek that feeble refuge against
those who object this passage, that *the precept is the same concerning food
and days: just as it is still lawful to set apart certain foods for certain
uses, so it is also lawful with days.* For the Adversaries do not
dispute with us about what is lawful but about what is obligatory. And *just
as we are not restricted to this or that kind of food by any moral precept, so
they contend that we ought not be restricted in the observance of days*. But,
we say, with that sense established, there is no need for us to pursue this
escape. For we concede that those days about which the Apostle speaks here
were wholly abrogated, no less than the foods, with respect to their moral
nature. But we deny this concerning the Sabbath, which, as we have
sufficiently demonstrated from the premises, is not to be ranked among
ceremonies and types. Yet if the rejoinder based on the comparison between
foods and days to be observed is proposed in its full force, I do not see why
it does not greatly serve the point. The observation is as follows. *In the
words objected, the observance of some day for religious use by Christ's
institution is no more condemned or denied than the choice of some particular
food for religious use by the same institution of Christ. But no Christian
is so entirely destitute of reason that he would wish to conclude from these
words either that the choice of bread and wine for religious use in the
Lord's Supper is unlawful or that it was not instituted by Christ. Therefore
nothing can be concluded from them against the observance of the Lord's Day
by the use and institution of Christ.*]

Dr. *Walaeus* continues: *Nor can the Anabaptists therefore prove from this
passage that there is nothing moral in this fourth precept, because the
Apostle ranks Sabbaths here among the shadows and joins them with the
observance of food or drink, feasts, or new moons. For just as a moral distinction
among foods or drinks at any time, or the use of a particular drink and
particular foods in a time of sorrow and mourning, is not abolished because
the thing typical in that distinction is here forbidden by the Apostle. Thus
neither does it follow that there is nothing moral in the character of the
Sabbath because it too is in a certain respect ranked among ceremonies. For
all things recounted in the same discourse do not therefore possess the same
character in every respect, as may be seen from the apostolic decree in Acts
15 and very many passages elsewhere.*

*Response.* These things are indeed true, but they harm him. For he refuses to
exclude the Sabbath of the fourth precept here because the Apostle speaks
generally and no shadow of that restriction appears in the text. But this
reason militates as much against him as against us. For according to this
reason it no less follows that the Sabbath without qualification is ranked
among the shadows than that the Sabbath of the fourth precept is. And if he
denies that it follows from this that there is nothing moral in the character
of the Sabbath, we can likewise deny that it follows from this that the
Sabbath of the fourth precept is not moral.

These are the arguments of this most illustrious man—and Dr. *Strangius*
brings forward no others—by which he contends that the fourth precept is
partly Moral and partly Ceremonial. The Reader can judge from what has been
said whether they demonstrate this sufficiently and invincibly.

On *page 24 and following* he adduces many testimonies of the Ancients to
confirm his conclusion. But they make no less against him than against us,
for from them one may conclude no less firmly that the fourth precept is
entirely Ceremonial than that it is only partly Ceremonial. Thus these words
of *Justin Martyr in the Dialogue with Trypho the Jew*—*Before Abraham there
was no use of circumcision, nor before Moses any celebration of the Sabbath,
feasts, and offerings; nor is there any need of them now, after Jesus Christ,
the Son of God, born without sin by the counsel and will of God from a virgin
of Abraham's race*—prove that the fourth precept (about which they do not
actually speak at all, but rather about the Jewish Sabbath) is entirely
Ceremonial just as much as that it is only partly Ceremonial. For if, as Dr.
*Walaeus* holds, Justin in these words joins the use of the Sabbath with the
use of circumcision, feasts, and offerings, and thus testifies that it ceased
through the coming of Christ, it appears that he thought the Sabbath was
entirely Ceremonial, just as the other feasts, offerings, and circumcision
were, and not only partly Ceremonial. For no one will say that Circumcision
was partly Moral and partly Ceremonial.

Next come these words of *Tertullian, Against the Jews, chapter 4*: [From this
(*he says*) we discern that the temporal Sabbath is human and the eternal
Sabbath is regarded as divine, concerning which he proclaims through Isaiah:
*And it shall be, he says, month after month, day after day, and Sabbath after
Sabbath, and all flesh shall come to worship in Jerusalem, says the Lord.* We
understand this to have been fulfilled in the Lord's times, when all flesh,
that is, every nation, came in Jerusalem to worship God the Father through
Jesus Christ his Son, just as it was proclaimed through the prophet: *Behold,
proselytes shall come to you through me.* Therefore, before this temporal
Sabbath the eternal Sabbath was also foreshown and foretold, just as before
carnal circumcision spiritual circumcision also was foreshown, and so forth.]
How it can be proved from this that Tertullian thought the fourth precept, of
which he makes no mention, or the Sabbath, was partly Ceremonial, I do not yet
perceive. For if the Sabbath was a type of the time of the New Testament or a
type of the eternal Sabbath, does it not follow that it was entirely
Ceremonial, if it must on this account be called ceremonial, just as
circumcision was?

The same can be gathered from the words of *Theodoret, Questions on Exodus,
near the end*: [Just as when he gave circumcision to Abraham he said, *and it
shall be a sign between me and you*, so when giving the law of the Sabbath he
says, *it is a sign between me and

[Printed p. 297 | IA PDF 303 | IA leaf 303 | IA decausadei02brow]

the children of Israel throughout your generations; for the new form of
government preserves the memory of the lawgiver.*] For these words intimate
that there is nothing more Moral in the Sabbath than there was in
circumcision. And we are not accustomed to say that Circumcision was partly
Moral and partly Ceremonial. He is therefore bound to weaken this authority,
and to respond similarly to the words of Cyril and Epiphanius which he cites,
as well as those of Damascene. And the words of Augustine make just as much
against him as against us. So also do the words of Lombard, as can be evident
to anyone who reads the words themselves.

As for *Thomas*, he indeed says that the precept concerning observance of the
Sabbath is moral in a certain respect, insofar as it commands that a person
devote some time to divine things, but not with respect to the designation of
the time, or insofar as a particular time is determined in this precept as a
sign of the creation of the world; in this latter respect, he says, it is a
ceremonial precept. But Thomas does not say that this precept is Moral insofar
as it determines the observance of one day in the weekly cycle, which the
most illustrious *Walaeus* nevertheless concedes and proves. Hence it is
evident that these words of Thomas are no less a hindrance to him than to us.

It must therefore be concluded that the fourth precept is not, properly
speaking, partly Moral and partly Ceremonial.
