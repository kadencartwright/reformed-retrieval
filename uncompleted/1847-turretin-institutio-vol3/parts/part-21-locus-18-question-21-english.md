QUESTION XXI.

ON THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN BISHOP AND PRESBYTER.

Is the episcopate an order or rank of the ecclesiastical hierarchy distinct
from and superior to the presbyterate by divine right? We deny it.

I. Now that the primacy of Peter and the pope has been discussed, we must
proceed to the distinction between bishop and presbyter. On this question we
are engaged not only with the papists, who defend the affirmative with great
effort as the foundation of their hierarchy, but also with various
Protestants. Having retained the episcopal order after the Reformation, they
contend warmly for this superiority. There is certainly a great difference
between them and the papists concerning the antichristian tyranny which the
latter erect over consciences and from which the former are utterly remote.
Nevertheless, both agree in holding that bishops are superior to presbyters
by divine right with respect to the power of order and jurisdiction. For
although the Council of Trent debated sharply and at length by what immediate
right bishops are superior to presbyters—divine or papal—it is certain that
the voice of nearly all the bishops was that “the episcopate is of divine
right.” The exceptions were a few Italian bishops who, having hired out their
tongues for a price, fought for papal right, lest if bishops were said to have
been instituted immediately by Christ, they should become equal to the Roman
bishop and he should have no power or jurisdiction over them. The former
view is the one received among them today. Bellarmine, in his book *On the
Clergy*, chapter 14, holds that “the bishop is greater than the presbyter by
divine right, both with respect to the power of order and with respect to
jurisdiction.”

[Marginalia: State of the Question.]

II. For the state of the question we observe: 1. The question does not concern
the sort of presbyters now spoken of among the papists. By that name they
understand mass-priests, who boast that the right and power have been
conferred upon them to sacrifice and offer Christ’s body to God as a
propitiatory sacrifice. We maintain that this office is fictitious and indeed
sacrilegious. Rather, it concerns those who were appointed by the apostles in
particular churches and equipped with power to preach the Word, administer
the sacraments, and govern the church over which they had been placed. The
apostles could have named them by allusion to the custom of the Old Testament,
where the judges appointed in individual cities were called *zeqenim*,
“elders,” because men of advanced age are or ought to be grave and prudent,
as judges should be.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 170 | IA leaf 204 | PDF p. 204]

The men of the great Sanhedrin, however, were called *ziqne ha‘am*,
“the elders of the people,” in the New Testament. Thus those appointed by the
apostles to care for the church were called presbyters, not only on account
of age, because they were commonly chosen from the number of older men, but
especially on account of the honor and office which they held in the church
as its governors and judges.

III. 2. The question does not concern a distinction of order or polity among
several ministers of the same church, so that one presides over the others
and chairs sacred assemblies, whether under the title of bishop or
superintendent. We do not deny that this can fittingly be done, so that all
things may be done decently and in good order in the house of God. This is
practiced in many Protestant and Reformed churches, which have wished to
preserve among their pastors a certain good order, presidency, and
preeminence. They have their bishops, superintendents, prelates, deans, and
inspectors, to whom a certain more eminent dignity and authority than that of
the other pastors belongs by ecclesiastical right. Rather, the question
concerns a distinction of power: both as to the rank of order, by which the
bishop is superior in office and dignity and the presbyter inferior, and as
to jurisdiction, by which the latter is subject to the command of the former.
Our opponents allege such a distinction; we deny it.

IV. 3. The question does not concern ecclesiastical right derived from an
ancient custom which began to prevail not long after the time of the
apostles. We readily grant that, as will be seen later. It concerns divine
right founded on Christ’s institution and the apostles’ practice, and this we
deny.

[Marginalia: The identity of presbyter and bishop is proved. 1. From the
denial of hierarchy.]

V. The reasons are as follows. First, the papal hierarchy has no foundation
in the Word of God, either with respect to the word—which is not only
unwritten but anti-scriptural, contrary to the words *ministry*, *service*,
and the like by which ministry is described—or with respect to the thing
signified by the word, which is utterly foreign to the nature of ministry and
is strictly forbidden (Luke 22:25–26; 1 Pet. 5:3). Nor is it mentioned by
Paul anywhere that the orders of the church are discussed (1 Cor. 12:28;
Eph. 4:11). This certainly ought to have occurred if such an institution had
been made by Christ and rested on divine right. Hence it has rightly been
observed that if the Fathers had wished to follow our Savior’s manner of
speaking and acting, that of the apostles, and that of the ancient church,
they would have established not a sacred rule but a sacred ministry or sacred
service. For Peter Messia, a papist, long ago wrote with good reason in his
*Forest of Various Readings* that hierarchy as it exists today, joined to
sovereign power, was born not of the spirit of Christ but of the spirit of
Romulus.

[Marginalia: 2. From passages of Scripture.]

VI. Second, bishop and presbyter are everywhere taken in Scripture for one
and the same thing, so that the difference is only in the name, not in the
thing. One is called a bishop by reason of his office and duty, but a
presbyter by reason of his age and dignity; the same characteristics and the
same functions are assigned to each. There are various passages which prove
this, but the following stand out. In Acts 20:28 Paul summons the Ephesian
presbyters to himself to bid them farewell and designates them all by the name
of bishops. Thus there were none in that church but presbyters, and those same
men were bishops. “Take heed,” he says, “to yourselves and to the whole
flock, over which the Lord has appointed you bishops.” In Philippians 1:1 the
apostle greets all the saints dwelling at Philippi together with the “bishops
and deacons,” making no mention of presbyters. He undoubtedly includes them
under the name of bishops, since there have never been several bishops in one
church after they obtained a singular and principal power over presbyters.
Nor can it be doubted that there were various presbyters or pastors in that
church who governed it. Likewise, in 1 Timothy 3:2 he speaks of the various
virtues of a bishop without mentioning the presbyter. He certainly would not
have omitted him if he had thought the offices distinct by divine right,
since it is no less necessary to know the qualifications

[1847 vol. III printed p. 171 | IA leaf 205 | PDF p. 205]

of a presbyter than of a bishop, so that it may be established who should be
called to this office. This is confirmed from Titus 1:5, 7, where, after
saying that he left Titus in Crete to “appoint presbyters in every city,” he
teaches what those presbyters ought to be like and adds, “For a bishop must be
blameless.” This would be said without reason unless, in Paul’s judgment, the
same men whom he had previously called presbyters were also bishops. Here the
Greek scholia note that “he calls the presbyters bishops.” Jerome comments
excellently on the passage: “A bishop must be blameless as God’s steward.
Therefore a presbyter is the same as a bishop; and before factions arose in
religion at the devil’s prompting and it was said among the peoples, ‘I am of
Paul, I of Apollos, and I of Cephas,’ the churches were governed by the
common counsel of the presbyters. But after each man regarded those whom he
had baptized as his own and not Christ’s, it was decreed throughout the world
that one chosen from among the presbyters should be set over the others, to
whom the whole care of the church should belong, and that the seeds of
schisms should be removed.” This passage will be discussed more fully later.
The passages in which presbyters are listed together with the apostles
without any mention of bishops also belong here. No other reason can be given
for this except that those presbyters were also bishops, who were included
under the name of presbyters. Otherwise, if bishops by then had constituted
an order distinct from presbyters, how could no mention of them be made? Why
would Luke not have spoken of bishops, who held the higher rank, rather than
of presbyters? Consult Acts 11:30; 14:23; and 15:2, 6, 22, 23.

VII. The exceptions raised against these various passages are futile. (1.) It
is said that “the name presbyter can belong to a bishop because every bishop
must first be a presbyter, for the presbyterate is a step to the episcopate;
but not every presbyter is likewise a bishop.” Yet the passages cited show
that all the presbyters under discussion were also bishops. Thus in Acts 20
all the Ephesian pastors who are called presbyters are also called bishops;
and when the apostle notes the characteristics of a presbyter in the epistle
to Titus, he designates him by the name bishop. (2.) It is said that “one is a
bishop of the clergy, or a diocesan, while another is a bishop of the flock,
or a parish bishop. The latter is ordained by the diocesan and could properly
be called the bishop or overseer of the particular flock to which he was
appointed, although with respect to the diocesan who ordained him he holds
only the seat of a presbyter.” But this distinction between a bishop of the
clergy and a bishop of the flock is unheard of in Scripture. Scripture
nowhere presents a difference between the clergy and the flock; indeed, they
are taken for the same thing (1 Pet. 5:3). Nor can there be a bishop who is
not the bishop of some flock. (3.) It is said that “the Ephesian and
Philippian presbyters were designated by the titles of bishops with respect
to the flocks over which they had been placed, while they in turn had the
apostle as their bishop, who admonished them by his own right.” But this
assumes without proof that the apostle was bishop of those churches. He was
an ecumenical apostle, and the Ephesians and Philippians were subject to him
as an apostle, not as a bishop.

[Marginalia: 3. From ordination in common.]

VIII. Third, no ordination of a bishop separately from a presbyter is recorded
in Scripture. The apostles undoubtedly would not have omitted it if they had
believed the office to be distinct from and more honorable than the
presbyterate, just as they handed down the ordination of presbyters in various
passages (Acts 14:23; Titus 1:5). Nor should it be replied that such
presbyters were ordained by Paul, Barnabas, and Titus, who were manifestly
most eminent bishops. For it is assumed without proof that Paul and Titus
were bishops and ordained presbyters as bishops, since they were equipped
with a far greater authority, namely, as either apostles or evangelists. But
if they had established a hierarchical order, should they not have created
some bishop among the many presbyters

[1847 vol. III printed p. 172 | IA leaf 206 | PDF p. 206]

to ordain the presbyters? Since they are nowhere read to have done so, it is
clear that by appointing
presbyters town by town they also gave them authority and the right to ordain
other colleagues for the preservation of the ministry and the edification of
the church. In the same way, Timothy had already been told that grace was
given to him “through the laying on of the hands of the presbytery” (1 Tim.
4:14), which clearly proves that the presbytery possessed the right of
ordination. If Chrysostom and Jerome wish bishops to have been superior to
presbyters by ordination alone, they speak of their own times, when the
distinction had already been made, not of apostolic times.

[Marginalia: 4. From the silence of Scripture.]

IX. Fourth, when Paul lists the various orders of ministries in the church,
he makes no mention of the episcopate. That office certainly should not have
been omitted if it was to constitute the principal rank of ecclesiastical
ministry distinct from the presbyterate. Otherwise Paul would have failed in
his duty and given the faithful occasion for error by neglecting an
institution which rested on divine right and upon which the authority of the
entire gospel ministry was to be founded. Nor should it be said that this
rank is included either under apostles and evangelists, since those offices
were extraordinary and temporary, or under pastors, since the pastorate also
belongs to presbyters. Common features do not establish a distinction.

[Marginalia: 5. From the common right of the keys.]

X. Fifth, both presbyters and bishops received from Christ the same right to
bind and loose and to retain and forgive sins through the use of the keys
(Matt. 18:18; John 20:21–22). They possess the honor of the same embassy and
the same ministry of reconciliation (2 Cor. 5:18–20). This could not be said
if those two offices were essentially different by divine right. Nor can it
be answered that this power was granted to all the apostles in such a way
that through them and their successors, the bishops, it was also communicated
to presbyters, so that they possess it not immediately from Christ but
mediately through the hand of superiors. For it is assumed without proof that
bishops rather than presbyters are the apostles’ successors. Apostolic
preeminence and power, which depend upon their immediate and extraordinary
calling, admit successors no more than do their infallibility and right to
found the church. In the matters belonging to the ordinary ministry, all
presbyters are equally successors of the apostles, insofar as by their calling
the power of the keys, the preaching of the gospel, and the administration of
the sacraments belong equally to them. None of these requires the preeminence
of one over others. Nor can any part of the ministry be given which does not
regard them all equally.

[Marginalia: 6. From the testimony of the ancients.]

XI. Sixth, the ancients attribute this distinction not to divine right but to
human custom. Cyprian says in *On the Unity of the Church*, “There is one
episcopate, of which a share is held entire by each.” To Jerome’s passage on
Titus 1 cited above should be added another from his epistle to Evagrius,
where he confirms with many arguments “that the bishop and presbyter are the
same, and that one was later chosen to be set over the others as a remedy for
schism, lest each man, drawing the church of Christ to himself, should rend
it.” And a little later: “Presbyter and bishop: one is the name of age, the
other of dignity. Hence in the epistles to Titus and Timothy the ordination of
the bishop and deacon is discussed while presbyters are passed over entirely,
because the presbyter is included in the bishop.” Thus also Ambrose, or
whoever is the author of the commentary on the epistles, who was either older
than or contemporary with Ambrose. For he is cited under the name “the Holy
One” by Augustine, Ambrose’s disciple, in book 2, chapter 4. Speaking of the
succession of presbyters on Ephesians 4, he says: “The
apostle’s writings therefore do not agree in every respect with the order
which is now in the church, because they were written amid the very
beginnings. For he calls Timothy, whom he created a presbyter, also a bishop,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 173 | IA leaf 207 | PDF p. 207]

because at first bishops were called presbyters, so that when one departed,
the next succeeded him.” The same is clear from the epistles falsely
ascribed to Ignatius the Martyr. For whoever the author may be, who wrote
around the year of Christ 150, in the epistle to the Magnesians he calls the
presbytery, and the administration of the church through it, “the law of
Christ,” because it was established by the apostles according to Christ’s
institution and will. But he calls the arrangement which in his own time set
the bishop over the presbyters “the new institution.” Many more testimonies
of this kind could be brought from the ancients, by which it is established
that the order of bishops and presbyters was the same from the beginning.
The learned Blondel collected them in his *Apology for Jerome*, where he
invincibly proves Jerome’s position by many arguments from earliest, middle,
and late antiquity.

XII. To evade the force of these testimonies it is objected that a college of
presbyters could indeed have been established by the apostles at the church’s
beginning for its government, but that this lasted only a short time, until
experience found that manner of governing to be unsuitable because of the
presbyters’ disagreements and quarrels. Through them the church was split
into factions when some said, “I am of Paul, but I of Apollos, and so forth”
(1 Cor. 1:12). Thus, to remove those disagreements which had arisen while the
apostles were alive, the apostles themselves appointed one over the others,
with whom the entire care and power of administering the church should
reside. It is said that Jerome means nothing else when he says that “it was
decreed throughout the whole world” that one should be placed over the
others—namely, by the apostles themselves and with the consent of the whole
church. But this is alleged in vain. Apart from the fact that it is wrongly
asserted that the form of government admitted to have been first instituted
by the apostles through a college of presbyters and an aristocracy was not
good because quarrels and schisms arose from it—these arose not of themselves
but accidentally from human vice—even if those disagreements began to grow
during the apostles’ time, it does not follow that it was then decreed
throughout the world that one should be placed over the others. It followed
only in the course of time, after others everywhere became demented after the
example of the Corinthians and were divided into factions, which cannot be
proved to have occurred before A.D. 150. Thus Jerome does not say that it was
decreed throughout the world as soon as this was said among the Corinthians,
but “when it was said among the peoples,” something no one would refer to the
apostolic age. Therefore Jerome refers it to “the custom of the church which
gradually prevailed,” not “the Lord’s ordinance.” Yet he ought to have called
it the latter if such a decree had been made by the apostles.

XIII. If it is asked when this distinction began and how it was introduced
into the church, the answer is not difficult. The apostolic practice was such
that in places where they had converted a considerable multitude of people,
they immediately ordained a presbytery or ecclesiastical council, an assembly
of presbyters chosen by the votes of the faithful and consent of the church,
to govern the churches that had been established. Hence, after Paul had
established several churches in Asia, he is said in Acts 14:23 to have
“appointed presbyters in every church,” that is, several presbyters in each
church, who constituted its presbytery. These men were the ordinary pastors
of those churches and had the care of feeding them both by preaching the Word
and administering the sacraments and by exercising holy discipline. This was
done by the common counsel of all, so that none of them possessed power and
authority over his colleagues, but the care of all for the whole flock was
equal. Yet because in every assembly some order must be given among its
members to avoid confusion, one among the presbyters was first, whether this
place was given to age, gifts, or calling, and he presided over the assembly.
But this primacy was

[1847 vol. III printed p. 174 | IA leaf 208 | PDF p. 208]

only one of order and honor, such as is customary in synods and classes, not
of authority and jurisdiction. Otherwise they would not have been colleagues
and fellow-presbyters, as they are everywhere called in the writings of the
Fathers. Matters having been so established by the apostles, the churches
were governed with equal authority and by the common counsel of the
presbyters. When the first presbyter of the college died, the second succeeded
him, and the third succeeded the second according to the order of
cooperation. This manner of succession remained for some time until it was
changed near the end of the second century. Then the first member of the
council was selected by the choice of the other presbyters, and some power
over his fellow-presbyters was granted to him. This power gradually increased,
in part through the ambition of teachers, which was already creeping in under
the appearance of merit and dignity. Those who excelled above others in
learning and gifts bore it ill that less suitable men were placed over them;
thus they could have been responsible for having the first presbyter elevated
to the primacy by election rather than succession. It increased in part by
the consent of the churches themselves and the authority of councils, to whom
this manner of governing the church appeared better suited to avoid disputes
and the schisms born from them, as the passages of Ambrose and Jerome teach.
But the outcome taught that the remedy was worse than the disease, since in
this way schisms were not prevented, but the foundations of antichristian
dominion were laid. Finally, it increased in part through the unworthiness
and idleness of the presbyters. The commentary ascribed to Ambrose says on
Paul’s epistles, “Because presbyters began to be found unworthy to hold the
primacy, the method was changed by the foresight of a council, so that not
order but merit should make a bishop.” Certainly, as long as the apostles
lived, no such thing could be feared. Those whom they appointed, being
endowed with the discernment of spirits, could not be unsuitable. But after
their deaths, when favor and partiality rather than the merit of learning and
piety prevailed among men in choosing presbyters, many less worthy and
suitable men were drawn into that order, who obtained not only the primacy
but the presbyterate itself. Therefore the ancient custom was changed and
equality abolished.

XIV. Whatever the cause of this change may have been, it is certain that the
primacy conferred by election was at first no different from the preceding
one, namely, one only of order and honor. The only difference was that the
name bishop was assigned exclusively to the elected president, while the name
presbyter remained with the others. The president so moderated the assembly
that he ought to do nothing without consulting the presbytery; and if a
presbyter was to be ordained, he could not do so alone, but presbyters were
required to be present with him, placing their hands alongside the bishop’s
hand in consecrating him. Then the bishop ordained presbyters in such a way
that presbyters also were permitted to ordain presbyters and deacons. But
later, as the bishop’s authority increased over time, the power of ordaining
was transferred to him alone. Thus a superior order was created, yet all
bishops were equal to one another. Afterward those who occupied the see of
the chief city in each province held the primacy. They were therefore called
metropolitans or archbishops, and it belonged to them to convene provincial
synods, preside over them, and confirm newly elected bishops. In the beginning
the name patriarch was also common to them, as if they were rulers of one
people. For *patrē* or *patria* is the same as *ethnos*, as the learned
Salmasius properly observes from Socrates, book 5, chapter 8, in *On the
Primacy*, chapter 5. There he uses *patriarchia* for the province of a
metropolitan and *patriarchēs* for a metropolitan. But later, when Constantine
combined several provinces into one diocese, patriarchal sees were
distinguished from metropolitan sees, for someone was established as first
even among the several men aspiring to primacy with equal honor. Thus
patriarchs or primates, called exarchs of dioceses, were made superior to
metropolitans. So a vast structure was raised from very slight

[1847 vol. III printed p. 175 | IA leaf 209 | PDF p. 209]

beginnings and gradually rose to that height and pinnacle of honor. Thus by
various stages they came from the lowest to the highest summit of priestly
power. From the first presbyter came the bishop; from the first bishop, the
metropolitan; from the first metropolitan, the patriarch; and finally, from
the first patriarch, the one now called the pope. Thus the aristocracy in the
church became an oligarchy of patriarchs, from which the antichristian
monarchy was at last born. Pious men therefore had good reason to wish that
there had never been any departure from the apostolic institution by which
“unequal things were assigned to equals,” since experience has taught all too
clearly that various evils and disputes arose from this and that through such
a hierarchy the way was gradually paved for the papacy and antichristian
tyranny.

XV. Hence three things occur in the present Roman episcopate which must be
carefully distinguished. (1.) There is something divine, which it has from
Christ’s institution through the calling to the ministry of the Word and the
care of the church. (2.) There is something human, which accrued to it from
human prerogatives gradually usurped by human zeal and custom. (3.) There is
something antichristian, which was added to it through papal arrogance and
tyranny. With respect to the first, it cannot but be approved as a lawful and
divine office instituted by Christ himself. With respect to the second, it
can be tolerated as lawful. But with respect to the last, it cannot but be
condemned and rejected as unlawful and tyrannical.

[Marginalia: Sources of Solutions.]

XVI. A consequence from the government of the Old Testament to the government
of the New is invalid. (1.) When the priesthood is changed, the law and order
are also changed. Nor can the argument be urged for episcopal preeminence any
more than for the primacy of the pope, which is solidly refuted by our
writers; different times require different customs. (2.) If the Old Testament
is called the image and shadow of the New, this must not be understood with
reference to the ecclesiastical hierarchy, but to the presentation of the
things promised. These were proposed more obscurely and under shadows in the
Old Testament, but more clearly and with unveiled face under the New. (3.)
The high priest of the Old Testament, who was one, was not a type of bishops,
who are innumerable, but of Christ, according to Paul’s declaration in
Hebrews.

XVII. The angels of the churches whom Christ addresses in Revelation 2 and 3
should not be taken particularly and individually, but comprehensively and
collectively—not for some single bishop to the exclusion of the other pastors,
but for the whole presbytery or their college, to whom the episcopate, or care
of oversight over the flock, belonged. Nor is it novel for a singular noun to
be taken collectively. Thus the college of priests, whose duty was to teach
the people, is designated by the name “angels of Jehovah” (Mal. 2:7).
Likewise, the “angel of Jehovah” is said to “encamp around the faithful,” that
is, the legions of angels which constitute an army. So also the name Son of
God is taken collectively for the people of Israel (Ex. 4:23; Hos. 11:1).
That the word *angel* should be taken in this manner here can be proved from
various considerations. (1.) Christ is said to write to the churches, and in
verse 11 of the first chapter John is ordered to send the message to the seven
churches; and in chapters 2 and 3, “He who has ears, let him hear what the
Spirit says to the churches.” In verse 10 he collectively addresses the
whole church, or at least the presbytery: “Behold, the devil is about to cast
some of you into prison.” Likewise, in verse 24, “But I say to you and to the
rest,” that is, to the members of the church. (2.) It is certain from Acts
20:28 that the Ephesian church had several bishops or pastors at the same
time. Nor can it be said that not only the Ephesian bishops but those of all
Asia had gathered at Miletus, because Luke expressly says that Paul summoned
the Ephesian pastors to himself. (3.) Aretas, bishop of Caesarea, properly
observes in his commentary on Revelation, following Andrew, likewise bishop
of Caesarea, that the angels designate the church itself, because whatever is
said here either for or against it concerns not

[1847 vol. III printed p. 176 | IA leaf 210 | PDF p. 210]

the pastor separately but the whole church. He says, “He calls the angel of
Ephesus the church which was there; for the angel set over it did not sin, to
whom the command ‘Repent’ is to be heard,” and so forth. (4.) The Holy Spirit
could also have understood the presbytery or church collectively while
designating it in the singular under the one who was first in order and honor
among the presbyters. The principal care and oversight rested upon him, and
he was consequently blamed or praised if anything was done well or badly in
the church. (5.) If some of our writers wish bishops to be understood, by
them they do not understand bishops in the strict sense as distinguished
from presbyters, but in the broad sense, in which bishops are identified with
presbyters and the whole church is included under them. Furthermore, it is
not obscure why pastors are designated by the name angels. They have a
remarkable kinship with angels with respect to calling and office, because
they are sent by God; with respect to knowledge and understanding of divine
things; with respect to holiness and chastity; with respect to ardor, zeal,
and charity; with respect to vigilance and readiness in carrying out their
office; and in other matters of this kind.

XVIII. Titus and Timothy, who presided for a time over certain churches, are
not to be regarded as ordinary pastors but as evangelists (2 Tim. 4:5), that
is, extraordinary ministers who served as the apostles’ deputies. They were
sent there for a time to set in order the remaining things which Paul had
been unable to arrange because of the brevity of his stay. But they were not
specially attached to any church and diocese as diocesan bishops. Paul never
calls them bishops. Nor, if they are so named in the subscriptions of the
epistles—the one bishop of the Ephesians and the other of the Cretans—does it
follow that they actually were such. Learned men admit that those
subscriptions are not canonical, and the variation which occurs in them
abundantly proves that they are of more recent date.

XIX. Aerius was marked and condemned as a heretic not principally because he
asserted the equality of ministers, but because of Arianism, as Augustine
says in chapter 53 of *To Quodvultdeus*. 2. If the former reason also had a
place in his condemnation, the consequence from a human act to divine right
is invalid. And just as he was unjustly condemned because he opposed the
superstition then creeping in and gaining strength concerning sacrifices for
the dead and the invocation of saints, so too in this instance the mark of
heresy was branded upon him groundlessly and unjustly. Otherwise Jerome, who
defended the same equality of rank, could have been charged with the crime of
heresy.

XX. Episcopal government is either primitive and apostolic, the same as
presbyterial government and instituted by the apostles according to Christ’s
will and command; or it is secondary and ecclesiastical, distinct from
presbyterial government and introduced by the custom of the church and human
counsel rather than by the truth of the Lord’s ordinance. We acknowledge
that in the former sense bishops are frequently mentioned among the ancients
immediately after the apostolic age. In the latter sense we deny it.

XXI. The testimonies of the Fathers concerning a succession of bishops in
individual churches drawn from the very age of the apostles do not overturn
the identity of presbyterate and episcopate which we assert. For those
testimonies do not prove that in those earliest times anyone was set over the
college of presbyters other than the men first ordained, who did not cease to
be presbyters. Though they obtained a new weight of authority by the brethren’s
discretionary delegation, this is illustrated by the example of the ephors
among the Lacedaemonians and the archons among the Athenians. Although there
were several men endowed with equal power, the name of only one—the
first—was placed at the head of the city’s annals. This does not prove that
there was only one ephor or archon, but that he was the first in that year.

XXII. Although the precise time cannot be assigned for the ecclesiastical
institution according to which the bishop became superior to the presbyter,
it does not follow that it did not occur. It is enough that matters were not
so from the beginning. Who does not know that many events are noticed after
they have happened rather than while they are happening, especially when
changes occur gradually and imperceptibly? It is sufficiently clear from the
foregoing that there were various stages of this change, which increased
through the course of the centuries and at last reached the summit where it
now stands.

XXIII. The canons which assert that a presbyter cannot consecrate and ordain a
bishop but that a bishop can ordain a presbyter, because the lesser cannot
bless the greater and no one gives what he does not have, are later than the
change of the ancient order and the new institution by which it was decreed
that one elected bishop should be consecrated by bishops and not presbyters.
Meanwhile, the right and power of electing and ordaining remained with the
whole presbytery, as that in which the whole power of governing the church
resides. Thus the bishop was indeed greater than individual presbyters taken
separately, because he was first among them, but not greater than the whole
presbytery taken together, from which he received his power.

XXIV. It is assumed without proof that bishops succeeded the apostles and
presbyters the seventy disciples. 1. No one could succeed the apostles in the
apostolic office as such, because it was extraordinary and temporary; but
with respect to the ordinary office of teaching and governing the church, not
only bishops but presbyters—that is, all pastors—succeed them. 2. Although
the rank of the apostolic order was eminent, nevertheless in their first
mission equal rights were given to the apostles and the seventy disciples,
nor did the former ever usurp jurisdiction or power over the latter. 3. If
some of the Fathers say that presbyters succeed the seventy disciples and
bishops the apostles, they are speaking after the change had already been
made. Thus they do not signify that this inequality of ranks was established
by divine right, but that, being introduced by human custom, it has some
similarity to that ancient distinction.

XXV. Although Timothy was appointed by the apostle as a judge of presbyters in
1 Timothy 5:19, when Paul says that “an accusation is not to be received
against a presbyter except under two witnesses,” it does not follow that he
is presupposed to be a bishop superior to presbyters. 1. Timothy is not
regarded here as a bishop and ordinary minister, but as an evangelist endowed
with apostolic and infallible authority. 2. Even if he were regarded as a
bishop, it is assumed that inferiors are judged only by superiors. Yet who
does not know that equals are often judged by equals? This occurs especially
in ecclesiastical judgment, whose power belongs to the presbytery not only
over inferiors but also over equals as colleagues. Therefore, an accusation
had to be brought before Timothy not because he alone was judge of the
presbyters, but because he was first in the college by which judgment was to
be rendered. 3. Here *the elder* is properly a name not of office but of age,
being opposed to *the younger*.

XXVI. It was long ago observed by learned men—Cajetan, Erasmus, Valla,
Beatus Rhenanus, Sirmond and Petavius themselves, and others among the
papists—and demonstrated by the celebrated Dallæus with many arguments that
the book *On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy* was not the genuine offspring of
Dionysius the Areopagite, who was a contemporary of the apostles, but a
spurious work by a much later author who arose only in the fifth century.
Before that time the book is never mentioned: not among those who praise
Dionysius, such as Aristides and Dionysius of Corinth; not among those who
compiled catalogs of ecclesiastical writers, such as Jerome and Gennadius;
not among those who write on Acts 17, where Luke speaks of him; and not among
those who refute the heresies condemned in the Areopagitic writings.

Hypatius is the first. In A.D. 533 he rejected certain passages from that work
which the Severian heretics had presented to him and denied that the work was
by the Areopagite.

XXVII. Although we believe that the presbyterial order and equality of rank
among pastors received among us more closely approach the institution of
Christ and the apostles and the practice of the apostolic church, we would
nevertheless be unwilling either to trouble anyone over this point or to
condemn the practice of the evangelical and Reformed churches among our
brethren which have retained episcopal government until now, as more suited
to the disposition and customs of their people and to their form of civil
government, and more useful for good order and the exercise of discipline.
Only let Babylonian arrogance and papal tyranny always be absent, and let
that order be acknowledged as belonging to ecclesiastical, not divine, right.
In turn we expect such fairness and moderation from the brethren who differ
from us here that they do not presume by a definitive sentence to condemn as
schismatic and destitute of a lawful calling other churches which differ from
their own and, in the liberty granted them by Christ, employ the presbyterial
form of government as one better suited to their condition and to the
inclinations and customs of their people. Rather, let them not cease to
embrace those churches with brotherly affection, so that the bond of union
and brotherhood may always remain unbroken and, with minds and strength thus
joined, the work of God may be advanced for the increase of Christ’s kingdom
and the destruction of Antichrist.
