QUESTION V.

THE UNITY OF THE COVENANT OF GRACE.

*Was the covenant of grace one and the same in substance in both Testaments?
We affirm against the Socinians, Anabaptists, and Remonstrants.*

I. This most weighty controversy is ours with the ancient and more recent
Pelagians—namely, the Socinians, Remonstrants, Anabaptists, and others—who deny
the identity of the gracious covenant and are unwilling that the fathers of
the Old Testament were saved by God’s gratuitous mercy in Christ the
Mediator—the θεανθρώπῳ, *God-man*—through faith in him who was to come.

[Marginal heading: The opinion of the Socinians.]

II. The Socinians express their position in chapter 5 of the *Racovian
Catechism*, where they profess that Christ’s law contains two promises,
namely, eternal life and the Holy Spirit, which were not contained in the law
of Moses: “Nowhere,” it says, “will you find in the law of Moses either
eternal life or the gift of the Holy Spirit promised to those who obey the
precepts of the law, as it is agreed they are promised in the law given
through Christ.” Socinus confirms this more clearly in *On the Office of
Christ*: “We therefore say that the new covenant has most openly brought us
the promise of eternal life, besides the promise of the remission of sins,”
and so forth; “and finally the promise of the Holy Spirit to be obtained by
all the faithful. Since that old covenant lacks and has lacked all these and
has proposed earthly happiness, it is easy to recognize the excellence of the
new covenant in this respect.” Volkelius endeavors to prove the same by
various arguments in book 3, chapter 11 of *On Religion*, and Smalcius in
chapters 2 and 7 of *On the Divinity of Christ*.

They nevertheless do not deny that the Old Testament fathers were saved, or
will be saved, with eternal blessedness. “To conceal nothing,” Smalcius says
in chapter 7, “we firmly maintain that even if no open promise of eternal life
appears in the Old Covenant, it will nevertheless one day be given to all
those who ever trusted and obeyed God.” Volkelius agrees with him, likewise in
chapter 11. Nor do they deny that God remitted their sins, although he had not
promised this or signified that he would do it (Socinus, *On the Savior*, part
3, chapter 2, and *On Justification*, chapter 8); that the fathers had some
knowledge and hope of eternal life, although it was not promised to them
(Smalcius, *On the Divinity of Christ*, chapter 7); or that Christ and grace
and salvation in Christ were once foretold in the Old Testament. Socinus says
in his lectures: “It is certain not only that Christ truly was and is our
Savior, but also that this was foretold through the prophets, as among other
passages chapters 23 and 33 of Jeremiah establish,” and so forth.

But on that account they are unwilling to say either that the ancients looked
to Christ or that they were saved in the hope of him who was to come. The same
author writes in his second letter to Radecius (*Bibliotheca Sociniana*, p.
377): “I disagree with you in this: you seem to concede that the godly men who
lived under the Old Testament looked to Christ in those ceremonies and
sacrifices in which he is foreshadowed, and were saved in the hope of him who
was to come. I can by no means persuade myself of this,” and so forth. Hence
they distinguish the faith of those times from ours: the former faith was in
God simply, but the latter is also faith in Christ. Smalcius says in his final
disputation against Frantzius: “Nor was that faith

[Printed p. 172 | Internet Archive PDF 200 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

faith in Jesus Christ, but faith placed in God and his promises.” And in book
4, chapter 3, Volkelius wishes the faith of the ancients and ours to differ in
three respects, especially this: “Today we are commanded to believe both in
Christ and in God, but that such faith by no means existed of old is evident
even from the fact that Christ did not yet exist then.”

[Marginal heading: The opinion of the Remonstrants.]

III. The Remonstrants are not far removed from the Socinians. Arminius indeed,
in his disputation comparing the law and the gospel, correctly places the
agreement of the two Testaments in one and the same matter of each: the
obedience of faith required in each, the inheritance of eternal life promised
through the imputation of the righteousness of faith and gracious adoption in
Christ, and Christ as their one object. He places the difference in certain
accidents which detract nothing from their substantial unity. But those who
followed him speak otherwise. In chapter 7 the Apologists call “the question
of faith in Jesus Christ—whether and how it had a place under the Old
Testament—ill-framed, ambiguous, and useless, and it is certain that there is
no passage from which it appears that this faith was commanded or flourished
under the Old Testament.”

They ultimately distinguish the manner of revelation as clear or obscure and
say that faith in Jesus Christ was prescribed in the Old Testament neither
openly nor directly, yet indirectly and obscurely, being comprehended and
wrapped up under faith in God’s general favor and grace. They teach the same
about the promise of eternal life in chapter 15. Episcopius confirms this in
book 3, section 4: “From these things it is easy to gather what must be held
about that famous question: whether the promise of eternal life had a place
in the old covenant, or rather was comprehended in the covenant itself. If
the specific promises expressed in the old covenant itself are considered,
it must be confessed that no explicit promise of eternal life is found among
them. If anyone thinks otherwise, it belongs to him to produce the passage in
which it appears, which I think impossible for him. But if the general
promises are considered, on the other hand it must be confessed that they are
of such a kind that the promise of eternal life not only seems to underlie
them, but it must also be believed that by God’s intention it did underlie
them.”

[Marginal heading: The opinion of the Anabaptists.]

IV. Not a few *Anabaptists* err with the same error, as is evident from
articles 15 and 16 of the Frankenthal Colloquy. There they speak of the
Israelite people no differently than of some herd of pigs, foolishly claiming
that the Lord fattened it in this land without any hope of heavenly
immortality, and asserting that only temporal goods and earthly promises were
given to them. This was also the impiety of Servetus, against which Calvin
argues solidly in book 2, chapter 10.

[Marginal heading: The state of the Question.]

V. From these things the state of the Question can be gathered without
obscurity. It is not asked, 1. Whether the covenant of grace varied with
respect to accidents and the different modes and degrees of administration
and revelation. For we confess that under this σχέσει, *relation*, there is a
manifold difference between the Old and New Testaments, or the old and new
economies, as will be proved in its place. Rather, it is asked whether it was
the same with respect to its *substance* and the essential parts of the
covenant, that is, both with respect to the covenant people themselves and
with respect to the covenant compact consisting in the parties’ mutual
obligation. Is Christ the same Mediator in each? Is there the same faith in
Christ, the promises of the same spiritual and heavenly benefits, and the
same way of reconciliation and salvation, with only the economy and
administration of the covenant varying? Our adversaries deny this; we affirm
it.

VI. It is not asked whether the Old Testament fathers were saved; whether
their sins were remitted; whether they had some hope of eternal life; or
whether Christ was foretold to them. For most of our adversaries do not dare
deny these things. Rather, it is asked whether they looked to Christ and were
saved in the hope of him who was to come;

[Printed p. 173 | Internet Archive PDF 201 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

whether not only temporal but also spiritual and heavenly promises of eternal
life and the Holy Spirit were given to them; and whether the same covenant
made with us in Christ had already been contracted with them, although more
obscurely and under a covering. They deny this; we assert it, maintaining that
Christ was not only foretold but also promised to the fathers, and that they
were saved by his grace no less in the Old Testament than we are saved in the
New. From the beginning no other name was given under heaven from which
salvation could be hoped (Acts 4:12), and this according to the inviolable
promise of the gracious covenant.

[Marginal heading: The identity of the covenant of grace is proved, 1. From Scripture.]

VII. The arguments are these. 1. Generally, Scripture teaches that the
covenant of grace which God contracted with us in the New Testament is the
same as the covenant made long ago with Abraham. Hence Zechariah says in his
song (Luke 1:68, 70, 72, 73) that “God visited and redeemed his people,
according to what he had promised through the mouth of the holy prophets who
were from every age, to show mercy toward the fathers and remember his holy
covenant, according to the oath made to Abraham.” And Peter, addressing the
Jews concerning the days of Christ which all the prophets had foretold, says
in Acts 3:25: “You are the children of the prophets and of the covenant made
with the fathers, saying to Abraham, In your seed all nations will be
blessed,” and so forth. Paul designates the promise by the name of the gospel
in Gal. 3:8: “Scripture, foreseeing that God justifies the nations by faith,
preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham, saying, In you all nations will be
blessed.”

Hence in Rom. 4:3 he presents Abraham’s justification through faith as an
example of ours and proposes him as the *father of believers*, both Jews and
Gentiles. This could not have been done without the identity of the covenant.
It is confirmed by the fact that in Gal. 3:17 he calls the promise given to
Abraham διαθήκην προκεκυρωμένην ὑπὸ τοῦ Θεοῦ εἰς Χριστὸν, *a covenant
previously ratified by God in Christ*.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the identity of the covenant clause.]

VIII. 2. Specifically, from all the parts of the covenant of grace, which were
the same in each. Such is the *clause of the covenant*, that God will be our
God and the God of our seed. Just as it was already proposed to Abraham in
Gen. 17, renewed to Moses in the vision of Exod. 3 and everywhere in the
legislation, and confirmed during and after the captivity in Ezek. 36, so no
other clause was proposed in the covenant of grace as the foundation of all
benefits, both spiritual and heavenly (Matt. 22; 2 Cor. 6:16; Rev. 21:3).

[Marginal heading: 3. From the identity of the Mediator.]

IX. 3. The *identity of the Mediator*, who was given in the Old and New
Testaments and is the foundation of the covenant: namely, the blessed Seed of
the woman, who had to crush the serpent’s head (Gen. 3:15, compared with Heb.
2:14 and Rom. 16:20); the Seed of Abraham in whom all nations of the earth
were to be blessed (Gen. 22:18), that is, Christ (Gal. 3:16). In the Old
Testament he is the *Angel of Jehovah’s presence* (Isa. 63:9), the *Angel of
the covenant* (Mal. 3:1), and the *covenant of the people and the nations*,
that is, its Mediator (Isa. 42:6; 49:8), who bore the people’s diseases and
was bruised for their sins (Isa. 53:5, 6). In the New Testament he is the
Mediator between God and human beings (1 Tim. 2:5) and the Mediator of the New
Testament and a better compact (Heb. 8:6; 9:15), who “is the same yesterday,
today, and forever” (Heb. 13:8), through whose grace the fathers were saved no
less than we (Acts 15:10, 11), to whom all the prophets bear testimony (Acts
10:43), the one name given under heaven through which we must be saved (Acts
4:12), and the way, the truth, and the life, so that no one can come to the
Father except through him (John 14:6).

X. It is vainly objected to Heb. 13:8, where Christ is said to be “the same
yesterday, today, and forever,” either that it treats the doctrine, not the
Person and the covenant established in it;

[Printed p. 174 | Internet Archive PDF 202 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

for if the doctrine is eternal, so are the Person who delivers it and the
covenant proposed in it. Or it is objected that *yesterday* denotes only the
days of his flesh before the ascension and *today* the time after the
ascension. This conflicts with Paul’s purpose, which is to exhort to constancy
of faith by an argument drawn from the identity and perpetuity of the
evangelical doctrine whose object is Christ the Mediator. Or it is objected
that *yesterday and today* denote a new and recent matter which began not long
ago, just as in Job 8:9 human beings are called men of yesterday, and among
the Greeks ἐφήμεροι, *of today*, on account of the brevity of their life.

But the expressions are plainly dissimilar. To be מתמול, *of yesterday
and the day before*, tends to abase human nature. Here, however, Christ is said
already to have been yesterday, to be today, and to be forever through all
the differences of time, to designate his glory and eternity. Nor is he only
said to be yesterday, today, and forever, but *the same*: that is, not one
person and another, whether now, then, or afterward, but always and everywhere
the same Jesus Christ. This could not be said unless Christ had been the
Mediator of the covenant of grace from the beginning, and the Prophet and
Teacher of the church.

XI. Acts 15:11 cannot be understood of the apostles compared with converted
Gentile believers, but not with the fathers of the Old Testament, as they
would have it. 1. Peter had just spoken about the fathers, and thus the words
that follow are fittingly referred to the fathers: *But through the grace of
our Lord Jesus Christ we believe that we are saved καθ’ ὃν τρόπον κἀκεῖνοι,
just as they were*. 2. Peter’s purpose requires this. He wishes to prove that
the yoke of the ceremonial law must not be imposed upon the necks of the
Gentiles, because the fathers themselves could not bear it, but were saved by
the same grace of Christ as we are. 3. If the apostles had wished to associate
themselves with the Gentiles in the manner of salvation, they would not have
said, *We believe we are saved just as they are*, but the reverse: They, just
as we. For it did not belong to the apostles to follow the Gentiles and set
them before themselves as an example in the manner of salvation, but the
contrary. 4. Nor is it absurd for Peter, while discussing the manner in which
the Gentiles had to attain salvation, to conclude with an argument drawn from
the fathers, because the manner of salvation is one for all. And if the yoke
of the law was ἀβάστακτον, *unbearable*, to the fathers, it is rightly
gathered that the Gentiles must not be pressed with that yoke.

XII. It is also falsely contended that the words of Acts 4:12, “No name other
than the name of Jesus has been given for salvation,” must be understood only
of the time when Christ was manifested and cannot be extended into the past.
The words themselves refute the vanity of this gloss. They do not assert that
now no name is given in which human beings may be saved, but that no name has
been given εἰς ἀνθρώπους, *among human beings*, at any time. The matter itself
also demands this. Since no salvation can be given to sinful man without a
Mediator, and there is no Mediator besides Christ, it follows either that the
fathers had no salvation or that they were saved through Christ. Nor, if the
words are stated in the present, are they thereby denied of the past, any more
than of the future. Rather, this is done to assert the truth of Christ’s
mediation at every time.

XIII. The words ἀπὸ καταβολῆς κόσμου, *from the foundation of the world*, in
Rev. 13:8 must not be separated from those immediately preceding, *the Lamb
slain*, and joined by transposition with *the book of life*, as is done in
Rev. 17:8. 1. There is no necessity for this transposition, which does violence
to the course of the discourse. It flows far more fittingly when we read, *whose
names are not written in the book of life of the Lamb slain from the beginning
of the world*, than according to the adversaries’ position, *whose names from
the beginning of the world are not written in the book of life of the Lamb
slain*. For since the book of life is called *the Lamb’s book of life* in Rev.
21:27, it must not be torn away from the words ἀρνίου ἐσφαγμένου, *of the Lamb
slain*. 2. *The Lamb slain* is never stated without qualification; mention is
always made either of the time from which he was slain, the persons for whom
he was slain, or the reasons for which he was slain. 3. He is very properly
said to have been *slain from the foundation of the world*, not

[Printed p. 175 | Internet Archive PDF 203 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

in himself, but *intentionally* in God’s decree, *objectively* in the promises
of the Word and the fathers’ faith, and *typically* in the sacraments and
sacrifices. 4. Even if it were granted, which it is not, that the words must be
explained in the other way, the force of the argument would not perish,
because no one can be written in the book of life except in Christ slain, or
in his death.

[Marginal heading: 4. From the identity of the condition.]

XIV. Fourth, because the *condition* of the covenant is the same in each,
namely, *faith*, through which they obtained salvation. This is evident in
Abraham, whose faith is said to have been imputed for righteousness (Gen.
15:6) and is set before us as an example (Rom. 4:11; Gal. 3:6, 7, 8); in David
(Ps. 116:10, compared with 2 Cor. 4:13); and in the ancients whose faith is
celebrated throughout Heb. 11. To this belongs the statement in Hab. 2:4 that
the just shall live by faith, where mention is made not only of faith but also
of justification depending upon faith (Rom. 1:17). Hence Peter testifies that
the prophets bear witness to Christ that “whoever believes in him will receive
the remission of sins” (Acts 10:43).

Nor can it be objected that the faith of the ancients was a general faith in
God, not a specific faith in Christ the Savior, because the contrary is
evident from various things. 1. No faith can be saving unless it rests upon
Christ. 2. The subject is the faith by which they regarded God as their God
and heaven as their homeland (Heb. 11), but this cannot be done without
Christ. 3. It is the faith by which they looked to Christ himself and preferred
his reproach to all treasures (v. 26). 4. Not only a general but also a
specific command to believe in Christ is found in the Old Testament (Exod.
23:20, 21; Deut. 18:18, 19; Ps. 2:12; Isa. 53:1, 5). If the faith of the
ancients were not the same as ours, it would wrongly be proposed for our
imitation (Heb. 12:1, 2; Rom. 4:12), and Paul could not solidly argue from the
faith and justification of the father of believers to ours (Gal. 3; Rom. 4).

[Marginal heading: 5. From the identity of the promises.]

XV. Fifth, because the same *spiritual promises* were given to them as to us,
although often beneath the veil and husk of temporal things. This is evident,
1. *Generally*, with respect to the promises, from Acts 13:32: *We preach to
you as gospel*, Paul says, *the promise made to the fathers*. Hence it is
evident that the gospel preached by Paul was no different from the promises
made to the fathers, all the more because Paul, the most faithful herald of
the promises, taught nothing beyond what Moses and the prophets taught (Acts
26). Specifically, certain spiritual blessings conferred upon believers in
the New Testament had already been promised and given to Abraham (Acts 3:25,
26; Gal. 3:8, 9): the *gift of the Holy Spirit* (Gal. 3:14; Ezek. 36:26, 27),
*justification and the remission of sins* (Gen. 15:6; Ps. 32:1; Isa. 43:25;
Jer. 31:33; Acts 10:43), and *sanctification* (Deut. 30:6; Ezek. 36:26; Ps.
51:12).

*Eternal life* is evident both from the *covenant promise*, in which Christ
invincibly taught that eternal life and the resurrection are included (Matt.
22), and from the *inheritance* given to Abraham and his seed, which is none
other than eternal life (Gal. 3:18; Heb. 9:15); from the *city which has
foundations* and the heavenly homeland which the fathers expected (Heb.
11:10); and from the life promised to believers (Isa. 55:3). Hence Job knows
that his Redeemer lives (Job 19:25); David is persuaded of his resurrection
and life (Pss. 16:10; 22:26); and Daniel expects the resurrection to eternal
life (Dan. 12:1, 2).

XVI. It cannot be objected, 1. With respect to the promises concerning Christ
in the Old Testament, that they were indeed given to the fathers but not for
the fathers, but for believers of the New Testament, just as promises of the
calling of the Gentiles were given to the ancients but only for New Testament
times. For it is absurd that the prophets gave such promises to believers,
and believers heard them, but did not apply them to themselves, although they
were oppressed by the same evils as the others for whom the promises are
supposed to have been made, and equally needed the same remedy. Since the
prophets were sent to the ancient people to sustain them in the hope of the
coming Messiah and of salvation to be

[Printed p. 176 | Internet Archive PDF 204 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

acquired through him, who does not see that such promises were proposed to
the fathers for their own consolation? 2. The promises pertained to them in
the same manner as the adoption, glory, covenants, legislation, and worship
(Rom. 9:4), but these properly pertained to the ancients. 3. If the promises
had not pertained to them, they could not and ought not have applied them to
themselves, as was done by Abraham (Gen. 15:6), who was also justified for
this reason (Rom. 4); by Jacob (Gen. 49:18); by David (Pss. 16; 23; 40; 110;
118, and everywhere else); by Isaiah (Isa. 9:6), “A child is born to us, a Son
is given to us”; by the whole Israelite church throughout Isa. 53; by Jeremiah
(chapter 23); and in Mic. 5:2. Peter could not say that all the prophets
testify that anyone who believes in Christ will receive remission of sins,
with no exception among believers of either the Old or New Testament (Acts
10:43). Nor could Paul say that Christ became Mediator for the expiation of
sins committed under the Old Testament, so that those called might obtain the
promise of the eternal inheritance. 4. The calling of the Gentiles, which
contributed nothing at all to the Jews’ salvation, is one thing; the coming of
the Messiah and grace in Christ, without whom no hope of salvation could have
shone upon them, is another.

XVII. 2. It is no better answered that the oracle cited by Christ in Matt. 22
was too obscure to be perceived by believers, or that it is not a general
promise of future life but a specific one which regarded Abraham, Isaac, and
Jacob by name, as Volkelius says in book 3, chapter 11. For although the
promise of eternal life in this oracle is proposed more obscurely in
comparison with the clarity of New Testament promises, it does not follow
that the fathers could not perceive it so far as it could contribute to their
consolation. Indeed, unless the proof had been evident and clear, the
Sadducees would not have been silenced by it. They were silenced not because
of the speaker’s authority but because of the evidence of the reasoning. Nor
can it be called a special grace given to the patriarchs, because it extends
to their seed (Gen. 17:7). Otherwise Christ’s proof against the Sadducees would
not be sufficiently firm, nor could the psalmist call the nation whose God is
Jehovah blessed (Ps. 33:12).

XVIII. 3. It is wrongly added that the fathers are said to have expected the
city which has foundations (Heb. 11:16), not because it was promised to them,
but because it was suited to their wishes and agreeable to their deeds, and
because it was credible that God would give it to his worshipers, as the same
Volkelius says, along with the *Racovian Catechism* on Christ’s prophetic
office. For what hope can there be which rests upon no Word? Indeed, since it
is proposed as an act of faith, it necessarily regards and presupposes a
promise. Hence they are said to have “died according to faith” (v. 13), and to
“seek and desire a homeland” (vv. 14, 16). But how can something be sought and
desired which is not known, since there is no desire for the unknown? Nor
must the empty and doubtful opinion of immortality which the heathen, having
no hope (Eph. 2:12), could conceive be confused with the firm and infallible
hope of eternal life which the ancients had from the Word.

XIX. 4. It is vainly said that Job 19:25 treats temporal deliverance from the
troubles with which the man of God was contending and a vision, or clearer
knowledge, of God’s power, wisdom, and providence, as in chapter 42:5, “Now my
eye sees you.” Although this is the view of the Jews and also of some
Christians, many things prove that Job looked farther and consoled himself
with confidence in the resurrection and eternal life against his calamities
and human beings’ perverse judgments about him. 1. He expects this benefit
from his גאל, *Redeemer*, and גאל is none other than Christ. 2. He expects it
at the time of the last judgment, for he says that he knows his Redeemer lives
and will at the last stand upon the earth. 3. He expects it only after his death,
after his body has been consumed and eaten away by worms, since

[Printed p. 177 | Internet Archive PDF 205 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

after his skin has been laid aside and his body consumed by worms, he is to be
restored whole and will see God in his flesh. 4. This vision cannot simply be
referred to the knowledge of God’s power and providence of which chapter 42:5
speaks. There Job hears God addressing him immediately and experiences his
presence more nearly than before, which excites serious compunction in him.
That contributed little to his consolation, could not certainly persuade him
of his restoration, and, even if it had persuaded him, could not have produced
such great joy and πληροφορίαν, *full assurance*, of faith in him. Therefore,
it is more correctly understood of the beatific vision, by hope of which he
consoled himself in his troubles and through which, secure of eternal
salvation, he most certainly expected his resurrection.

XX. It is no more truly contended that Dan. 12:2 does not treat the universal
resurrection of the dead, because it is referred only to *many*, but the
restoration of the Jewish people from the immense calamity it was to suffer
under Antiochus. This, they say, is denoted by *that time*, namely, the time
discussed thus far, which was the time of Antiochus. 1. The words themselves
prove the contrary, for they treat a resurrection properly so called from the
dust of the earth, and one that is either to eternal life or eternal reproach.
But temporal deliverance cannot be called a deliverance to eternal life,
since believers are still liable to death, nor to eternal reproach, since
human beings can repent. 2. It is not necessary that “that time” discussed in
verse 1 be understood as the time when Antiochus was to persecute the church
most severely, because that phrase is often used without any reference to
what precedes, as in Matt. 3:1 and 4:1, and τὸ אז can signify *after*, as in
Josh. 5:5.

Thus the sense is: After that deliverance of the people through Antiochus’s
death, another far more glorious deliverance will follow through Michael,
that is, Christ, yet so that most severe calamities precede it, which the
blessed resurrection will then follow. Therefore, that time which followed
the death of Antiochus was the time of the gospel, in which Christ had to
accomplish the work of the church’s redemption. And because it is contained
in Christ’s two advents, the first and the second, the whole is comprehended
here by the Holy Spirit, who does not pursue an unbroken series of events and
times but only marks notable changes in the church’s condition. Therefore,
here are designated, 1. The time of Christ’s first advent and what he would do
for the church; 2. The time of the second advent in the resurrection, or the
completion of happiness (v. 2). Nor should it seem strange if a transition is
made here from Christ’s first advent to the second and widely separated times
are joined on account of their similarity, just as the destruction of the
temple and of the world are joined in Matt. 24. Thus the salvation of the
church is most fittingly joined with the final resurrection, because in the
world all things are confused and no matter of salvation is found anywhere
unless we raise our minds there. Moreover, *many* are here put for *all*, as
often elsewhere.

[Marginal heading: 6. From the identity of the sacraments.]

XXI. Sixth, because the sacraments, which are seals of the covenant, were the
same in substance in each Testament and signified and sealed Christ and his
benefits. This is evident both with respect to the ordinary sacraments—the
circumcision called the seal of the righteousness of faith (Rom. 4:11), and
the Passover which is fulfilled in Christ (1 Cor. 5:7)—and the extraordinary
ones: the passage through the sea and the waters of the flood, which
correspond to our baptism (1 Cor. 10:1, 2; 1 Pet. 3:20), and the manna and
water from the rock, which correspond to the Eucharist (1 Cor. 10:3, 4).
Hence arises the interchange of names which often occurs here, through which
New Testament names are given to Old Testament sacraments and, conversely,
new sacraments are expressed by the names of the old. *Baptism and the Feast*
are attributed to the fathers (1 Cor. 10); *circumcision* (Col. 2:11, 12),
*Passover* (1 Cor. 5:7), and *sacrifices* (Heb. 9) are attributed to us. Such
an ἐναλλαγὴ, *interchange*, could not occur unless there were some identity.
It cannot be with respect to the *sign*, because the signs are

[Printed p. 178 | Internet Archive PDF 206 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

different. Therefore, it must be with respect to the *thing signified*, which
was always the same in all the sacraments, namely, Christ with his benefits.
In him the types and sacraments, no less than the promises, are Yes and Amen,
that is, they obtain their truth and fulfillment.

XXII. Nor must our adversaries’ exception be admitted that the ancients ate
the same food among themselves, but not the same food as we. This conflicts
with Paul’s purpose, which is to compare the ancients with Christians and to
argue for parity of punishments, if their sins had parity, from the examples
both of the sacraments and benefits bestowed upon them and of the Israelites’
sins and punishments. 1. This argument would have no weight if the Israelites
were compared only among themselves and not with us. 2. It conflicts with
Paul’s own words, by which he teaches that Christ was already known to the
ancients when he says that the Rock which followed them was Christ and that
they tempted Christ in the wilderness (v. 9). 3. The apostle would speak of
*baptism* in vain if it were not true that the sacraments of the Old and New
Testaments were the same at least analogically and in substance.

[Marginal heading: 7. From the use of the law.]

XXIII. Seventh, because the law of Moses itself, under which the fathers
lived, instructed them concerning the covenant of grace and prepared and
spurred them to embrace it. This could not have occurred unless the covenant
of grace already had a place. Hence “the law is called a schoolmaster to
Christ” (Gal. 3:24), and Christ the end of the law (Rom. 10:4). This is easily
demonstrated from the law’s three parts. 1. From the *moral* law, because by
convicting the fathers of sins committed against the law (Rom. 3:19, 20; Gal.
3:19) and subjecting them on account of these to the divine curse (Gal. 3:10),
it aroused and spurred them to seek and embrace expiation and the pardon of
sins in Christ (Gal. 3:24). 2. From the *forensic* law, insofar as it
constituted the polity of the Israelite people until Christ (Gen. 49:10) and,
as though by an impressed mark, designated the nation from which the Messiah
was to arise. 3. From the *ceremonial* law, because the ceremonies of the law
were types and figures of Jesus Christ who was to come, foreshadowing his
sacrifice for sins and confirming believers’ minds concerning its fruit (Col.
2:17; Heb. 9 and 10).

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XXIV. *Gospel*, insofar as it denotes the doctrine of God’s grace in Christ,
is used in two ways: either for the *gospel promised*, or ἐπαγγελίᾳ, or for
εὐαγγελισμῷ, the *gospel fulfilled and exhibited*. In the former sense it was
in the Old Testament; in the latter, only in the New. In this respect it is
said to have been *kept silent* in preceding ages (Rom. 16:25), not absolutely
and simply, since in the same place it is said to have been promised and
manifested through the prophetic Scriptures (Rom. 1:2), but relatively: 1.
With respect to *natural knowledge*, because flesh and blood do not reveal
this; 2. With respect to *promulgation* among the nations, because God allowed
them to walk in their own ways and revealed nothing to them concerning
Christ; 3. With respect to its *fulfillment* among the Jews, because he
reserved its fulfillment not for their times, but for ours. Therefore, the
apostle does not intend that the gospel was wholly unknown in the Old
Testament, but that, *comparatively* with the light of the New Testament, it
became known far more clearly and to many more (Eph. 3:5).

XXV. The *beginning of the gospel*, insofar as it denotes the evangelical
history of the ministry of John the Baptist, Christ’s nativity, and so forth,
is one thing; Mark 1:1 speaks of it in this sense. The beginning of the
doctrine of God’s grace in Christ is another. In this sense it did not first
begin in the New Testament, but was declared in the Old Testament by Moses
and the prophets, as is confirmed in that same place by prophetic testimonies
(Mark 1:2, 3).

XXVI. “The law and the prophets” are said to have “continued until John”
(Matt. 11:13) with respect to the *mode of administration*, not the covenant’s
*substance*,

[Printed p. 179 | Internet Archive PDF 207 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

as though there had only been a legal covenant in the Old Testament. For the
law and the prophets always had promises of grace along with the promulgation
of the law. Rather, it treats only the legal and prophetic ministry as opposed
to the evangelical ministry, especially with respect to the clarity of
revelation. It is not the ministering persons or the things administered that
are opposed, but only the different modes by which the same things were
administered: formerly through προφητείαν, a more obscure manner of teaching,
namely, symbolic and enigmatic, and afterward through εὐαγγελισμὸν, a clear
and open manner of teaching. Therefore, τὸ ὂν καὶ τὸ ποῖον, *the thing and its
mode*, must not be confused here.

XXVII. For the covenant of grace to be renewed and proposed in a more
illustrious manner is one thing; for it to be made anew when it did not exist
before is another. The former, not the latter, is stated in Jer. 31, because
the same covenant clause had already been placed in the Abrahamic covenant.
Nor is it unusual in the style of Scripture to say that something is made
when it is manifested more clearly and effectively. Thus the *commandment of
love* is called *new* in John 13:34 and 1 John 2:8, not absolutely, because it
had already been proposed in the law, but relatively, because it was newly
inculcated, supported by new promises, and sanctioned by a new example. So
God’s “power is perfected in weakness” (2 Cor. 12:9), that is, demonstrated to
be perfect; and a friend is born in adversity and stars arise when the sun
sets, that is, they are manifested.

XXVIII. Not seeing Christ exhibited in the flesh is one thing; not receiving
the benefits and fruits of his mediation is another. Peter says the former
was denied to the fathers in 1 Pet. 1:12, when he says that it was revealed
to them that they administered these mysteries not to themselves but to us,
because Christ was to be manifested only in our times, not theirs. Yet the
latter was not therefore denied to them, because the power of Christ’s death,
although still future, could exercise its efficacy toward them to obtain the
expiation of sins and peace of conscience, in the manner in which moral causes
customarily act before they exist.

XXIX. Although God provided something better for us than for the fathers of
the Old Testament (Heb. 11:39, 40), with respect to the fulfillment of the
promises and manifestation of the Messiah—in which sense the disciples’ eyes
are called blessed because they saw the things kings had desired to see and
had not seen (Luke 10:23, 24)—it does not follow that they did not have the
same substance of the covenant as we, since God willed to be their God no less
than ours. Therefore, they are said “not to have been perfected without us,”
χωρὶς ἡμῶν, that is, *separately from us*, because they were saved by the same
merit, the same righteousness, and the same grace of Christ, although they
could be perfected *before us* with respect to order and time.

XXX. Although Christ brought life and immortality to light through the gospel
(2 Tim. 1:10), it does not follow that they were absolutely unknown under the
Old Testament. 1. Paul does not speak of an absolute and simple manifestation
among all, but *among the nations*. Hence he immediately adds that for this
reason he was appointed a herald among the nations. 2. He taught in verse 9
that this grace was not new when he said that it had been given πρὸ χρόνων
αἰωνίων, *before eternal times*, both by eternal designation and temporal
promise. 3. It does not properly treat the promise of eternal life, but the
procurement of it through Christ’s actual merit, which was peculiar to the
New Testament.

XXXI. When the apostle says that the law perfected nothing (Heb. 7:19), he
does not take law broadly for the Mosaic economy, as if the ancients were not
perfected under the Old Testament with respect to the possession of grace and
glory. Rather, he regards the law strictly and as opposed to the gospel,
because the moral law could neither justify nor sanctify, since it was weak
through the flesh, much less could the ceremonial law.

XXXII. If he asserts the same in Heb. 9:8—“The way into the sanctuary was not

[Printed p. 180 | Internet Archive PDF 208 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

manifested while the first tabernacle stood”—he does not intend to deny that
the fathers under the Old Testament were received into heaven, which the
example of Enoch and Christ’s reasoning in Matt. 22 invincibly prove. Rather,
he intends to intimate two things. 1. The ceremonies as such, which are
designated by the first tabernacle, could not in themselves make entrance into
heaven because of their weakness and inefficacy. 2. As long as those
ceremonies subsisted, that entrance into heaven, or its rationale and manner,
was more obscure and was not proposed so clearly as it was when Christ had
been exhibited and the ceremonies removed. But the inference from the denial
of the mode to the denial of the thing is invalid. Not being manifested in
any manner is one thing; not being clearly and fully revealed is another. The
apostle says the latter, not the former, for τὸ πεφανερῶσθαι denotes the full
manifestation of a thing, which was denied to the Old Testament.

XXXIII. The *old covenant* is taken in two ways: either for the *covenant of
works*, or the legal covenant strictly taken, which was made with our first
parents before the fall and afterward renewed in the wilderness; or for the
*second covenant of grace*, established with our first parents after the fall
and confirmed in the Mosaic economy. The *new covenant*, however, is taken
either generally for the covenant of grace or for the covenant of grace
illustrated in the New Testament. Hence a twofold opposition between the old
and new covenants must be carefully distinguished: one with respect to
*substance*, if they are taken in the first manner, and the other with respect
to *accidents*, or the accidental mode of administration, if they are regarded
in the second sense.

Although with respect to the husk of the letter the Mosaic covenant seemed
legal, because it promised life only under the condition of one’s own—and
indeed perfect—obedience, without any ἐπιεικείᾳ, *equity*, nevertheless, with
respect to the Legislator’s intention and the end of the law’s renewed
promulgation, it led to the coming Messiah. Thus it is rightly said to pertain
to the covenant of grace. In this sense the old covenant is called obsolete
only with respect to its mode of administration, not the thing administered;
the covenant’s accidents, not its substance; the external observance of the
appendages annexed to the covenant, not the internal form of the covenant
itself.

XXXIV. The *promises of the new covenant* are called *better* in Heb. 8:6
relatively, not simply: not with respect to the substance of the things
promised, but with respect to the manner of proposing them more clearly,
extending them more richly, impressing them more effectively, and expanding
them more widely also to the nations. Therefore, that ὑπεροχὴ, *excellence*,
of the New Testament pertains wholly to the mode, not the thing, and regards
the covenant’s τὰ ἐπουσιώδη, *accidents*, not its οὐσιώδη, *substance*. For no
promises better in their matter can be given than those
made to Abraham, Isaac, and the other fathers.

XXXV. For the *righteousness of faith now*, that is, under the evangelical
economy, to be φανερωθεῖσαν ἁπλῶς, *manifested simply*, is one thing; for it to
be φανερωθεῖσαν χωρὶς νόμου, *manifested apart from the law*, that is, through
a word other than the law, namely, the word of the gospel, is another. Paul
does not assert the former in Rom. 3:21, but the latter, because he says that
this righteousness is μαρτυρουμένην, *testified to*, by the law and the
prophets. Nor can it be said to be μαρτυρουμένην as future, not present, as
destined for believers of the New Testament but not the fathers. For then it
would follow either that none of the fathers was justified, which the examples
of Abraham, David, and the others prove false, or that they were justified in
a manner other than through the righteousness of faith, as Paul asserts in
Rom. 4.

XXXVI. Although Moses was the Mediator of the Old Covenant, Christ is not
excluded from this office even in the Old Testament, because Moses was only a
typical Mediator and a mere messenger between God and the people. But Christ
is the true and real εἰρηνοποιός, *peacemaker*, who reconciles the Father to us
by his merit, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever: in the Old
Testament ἀσάρκως, *without flesh*, but in the New ἐνσάρκως, *in the flesh*.

XXXVII. The fathers’ faith was not merely *to believe that Christ was to
come*,

[Printed p. 181 | Internet Archive PDF 209 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

but *to believe in Christ who was to come* (Acts 19:4, 5). They could not
believe in him except as he was promised, namely, as the Messiah and sole
Savior, outside whom there can be no salvation. Although Christ did not yet
exist physically as incarnate, nevertheless from the beginning he existed
morally by reason of designation and promise as the one to be incarnate. Thus
through the efficacy of his divinity he could already confer his benefits
upon believers. The faith which was to be revealed under the New Testament
(Gal. 3:23) was *objective faith*, which is believed, with respect to its
clearer manifestation, not *subjective faith, by which one believes*.

XXXVIII. Since faith and the Word are correlative, faith in Christ had to be
such as was the Old Testament Word concerning Christ. But the Old Testament
Word teaches Christ and testifies about him (John 5:39), though more obscurely
on account of its promises and types of the one who was yet to come. Therefore,
their faith was also in him, though more obscure and less illustrious. The
*thing* was always the same, known and proposed as the object of faith, but
the *mode* was apprehended less clearly before Christ’s advent than after it,
just as knowledge of all future things is less evident than knowledge of past
things and sight of present things. Yet when a greater degree of light is
denied, lesser degrees are not immediately denied.

XXXIX. The manner of redemption through the Messiah’s death could not have
been wholly unknown to the fathers, since it was indicated so often both by
verbal oracles and by real and personal types. Consider Pss. 22 and 69; Isa.
53 and 63; Dan. 9:27; and Zech. 12:10. Christ himself teaches from the Old
Testament that he had to suffer and so enter into his glory (Luke 24). The
same thing was discerned in the paschal lamb, in the two goats of Lev. 16, and
everywhere in the sacrifices. It is therefore necessary to hold either that
the fathers received all these without faith and believed that sins could be
expiated by the blood of bulls—in which manner their faith would have been
inferior even to the lights of the heathen, who did not believe such a
thing—or that they perceived a spiritual meaning in these things and referred
them all to the Messiah.

XL. If the two disciples on the road to Emmaus, still bewitched by the opinion
of a worldly kingdom to be established on earth by Christ, did not think of
the cross Christ had to undergo (Luke 24:21), it does not follow that all
believers thought the same or that nothing about Christ’s death had been
revealed to the fathers, because this was a defect in the subject, not a
deficiency in the object. For this was foretold clearly enough in the Old
Testament and proposed by Christ, if they had been willing to attend to it.
Therefore, their faith could be true with respect to its object but still
mixed with error, drawn not from Scripture, from which it is refuted (Luke
24:27), but from the Jews’ erroneous persuasion concerning Christ’s earthly
kingdom (Matt. 20:21).

XLI. If the apostles sometimes imagined for themselves an earthly and carnal
kingdom of Christ, this was not so much from ignorance as from ἀβλεψίας,
*inattention*, and προπετείας, *rashness*. Through these it happened that they
either did not always think about this mystery, were carried astray by various
carnal affections, or sometimes did or said in a certain headlong impulse
things alien to what they knew well enough. For the same reason they often
stumbled in many other things which they knew were alien to their office, as
when they wished to send away human beings seeking Christ’s aid, wished fire
sent down from heaven upon the Samaritans, were unwilling for him to ascend to
Jerusalem, and the like.

XLII. If Peter rebuked Christ when he spoke about the cross (Matt. 16:22), the
cause of that ἐπιτιμήσεως, *rebuke*, was not absolute ignorance of the mystery
of redemption or of Christ’s death. For he had heard John testify that Christ
was the Lamb of God who takes away the sins of the world, and Christ himself
most explicitly foretell the destruction of the temple of his body and his
three-day stay in the tomb (John 2:19; Matt. 12:39, 40), besides the prophets’
own oracles. Rather, it was Peter’s customary προπέτεια, *rashness*, and
θερμότης, *ardor*, which often carries a man

[Printed p. 182 | Internet Archive PDF 210 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

astray so that he produces things alien even to those which he knows best,
because at that moment he does not recall them to mind.

XLIII. The disciples’ stupor and consternation do not prejudice either the
revelation or the faith and understanding of others. They did not understand
what they could and ought to have understood, and what the godly before them
and alongside them rightly understood according to the measure of their faith
(Matt. 26:12). And if the mystery could in no way have been understood then,
the disciples would not have sinned by not understanding it, because no one
is obligated to such an impossibility. Yet they did sin, and hence they were
so severely rebuked more than once by Christ on that account (Matt. 16:23;
Luke 24:25, 26). Nor did believers need eyes other than those of faith to know
these mysteries, eyes by which they might see the body in the shadows and the
kernel and truth in the prophecies.

XLIV. Although God promises bodily goods through Moses in the Old Testament,
this does not exclude spiritual goods, which are often promised (Exod. 34:6,
7; Lev. 26:12, 41, 42, 44; Deut. 18:15 and following; 30:2, 3, 6, 11, 12).
Indeed, they are contained openly enough in the formula of the covenant,
because the bodily goods themselves were evidences and symbols of spiritual
goods (Rom. 4:12 and following; 1 Cor. 10:1, 2, 3; Heb. 11:8, 9, 10). Eternal
life is also expressly mentioned in Isa. 45:17; Dan. 12:2; and Ps. 16:11,
although it was revealed neither as clearly nor to as many as now.

XLV. The gift of the Holy Spirit is promised under the New Testament not
simply, as if it had been absolutely lacking to the ancient church, contrary
to the testimony of Pss. 51:11, 12; 143:10; Isa. 44:3; 59:21; Ezek. 36:27;
39:29; and Hag. 2:5, but relatively, insofar as it was then dispensed both more
sparingly and to fewer people, as though drop by drop. Now, however, it floods
the whole church in great abundance and like a *river* (John 7:39). This began
visibly on the day of Pentecost (Acts 2:17, 18) and must continue invisibly
under Christ’s reign until the consummation of the age.
