QUESTION XIX.

THE EIGHTH COMMANDMENT.

What is forbidden and commanded by the commandment against stealing? And is
every kind of interest included under it? Negative.

I. Just as the two preceding commandments provided for the *persons* and
*bodies* of our neighbors, that is, for their life and chastity, so this
eighth commandment secures their *goods*, or possessions. Its end is that,
since injustice is an abomination to God, each person should be given what is
his own.

[Marginal heading: The threefold rationale of the commandment.]

II. The rationale of this commandment, no less than of the preceding ones, is
threefold.

1. The *divine* rationale is that, since God is no less the giver of goods
than their creator, whoever does not expect them from his liberal hand but
seizes them through injury greatly offends God himself.

2. The *natural* rationale is that, since the distinction of ownership by
which some possess more and others less belongs to natural law, the
appropriation of another’s property cannot but be a grave sin contrary to
natural law.

3. The *civil* rationale is that, when the license of thieves and robbers
rages, public peace and tranquility, the bond of human society, is
undermined.

[Marginal heading: Theft and its various forms are condemned.]

III. In general, *theft* is forbidden here. It is customarily defined as *the
handling or appropriation of another’s property against its owner’s will*,
whether done by fraud or by force. Theft, however, is not only simple but
also *qualified*, and it is varied and manifold according to the diversity
of its objects. If it is the appropriation of a sacred thing, it is called
*sacrilege*; if of a public good belonging to the commonwealth,
*embezzlement*; and if of a person who is under another’s authority, whether
free or enslaved, *kidnapping*. Under this heading belong *robbery,
extortion, and fraudulent dealing*, for each of which the civil laws have
assigned its own penalties.

IV. Deceits, frauds, and

[Printed p. 109 | Internet Archive PDF 137 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

every kind of circumvention in contracts, merchandise, weights, measures,
coins, and monopolies are also derivatively included under theft, as are all
the evil arts and schemes by which the property of others is sought. Indeed,
those who sacrifice to idleness and vicious leisure, which is the devil’s
pillow, are guilty of theft before God. So too are those who are negligent
and unfaithful in their callings, professions, and offices. By applying
themselves lazily and drowsily to the work assigned to them, they unjustly
steal the wages granted to them.

V. *Covetousness* also belongs here. It is the root of all evils (1 Tim.
6:9–10) and consists in an insatiable desire to possess, the accursed hunger
for gold, or a disordered love of riches. This love is ἀσύστατος,
*incompatible*, with the love we owe to God and our neighbor. “Adulterers and
adulteresses, did you not know that love of the world is enmity with God?”
(James 4:4). Hence the apostle designates it by the detestable crime of
idolatry (Eph. 5:5), because through it a man sets aside the divine majesty
and places all his hope and confidence in riches as though they were his
highest good.

VI. From this it is also evident that this vice was not first forbidden in
the New Testament, as the Socinians and Anabaptists would have it, as though
Christ had thereby added a supplement to the law. They claim that the
possession of riches even beyond necessity was lawful under the Old
Testament, but under the New is restricted to what is simply necessary for
sustaining life, as the *Racovian Catechism*, question 101, has it in its
discussion of the precepts Christ added to the law.

Covetousness is forbidden throughout the Old Testament, not only in this
commandment and the tenth, where desire for one’s neighbor’s goods is
forbidden, but it is also condemned elsewhere (Exod. 18:21; 22:25; 23:8;
Lev. 19:13; 25:46; Deut. 16:19; Ps. 62:10; Isa. 5:8; Jer. 6:6–7; 22:17; Ps.
119:36, and so forth). Nor is possession of riches unlawful in itself,
provided that they are acquired by lawful means and that we use them as
means rather than enjoy them as our end. This was done by many of the
faithful who are said to have abounded in wealth even in the New Testament,
such as Joseph of Arimathea (Matt. 27), Cornelius the centurion (Acts 10),
Tabitha (Acts 9), and the like.

Nor, if the rich are said to be scarcely able to enter the kingdom of heaven
(Matt. 19:23), is this because of riches themselves, but because of their
perverse use. This is evident from Mark 10:24, where those who trust in
riches are condemned. Hence riches are called *unrighteous mammon* (Luke
16:9), not properly on their own account, but incidentally on account of
their possessors, because they are very often acquired unjustly and
possessed badly. And ἀπάτη, *deceitfulness*, is attributed to them in
Matthew 13:22, not in themselves, but with respect to the mind that possesses
them. In this sense care and the choking of the word are also attributed to
riches—not in relation to riches themselves, but to the possessor’s mind,
which, anxious with care, chokes the seed of the word within itself and does
not allow it to take root in the heart. Covetousness, therefore, consists
not in the outward possession but in the inward affection; not in riches,
but in the desire for them and their unlawful use.

[Marginal heading: Whether interest is lawful.]

VII. The celebrated question *concerning interest*, which some number among
the forms of theft, must be referred here: *Is any kind of interest lawful?*
A few things must be said about it so that it may be evident what must be
determined here from the word of God.

*Interest* is the profit exacted beyond the principal in addition to the
service of lending, whether it is money, grain, or something else. It is
called *usura* from *usus*, “use,” because it is received for the use of
money. In Greek it is τόκος, from τίκτω, *I give birth*, because it is like a
kind of offspring of the loan. The Hebrews customarily express it with two
words: תרבית, from רבה, *he multiplied*, because through interest the
principal is multiplied and grows; and נשך, from the root נשך, *he bit*,
because like a serpent it bites and wears down those who do not perceive it.

VIII. There are two principal positions on the question, one denying and the
other affirming. The canon lawyers maintain the negative here and consider
all interest simply unlawful in *Canon Law*, cause 14, question 3. With them

[Printed p. 110 | Internet Archive PDF 138 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

agree the Scholastics and Sententiaries on Lombard, book 3, distinction 37.
They are joined by some of the Lutherans and even some of our own, including
Zwingli, Musculus, and Aretius, as well as various English theologians:
Irellus in his *Dissertation on Interest*, Lancelot Andrewes, Wilson, and
others. The orthodox commonly embrace the affirmative and hold that interest
is lawful, though with certain distinctions in place.

IX. First, *interest* must be distinguished. One kind is *biting* and
immoderate, or exploitative. It is mercilessly exacted, without any regard
for Christian equity and love, from any borrower, even one who is poor and
impoverished or who has suffered some great loss in his household estate by
which, through no fault of his own, he has become unable to pay. The other
kind is *moderate and helpful*. In it such moderation is employed as suits
the need and benefit of both the lender and the borrower and the profit that
is or customarily can be acquired by means of the loan. It also corresponds
to Christian equity according to the rate prescribed by the magistrate in
view of places, times, and persons, for these vary according to the diversity
of regions and peoples.

Second, those who *lend money at interest* are either professional
moneylenders and usurers who do this alone, or they are not. They either make
only interest-bearing loans or sometimes also loans without charge. And they
are either people able honestly to put their own money to work, or people
unable to do so because they lack the strength or fitness to undertake it,
or because they are wholly devoted to public duties, whether sacred or
civil.

Third, *those who borrow* are either poor, prosperous, or at least in
moderate circumstances. They borrow money either for the necessary uses of
sustaining themselves and their families or for their own advantage and
profit.

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

X. With these points established, we say that the question does not concern
biting interest, which we judge unjust and unlawful, but moderate and
reasonable interest. It does not concern those who practice moneylending
professionally and do this alone, increasing their own advantages through
returns and interest to the disadvantage of others. We judge that this trade
deservedly has the worst reputation and that such usurers or bankers, who
follow this way of life and suck the blood of the poor like leeches, are most
justly condemned.

Rather, the question concerns those who attend to the advantages of others
as well as their own. They exact a fair return from those from whom it can
rightfully be exacted, while being prepared to relieve the poor freely from
their own resources and actually doing so. Or they are less able, whether
because of age, incapacity, or other business, to increase their income
through the use of their own money.

The question does not concern the poor and needy who borrow some money for
necessary and unavoidable uses, for we assert that to stipulate and exact
interest from them is unjust. Rather, it concerns others who are prosperous,
or at least in moderate circumstances, and borrow money to increase their
property, and who can receive some advantage and profit from putting the
money to work. We maintain that it is lawful and just to exact some interest
from them.

[Marginal heading: It is proved that not all interest is unlawful.]

XI. First, under the Old Testament it was permissible to take interest from
foreigners (Deut. 23:20). This could not have been done justly if interest
possessed intrinsic baseness, for what is evil in itself and in its own
nature cannot become lawful in relation to any object.

It cannot be said that only the seven nations devoted to destruction are
meant, because the passage concerns any foreigners, distinguished from
brothers, that is, Jews. For just as foreigners had the liberty of exacting
interest from Jews, so it was equitable for the Israelites to be granted the
same liberty, so that there might be commerce between the two, which rests on
equality. Nor can it be said that this was only a permission of fact, not of
right, permitting it not as something lawful but as a lesser evil, like

[Printed p. 111 | Internet Archive PDF 139 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

a writ of divorce. This is gratuitously assumed, not proved. Nor are evils to
be done or permitted by an express ordinance so that goods may result.

XII. Second, if interest were absolutely unlawful, Christ would not have
represented the spiritual duty of the lawful use of talents under that
similitude in Matthew 25 without any indication of disapproval, such as he
customarily employed in other parables drawn from a disapproved practice, as
in Luke 16:8. Nor would John the Baptist in Luke 3:13 have commanded the tax
collectors, who had leased the collection of taxes from the Romans for a
fixed price, merely to exact nothing beyond what was equitable while
retaining their condition of life.

XIII. Third, not every use of interest is forbidden in the law, nor is it
inconsistent with equity and honor. Indeed, it is founded on the following.

1. It is founded on *necessity and utility*, because without interest trade,
which is among the principal supports of human society, cannot subsist or be
carried on. Can something without which human society cannot stand, and
without which all human business and commerce would cease, truly be accused
of injustice?

2. It is founded on *natural equity*, because it is equitable that one who
receives an advantage from another’s money should also, by way of due
compensation, make the other person a participant in the advantage he
receives with that person’s aid.

3. It is founded on *just gratitude*, for what one person owes by right,
another receives by right. But one who makes a profit from another’s loan
owes some ἀντίδωρον, *return gift*, by the law of gratitude. For natural law
requires us to be grateful to those who benefit us, not only in words but in
deed.

4. It is founded on *Christian love*, which requires every head of a
household to provide for his own (1 Tim. 5:8); parents ought to store up for
their children (2 Cor. 12:14). But if someone helped others with his money
while neglecting his own, he would never increase his estate.

5. It is founded on *parity with other contracts* that have a place in
society and are approved by everyone, for example, contracts of
ἐμφυτεύσεως, *emphyteusis*, ἀντιχρήσεως, *antichresis*, leasing, lending for
use, and so forth. For if someone receives profit from the lease of a house
and land and from lending utensils for use, why not likewise from lending
money?

6. To these are added the ordinances of emperors and kings, by which a limit
has been set for lawful interest. Digest, book 22, has the title *On Interest
and Fruits*; Code of Justinian, book 4, title 32.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XIV. The following does not oppose interest. 1. The prohibition of interest
found in Exodus 22:26–27 and Leviticus 25:35–36 does not oppose it. This
prohibition is manifestly restricted to the poor and impoverished, so that it
is evident that the principle for the rich is different: *if you lend to my
people, to the poor*, that is, to the one among the people who is miserable
and needy, having nothing with which to relieve himself. This is explained
more clearly in Leviticus 25:35: “If your brother has become impoverished,
and so forth, you shall not take interest from him,” and so forth. See also
Deuteronomy 15:7–9.

If elsewhere it appears to be extended indiscriminately to all Israelites,
as in Deuteronomy 23:20, “You shall not lend at interest to your brother,”
and so forth, either the prohibition must be explained from the parallel
passages of Exodus and Leviticus, in which it is manifestly restricted to
the poor—and this is also intimated clearly enough when *interest on food* is
mentioned, namely, interest taken from one who borrows for his own support so
that he may have something to live on—or, if it refers without exception to
all Hebrews, it is a judicial law peculiar to this people. Its purpose was to
bind the society of the Israelites more firmly by this bond, like the many
other laws concerning slaves, pledges, fields, gleaning, and similar things,
which no longer have a place under the New Testament.

XV. 2. Nor does the fact that the one who does not lend at interest is
praised as just in Psalm 15:5 and Ezekiel 18:8 oppose it. These passages do
not concern every kind of interest. Otherwise this would contradict Moses,
who permits some interest. Rather, they concern biting and wicked interest,
by which the creditor, setting aside all equity, burdens and oppresses his
debtor, either by exacting too much or by exacting it too rigorously. The law
willed this kind to be proscribed.

XVI. 3. Nor do Christ’s words in Luke 6:35 oppose it: “Lend,

[Printed p. 112 | Internet Archive PDF 140 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

hoping for nothing in return.” For the passage commands what must be done,
not what alone must be done: even the poor and needy, from whom nothing can
be hoped, must be helped. 2. If Christ’s words were to be strained, it would follow
that not even the principal should be sought back from anyone, because we are
commanded to δανείζειν μηδὲν ἀπελπίζοντες, *lend, hoping for nothing*,
and thus to seek neither principal nor interest. Yet no one can deny that
this would be unjust absolutely and universally.

3. The purpose is to exhort us to beneficence toward the poor, so that when
necessity presses something may be given even to them, although no hope of
profit, or even of the principal, appears. Christ speaks about this kind of
loan for good reason, because we ordinarily neglect it. Nor should the word
δότε, *give*, therefore have been substituted for δανείζετε, *lend*, on the
ground that this seems to be pure almsgiving rather than a loan. In a loan,
the obligation to repay always remains with the recipient, although the
lender ought not to enforce his right. But one who receives alms is not bound
to repay them. Add that such a grant can properly be called not only a
δώρημα, *gift*, but also a δάνεισμα, *loan*, on account of the great reward
the Lord promises him, because one who gives to the poor lends to God.

4. Some explain the passage as though it read μηδένα ἀπελπίζοντες, *making
no one despair*, so that it is understood of persons, not things. This is
derived from the Syriac interpreter, who renders it, *Lend, and do not cut
off a man’s hope*. This would agree with the parallel passage in Matthew
5:42, τὸν θέλοντα ἀπὸ σοῦ δανείσασθαι μὴ ἀποστραφῇς, *do not turn away the
one who wishes to borrow from you*. Thus we should not turn away someone who
wishes to borrow from us or cut off his hope of a loan, whatever the state
of his affairs, looking instead to the reward laid up for us with God.

Others, not implausibly, explain it according to the force of the word
ἀπελπίζειν as *despairing of nothing*. That is, do not suppose your benefit
is lost or your money badly placed, as though you will receive nothing from
it, because your reward will always be great with God if the service is lost
among men.

XVII. Although money appears sterile in itself, it can nevertheless be made
fruitful through its use, just as a field and land become fertile not by
themselves, but when human industry is applied. Nor through lawful interest
is the same thing counted twice or a double payment exacted, because the
money lent is one thing and the fruit hoped for from it another, just as land
is one thing and the return received from the land another.

XVIII. Christ overturned the tables of the money changers in the temple in
Matthew 21:12 and drove them out, as he did the buyers and sellers. He did so
not because buying, selling, and exchanging money are unlawful, but because
the house of prayer ought not to become a marketplace. As for their being
accused of making the house of God a den of robbers, this is said not so much
with respect to them as because, by bringing their merchandise into the
temple while the priests connived at it, they served the priests’ greed,
since the priests sought their own profits from that money changing and
trade.

XIX. The canon lawyers’ maxim that *every loan ought to be without charge*
cannot be admitted without qualification concerning a loan properly so
called, because no one’s duty ought to cause him loss. Nor is it any more
equitable to use another’s money for our own affairs without charge than to
use his house or carriage, so that he gains no fruit or benefit and your
περίσσευμα, *abundance*, becomes his ὑστέρημα, *lack*, so that he has
θλῖψις, *affliction*, while you have ἄνεσις, *ease*.

XX. The name of usurer has become infamous, and the trade is not only
illiberal but wicked, when someone professes the usurer’s trade and opens a
public house of lending at interest; when he exacts immoderate interest
condemned by the civil laws; or when, impatient of delay, he pursues his
right too rigorously against the poor. For here the highest right is often
the highest injustice. On this account the Jews and Lombards called bankers
are censured, not without reason, because they gnaw at the people, and
especially the poor, with extremely burdensome interest and

[Printed p. 113 | Internet Archive PDF 141 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

gorge themselves on their blood. They indiscriminately accept pledges from
anyone, by which they are secured not only for the principal but also for
immoderate interest, even from those who seek bread from door to door, from
servants without their masters’ knowledge, and from sons under family
authority without their parents’ knowledge. They practice the moneylender’s
trade professionally and for the sole purpose of becoming rich from it. It
would be desirable for them to be cast out and banished from Christian
commonwealths as public plagues and plunderers of society.

But the case is not the same for those who employ honorable means and, under
equitable conditions established by civil law, take care that the money they
have at home is not unfruitful for them.

XXI. The Roman Catholics, and especially Jesuits such as Baylius in tractate
4, question 5, unjustly fasten this charge upon our Calvin as though he were
the patron of usurers, because he does not condemn interest absolutely. The
Jansenists more justly object to the Roman Catholics themselves that they
permit and practice nearly every kind of interest, including very wicked
kinds. See the *Provincial Letters* and Amadeus Guimenius, *Treatise on
Interest*.

But it is more surprising that Lancelot Andrewes, bishop of Winchester, a
great man of God, should heap odium upon Calvin for this reason in his
*Theological Determination on Interest*, because Calvin defends interest in
a certain letter and in his work on Ezekiel 18. In this matter we cannot but
miss equity in so great a man, who burdens Calvin with so unjust an
accusation. For Calvin spoke so sparingly and soberly about interest that he
did not conceal that he would have preferred not to answer on this matter,
and he adds so many precautions that anyone can readily see how far removed
he was from his adversaries’ slander.

In letter 383 he says, “I have not yet attempted to find what suitable answer
can be given to the proposed question, but from the experience of others I
have learned how closely this matter is joined with danger. For if we condemn
interest altogether, we put a tighter snare upon consciences than the Lord
himself willed; if we concede the least amount, many will immediately seize
unbridled license under that pretext.” After saying that interest is
condemned by no testimony of Scripture, he adds shortly afterward, “It would
indeed be desirable for all interest, and even its very name, to be banished
from the world,” and so forth. He confirms the same in chapter 18 of his work
on Ezekiel. Anyone who desires more may consult the celebrated Voetius, part
4, *Select Disputations*, p. 555.

XXII. The virtues commanded by this commandment are the following.

1. *Αὐτάρκεια*, *contentment*, or acquiescence in one’s lot, so that each
person, attending to his own calling, lives from his honest labor (Gen. 3:19)
and, content with his own lot, does not covet what belongs to others (1 Tim.
6:6; Heb. 13:5).

2. *Thrift and frugality*.

3. *Sincerity and justice* in all social life, so that we always conduct
ourselves ἄνευ δόλου καὶ ἀπάτης, *without guile and deceit*, and render to
each person what is his own.

4. *Liberality* toward the poor and needy, so that we not only refrain from
unjustly seizing our neighbor’s goods, but also liberally bestow our own upon
him whenever necessity and occasion require it.
