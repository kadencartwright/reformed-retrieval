13. The doctrine of Thomas Jackson, an English petty theologian as superficial
as any, likewise maintains that God's decrees are both temporal and mutable.
For he says that “it is a fantasy to think of the divine decree and the
predetermination of future things as acts already irrevocably finished and
complete, and, by the resulting error, to think it as impossible for anything
to be otherwise than it is, will be, or has been, as it is to revoke what is
past.” He perhaps saw this ancient error in Molina or extracted it from Suarez.

But first, let the reader observe the charming distinction between a decree
finished and completed irrevocably and a decree finished revocably. Scripture
says that Jehovah's decree, Psalm 33, cannot be rescinded; Jackson wishes it to
be rescinded and revoked. Second, either he speaks of an absolute decree, which
to everyone with a sound head is irrevocable, or of a conditional decree,
which, whether or not the condition is fulfilled, is always irrevocable,
because it includes foreknowledge of the condition in its object. Third, let
him explain whether there are irrevocable decrees in God, and which they are.
For he speaks here of decrees in general. But had the decree of the Ephesians’
election, made before the foundation of the world, Ephesians 1, and of those to
whom grace was given before the ages

*[Marginalia: Molina, *On the Concord*, question 14, article 13, disputation
51; Suarez, *On Divine Grace*, prolegomenon 2, chapter 8, p. 61, section 8.]*

[1651 logical printed p. 37 | IA leaf 55 | PDF p. 55]

began, 2 Timothy 1:9, or the decree concerning Jacob and Esau—one rejected and
the other loved before they had done anything good or evil—not yet been
completed before the foundation of the world? Is this not to contradict the
Holy Spirit?

Fourth, if decrees are not completed until the decreed things are actually put
into execution, God does many things in time which he did not decree to do from
eternity, and thus he will be an unwise agent; a prudent man does not take
counsel in the arena. Fifth, the decree concerning the world's creation and
Christ's resurrection was completed long ago. Otherwise, on this very day God
decrees the world's creation, and thus it is possible that the world has not
been created—which is impossible. He will say that decrees are completed when
the decreed things actually exist. But then a decree is temporal, not eternal,
and an actually transient, not immanent, act. God would postpone making decrees
until the decreed things actually exist for no other reason than because he is
ignorant of future contingencies—which is blasphemous—or because an
irrevocably completed decree would impair God's freedom. But in that case God
never wills Peter's free act of believing until Peter actually believes. Thus
he accomplishes the end by chance and by an entirely new and temporal counsel,
and he concurs with all contingent causes.

Sixth, through God's decrees all future things possess a complete futurity from
eternity; otherwise, let him give us another cause of futurity. Therefore,
God's decree existed from eternity, just as the futurity of things existed from
eternity, and consequently the decree was the actual cause of all things under
future being, long before

[1651 logical printed p. 38 | IA leaf 56 | PDF p. 56]

the things themselves existed. Seventh, in Scripture God invincibly proves
from a decree that a thing will be future, Jeremiah 1:5 and Isaiah 14:24. But
if decrees are not irrevocably complete before the actual existence of the
things decreed, God's inference has no force: God decreed the Assyrians’
devastation; therefore they will be devastated. For that decree, since it was
not yet finished, could be rescinded, and then the Assyrians will not be
devastated.

Eighth, Jackson later says that even all possible things are decreed by God.
Therefore, many of God's decrees will never be completed, for many possible
things will never actually exist: for example, the Egyptians’ safe passage
through the Red Sea, Christ's deliverance by twelve legions of angels, and so
forth. But it is absurd for God to decree many things and for his decrees never
to be completed when there are no obstacles capable of preventing their
completion.

Ninth, it does not follow that all things so happen that it is as impossible
for things to be otherwise as it is to revoke the past. For all future
contingent things could never have been future; but to revoke the past involves
a contradiction and is absolutely impossible.

Tenth, it also follows that God's supremely wise evangelical decree concerning
the salvation of human beings through Christ has not yet been completed and
will not be completed before the dissolution of the world. Thus many things in
the gospel could be changed, and God has spoken falsehoods in it. Nor can he
say that the gospel is a decree concerning things to be done by God himself
and is therefore already completed, whereas a decree concerning things to be
done by contingent causes

[1651 logical printed p. 39 | IA leaf 57 | PDF p. 57]

is not completed until contingent causes produce their effects. For the gospel
is also a decree concerning many contingent effects: the future coming of
Antichrist, the conversion of the Jews, and the rise of false christs in the
last days. I see no reason why Jackson could not say that, notwithstanding
God's decree and Paul's prediction in Romans 11, it is possible that the Jews
will never be converted.

14. Jackson adds in section 16: “God does not now begin for the first time to
decree all things, but he does decree them now; and it is safer for everyone to
regard God's decree as it coexists with the whole course of life than as it was
made from eternity.” First, if this is true, Paul handed down unsafe doctrine
to us when he wanted the Ephesians to regard election as made before the
foundation of the world. Likewise, he taught less than safely that grace was
given to us before the ages, 2 Timothy 1:9. What? Does not God himself prove
that Jeremiah must prophesy by an argument from God's decree? Jeremiah 1:5:
“Before you came out of the womb, I appointed you a prophet to the nations.”
Would not this innovator say that there was nevertheless no necessity of
prophesying, because while Jeremiah refused to prophesy, that decree was still
in the process of being made, and notwithstanding the decree made before
Jeremiah was born, he could have refrained from prophesying, and it was unsafe
for the prophet to consider such an argument of God?

Second, here too he exposes all decrees to arising and perishing, establishment
and rescission, as the creature's freedom wishes. Third, if it is unsafe to
regard

[1651 logical printed p. 40 | IA leaf 58 | PDF p. 58]

the decree as made from eternity, then either that regard is empty and false,
or God's decree is from eternity and therefore was irrevocably complete in its
being for infinitely many imaginary ages before the decreed things exist.

Fourth, what danger is there in regarding the decree's eternity? Because, he
says, we will thereby view God's decrees as acts irrevocably past, like the
statutes of the Medes and Persians. If it is erroneous so to regard the decrees,
then decrees are revocable and mutable, and thus Jehovah changes. And then it
is dangerous to believe that God foreknew free thoughts and free acts from
eternity, for free acts are no less irrevocable to God as foreknowing than to
God as decreeing. Thus he will deny God's eternal foreknowledge. Fifth, he
acknowledges the statutes of the Medes to be immutable, but God's decrees to be
mutable. Sixth, today God decrees the world's creation, his Son's incarnation,
and the destruction of the Chaldeans, and so forth. Who would not say that this
petty philosopher had suffered damage to his brain and, while feverish, cast
these things onto paper?

The Arminian doctrine of justification forms the other side of the matter.
According to Episcopius, justification is “a continuous act which exists and
lasts as long as the required presence of its condition lasts, but is always
and as often interrupted whenever acts are performed which cannot coexist with
true faith and a good conscience.” Arminius himself denies that justification
is completed before the end of life and holds that God changes the benevolent
affection in Christ which he had toward the truly justified person into

*[Marginalia: Episcopius, disputation 22, thesis 12, *On Justification*;
Arminius, disputation 48, thesis 12; Arminius, *Antiperkins*, p. 746.]*

[1651 logical printed p. 41 | IA leaf 59 | PDF p. 59]

wrath and hatred. This is to make God change his will at the pleasure of human
choice, so that today God imputes sins, tomorrow does not impute them, and then
imputes them again. I judge this contrary, first, to Arminius himself; second,
to Scripture; and third, to reason.

It is contrary to Arminius, who says, first, that God's will is God's very
essence; second, that it is infinite; and third, that it is eternal and
immutable, “because nothing can either be or appear good to him anew.” But if
God's will is moved by an external cause—namely, Christ's satisfaction and a
person's faith and repentance—so that he wills not to impute sins to the person
who now believes and afterward to impute them when he does not believe, then,
according to Arminius, God's will will be mutable, and consequently God's very
essence is mutable.

Second, it is contrary to Scripture, which establishes that Jehovah is
immutable, Malachi 3, and that God's election is eternal, Jeremiah 3:3 and
Isaiah 54:7. “You are our God from age to age,” says the Psalm; and his love
precedes even our acts and sins. John 3:16: “God so loved the world.” Ezekiel
16:8: “Your time was the time of loves”; but at that time the people were all
but expiring in their sins.

Third, it conflicts with reason. If in time God does not impute sins to a
person, then imputes them again, and both justifies and condemns him, since
these acts of imputing and not imputing are immanent acts in God and therefore
eternal, it will follow that God from eternity both loved the same person for
eternal life and hated him for eternal destruction. Second, suppose that many
sins have been forgiven to Saul, Judas, and others justified for a time,

*[Marginalia: Arminius, public disputation 4, theses 50–51.]*

[1651 logical printed p. 42 | IA leaf 60 | PDF p. 60]

and therefore erased and expiated by Christ's blood. They fall back into the
most grievous sins, so that God cannot fail to will eternal condemnation for
them. Are those former sins, once forgiven, imputed anew or not? If they are
imputed, we ask by what right this is done, for God does no injustice even to
apostates. Must sins once erased and expiated in Christ's blood also be paid
for by apostates in hell? Then two satisfactions will be rendered for the same
sins.

If they are not imputed, we ask by what Scripture some sins of one and the same
person are expiated in Christ and others are not. Why would Christ pay the
penalty for some of Judas's sins and not for others? What is the reason for the
distinction? Is it because he foresaw that some of Judas's sins would follow
his temporary faith but others his unbelief, and therefore he did not will to
expiate the latter but did expiate the former? These are trifles and Sicilian
conjectures. Yet it is their own fiction that Christ, when dying on the cross,
had regard to no person's faith or unbelief, but that after Christ's death had
been accomplished, God could either convert and save everyone or no one, and
thus condemn everyone.

But if they say that Judas's sins preceding his temporary faith were expiated
in Christ conditionally and not absolutely—namely, forgiven provided that
Judas persevered in faith—then no sins at all are forgiven in this life, and
no one is justified here, contrary to the clear Scriptures to the contrary,
Romans 4:10 and Romans 5:

[1651 logical printed p. 43 | IA leaf 61 | PDF p. 61]

1, Romans 8:30, Ephesians 1:7, and 1 Corinthians 6:11. Indeed, Abraham had been
justified when he offered Isaac, James 2:21, verse 25. Moreover, Christ's
satisfaction for sins cannot be imagined as conditional unless he had died
conditionally for some; but he died absolutely for everyone for whom he
underwent death.

Then the Arminians face an unavoidable question: Did Christ not expiate even
Judas's final apostasy by his blood? If he did not expiate it, then either that
apostasy was a sin absolutely incapable of forgiveness—and supposing God had
prolonged Judas's life and granted him repentance and faith, Judas would then
have obtained forgiveness for that apostasy without the shedding of blood,
which Scripture openly denies in Hebrews 9:22—or, for that sin to be forgivable
according to the order of justice, Christ would have had to die again. These
are theological monstrosities.

Third, Christ first communicates to believers the same remission he obtained.
But he obtained the remission of all sins: “Having obtained eternal
redemption,” Hebrews 9:12; “He erased the handwriting of ordinances that was
against us,” Colossians 2:13; “He made peace through the cross,” Colossians
1:20. He therefore removed all sins and every breach between God and us.
Consequently, he communicates such a remission to us. The reason for the major
is that he did not furnish several remissions or bonds for us. The adversaries
cannot give a reason why he satisfied for some of Judas's sins and not for
others, for those for which he

[1651 logical printed p. 44 | IA leaf 62 | PDF p. 62]

did not satisfy would be absolutely unforgivable, which is absurd. Nor is
there any reason why he would communicate to us in parts the remission he
obtained, unless he both loved and hated the same person from eternity, which
are inconsistent things.

15. Nothing is more central to the Arminians’ wishes than that remission of
sins does not occur before a person actually believes in Christ. For the
Remonstrants say in their *Apology* that nothing is more false than that human
beings are granted remission of sins before they believe. In this they court
their Socinus, who says that sins cannot be forgiven through the act of
believing if they have already been forgiven before we believe, and their
Bellarmine, who has this: “For how is it true faith by which I believe my sins
to have been forgiven, if, while I so believe, they have not yet been forgiven
but must be forgiven through the act of faith?” For every object precedes its
act. The Remonstrants acclaim them.

*[Marginalia: Remonstrants, *Apology*, chapter 6, folio 74.]*

*[Marginalia: Socinus, part 4 of *On Salvation*, chapter 10.]*

*[Marginalia: Remonstrants, *Confession*, section 11, thesis 2.]*

But here I wish three acts to be distinguished: first, the act performed by
Christ of satisfying for sins and reconciling us to God; second, God the
Father's act of acceptance by which he acknowledges that most abundant
satisfaction has been made to him for all the sins of all the elect; and
third, the act of justifying, to which faith is subordinated as an instrumental
cause.

16. The act of reconciling posits nothing new in God. Nor does Christ's merit
move the divine will or have the power to transform God from unwilling to
willing, or from one who hates us to one who loves us—as Grevinchovius
fables—first, because

*[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, p. 109.]*

[1651 logical printed p. 45 | IA leaf 63 | PDF p. 63]

God is immutable; and second, because a cause of the divine will can no more
be given than a cause of God himself. The whole multitude of Scholastics
testify to this with Aquinas, including Durandus, Bonaventure, Alexander of
Hales, Giles, Alvarez, Estius, Valentia, Molina, Suarez, Vasquez, and others.
By nature God first loves us and destines us to eternal glory before he
decrees to give Christ to us as mediator. Hence he truly loves us while we do
not yet believe, so that he wills to give Christ the reconciler to us while we
are still God's enemies; he loves us while we do not yet believe. John 3:6:
“When we were enemies, we were reconciled,” and so forth. Ezekiel 16:6: “I
said to you in your blood, Live.” Ephesians 2:4: “God, rich in mercy, on account
of his immense love for us, made us alive together with Christ even when we
were dead in sins.”

The Arminians wash these texts away by muttering something about God's
universal and common love, as though the love spoken of in Ezekiel 16 did not
separate the Jerusalemites from the Amorites and Moabites, and as though the
love called “great love” in Ephesians 2 were the common love by which God has
not ceased to bless the reprobate with sun, rain, and bread. Indeed, nothing is
more false than this Arminian doctrine: that no one is loved in Christ unless
he actually exists in him through faith. For the Holy Spirit is given through
and on account of Christ, and by his grace we believe, John 7:36 and John
15:16. Therefore, God loves us in Christ before we believe. Indeed, that love
of God is one and the same and immutable, the love by which God embraces us

[1651 logical printed p. 46 | IA leaf 64 | PDF p. 64]

both before and after Christ's death.

And because God's love unto life is neither a natural act in God—for then he
could not fail to will eternal life for us, and what grace would there be if it
were not freely given?—nor formally an act of mercy or justice, but an act of
supreme dominion, it was not necessary for God to seek a satisfaction of
justice before he could justly love us unto eternal life. Nor was it necessary
for the satisfaction of justice to complete this act of love as though it were
previously incomplete, because satisfaction of justice was in itself free to
God and necessary only on the hypothesis. For, absolutely speaking, God could
have brought us to eternal life by another mode than through his Son's
satisfaction.

*[Marginalia: Walaeus against Corvinus, chapter 25, p. 555.]*

17. You will ask, then, what reconciliation posits in God. I answer: it posits
nothing in God which completes the act of election, as Antonius Walaeus seems
to suggest, nor does it posit anything new in God's will which was not
previously in it. Rather, it only makes it possible for God, who most freely
loves us unto eternal life, actually to impart eternal life to us in a manner
right and fitting to his justice, as he freely decreed—that good which was
willed for us in eternal election.

From this observe Arminius's deception. He everywhere contends that the act of
willing life for us is restricted by divine justice as much as the actual
bestowal of life is, which is openly false. For the act of willing life for us
is an act of God's supreme dominion, not an act of justice;

[1651 logical printed p. 47 | IA leaf 65 | PDF p. 65]

but the actual bestowal of life is restricted by justice, though not by an
order of justice as if justice itself formally dictated to God this mode of
bringing human beings to eternal life. I prove it as follows. If God, insofar
as he is just and by the necessity of justice, brought sinners to life through
Christ's satisfaction, he could not bring them to life in any other way than
through satisfaction. But this confines God's freedom and infinite wisdom
within far too narrow boundaries. Therefore, the reason for the major premise
is that whatever God does insofar as he is just and from justice as a formal
principle, he does by the necessity of his nature and not freely; and if he did
not do it, God would be unjust. But he would not have been unjust if he had
brought everyone immediately to life and made them participants in glory, as
he did in the personal union of human nature with the divine.

It follows that God's wrath toward the elect when they sin is not hatred
opposed to the love with which he previously pursued them, but only displeasure
and an unmoved hatred against sins, not against persons, and at times a fatherly
chastisement. Hence God always loves the elect and always hates the reprobate.

I therefore wish it to be noted here how ridiculously the Remonstrants construe
our position on eternal election when they rave as follows: “An efficacious
intention to save, or an irrevocable and peremptory will by which God wills to
save Peter and Paul by name, cannot be conjoined with an executive will by
which he then wills to be reconciled to them or to give them a mediator through
whom he may be reconciled to them. Effectively to will

[1651 logical printed p. 48 | IA leaf 66 | PDF p. 66]

the salvation of sinners as the end and then to will reconciliation and merit
as the means by which you are moved to will the salvation of sinners are
contradictory.” But they fight with shadows. For we teach that God's
efficacious willing of the elect's salvation as the end and his being moved by
Christ's merit as the means most fitting to his justice, so that he actually
gives eternal life to them, are not only not contradictory but, according to
Scripture, harmoniously subordinated. But that God wills salvation for the
elect as the end and that God's will is moved by Christ's merit so that he
wills salvation for them is what the Arminians dream; it has never entered our
mind. Indeed, it is blasphemous for God's will to be moved by any external
cause or agent.

The Remonstrants ask in the same place: “What is the formal effect of Christ's
merit? For where such an efficacious will to save is presupposed, to say that
the effect of Christ's merit is the sinner's actual restoration to God's favor
is trifling. For actually to restore someone to another's favor is nothing
other than to bring it about that the latter actually and efficaciously wills
well to him. But this efficacious will precedes Christ's merit. Therefore, on
our doctrine, there is no formal effect of Christ's merit.”

I answer: these good men who thus divine are ignorant of the Reformed church's
doctrine. For Christ's merit does not change God's will toward the elect from
hatred into love; the Reformed churches do not worship a mutable God. The
formal effect of Christ's merit is that God, without prejudice to his justice,
can actually communicate to the elect the effects of grace and of his
gratuitous

[1651 logical printed p. 49 | IA leaf 67 | PDF p. 67]

eternal love—which indeed precedes Christ's merits—namely grace, immunity and
release from the penalty of sin, and eternal life. For God can efficaciously
will glory and grace for us which, by reason of his free decree, he cannot
actually give without injury to his justice apart from either our satisfaction
or Christ's. Human beings efficaciously will many things which they cannot
actually do without injustice. A king can efficaciously will that his
courtier, imprisoned for debt, be released from prison, yet he cannot actually
release him without violating justice unless he first arranges for the
courtier's debts to be paid. God efficaciously and absolutely willed eternal
glory for his beloved Son, yet he does not will him actually to enter it except
through many sufferings. But the Arminians always measure God by their own
little measure, as though in human fashion he passed from hatred into love and
then from love back into hatred.

*[Marginalia: Remonstrants, chapter 8 of the *Apology*, p. 98, Reply.]*

18. They therefore assign a false definition of reconciliation, namely, the
production of a will to save in place of a will to destroy, as though one
contrary will in God succeeded another.

19. Here too “to believe” must be distinguished: either it means to assent or
it means the heart's fiducial act and embrace by which we rest upon Christ. In
the former sense, we are not justified by faith, unless we say that Cain,
Judas, or others of that kind who believe the law's threats with horror are
justified. In the latter sense, faith rests upon the thing as

[1651 logical printed p. 50 | IA leaf 68 | PDF p. 68]

good, not as true or false. For the heart and affections in which the act of
trust lies apprehend good or evil, not truth or falsehood. Hence it is not
properly the case that “I believe” sins either are to be forgiven or have
already been forgiven.

Now to the matter. Satisfaction as rendered by Christ is not justification,
because it is the foundation of God's act of justifying. Nor is faith in any
sense the instrument of Christ's satisfaction. For who can by believing bring
it about that Christ has satisfied for him? Neither can faith by any reasoning
be called the moving or instrumental cause even of the divine acceptance by
which God accepts Christ's satisfaction for us.

First, by believing we do not bring it about, even instrumentally, that God
accepts Christ's death for our sins; nor can any external causality move God's
will. Second, whether or not we who now live after Christ's death believe,
indeed from eternity, God accepted Christ's death for the elect's sins and,
antecedently to our faith, resolved not to punish us but freely regarded us as
children in Christ. But the Arminians seem to suggest that faith is a
collateral part of the ransom paid to God by Christ, or that our faith
exercises some causality upon God's will by which that will is changed from
hatred and wrath into love and favor toward us. Consequently God too would
change, and an immanent and vital act would begin in God in time—namely,
love—while another act would perish—namely, displeasure and hatred. This is
blasphemous. For God wills nothing new or in

[1651 logical printed p. 51 | IA leaf 69 | PDF p. 69]

time; his volitions are eternal. We also deny that our faith—namely, assent—is
unable to apprehend the remission of all sins as past. Every justified person
truly apprehends this: namely, that Christ long ago made satisfaction and that
God from eternity acquiesced in that sufficient price of redemption, laying
aside displeasure and wrath toward their sins. Faith does not produce this;
rather, it presupposes its object as already accomplished.
