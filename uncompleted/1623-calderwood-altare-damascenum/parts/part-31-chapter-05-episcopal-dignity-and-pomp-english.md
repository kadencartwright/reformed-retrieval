# David Calderwood, *Altare Damascenum* (1623)

[1623 printed p. 319 | IA leaf 331 | PDF p. 331 | begins at `Sunt & alia praeter quatuor`]

There are also other things besides those four to be observed in the English
episcopate, such as pomp and dignity and other matters that will occur to us
in what follows. Thus far concerning archbishops, who are so called with an
addition; there follow:

Bishops simply so called. Of whom there are 24 in England. Their status must
be considered in the commonwealth and in the church.

In the commonwealth: that they have the title of lords by reason of the barony
annexed to the episcopate; precedence before other barons in the assembly of
the three estates and in other gatherings.

We spoke above at pp. 74, 75, and 76 about the title of lords, *my Lords*. Here
bishops are said to be adorned with that title by reason of the barony annexed
to the episcopate. Bishops are not owners of the estates, but usufructuaries
only. For a bishop cannot sell, alienate, donate, or exchange them at his own
discretion. Whatever was once given to the church was given for pious use.
Owners sold estates and possessions and laid the prices before the apostles'
feet. *After the church began to retain possessions of this sort, I cannot
deny the church's ownership of things whose true ownership I see transferred
to the church itself by the will of the donors. And when I say “to the
church,” I understand with Augustine not bishops, not other prelates, not the
priestly*

[Marginalia: *De Republ.* lib. 9. cap. 6. num. 10.]

[1623 printed p. 320 | IA leaf 332 | PDF p. 332 | split `col-` / `legium` joined]

*college, not ecclesiastics alone, but the whole assembly of the faithful: in
the first and immediate instance, that of the particular churches. But it is
plain that ownership in an entire particular church—that is, in the whole
assembly of the faithful of that church—is transferred by the lords who give,
because the intention of them all is that these goods should serve the most
common ecclesiastical use; and in ecclesiastical men the church itself has
never acknowledged any power other than that of mere and simple
administration,* Spalatensis says. Ecclesiastical goods, therefore, are the
goods of the community, and ownership of them rests with that entire
particular community insofar as it is a spiritual and ecclesiastical, not a
temporal, community. That whole community is the first and immediate owner of
its goods; and although the whole assembly of the faithful is the owner of
these goods with respect to possession, nevertheless with respect to use only
that part of this assembly which is in need and ought to live from the gospel
enjoys these goods, such as the poor and the ministers, of whom the bishop is
one. Nor is the church's ownership of those goods absolute, but in some
measure conditional, as the same Spalatensis says, for the church cannot
divert these goods beyond pious and ecclesiastical use, because the owner's
intention when he gave to the church was to transfer conditional ownership to
the church for pious uses alone. *Full ownership, therefore—though not for
profane uses—of all ecclesiastical things, whether immovable or also movable
things which continue to exist and whose substance endures and which are not
immediately consumed by use, according to Spalatensis, rests with the church
and with the entire community of the faithful.*

[Marginalia: Num. 21.]

The administration of the church's goods was indeed entrusted to certain
persons, especially ecclesiastics, but they were not to be administered at
the discretion of the bishop alone, so as to be furnished and divided at his
discretion through deacons or stewards, although he gradually arrogated this
singular power to himself. And if the community of the faithful sees
ecclesiastical administrators dividing or administering those goods badly, it
can correct that administration by divine right. But after the division of
movable and immovable goods had been made and his portion assigned to each,
and among others a portion to the bishop, though a greater one than was
fitting, as avarice and ambition now grew strong, the bishops nevertheless
were not owners of the estates assigned to them, but administrators or
usufructuaries only. Yet English bishops are not adorned with the title of
lords, *my Lords*, by reason of their being usufructuaries of estates. For in
that case all other usufructuaries, because they are owners of the fruits,
would have to be adorned with the same title in the English language. Indeed,
Spalatensis says, *both nobles and theologians or canonists make bishops users
only, and not properly usufructuaries, because, apart from pious use alone,
they cannot even dispose of the fruits themselves according to the pleasure
of their own will.*

[Marginalia: Lib. 9. cap. 7. num. 46.]

However it may be, the bishop is not the owner of the estate. Moreover, not
everyone who is the owner of some estate or property is by that very fact
adorned among the English with the title of lord, *my Lord*. It is nevertheless
a vice of

[1623 printed p. 321 | IA leaf 333 | PDF p. 333]

more recent centuries that the temporal estates of an episcopate are styled a
barony and the bishops themselves are adorned with the title of barons and,
consequently, of lords. *Barons in England occupy the lowest place among the
higher nobles,* Camden says.

[Marginalia: *Brit.* edit. Franc. p. 104.]

And Smith says in *The English Commonwealth* that this title *my Lord* belongs
to no one below the rank of baron, except those who are honored with it by
reason of some office.

[Marginalia: Pag. 29.]

Christ forbade his apostles not only civil jurisdiction and dominion, but also
the splendid titles with which magnates, princes, and those placed in secular
dignities are decorated. And he ordered both body and shadow—worldly
dignities and their titles, and all the clamor of the world's glory—to depart
from his church. But enough was said about these matters above at pp. 76–77. I
shall add only the judgment of Jean Bédé. *From this one may gather how great
is the malice and asinine ignorance of those who call the bishop Lord of Paris,
Lord of Chartres, and so forth. For the king alone is lord of those places. It
is true that in some provinces bishops are territorial lords, as is the bishop
of Rome and are some bishops in Germany. Against them runs the saying of Pope
Nicholas's eighth letter, which says that those who under the gospel are kings
and priests are members of the Devil. Whatever ecclesiastics may allege,
therefore, it is certain that they have neither territory nor jurisdiction by
any authority of law, nor any part of pure or mixed dominion, such as
jurisdiction is.*

[Marginalia: *Jus Regum,* pag. 45.]

The rationale of precedence is the same as that of titles. They are prohibited
by the same injunction of Christ. For sitting at the right and left of Christ,
whom the disciples dreamed would be a monarch, is of the same kind as
precedence before others. We treated the order of sitting in public assemblies
and precedence at pp. 77–78.

Christ wished his disciples to guard not only against dominion, jurisdiction,
dignities, titles, and precedence, but also against every other display of
pomp and luxury in clothing, retinue, diet, magnificence of houses, and the
like, in which the English bishops also sin, as was said at pp. 79, 80, and 81.
Spalatensis, adducing Gerson's disapproval of that pomp and luxury in his
treatise on the temperance of prelates in food, drink, and clothing, says:
*He then reduces the dispute to three heads. For, he says, ecclesiastics seek
pomp in clothing, dishes, and servants either to please God, to benefit their
neighbor, or for the honor and authority of the church. Not the first, he
says, because according to Jerome it is not fine clothing but cleanness of
mind that makes the adornment of clerics. And works of piety in which the
things that such pomp requires could be spent are more pleasing to God. Not
the second, because, he says, this pomp of the clerics scandalizes the laity
more than it edifies them; it rouses them more to indignation, murmuring, and
envy than to love, and it increases avarice and pride in them more than it
extinguishes them; nor can a pompous priest persuade others to humility,
frugality, and*

[Marginalia: *De rep.* lib. 9. cap. 5. 44.]

[1623 printed p. 322 | IA leaf 334 | PDF p. 334 | split `so-` / `brietatem` joined]

*sobriety by word and example. And below: When those pompous priests object,
he says, “We do this not for pride, not for preeminence, but so that the church
may thereby be honored together with her servants and, being so honored, may
grow in her authority,” he answers: “Honor ought to be given as testimony to
virtue, not on account of outward riches or ornament.” And so they lead the
people into error, when the people learn from them to honor the church on
account of those temporal things and not on account of future and spiritual
goods. Otherwise Christ and his apostles would not have adorned and honored
the church sufficiently when they themselves despised such things and
persuaded others to spurn them.* After Spalatensis had brought forward many
things against the pomp and luxury of bishops, he finally says: *And, to allow
myself something, I am not so strict as to wish a bishop to live altogether
the life of a mere slave or a lowly hireling and beggar. I wish, therefore, a
bishop to take and set aside only so much that, besides sustenance short of
gluttony, he may have respectable, not pompous, clothing, nor excessively
costly clothing, suitable and appropriate according to the variation of the
times; and only the necessary servants, not for pomp and a mere following, but
those suited to receiving and treating guests well.*

[Marginalia: *De rep.* l. 9. cap. 7. nu. 48.]

And elsewhere: *The reverence which the people show us solely on account of
outward splendor is also extreme, popular, applause-seeking reverence,
entirely vain and of no benefit to the spirit; and this splendor, as Bernard
used to say, excites admiration, not devotion. If, therefore, spiritual
reverence has now collapsed—the reverence which contributes greatly to the
people's spiritual progress when they believe me as I preach good things and
willingly hear me with reverence and devotion—what does it contribute to the
spirit, which alone the church regards, if the people revere me as rich,
powerful in the world, and honored, with worldly honor alone? Let even this
reverence perish, lest from it and through it the people are also led into
error and become still more carnal from what is carnal.*

[Marginalia: Lib. 9. cap. 5. num. 35.]

Ammianus Marcellinus, whose words may be seen above at pp. 9–10, although a
pagan, declares blessed not the Roman bishops but certain provincial ones,
*whom frugal eating and very sparing drinking, the cheapness of their clothing,
and brows looking toward the ground commends as pure and modest to the eternal
deity and his true worshipers.* A pastor's reverence is not to be secured by
outward splendor, display of urban possessions, lavish banquets, a numerous
retinue, and magnificence of houses, but by the adornments of virtues. *Those
who think this extraordinary splendor in prelates and churches necessary, or
at least useful and expedient, are greatly mistaken; and those who teach it
accomplish nothing other than bringing out certain cloaks with which we
wretches may somehow cover our shame.*

[Marginalia: *Spalat.* li. 9. cap. 5. num. 40.]

*Those who applaud us prelates excessively,* Spalatensis says, *and desire to
foster our pomps, nurture avarice, smile upon ambition, and veil luxury with an
assumed mask of a certain decorum and respectability.*

[Marginalia: Lib. 9. ca. 7. num. 31.]

When Marcellus II, the Roman pontiff, said that a reformation must be
instituted, he also said that the fear of diminishing pontifical authority,
which his predecessors alleged, was groundless. *For by that reformation
nothing substantial would be taken away,*

[1623 printed p. 323 | IA leaf 335 | PDF p. 335 | ends before `In Ecclesia ratione Vocationis`]

*but only showy vanities, indeed things costly and burdensome: the luxury and
pomp of prelates, the frequency of their retinues, immoderate and superfluous
expenses, and other things by which a pontiff's dignity becomes cheap rather
than producing greater reverence. If these follies were cut away, true
authority, power, and esteem would undoubtedly be increased among everyone.*

[Marginalia: Petri Suavis *hist. Conc. Trid.* l. 5. p. 438. edit. Francof.]

Luther therefore rightly calls the falsely named order of bishops *an
equestrian and heroic race of priests which dares to call itself princes of
the churches.*

[Marginalia: Tom. 2. pag. 290. de abrog. miss. privat.]

After he had proved from Acts 20 that all presbyters, and those who in our age
are commonly called parish priests and preachers, are called bishops and
rightfully possess the title and name of bishop, he says: *But perhaps Paul
did not read the papists' booklets and those wretchedly stitched-together
apologies. Otherwise, who would have dared to place several bishops over one
city and to call all the presbyters of one city bishops, when they were not
all princes, nor did they maintain a retinue or horses, but were scarcely
certain common and low-born men? For perhaps Paul did not know—what has grown
customary in our age—that no one can be a truly great bishop unless he keeps
a hundred horses (as that man says) in high mangers, unless his house resounds,
splendid with royal luxury, and unless he shines forth dominion with royal
titles.*

[Marginalia: Tom. 2. pag. 322. *Advers. falso nominatum ordinem Episcopor.*]

Gratian has the following from the Council of Tribur: *The vessels in which
the most holy mysteries are performed are chalices and patens. When Boniface,
martyr and bishop, was asked about these, whether it was lawful to perform the
sacraments in little wooden vessels, he answered: “Once golden priests used
wooden chalices; now, on the contrary, wooden priests use golden chalices.”*

[Marginalia: *De consecr.* dist. 1. cap. Vasa.]

Ferrier, the French orator, said to the bishops at the Council of Trent, *If
they first fashion themselves into Ambroses, Augustines, and Chrysostoms and
purge the church, they will bring it about that the princes also become
Theodosiuses, Honoriuses, Arcadiuses, Valentinianuses, and Gratians.*

[Marginalia: Petrus Suavis in *histor. Con. trid.* li. 8. p. 853. edit. Francof.]

There is no need for me to dwell on so evident a matter. *Let experience be
asked, and it will cry out with a loud voice: “It is so; nothing is truer.” For
whence came such great devotion of princes and secular people toward the poor
and ragged ecclesiastics of old? Whence such great honor, such great reverence,
such great veneration? Surely because they esteemed pomps of this kind as
nothing. For that reason riches in the greatest abundance were supplied to
them as to good and faithful stewards. But after they began to grow insolent
and abuse those riches, the former largesse was turned into its opposite,*
Gerson says in his treatise on the temperance of prelates in food, drink, and
clothing. *The matter itself declares that the immense largesse of certain
princes, however pious their intention may have been, spread so much poison in
the church that no more evils and calamities seem to have issued from that box
of Pandora celebrated by the poets,* Duarenus says.

[Marginalia: *De benef. & ministeriis* l. 2. c. 4.]
