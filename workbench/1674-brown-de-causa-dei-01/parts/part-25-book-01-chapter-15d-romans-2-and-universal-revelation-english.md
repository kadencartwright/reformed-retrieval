What next? “For if anything,” he says, “is repugnant to reason, surely it is
this very thing. For does not Paul, speaking in Romans 2:14–15 about the
Gentiles and the natural law, plainly testify that although they do not have
the law (that is, the revealed law), nevertheless, when by nature they do the
things of the law, those who do not have the law are a law to themselves? And
does he not expressly say that they show the work of the law written in their
hearts, while their conscience bears witness, and so forth? This is the
genuine description of the natural and therefore moral law. Can any such thing
be said about a positive law which binds the whole human race solely by
revelation? Do the Gentiles by nature do the things which belong to such a
law; and, not having such a law, are they in this respect a law to themselves?
Do they show the work of that law written in their hearts? And does their
conscience bear witness to them concerning this law?” Reply: 1. This

[1674 logical printed p. 203 | IA leaf 221 | PDF p. 221]

argument will have no less force against him than against us. For do the
Gentiles by nature believe in Christ, or do they know that duty? Do they show
the work of the law of faith written in their hearts? Does their conscience
bear witness to them concerning this law? If not, he will see that this is not
a genuine description of the moral law, for he concedes that the precept of
faith is moral. 2. We concede that by the light of nature the Gentiles do many
things materially commanded in the moral law, because in a certain manner
they have the natural law written in their hearts as regards practical
principles and the immediate conclusions drawn from those principles; they
also have a conscience which accuses or excuses. Yet, notwithstanding this, I
do not believe that the adversary will say that nothing pertains to the
natural or moral law except what the Gentiles have done by nature and have
shown by their deeds to be written in their hearts. If, then, he does not say
this, to what purpose, I ask, are these things? Is Paul speaking about the
whole moral law—that is, about the precepts of both the first and the second
table? What, I ask, did the Gentiles do by nature of those things which pertain
to the first, second, or third precept of the Decalogue? 3. Piscator, on this
passage, thinks that ὅταν ποιῇ should be translated “when they do”—that is,
whenever they do. “For I judge,” he says, “that the Apostle indicates that
profane people sometimes do the things which belong to the law, as when they
abstain from the possessions of others, from unlawful sexual intercourse,
from murder, and from injuries; when they do good to one another; and, in
short, when they perform the external works of the second table. For to
interpret this also of commands concerning punishments and rewards seems to
depart too far from the simplicity of the letter.” The Belgian Annotations
understand this only with respect to certain things: “and do some things, one
part of the Law.” Beza, on the passage, says: “That is, they command honorable
things and prohibit shameful things, as when they give commands concerning
religion and establish penalties for thefts, adulteries, and murders. For it
is one thing actually to do what the law commands, and another to do what the
law does—that is, to command what the law commands and forbid what it forbids.
Nor was this said by Paul about the observance of the law itself, but only
about the rule which even profane nations followed in framing laws. This is by
no means because human beings are by nature endowed with a true knowledge of
piety or justice, but because, whether they will or not, they possess some
sense of religion and some discrimination between shameful and honorable
things, which are sufficient to condemn them.” And afterward, on verse 15:
“Thus, in this passage, to have the work of the law inscribed upon the heart
means to possess a discrimination between shameful and honorable, just and
unjust things.” The most learned interpreter Calvin has similar things on the
passage: “For he proves,” he says, “that ignorance is alleged by the Gentiles
in vain, since by their deeds they declare that they have some rule of
justice. For no nation has ever so abhorred humanity that it did not confine
itself within some laws. Since, therefore, all Gentile nations have of their
own accord and without an adviser been inclined to make laws for themselves,
it is evident beyond doubt that certain conceptions of justice and rectitude,
which the Greeks call προλήψεις, have been implanted naturally in human
minds.” Thus, afterward, on verse 15 he says further: “Nor is it to be inferred
from this that human beings possess a full knowledge of the law, but only that
certain seeds of justice have been implanted in their disposition,” and so
forth. Therefore this testimony, as it is employed here, tells no more against
moral things which depend upon revelation in some manner than against other
things which he will doubtless confess to be moral.

He also says the same thing (so he continues on p. 11) concerning Romans 1:32:
“The Gentiles know τὸ δικαίωμα τοῦ Θεοῦ, God’s righteous judgment, that those
who do such things are worthy of death. Can this be said about such a universal
positive law? Indeed, since he says that the Gentiles, in the revelation of
God’s wrath from heaven upon all impiety and injustice, are ἀναπολογήτους,
without excuse, because τὸ γνωστὸν τοῦ Θεοῦ φανερόν ἐστιν ἐν αὐτοῖς, what can
be known of God is manifest among them (verses 18–20), can this even be
conceived of a positive law which”

[1674 logical printed p. 204 | IA leaf 222 | PDF p. 222 | ends before Haec sunt quae hîc habet]

“binds everyone and yet is not inscribed upon nature? Indeed, a law which it is
evident has not been revealed to everyone?” Reply: The Gentiles certainly knew
the righteous judgment of God insofar as it concerns the discrimination of
certain good and evil things and of just and unjust things, but not insofar as
it concerns all moral things. Nor does τὸ γνωστὸν τοῦ Θεοῦ, what can be known
of God, include all moral things; rather, it includes the existence of God
himself and his power and divinity, which they knew to some extent. But because
they clung to the surface and did not use this knowledge rightly, they did not
glorify God as they ought, as the following verses demonstrate. 2. When he asks
whether this can be said about such a positive law, and so forth, I in turn
will ask him whether it can be said about faith in Christ. Did the Gentiles
know that those who did not believe in Christ were worthy of death? Was this
precept inscribed upon their nature? Indeed, did they know τὸ δικαίωμα τοῦ
Θεοῦ, God’s righteous judgment, that those who did not believe that God is one
and that God must be worshiped in the manner prescribed by him were worthy of
death? To what purpose, then, are these things? 3. If by the positive law which
binds everyone he means the law concerning the Sabbath, we say that it was
inscribed upon Adam’s primeval nature and thus, in him, revealed to the whole
human race.

What else does he have? “Surely,” he asks, “are the Indians without excuse
because they do not observe the universal law of the Sabbath, which they could
not know from nature, and of whose having ever been revealed to them there is
not a trace or vestige? Or shall we say that a law of this sort was revealed
to them in their ancestors, whether Adam or Noah, and that, since their
descendants allowed that law to disappear, it is no wonder that, because of
their ingratitude, knowledge of this law was taken from them? We rightly
reject this in the patrons of universal grace, who must maintain something of
this sort concerning the proclamation of universal grace.” Reply: 1. He who
confesses that the precept of faith in Christ is moral is bound by the knot of
this difficulty no less than we are. For either he will say that the Indians
are without excuse because they do not observe this law, or he will not. If he
says they are without excuse, the reason will then be that this law was
revealed to them in their ancestors, and so forth; and thus he will favor the
patrons of universal grace. If he says that they are excusable, then he will
not regard that precept as moral. Or even if we were to say that they are
excusable for not observing the law of the Sabbath, it could not be inferred
from this that the precept is not moral. 2. But we plainly say that they are no
less without excuse for not observing this universal law of the Sabbath than
for not doing other things which lie farther from the implanted principles
and of which they cannot now have distinct knowledge without some revelation.
The reason is that it was revealed to them, as the others were—not in Noah,
but in Adam. Yet we say nothing which the orthodox rightly reject in the
patrons of universal grace, because Adam before the fall, when he represented
the whole human race and in the name of all, as its universal head, entered
into covenant with God—or rather assented to and pledged himself to the
covenant—stands in a different relation. Adam, as well as any other ancestors,
after the fall, when they stand or fall for themselves and are considered
personally, stands in another relation. We say that the precept concerning the
Sabbath was revealed to Adam before the fall, when he represented the whole
human race. The promise of grace in the seed of the woman, however, was made
and revealed to Adam after the fall, when he was considered personally and not
as the head of absolutely all human beings. Therefore we cannot say that what
was revealed to him in this condition was, in him, revealed to the whole human
race. These things, then, do nothing to the point.
