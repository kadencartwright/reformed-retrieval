But here Castellio rises up and contends that the whole passage is against
us, because “God answered Paul, ‘My grace is sufficient for you; my power
is perfected in weakness.’ Now what, I ask, is ‘to perfect’? Is it not to
accomplish perfectly? It is as though God were saying, ‘Unless you were
tempted, you could not conquer; now that you are tempted, you will conquer
by my kindness.’ Paul therefore conquered. Consequently this struggle did
not remain until death.”

I answer that this certainly does not follow. For:

Paul did conquer, but he conquered in his own manner and time. While he was
engaged in the struggle here, he conquered by not yielding, by bravely
enduring the enemy's assaults, and by gradually diminishing his strength;
at death, finally, he triumphed over the enemy now completely defeated, as
Christ himself did on the cross.

God's grace was sufficient for him. If “God's grace” means God's favor, the
sense would be, “Despite these thorns of your flesh, I still embrace you in
grace and regard you as beloved,” as some interpret it. What would this do
against us or for Castellio? Or if, according to Calvin, “grace” here does
not mean God's favor, as elsewhere, but metonymically signifies the help of
the Holy Spirit that comes to us from God's gratuitous favor, what follows
in Calvin removes every scruple: “It ought to suffice the godly, because it
will be firm and unconquerable, so that they never succumb.” In brief,
although this thorn remained embedded in the flesh until death, God's grace
was sufficient in life for the fight and in death for complete victory.

In precisely the same sense, God's “power is perfected in our weakness.” It
does not mean, as Castellio wishes, that it is accomplished perfectly—that
is, immediately or while we are still struggling. Rather, it is perfected
because it is more perfectly recognized, as Calvin and Grotius explain,
just as Hebrew verbs in the Hiphil conjugation frequently refer to the
effect of making something known. Or if it is understood of real
perfection, without which that true and real recognition cannot exist, it
must nevertheless be understood as gradual here on the way, not as complete
in every respect before the end of our way and life. As Grotius rightly
observes, in these words God gives the reason why he does not remove this
evil from Paul: his power is the more perfected—that is, illustrated—the
more those through whom he acts are subject to adversity. Thus the thorn is
not yet removed, but remains, though increasingly blunted each day. To
encourage Paul further in the fight, Christ says that his strength and
power are perfected—that is, more perfectly exerted, illustrated, and
recognized—while Paul's weakness still remains. Though Paul is weak,
subject to the thorn's frequent blows, and wounded, Christ's assisting and
strengthening power keeps him from yielding to evils. Instead, he advances
more boldly against them toward final victory, so that they are either
overcome or endured without doing harm. He repeatedly meets and weakens the
enemy bravely in battle; when this warfare ends together with life, he
gains complete victory and celebrates an eternal triumph.

Nevertheless, evangelical perfection can also consist at a lower level in
our recognition of our imperfection. Estius truly comments on this passage:
“This does not mean that

[1679 printed p. 291 | IA leaf 299]

the Christian person's virtue or courage is properly perfected in this
kind of weakness, since weakness is contrary to it. Rather, as Augustine
says, a person cannot be perfected who does not confess himself weak. Hence
Jerome also affirms in his letter to Ctesiphon that the one perfection of
the present life is to recognize yourself as imperfect.” But we have now
proceeded too far; let us return to Castellio.
