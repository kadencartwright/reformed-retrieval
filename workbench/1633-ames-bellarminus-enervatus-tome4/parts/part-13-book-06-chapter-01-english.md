## Book VI

### Concerning Justification

### Chapter I. Concerning Its Formal Cause

#### Thesis I

Bellarmine. *The state of the whole controversy can be reduced to this simple
question: whether the formal cause of absolute justification is righteousness
inhering in us or not.*

Protestant reply. The state of the question can indeed be reduced to this, but
with this qualification: that inherent and infused righteousness or holiness,
which in this life is begun and imperfect, is not excluded by the word
*absolute,* and that this absolute justification is referred to the judgment
of God, as the apostle speaks in Romans 3:20, *in his sight.* Cardinal
Contarini, in his treatise on justification, proposes this question much more
accurately and at the same time also resolves it. He says: *Since through
faith we arrive at a twofold righteousness—righteousness inhering in us, and
love and grace, by which we are made partakers of the divine nature; and the
righteousness of Christ, given and imputed to us, since we are grafted into
Christ and have put on Christ—it remains to inquire upon which of the two we
ought to rely and to consider ourselves justified before God, that is, held
to be holy and righteous. I judge altogether that it is piously and
Christianly said that we ought to rely—I say, to rely as upon a stable thing
which certainly sustains us—upon the righteousness of Christ given to us, and
not upon holiness and grace inhering in us. For this righteousness of ours is
begun and imperfect; it cannot protect us from offending in many things and
from sinning continually. Therefore, in the sight of God, we cannot be held
righteous and good because of this righteousness of ours, as it would befit
the sons of God to be good and holy. But the righteousness of Christ given to
us is true and perfect righteousness, which is wholly pleasing in God's eyes,
in which there is nothing that offends God, nothing that is not supremely
pleasing to God. Therefore we must rely upon this alone as sure and stable,
and believe in it*

[Printed p. 323 | Google Books PA323 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*alone that we are justified before God, that is, held righteous and called
righteous.* In this sense we deny that righteousness inhering in us is the
formal cause of our absolute justification.

#### Bellarmine's Arguments

2. Bellarmine. Romans 15:17–19. *The apostle declares: 1. To be justified is
to be constituted righteous. 2. We are so constituted righteous through the
obedience of Christ, in the way that we were constituted unrighteous through
the disobedience of Adam. But it is agreed that through Adam's disobedience we
were constituted unrighteous by unrighteousness truly and actually inhering
in us, not by Adam's unrighteousness imputed to us. Therefore the formal cause
of absolute justification is righteousness inhering in us.*

Protestant reply. 1. From that phrase *to be constituted righteous,* nothing
can be concluded for inherent righteousness against imputed righteousness.
For when God imputes righteousness to someone, by that very act he constitutes
him righteous by imputation in his sight.

2. From the comparison between Adam and Christ, the Pontificians' position is
plainly enough refuted and ours confirmed. First, in verse 15, *grace and the
gift through grace* are opposed to death entering through sin (as Cajetan
observes), *so as to comprehend all divine benefits, both outward and inward,
bestowed on the human race through Christ.* Second, Bellarmine himself admits
in *On the Loss of Grace,* book 4, chapter 10, that *Adam's sin is imputed to
all his descendants just as if they all had committed the same sin,* and in
book 5, chapter 17: *Adam's actual sin is communicated to us through
imputation, although besides the imputation of Adam's disobedience there is
also a similar perversity and crookedness inhering in each person.* In chapter
18 he proves that imputation from the apostle's very words in Romans 5:
*Through the disobedience of one many were constituted sinners. Through the
offense of one, condemnation came upon all men. In whom all sinned.* It
therefore follows from the proportion of the comparison that Christ's
righteousness is first imputed to us, and by it we are made righteous before
God, although a certain holiness of righteousness also inheres in every
believer. Bellarmine therefore betrays the utter poverty of his cause when he
seeks its principal defense from the passage which (by his own admission)
plainly testifies to the contrary.

3. Bellarmine. Romans 3:24. *We are justified through grace, that is, through
righteousness given and infused by him. This is the formal cause of our
justification.*

Protestant reply. Here too, as with the previous testimony, with a certain
deliberate purpose he seeks a defense for his cause from the very words of
Scripture which are most opposed to it, so that he may be regarded as a more
courageous disputant. For the word *grace,* both in that passage and
everywhere in Paul where justification is treated, sig-*

[Printed p. 324 | Google Books PA324 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*nifies nothing other than God's gracious favor toward us revealed in Christ.
I. In Romans 4, *grace* is opposed to the *debt* of obligation that exists in
the man who gives wages to a worker. It is therefore not in man but in God.
II. Righteousness is said to be *imputed from grace* by the very fact that
sins are remitted; but remission is not infused and inherent righteousness,
in the same passage. III. *Grace* gives, Romans 5:15. IV. *Grace* appears more
superabundant and greater from the abundance of sin remitted, in the same
passage, verse 20 and 6:1, 5. Gracious election proceeds *from grace,* Romans
11:5–6. V. *Grace* is the end of predestination and the cause of our
redemption, Ephesians 1:6. VI. *Grace* is the cause of God's kindness toward
us, Ephesians 2:7. Not one of these can be affirmed of a quality inhering in
us. Add first that it is not yet perfectly certain among the Pontificians
whether such infused grace inhering in us is given. Medina's conclusion on
1.2, question 110, article 2, is: *It is not certain according to the doctrine
of faith that grace is an infused habit inhering in the soul.* Second, God can
justify a man without righteousness of that kind inhering in him. *Let the
theologian assert this without doubt: God can most rightly make men pleasing
and lovable to himself without any inherent quality. Certainly, according to
the opinion of all theologians, God can most rightly justify a man without
infused habits.* Medina, in the same place, article 4.

Bellarmine. 1. *God's favor is sufficiently explained by the word “freely” in
Romans 3:24. Therefore the addition “through grace” signifies something
else.*

Protestant reply. *Freely* signifies a gratuitous benefit; *his grace*
signifies whose benefit this is (as Cajetan observes) and the manner in which
it is conferred. The distinction in chapter 5:15 is the same: *the grace of
God and the gift through grace.*

Bellarmine. 2. *The preposition “through” is not rightly applied to favor.*

Protestant reply. I. It is applied to mercy in Luke 1:78: *through the tender
mercies of God.* II. No preposition appears in the Greek text of Romans 3:24:
τῇ αὐτοῦ χάριτι.

Bellarmine. 3. *God's favor is not ineffectual; therefore it makes us such as
he wishes us to be.*

Protestant reply. It does so both by imputing and by sanctifying.

Bellarmine. 4. *Grace is said to be given, received, and made.*

Protestant reply. Mercy too is said to be given, received, and made: 2 Timothy
1:16; Luke 1:72 and 10:37; as also is the love of God, 1 John 3:1. Yet by
*grace given* Scripture does not always mean justifying grace.

Bellarmine. 5. *Grace is compared to light, but light does not make a body
bright unless it inheres in it.*

Protestant reply. The light of the Lord's countenance is grace inhering in
God, but justifying us; the light of grace which makes us inwardly and
formally bright is something else.

[Printed p. 325 | Google Books PA325 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Bellarmine. 6. *The love of God is poured out in our hearts,* Romans 5:5.

Protestant reply. I. Cajetan says: *It is apparent that he explains the first
foundation of hope—the love of God toward us abundantly poured out in us
through the Holy Spirit.* II. This experience of God's love is not justifying
grace but certainty of it.

4. Bellarmine. 1 Corinthians 6:11. *Justification is placed in washing and
sanctification, and it is indicated as occurring through the invocation of
Christ's name and the operation of the Holy Spirit. But these things cannot
be affirmed of the remission of sins and the imputation of righteousness
without inward sanctification.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine first assumes what is not conceded: that in
this passage justification is the whole whose parts are washing and
sanctification. We, on the contrary, say that washing denotes the whole power
of Baptism, whose parts are sanctification and justification. II. The
invocation of Christ's name and the operation of the Holy Spirit also
contribute to justification. For the Holy Spirit engenders faith, through
which we apprehend Christ and obtain justification in him. Yet it is not
necessary that by *the name of Jesus* we understand anything other than Jesus
himself, as is apparent in Acts 4:12.

5. Bellarmine. Titus 3:5. *Justification is described as being regeneration
and renewal, which formally occur through some inherent gift.*

Protestant reply. We deny that justification is described there; rather, it
is demonstrated or declared from its end and effect. For when the apostle
wishes to describe justification, as in Romans 3 and 4, he ascribes it not to
regeneration and renewal but to remission, imputation, and faith in Christ.
But justification is introduced incidentally in this passage, so that
Christians may understand that life is not to be hoped for by them from
justification while good works are neglected, and yet that justification,
not good works, is the proper cause of life.

6. Bellarmine. Hebrews 11. *Some men are called truly and absolutely
righteous, and their righteousness is proved from the effect of that
righteousness: good works, whose cause is inherent, not imputed,
righteousness.*

Protestant reply. I. There is no controversy that some are truly and
absolutely righteous. But we deny that anyone is absolutely righteous by
inherent righteousness according to the ordinary law, so that he has no need
to ask for the remission of sins. II. Not only inherent righteousness, but
also imputed righteousness (in its own manner), is the cause of good works.
For imputed righteousness is the cause of inherent righteousness and of all
its effects.

7. Bellarmine. *The apostle* in Romans 8 and 1 Corinthians 15 *says that the
righteous are to be conformed to the image of Christ. But Christ is not
righteous by imputation, but by righteousness inhering in him. Therefore we
too, if we ought to be like him,

[Printed p. 326 | Google Books PA326 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

if we ought to be his brothers and images, must have true and inherent
righteousness.*

Protestant reply. The conclusion is conceded. For at present we bear the image
of Christ in righteousness, and hereafter we shall bear it in glory. But that
image of righteousness is from the grace and righteousness of Christ given to
us, and it does not equal its exemplar. It therefore requires the gracious
imputation of righteousness as the foundation both of its gift and of its
acceptance before God.

8. Bellarmine. *Through justification, by which we die to sin and rise to
righteousness, we represent Christ's death and resurrection,* Romans 6. *But
Christ truly died and truly rose again; therefore so do we.*

Protestant reply. The conclusion is not doubted. But we deny that dying to sin
and rising to righteousness are parts or acts of justification. All the
things discussed in Romans 6 belong to the sanctification of life flowing
from the justification of faith, as the sequence of the context plainly
shows.

9. Bellarmine. *The adoption we have in the Spirit through justification is
real, not imaginary.*

Protestant reply. For who has denied it?

10. Bellarmine. Romans 4, last verse. *He rose again for justification. The
apostle attributes the name of justification to inward renewal rather than
to the remission of sins.*

Protestant reply. No such thing appears.

Bellarmine. *It cannot be doubted that the apostle wishes Christ's death to
have been the exemplar of death to sins, and his resurrection the exemplar of
justification.*

Protestant reply. Toledo rightly answers on the passage: *This exposition
does not accord with Paul's present meaning, for he is said to have been
delivered for offenses, not as an exemplar but as a true satisfaction.
Therefore neither is he said to have risen for justification as an exemplar,
but so that the redemption accomplished through death might be applied to us
and we might partake of its fruit. This is the legitimate sense.*

11. Bellarmine. Romans 5:21. *As sin reigned unto death, so grace reigns
through righteousness unto eternal life. Here “righteousness” is understood
as inward renewal, from which the works of life proceed. Justification
therefore consists in that renewal.*

Protestant reply. I. By righteousness is not understood (at least principally)
inward renewal, but that gift through grace in verse 15, which is opposed to
the guilt of sin in verses 17–18, that is, the gift of reconciliation with God
through and because of Christ. II. Although it were conceded that grace reigns
through inherent righteousness, it would still not follow that justification
consists in it, because justification precedes the reign of righteousness,
just as in Adam guilt preceded the continued reign of sin.

[Printed p. 327 | Google Books PA327 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

12. Bellarmine. Romans 6:13. *Your members are instruments of righteousness.
Something inherent from which works proceed is understood there.*

Protestant reply. As though any of us denied all inherent righteousness.

13. Bellarmine. Romans 8:10. *The Spirit is life because of righteousness.
The righteousness which causes life is something inherent.*

Protestant reply. Quite the contrary (as Cajetan notes): the life of the
spirit of those in whom Christ dwells is because of the righteousness of
Christ, by which he satisfies God, paying the price of human redemption. For
because of that righteousness outwardly, and through participation in it
inwardly, there is life in those in whom Christ dwells.

14. Bellarmine. Galatians 3:21. *If a law had been given which could give
life, righteousness would truly be from the law. It is something that gives
life to the soul and establishes it in movement and action.*

Protestant reply. I. To give life in this passage does not signify to
establish in movement and action, but to impart blessed life, as in verse 12:
*Whoever does these things will live through them.* II. Legal righteousness
establishes in movement and action as an inward real principle, but the
righteousness of the Gospel by which one is justified before God does so as a
procuring cause.

15. Bellarmine. Ephesians 4:23. *The apostle calls renewal righteousness and
holiness.*

Protestant reply. So do we.

16. Bellarmine. *Justification is without doubt a certain movement from sin
to righteousness, and it takes its name from the terminus to which it leads,
as do all other similar movements—illumination, heating, and the rest.
Therefore true justification cannot be understood unless some righteousness
besides the remission of sin is acquired.*

Protestant reply. This supposedly indubitable description of justification
had Thomas Aquinas, 1.2, question 113, as its author. By this invention he
surpassed the insane imagination of his teacher Alexander of Hales
(*justification is the rectitude of free choice,* part 1, question 17, member
4). For it is a horrible corruption of the Gospel and the head of the
Scholastics' errors, offending not only against Scripture but against reason
and common sense. For I. Justification is no more a movement than any action
can thus be called logically. II. It is the sort of movement that condemnation,
to which it is opposed, and remission, absolution, reconciliation, and
acceptance are—not the sort that *illumination* and *heating,* Bellarmine's
examples, are. III. It is not a physical transmutation of inherent qualities,
but a moral or relative transmutation of status, such as the change of a man
who, through his creditor's forgiveness, becomes a nondebtor from being a
debtor. IV. It conflicts with common sense that the remission of sins and the
infusion of righteousness should be one and the same movement, because
remission is a moral action and the infusion of a quality is

[Printed p. 328 | Google Books PA328 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

a physical action that actually changes its subject.

17. Bellarmine. *Justification bestows glory, but remission of sins alone
merely frees from punishment. Therefore remission alone is not
justification.*

Protestant reply. Nor do we maintain that remission alone, without acceptance
unto life, is complete justification. Rather, we maintain remission and
acceptance alone, without infusion as a cause or part, although not without
infusion as an effect which follows by nature and is joined in time.

18. Bellarmine. *Justification makes friends out of enemies,* and so forth.

Protestant reply. I. Medina answers, as above, on 1.2, question 110, article
4: *Let the theologian assert this without doubt: God can most rightly make
men pleasing and lovable to himself without any inherent quality. Certainly,
according to the opinion of all theologians, God can most rightly justify a
man without infused habits.* II. Justification properly makes us friends of
God passively, and sanctification actively. In justification God testifies
that we are loved by him in Christ; in sanctification he causes us to love him
with reciprocal love.

19. Bellarmine. *No passage of Scripture at all is found where it is written
that Christ's righteousness is imputed to us for righteousness, or that we
are righteous through Christ's righteousness imputed to us.*

Protestant reply. I. Although this were not read in precisely the same words,
it would still not follow that the teaching itself is not contained in the
Scriptures. So Bellarmine a little afterward says: *If they merely wished
Christ's merits to be imputed to us,* and so forth, *their teaching would be
correct, although that manner of speaking is never or very rarely found in
Scripture and the Fathers.* II. Scripture and the Fathers are badly joined in
this respect. For it is plain that the Fathers introduced into the church
from philosophy various manners of speaking, especially about human merits
and the righteousness of the Gospel, which do not appear in Scripture; and
from this occasion was given and taken by the Scholastics to fabricate
pernicious errors. III. We read everywhere in Paul that we are made righteous
and justified through Christ, through Christ's death, blood, redemption,
obedience, and righteousness; and that righteousness is imputed to us by God
without works. This is the whole of what we profess on this subject.

20. Bellarmine. *No necessity for such imputation can be offered. For if this
imputation were necessary, it would be necessary chiefly because a man is
still truly a sinner after the remission of sin. But this is false.
Therefore.*

Protestant reply. It is necessary. I. So that sins may be remitted. II. So
that this remission may be continued to us and through it we may not only
escape the punishments of hell but also arrive at

[Printed p. 329 | Google Books PA329 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

eternal life. III. So that our imperfect endeavors in glorifying God may be
pleasing and acceptable before him.

21. Bellarmine. *Inherent righteousness, faith, hope, and love can be perfect
even in this life. The imputation of righteousness is therefore not
necessary.*

Protestant reply. I. There is no consequence: *Inherent righteousness can be
perfect; therefore imputed righteousness is not necessary.* For imputed
righteousness is first necessary where inherent righteousness is imperfect,
although it can be perfect. It is also necessary so that God's grace may be
communicated to us for attaining that perfection. II. What Bellarmine here
implies—that faith, hope, love, or inherent righteousness can and even
ordinarily does, under the common law, become simply and absolutely perfect
in this life—is so abhorrent to the teaching of Scripture and common
experience that it should not require a painstaking refutation (especially in
this place). We will merely oppose to it the weighty confession of Cardinal
Contarini in his treatise on justification: *I judge altogether that it is
piously and Christianly said that we ought to rely—I say, to rely as upon a
stable thing which certainly sustains us—upon the righteousness of Christ
given to us, and not upon holiness and grace inhering in us. For this
righteousness of ours is begun and imperfect; it cannot protect us from
offending in many things and continually sinning, and consequently from
needing the prayer by which we daily ask that our debts be forgiven us.
Therefore, in the sight of God, we cannot be held righteous and good because
of this righteousness of ours, as it would befit the sons of God to be good
and holy. By experience we see holy men who, the more they advance in
holiness, are the less pleasing to themselves, and consequently understand
all the more that they need Christ and Christ's righteousness given to them;
and so they abandon themselves and rest upon Christ alone,* and so forth.

22. Bellarmine. *It is impossible for Christ's righteousness to be imputed to
us in the sense that through it we are formally called and are righteous,
although it is true that Christ's merits are imputed to us, because they are
given to us and we can offer them to God the Father for our sins, since
Christ took upon himself the burden of making satisfaction for us and
reconciling us to God the Father.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine here entangles himself in a contradiction in
order that he may contradict us. For if Christ's merits are given to us by
God so that we can offer them to God on our behalf as satisfaction for our
sins and the just cause of reconciliation, then they are certainly imputed to
us for righteousness, because the proper effect of perfect satisfaction is
that the one for whom it avails is made and held righteous in estimation. II.
We say that through Christ's imputed righteousness we are formally called and
are righteous in no other way than that in which one whose debt is paid by
another

[Printed p. 330 | Google Books PA330 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

is called and is free or immune from that debt, and in which one for whom
another's favor or grace has been procured is called and is pleasing to that
other. Common experience sufficiently proves that these things can occur.
III. Just as Adam's sin imputed to his descendants causes them to be formally
called and to be sinners before God—which Bellarmine admits in *On the Loss
of Grace,* book 4, chapter 10—so Christ's righteousness can be imputed to
believers so that through it they are called and are formally righteous (in
the divine estimation).

Bellarmine. *We contend that this conflicts with right reason. For although a
designation is sometimes made from an outward form, in the way that a thing
is called known through knowledge which is in the mind, and a wall is called
seen through the vision which is in the eye, yet when there are two contrary
forms in something, one inherent and the other outward, the absolute
designation is without doubt taken from the inherent form rather than the
outward one. An Ethiopian dressed in a white garment is not rightly called
white but black.*

Protestant reply. This quibble is utterly futile, and in many ways. For I. The
question is whether we are formally called and are righteous through imputed
righteousness. Bellarmine objects that the designation is to be taken
*rather* from the inherent form, as if the question now were from where the
designation should *rather* be taken. II. He knows that we understand
justification relationally; he trifles about an absolute designation. III.
The examples Bellarmine offers of designations from an outward form (*a thing
known, a wall seen, an Ethiopian white in his clothing*) have no proportion
to this: *a believer is justified before God by imputed righteousness.* In
this, the production and status of a moral relation is signified, which
depends upon an outward cause; but nothing of the kind appears in those
examples. Suitable examples are found in Scripture: *an unbeliever is
condemned; a sinner is redeemed,* and so forth. IV. Imputed righteousness and
inherent unrighteousness are ineptly called contrary forms. For a man who is
freed from a debt by his creditor's forgiveness and yet is detained in chains
for a time is called free and bound, but not by contrary forms, because the
opposition is not in the same respect. V. Bellarmine well knows that together
with imputed righteousness we join inherent righteousness in the same person,
although not under the same aspect of cause. So he himself says in chapter 6:
*Calvin acknowledges that renewal and sanctification are also begun.* Why
then does he pretend here that the inward state of the justified person is
(on our teaching) absolutely contrary to the outward state which is through
imputation?

23. Bellarmine. *If Christ's righteousness were truly imputed to us so that
through it we were held and accounted righteous, just as if it were our own
inward and formal righteousness, then certainly we ought to be held and
ac-*

[Printed p. 331 | Google Books PA331 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*counted no less righteous than Christ himself. Consequently, we ought to be
called and held the Redeemers and Saviors of the world,* and so forth, *which
is utterly absurd.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine here invents a question for himself in order
to give some color to his quibble: *whether through Christ's righteousness we
are accounted perfectly righteous, as if it were our own inward and formal
righteousness.* This is not our teaching. Rather, Christ's righteousness is
imputed to us to the extent that by its power we are accounted righteous
before God just as if we ourselves had in us that by which we are accounted
righteous before him. II. Christ's righteousness is imputed to individual
believers according to their particular need, not according to its universal
value, just as a jewel of the greatest price offered to redeem various
captives is applied to individuals not according to its entire valuation but
according to the need of each captive. Therefore infinite righteousness is
absurdly mentioned in the application to an individual. III. Christ's
righteousness is not imputed to us as causes but only as subjects. Therefore
Bellarmine most ineptly infers from it that we can be called Redeemers or
Saviors because we are redeemed and saved. IV. To make Bellarmine's wicked
sophistry manifest in directing this quibble against the imputation of
Christ's righteousness which we believe is made by God, it must be observed
that this same Bellarmine, in favor of the imputation of Christ's
satisfaction which the pope is imagined to unite with the sufferings of the
saints in his impious Indulgences, was able to distinguish between the thing
and the manner of its application. He says in *On Indulgences,* book 1,
chapter 4: *This seems certain: Christ's sufferings, which are of infinite
worth, are applied through Indulgences in a finite manner.* Great indeed is
the perversity of a man who can imagine that a finite application of infinite
righteousness can be made by any pope, and yet will not see how this is done
by God.

24. Bellarmine. *Christ restored to us what we lost in Adam. But in Adam we
did not lose imputed righteousness but inherent righteousness; therefore in
Christ we do not receive merely an outward and imputed righteousness.*

Protestant reply. I. We grant the conclusion. II. Christ did not merely
restore the things we lost, but also procured for us certain greater things,
just as Paul procured that Onesimus should be received by Philemon, *not as a
slave* (as he had been before) *but as more than a slave, a brother,*
Philemon 16. Imputed righteousness is with respect to its essence the same
status before God that we lost in Adam; the manner is different. Yet in the
same way the sophist could quibble that repentance or conversion is not given
to us by Christ, because we did not lose it in Adam.

25. Bellarmine. *If through Christ's righteousness imputed to us we can truly

[Printed p. 332 | Google Books PA332 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

be called righteous and sons of God, then through our unrighteousness imputed
to him Christ too could truly be called a sinner and, what the mind shudders
to think, a son of the devil.*

Protestant reply. We deny that the reasoning is equal on both sides. I.
Because it is not the end of our unrighteousness that it should be transferred
to Christ, but this occurs by God's singular dispensation. II. Our
unrighteousness is not imputed to Christ so that his status is absolutely
changed; rather, both our unrighteousness is imputed to Christ and his
righteousness to us so that our status is changed. III. Our unrighteousness is
imputed to Christ as to one who willingly removes, conquers, and swallows it
up by his satisfaction; but Christ's righteousness is imputed to us so that
it may remain upon us in its power and efficacy. IV. Christ admitted the
imputation of our unrighteousness on the condition that by his righteousness
we should be constituted righteous; but our unrighteousness was imputed to
Christ from no similar merit or condition. V. Christ's grace in the imputation
of his righteousness is much more abundant and effectual than our guilt
imputed to Christ.

26. Bellarmine. *Christ's bride has beauty inhering in herself, not imputed
to her by the bridegroom.*

Protestant reply. She has both.

27. Bellarmine. *Through justification the heart is cleansed so that it may
be prepared for the vision of God.*

Protestant reply. It is cleansed from guilt through justification and from
inherent stain and depravity through sanctification. The same answer is given
concerning the statement that Christ is said to sanctify his people, which is
Bellarmine's last argument.

#### Our Arguments

1. Protestant argument. To justify, as almost always in Scripture, and
especially when God is said to justify the ungodly, is understood in a
forensic sense: to hold, account, pronounce, or declare righteous, not the
actual communication of righteousness. This is manifest in Romans 5 and 8.

Bellarmine. 1. *The name “justification” is indeed often understood in the
Scriptures as the declaration of righteousness, but not always,* as in Daniel
12:3; Isaiah 53:11; Revelation 22:11.

Protestant reply. I. It is sufficient for us if, in the apostle, where he
disputes about a sinner's justification before God, it has that forensic
meaning. II. It does not appear that it has another meaning in the passages
cited. For in Daniel 12 the heralds of God's word can be said to justify
insofar as through their ministry men are brought to be held righteous before
God, by a metonymy of the effect. In Isaiah 53 Christ is said

[Printed p. 333 | Google Books PA333 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

to cause men to be pronounced righteous when apprehended through knowledge,
that is, through faith which he himself gives. In Revelation 22 believers are
aroused to continue declaring themselves righteous, or so to act that they
may be declared righteous by God.

Bellarmine. 2. In Romans 5 *justification is understood as that which makes or
constitutes righteous.*

Protestant reply. Thus far we agree. But since in verse 18 it is directly
opposed to condemnation, it is sufficiently apparent that men are constituted
righteous in justification in the same manner that they are constituted
unrighteous in condemnation.

Bellarmine. 3. In Romans 8 *it is clear that the verb “justify” is understood
as making righteous, not merely pronouncing righteous, from the similar words
placed there: magnify, give life, mortify, glorify together—all of which
signify making someone such and such.*

Protestant reply. I. Some of those words, as they are read in Greek, have no
similarity at all to *justify.* For what proportion is there between
ζωοποιεῖν and δικαιοῦν? Nor does an argument from similarity of sound hold in
words whose usage is dissimilar. II. It is so clear in verse 33 that
justification has a forensic meaning that in this passage the Jesuits
themselves are compelled to surrender. Toledo says: *The verb “to justify” in
this passage is understood in the signification opposed in the antithesis,
namely to condemnation, so that in this passage to justify is the same as to
pronounce righteous, just as a judge by his sentence absolves and pronounces
an innocent man. It is also understood in this signification in many passages
of Scripture.* Cornelius à Lapide, who elsewhere leaves no stone unturned to
make sport of Scripture in this doctrine, also agrees with Toledo here: *God
justifies, that is, he absolves from sins and from the accusation brought for
sin and the devil, and pronounces righteous.*

Bellarmine. 4. *An ungodly man cannot truly be pronounced righteous unless
the one who pronounces him righteous at the same time makes him righteous.*

Protestant reply. God at the same time makes him righteous by imputation and
also begins to make him so by sanctification.

2. Protestant argument. I. Romans 4:5. Faith is imputed for righteousness.

Bellarmine. 1. *Faith itself is imputed; but faith is not Christ's
righteousness but ours.*

Protestant reply. For faith to be imputed for righteousness in verse 5 is the
same as what is said in verse 6, *God imputes righteousness without works,*
and in verse 7, *he remits sins.* But faith itself, considered absolutely, is
neither righteousness without a work nor the remission of sins. Therefore it
is necessary that imputed faith relationally encompass its object, that is,
Christ apprehended by faith.

Bellarmine. *To impute does not signify a bare estimation, but an estimation
to which the truth corresponds in the thing itself.*

[Printed p. 334 | Google Books PA334 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. As though the truth of this divine statement—*I impute to
you Christ apprehended by faith as righteousness in my sight*—could not hold
in the thing itself unless our inherent righteousness were imputed. The thing
itself in that statement is not our inherent quality but God's judicial act.

3. Protestant argument. The righteousness believers have from imputation
made through grace is the remission and covering of sins, Romans 4:6–7.
Therefore it is not an inherent quality.

Bellarmine. 1. *It is not a perfect definition of justification.*

Protestant reply. It is sufficient for us that the apostle uses the remission
of sins and justification interchangeably.

Bellarmine. 2. *Paul adduces this testimony from the Psalm not to define
justification completely, but only to prove that the justification of an
ungodly man is God's gift and cannot be obtained by one's own powers.*

Protestant reply. Not even this is proved by that testimony unless it
describes the nature of justification for us.

Bellarmine. 3. *In these words a complete definition is contained, but
implicitly. For the remission of sins cannot occur unless righteousness is
infused.*

Protestant reply. I. If this is a complete definition of justification, then
it explains what justification is; but it says nothing about an infused and
inherent quality. II. Experience among men sufficiently teaches that the
remission of sin can by its nature be separated from the infusion of
righteousness, since common human experience shows men remitting debts and
injuries to those into whom they infuse neither riches nor righteousness.
III. Bellarmine himself admits the
contrary in chapter 16: *The guilt of punishment can indeed be removed
without the infusion of righteousness, for nothing seems to prevent God from
willing not to ordain a man to punishment, forgiving the offense, and not
holding as an enemy the man to whom he has not granted the gift of habitual
righteousness.*

Bellarmine. 4. *Nothing can be covered by God unless it is completely
destroyed. When sins are said to be covered by God, they are necessarily
destroyed and vanish.*

Protestant reply. Present sins are covered in precisely the same manner in
which past sins are forgotten. These things are not said properly but
metaphorically, because God thus restrains his vengeance and spares believers and the
repentant as if he had entirely forgotten their sins and did not see any
present sin in them.

4. Protestant argument. Romans 5:19. We are constituted righteous through
Christ's obedience, just as all the sons of Adam were constituted unrighteous
through Adam's disobedience. But the latter occurs by imputation. Therefore
so does the former.

Bellarmine. *Adam's disobedience constituted us sinners not formally but
efficiently.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine himself plainly testifies to the contrary in *On
the Loss of Grace,* book 4, chapter 10: *Adam's sin is imputed to all his
descendants just as if they had all committed the same sin.* Nor can it be
understood how

[Printed p. 335 | Google Books PA335 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Adam's disobedience could efficiently constitute his descendants sinners
unless that disobedience were first imputed to them. For sin which neither
inheres in us nor is imputed cannot effect anything in us. So Bellarmine says
in *On the Loss of Grace,* book 5, chapter 18: *We would not truly be
constituted sinners through the disobedience of one unless that disobedience
were our disobedience,* and so forth. How it is ours the same Bellarmine
declares in the same book, chapter 17: *Adam's sin is communicated to us in
the way in which something that has passed can be communicated, namely
through imputation. For it is imputed to all who are born from Adam.*

5. Protestant argument. 1 Corinthians 1:30. Christ is made righteousness for
us.

Bellarmine. 1. *Christ is called our righteousness because he is the
efficient cause of our righteousness, just as he is also called our wisdom
and redemption.*

Protestant reply. These words, considered in themselves, could indeed bear
this interpretation. But because inherent righteousness is signified by the
other word (*sanctification*), and our righteousness is customarily
attributed to Christ by the same apostle by way of imputation, the manner of
our justification appears to be declared by these words.

Bellarmine. 2. *Christ is called our righteousness because he made
satisfaction to the Father for us and so gives and communicates that
satisfaction to us when he justifies us that it can be called our
satisfaction and righteousness. In this manner it would not be absurd if
someone said that Christ's righteousness and merits are imputed to us when
they are given and applied to us, just as if we ourselves had made
satisfaction to God.*

Protestant reply. Here Bellarmine hands us the entire case under dispute; we
ask for nothing more.

Bellarmine. 3. *Nevertheless, it is not to be denied that there is in us true
and absolute inherent righteousness, to which glory is owed by God's just
judgment.*

Protestant reply. On the contrary, absolutely perfect inherent righteousness
to which glory is owed from justice cannot coexist with the
imputation Bellarmine has just conceded. For when Christ's righteousness and
merits are given and applied to us, they are applied for this end: that life
and glory may be adjudged to us because of them. Nor can we rely, without
injury and insult to Christ and loss to ourselves, upon some other merit by
which glory would be owed to us by just judgment. This little patch of human
presumption tears the garment of divine grace to which it is so inelegantly
sewn.

6. Protestant argument. 2 Corinthians 5:19. God was in Christ reconciling the
world to himself, not reckoning their offenses to them.

Bellarmine. 1. *Nonimputation carries with it the remission of sin and the
infusion of righteousness.*

Protestant reply. It is remission itself, and the infusion of righteousness,
as an effect of remission and reconciliation, accom-*

[Printed p. 336 | Google Books PA336 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*panies it; but reconciliation through nonimputation or remission is not the
infusion of righteousness: it is justification. Therefore justification is
not infusion.

Bellarmine. 2. Colossians 2:13. *The same thing he here calls reconciliation
he calls vivification or renewal, and the forgiveness of offenses.*

Protestant reply. If vivification in that passage signified regeneration, it
would nevertheless denote not the infusion of a quality but the application
of reconciliation through insertion into Christ. But Paul speaks more
properly about being made alive together with Christ and in Christ by
imputation, as in Ephesians 2:5–6: *He made us alive together with Christ,
raised us up together, and seated us together in the heavens in Christ
Jesus.* Here Cajetan rightly observes that *when Christ was raised and
elevated, all his members were raised together and seated together in heaven,
not in themselves but in Christ.* And Contarini says: *It is Paul's constant
aim to teach that we are grafted into Christ; hence all things that are
Christ's are attributed to us.*

Bellarmine. 3. Romans 5. *What is called reconciliation in verse 11 is called
the abundance of grace, gift, and righteousness* in verse 17.

Protestant reply. The superabundant grace by which righteousness is given to
us is not a quality inhering in us, but God's mercy by which he reconciled us,
his enemies, to himself through Christ's death, verse 10.

Bellarmine. 4. *The apostle explains the effect of reconciliation most
plainly,* Colossians 1:21–22: *that we may be presented holy, immaculate, and
blameless before God.*

Protestant reply. These are likewise effects of justification, not
justification itself.

7. Protestant argument. 2 Corinthians 5:21. Christ was made sin for us so
that we might become the righteousness of God in him.

Bellarmine. 1. *Christ's righteousness is imputed to us with respect to the
satisfaction which he rendered for us, but we cannot therefore be held
righteous if the stain and filth of sins truly inhere in us.*

Protestant reply. I. If Christ's righteousness is imputed to us, then we are
certainly reckoned righteous in him. No one imputes righteousness to a man he
does not hold righteous. II. If Christ's satisfaction is imputed to us, then
our debts for which he made satisfaction are not imputed but remitted, and we
are freed from their bond before the creditor.

Bellarmine. 2. *We are called righteousness in Christ—righteousness which is
in him, not in us—because we are his members. For that which belongs to the
head also belongs to the members, not insofar as they are members distinct
from the head but insofar as they make one and are one with the head itself.*

Protestant reply. This very thing is our teaching and faith, which Bellarmine
attacks and yet admits to be true and consonant with reason. Thomas, 3,
question 48, article 1, explains more fully

[Printed p. 337 | Google Books PA337 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

the nature of this communion we have with Christ as head: *Christ's works
stand in relation to his members as the works of another man established in
grace stand in relation to himself.*

Bellarmine. 3. *Righteousness inhering in us is called the righteousness of
God because it is given to us by God, or also because it is the effect and
image of God's righteousness.*

Protestant reply. The clause *in him,* together with the parallel *he made
him sin,* stands in the way. Cajetan says: *Consider, prudent reader, first
the righteousness of God in Christ in that God willed satisfaction to be
made for us by Christ himself. For the suffering of the innocent for sinners
is satisfying righteousness, a species of commutative righteousness proper
to one who pays the penalty. This is the righteousness of the New
Testament.* And Contarini says: *The righteousness of God in him, since his
righteousness has become ours, given to us and imputed to us.*

Bellarmine. 4. *Just as Christ was made a sacrifice for sin not imputatively
but truly, so in justification we participate not imputatively but truly in
the effect and likeness of divine righteousness when we receive inherent
righteousness.*

Protestant reply. Christ was indeed truly made a victim for sin, but in order
for him to be so, our sins were imputed to him. *For the sins of those for
whom it is offered are imputed to the sacrifice,* as Contarini observes and
Scripture testifies in Leviticus 16:21–22. In the same way we are truly made
righteous in Christ; but for this to occur, Christ's righteousness must be
imputed to us.

8. Protestant argument. Ephesians 1:6. By his grace he freely made us
acceptable to himself in the beloved.

Bellarmine. 1. *The Greek word* ἐχαρίτωσεν *signifies to render inwardly
gracious, beautiful, and comely.*

Protestant reply. I. It conflicts with this interpretation that the grace
treated there is *the glorious and rich grace of God.* II. That the praise of
this grace is the end of divine predestination. III. That the manner in which
this grace is said to come to us is not the Spirit's sanctification but
redemption and remission.

Bellarmine. 2. *The phrase “in the beloved,” “in Christ,” signifies nothing
other than that we owe all our good things to Christ.*

Protestant reply. This is indeed true in general. But because we owe good
things to Christ as a cause, and Christ is their cause in different ways,
according to the nature of the good under discussion the phrase *in him* also
has a particular signification. But here the good of reconciliation or
redemption is treated, and in verse 7 Christ's blood is called the price of
redemption. Therefore *in him* means the same as in his blood, satisfaction,
and righteousness.

9. Protestant argument. Acts 13:38–39. The remission of sins is proclaimed
through Christ, and through him everyone who believes is justified from all
the things from which they could not be justified through the law of Moses.

[Printed p. 338 | Google Books PA338 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Bellarmine. 1. *We are justified through Christ as through the efficient
cause, and through an infused quality as through the formal cause.*

Protestant reply. The remission of sins and justification from sins are
placed in the text in the same sense. Yet nothing is more absurd than for an
infused quality to be the formal cause of remission, which is not a thing
infused into us as subjects in which it inheres, but an act of God's will
directed toward us as objects.

Bellarmine. 2. *Christ or his righteousness is the efficient cause of our
righteousness; therefore it cannot be its formal cause.*

Protestant reply. Christ, as applying himself to us, is the efficient cause of
our righteousness, and yet, as applied, he is its formal cause. The price of
redemption, insofar as it is given for captives, is the efficient cause of
redemption, but insofar as it is applied to them, its formal cause.

Bellarmine. 3. *The remission of sins is proclaimed to all those who believe
in Christ as they ought, that is, by doing whatever he commands to be done.*

Protestant reply. But I. Remission is not proclaimed through that doing but
through Christ and faith in him. II. It is utterly foreign to holy doctrine
that observance of all the commandments is required beforehand for the
remission of sins and the communication of grace. III. True faith, although
weak like a grain of mustard seed, justifies. It is therefore not necessarily
required that everyone believe (in every respect) *as he ought.*

Bellarmine. 4. *Remission is not the mere forgiveness of punishment, but the
washing and cleansing of guilt.*

Protestant reply. Common sense teaches that remission is properly in the one
who remits, and cleansing in the one cleansed, so that one cannot be a part of
the other.

10. Protestant argument. Luke 18:14. The publican who received the pardon of
sins for which he asked is called justified.

Bellarmine. *He asked for complete reconciliation, and he obtained such.*

Protestant reply. This is complete remission and acceptance.

Bellarmine. *He was exalted.*

Protestant reply. In justification, as in the cause of all glory.

11. Protestant argument. The righteousness by which a man is justified
before God must be perfect. But the righteousness inhering in us is not such.
Therefore.

Bellarmine. 1. *We are formally righteous not by our work but by God's work,
who at the same time wipes away the stains of sins and infuses the habit of
faith, hope, and love. But God's works are perfect.*

Protestant reply. The work which God performs is indeed perfect with respect
to

[Printed p. 339 | Google Books PA339 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

God as its worker, that is, free from iniquity, *his work,* Deuteronomy 32:4.
But not every work of God in itself—or, as Cajetan speaks on that passage,
with respect to *the work wrought*—is immediately perfect, much less with
respect to the participation of the subject which receives some quality into
itself from the divine operation. The work of creation was not perfect on the
first day. In spiritual things too, God first begins a good work and
afterward perfects it, Philippians 1:6. Bellarmine himself says in *On Grace
and Free Choice,* book 1, chapter 13, that *through effectual grace God brings
the beginning of a good will, which is like a seed from which the fruit of a
perfect will afterward arises.* And in *On Justification,* book 1, chapter
20: *Faith first begins justification and then, when hope and love have been
added, perfects it. The righteousness of God begins from imperfect faith and
terminates in perfect, that is, living, faith.* Since Bellarmine was therefore
compelled here to evade a most weighty argument by such a childish subterfuge
and shameful contradiction, he certainly brought a weighty prejudice against
his cause among all who possess judgment.

Bellarmine. 2. *Even actual righteousness, although it is in some way
imperfect because of the admixture of venial offenses and needs the daily
remission of sin, nevertheless does not on that account cease to be true
righteousness and perfect in its own manner.*

Protestant reply. This escape is no less wretched than the one preceding it.
For I. The question is not about the truth but about the degree, dignity, and
perfection of inherent righteousness—whether it is such that it can sustain
God's judgment and examination. II. Righteousness which has offenses mixed
with it cannot be pure, much less perfect. III. Righteousness which needs
daily remission needs daily justification; therefore it cannot justify on any
single day. IV. Righteousness which can sustain God's judgment must be
perfect, not *in its own manner,* but in God's manner and that of the divine
law.

12. Protestant argument. Injury is done to Christ's merits and righteousness
if we are said to be justified through inherent righteousness.

Bellarmine. 1. *We detract nothing from Christ's merits when we confess that
our righteousness depends upon their origin and efficacy.*

Protestant reply. Would he therefore not detract from Christ's satisfaction
who said that his own suffering satisfies divine justice for the guilt and
eternal punishment of his sins, if he confessed that it depended upon
Christ's satisfaction?

Bellarmine. 2. *The adversaries greatly detract from Christ's glory when they
affirm that he was unable to obtain from the Father that our sins be truly
destroyed and that he reform us unto true righteousness.*

Protestant reply. These adversaries are imaginary. It therefore makes no
difference to us if, by another way no less than Bellarmine himself, they
detract from Christ's glory.

13. Protestant argument. Occasion for proud boasting (as also for
des-*

[Printed p. 340 | Google Books PA340 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*pair) is furnished to men by this doctrine of justification from inherent
righteousness.

Bellarmine. 1. *There is no such occasion, because Catholics say that men
have righteousness by God's gift.*

Protestant reply. Would the man who said that by God's gift he had inherent
righteousness equal to Christ's righteousness therefore not be proud?

Bellarmine. 2. *Those who boast that they have apprehended Christ's very
righteousness are proud.*

Protestant reply. Those who boast of this are truly proud;
but if those who receive Christ and profess this to God's glory are proud,
they are surely taught by Christ to be proud. Yet in truth nothing is more
consonant with Christian humility than to rely upon Christ's righteousness
and God's mercy. Contarini rightly says in his treatise on justification: *By
experience we see holy men who, the more they advance in holiness, are the
less pleasing to themselves, and consequently understand all the more that
they need Christ and Christ's righteousness given to them; and so they
abandon themselves and rest upon Christ alone. This does not occur because,
having become holier, they see less than before, nor because they
have become of a more abject and meaner spirit. On the contrary, the more they
advance in holiness, the greater they are in spirit and the more discerning
they are. Therefore, having become more discerning, they see more clearly the
meagerness of the holiness and righteousness inhering in them, together with
which they perceive many stains which more greatly offend their eyes now
rendered more discerning. Consequently they know in very fact that they must
not rely upon holiness, love, and grace inhering in them, but that they must
flee for refuge to Christ and to Christ's grace given to them and rest upon
it.*

worthwhile to observe here once for all), has among the Pontificians not only
the authority which it receives from its author, a learned and weighty man
and a Roman cardinal, but also all the authority it can borrow from the
approval of the *regent doctors of the Faculty of Theology of Paris.* By
their testimony and signature they twice publicly attested that *all these things
are approved by them and in no way differ from Christian truth,* as appears
at the beginning of Contarini's works published at Paris in 1572, in these
words: *We, the undersigned regent doctors of the Faculty of Theology of
Paris, candidly and solemnly testify that the following commentaries and
minor works of the most reverend and illustrious Cardinal Gasparo Contarini
have been read accurately and diligently by us, namely the commentaries on
Saint Paul's Epistles to the Romans, Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians,
Philippians, Colossians, Thessalo-*

*nians, Timothy, Titus, and Hebrews, as well as on the Epistle of Saint John;
