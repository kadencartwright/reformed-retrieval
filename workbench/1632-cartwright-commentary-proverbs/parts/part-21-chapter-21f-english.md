Verse 15. It is joy to the righteous man to execute justice; but destruction to the worker of iniquity.

In מחתה (*contritione*, destruction) there is a metaphor transferred from corporeal things, which are broken, to terror, by which the mind is broken and, as it were, crushed.

The purpose is, by comparing the righteous and the unrighteous, to furnish each person with judgments by which he can determine with certainty concerning himself and his salvation, or his perilous condition.

It is evident from very many testimonies of sacred Scripture that people in large measure flatter themselves and promise themselves every good thing from God when his anger hangs over their heads. From James 1.20 and likewise Deut. 29.19, it appears that there are those who, when God denounces a curse against them, bless themselves and promise themselves peace when God threatens war, even when they persist in continuing in the perversity of their mind. Besides this, pseudo-teachers and flatterers are not lacking, who, speaking those things which are dear to us, will boldly pronounce that every prosperous and joyful thing is going to befall us. Eph. 5.5, 6. Hence it is that we are commanded to examine ourselves, whether we are in the faith, 2 Cor. 13.5 and 1 Cor. 11.28. Since, therefore, it is of such importance to examine ourselves and to make trial of ourselves—to learn in what state, good or evil, happy or unhappy, we stand—we ought to value this doctrine and these Proverbs, by which we may acquire this knowledge of ourselves.

That it is joy to the righteous man to execute justice is evident because he loves God’s commandments, Ps. 119.48 and verse 127. The Prophet professes his love

[1632 witness | PDF p. 470 | printed columns 924-925]

to have been more fervent toward God’s precepts than toward even the purest gold, and at verse 72 he professes that he valued God’s teaching more than many thousands of gold and silver coins.

From this love of right and justice, delight is born, Ps. 119.47; Isa. 58.13, 14. And indeed it is greater than delight in food and drink, John 4; even in honey and the honeycomb, Ps. 19; than in sleep, and indeed the sweetest sleep, such as sleep at midnight, Ps. 119.62; than in all riches, Ps. 119.14; and than in play and jest, Ps. 119.16.

Let us therefore measure ourselves by this rule, and fashion and adorn ourselves according to this mirror, so that, if we are conformed to this image, we may rejoice solidly, as though we had received an answer from heaven that our names are written in the book of life; but if we are unlike it, let us endeavor to express its likeness as vividly as possible.

Here, however, lest we cast snares upon infirm consciences, it must be considered that this eagerness and readiness of the godly for doing good is not found so complete in all its parts and absolute that it is not retarded by the opposing desires of depraved flesh, as though by fetters and hindrances cast upon it. For as long as we carry two men about here on earth, it cannot happen that the respective fruits and effects of each do not stand opposed to one another. See Rom. 7.15, 18, 21, 22, 23; Heb. 12.1.

From this delight in doing good, moreover, a singular joy is born, which the consciousness of benefits conferred leaves behind it, because by this argument and indication the faithful know most certainly that they are justified and dear to God.

The former part of the Proverb is confirmed from the latter, just as the latter is confirmed from the former. For through hatred of the good, the wicked are not only held by no delight in it or desire for it, but they even recoil from it as from a pest or a harmful thing. For since they hate God and turn their backs upon him, they cannot pursue what is holy. Next, since they love evil alone and desire it most eagerly, they go so far as to prefer it to sleep, Prov. 4.16—an example of which is in Ahab—and to food, as is apparent in those who had bound themselves by vows to kill Paul; likewise it is seen in Ahab. They hold wickedness as their most delectable food, Job 20.12, and draw in iniquity as fish draw water, Job 15.16. An example of this is in Mal. 1.13, where the Jews are said to have been wearied by bringing sacrifices which anyone could have scattered with a breath.

Yet let no one deceive himself here because he sometimes embraces what is good and hates some evil, as we read concerning Herod, who willingly heard John and performed some of the works which were set before him by John. For unless he hates all sins, he is not truly and purely led by hatred of any sin. Next, unless he does what is good from love and zeal for God, and not merely from flight from punishment or attainment of reward, he does not truly embrace what is good.

For these wicked men, however, there is destruction, just as for the righteous there is joy. For there is no peace for the wicked, since in the midst of their greatest joys they are tormented by the worm of an evil conscience as by an executioner.

Verse 16. A man wandering from the way of understanding will rest in the assembly of those devoid of life.

In נוח (*quiescet*, will rest) there is a metonymy of the subject for continual remaining.

The purpose is, by setting forth the punishment, to deter people from ignorance of duty and arouse them to the investigation of truth.

People ignorant of God’s justice persuade themselves that ignorance of the truth is not harmful to them unto death; especially

[1632 witness | PDF p. 471 | printed columns 926-927]

however, if in their own opinion they persuade themselves that the pursuits which they follow are pleasing and approved by God. The Pontificians strengthen this error, which is innate in our nature, through their patronage of good intentions, with this design: that while the people are sunk in the darkness of ignorance, they themselves may be free to perpetrate anything with impunity and indeed without the slightest reproof. There are also very many who are still worse than these, who, wishing to enslave themselves to their desires, willingly close their eyes to the knowledge of the truth, lest, once enlightened by knowledge of their duty, they should by that same knowledge of the truth either be drawn away from pursuing their desires or commit, not without the bite and wounding of conscience, those things which they hold among their delights.

Therefore, not only are those condemned by the Lord who do what is evil against the resistance of conscience, and who oppose the light and are rebellious against it, as in Job 24.13, but also those who depart from duty through error. For the servant who does not know his master’s will shall be beaten. See John 16.2 and Acts 3.17, 18. For since knowledge of every kind of duty was given to us by God in Adam, it is necessary that in him we be held guilty for that same knowledge’s having been lost and effaced.

The punishment, or the effect of error, follows: eternal death, which he declares by this form of speech, namely, that the man will rest in the assembly of those devoid of life. For the godly, though they die, will nevertheless live, John 11.25, and for them death is a passage to life, John 5.24. For death does not have dominion over the godly, Rom. 6. Here there is an elegant antithesis between wandering from the right way and rest, or an evilly tranquil permanence from which no one will ever be able to turn or wander away.

Here it is asked how this applies to the wicked, since in Isa. 57 it is attributed as something peculiar to the godly that they rest in their tombs as in beds. The answer is that they are said to rest by κοινωνίᾳ ἰδιωμάτων, the communion of properties, because their souls rest, whereas the souls of the ungodly are tormented by the most dreadful infernal tortures. Next, the sleep of the bodies of the godly and of the ungodly differ very greatly. For the bodies of the godly—as usually happens with those who sleep tranquilly and on either ear—are awakened in the morning vigorous and cheerful; whereas the bodies of the ungodly—as though during the night they had been agitated by nothing but turbulent and savage dreams—are raised in the morning sad and savage. Therefore, when they are said to rest in death, it signifies nothing other than, as was said, to remain in death, namely, eternal death.
