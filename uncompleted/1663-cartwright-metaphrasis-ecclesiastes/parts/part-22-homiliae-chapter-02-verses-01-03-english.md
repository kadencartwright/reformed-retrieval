CHAPTER II.

VERSE 1. Then, because in much wisdom there is much indignation, and he who
increases knowledge increases sorrow.

VERSE 2. I said with my mind, Come now, let me test you with gladness; therefore
enjoy the good. But behold, that too is vanity.

VERSE 3. Of laughter I say, It is insane; and of gladness, what does it
accomplish?

Before he transferred his mind from the study of wisdom and knowledge to
bodily pleasure, he gives the reason for his change of plan: because as wisdom
and knowledge increased, his mind's grief and torment increased at the same
time, destined to increase further by the same steps by which an increase of
wisdom and knowledge would come. When someone applies his mind to the study and
investigation of some subject but makes no progress despite the greatest labor
and most strenuous effort, it is no wonder if he is tormented in mind. And if
he has made some progress, but only very slight and meager progress, neither is
it strange if, worn down by disgust with that matter and having contracted
vexation, he abandons its study and love. But for someone who has advanced very
far in the study and investigation not only of lawful and permitted knowledge,
but of knowledge both noble and celebrated, to be seized by disgust and
deterred by pain seems altogether strange and not free from some mark of
inconstancy. Nevertheless Solomon, writing by the guidance and impulse of the
Holy Spirit, openly and candidly confesses not only that torment of mind had
been joined to the study of wisdom and knowledge, but that he had contracted
so much more pain and torment in proportion as he had made greater and more
successful progress in the study of each. But if Solomon, with his incomparable
genius, could attain nothing in these studies in which his mind might rest, it
is readily evident that this is denied to everyone else. Here the objection is
raised that by this teaching everyone is deterred from the study of this
wisdom, eagerness is blunted, and industry and diligence grow cold—and that in
a matter whose investigation was commanded to men by God, as we learned
earlier. I answer that in this way our zeal is kindled to seek heavenly and
never-perishing knowledge. Just as this knowledge of itself fills the mind with
singular delight, so, like a kind of honey, it renders all other lawful and
honorable occupations pleasant with its sweetness.

For, first, by the very fact that it is a good work, undertaken from a
consciousness of obedience to God, it cannot fail to refresh the mind,
especially since it makes us more certain of eternal election in Christ.
Proverbs 12:15. Then, once the foundation of godliness has first been laid, by
knowledge of these matters we are carried more deeply into the knowledge of
divine wisdom, power, providence, and goodness. These are engraved upon the
creatures as if in somewhat larger letters and are clearly perceived through
true faith and the knowledge of God as if through spectacles. Therefore, if we
wish to receive solid benefit from honorable and lawful arts, sciences, and
labors, our first care must be to be imbued with the true fear of God. By it,
as by salt, all things are seasoned, and without it they are insipid; by it we
are greatly relieved of burdens, though they be heavy, and when weary are
refreshed; by it hard things are softened, bitter things become sweet, and
rough things are made smooth.

Someone may ask here how it happens that there are very many who consume their
whole lives in the study of natural Philosophy, others in acquiring knowledge
of History, indeed—and this is worse—in Judicial Astrology and the discovery
of the Philosopher's Stone, and finally in Alchemy, and grow old in those
studies. For these men

seem to attain something in these studies which delights them and in which
they rest, because they cannot in any way be torn away or drawn off from them.
They would not seem likely to do this if those studies were joined with so much
grief and torment of mind. Their knowledge therefore appears to embrace some
secret which Solomon, with his manifold and extraordinary wisdom, did not
penetrate. I answer that it is evident from the testimony of the Holy Spirit
that in the investigation of legitimate knowledge those men were far inferior
to Solomon. Therefore, their lingering and growing old in those studies arises
from nothing other than this: unlike Solomon, they do not cast their accounts
and make a total of their studies, nor do they ever consider with themselves
and their own minds how very little fruit they receive from such great and
constant expenditures and labors, or how far they are from the good which they
pursue in the study of these things. It is therefore a certain stupor and
foolish *insensibility* (`ἀναίσθησις`) that keeps them in the study of these
things. They imitate merchants who, keeping their books of receipts and
expenses without sufficient care and diligence, at last go bankrupt and lose
their whole estate. In just the same way, these bad accountants of their own
studies incur the loss, if not of their estate—as often happens—then certainly
of eternal salvation. If, therefore, there are no *taskmasters*
(`ἐργοδιῶκται`) in the Commonwealth—which is to be desired—to inspect our
studies and endeavors and call us to account for how we spend our time and
abilities, we ourselves ought to demand an account from ourselves: what fruit
of our labor and expenditure we bring back for the glory of God, the benefit
of others, the common fellowship of life, and finally our own salvation.

There is another class of men who, fickle and inconstant throughout the whole
course of their life, are driven at every moment as if by the wind, so that,
leaving the former way of life in which they exercised themselves, they flit
to one new way and then another and another. These men resemble boys, to whom
one and the same thing, however pleasant, cannot give pleasure for long. Either
because something troublesome and hard has mingled itself with the course of
life they undertook, or because they see that this pleases others, they abandon
their chosen pursuit in life without any solid reason underlying the change.
Their error also must be corrected by this design of Solomon, who withdrew his
mind from that wisdom and knowledge only for just causes and after mature
deliberation, and only so far as not to place man's happiness and peace of mind
in them.

*Translator's note:* The edition prints `Prov. 12. 15` after the statement

not appear to support that statement; 2 Peter 1:10 is a much closer biblical
parallel. The Latin and English retain the printed reference without silently
emending it.
