It is asked whether Arminian certainty—the certainty which they attribute to
believers—can coexist with Scripture or with faith and hope. They affirm that
it can; we deny it.

1. Their certainty is conditional. For in article 5, pp. 194–195, they say,
“On the basis of the divine promise, a believing person can be certain that
he will remain in God's grace and, far beyond every doubt, will obtain eternal
life and salvation, if he perseveres in faith and in observing God's
commandments. Nevertheless, he is not absolutely and simply certain that he
will necessarily persevere in that faith with which he was once endowed.”
Hence they deny, first, every absolute certainty of perseverance resting upon
God's promises and the immutable decree of predestination. They admit only
the certainty which depends upon the indifference of free choice to standing
or not standing.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 636 | PDF p. 685 | split word joined: dependeat]

Second, they deny the kind of certainty which excludes every fear of final
apostasy and eternal ruin. Third, they also deny the certainty which rests
infallibly upon God's grace and the testimony of the Holy Spirit.

2. This hypothetical certainty is so empty that it cannot coexist with God
and his truthfulness. For it conflicts with God's immutability and
truthfulness in promising our confirmation unto the glorious day of
redemption, with God's oath, and with Christ's intercession and prayer.
Indeed, according to the Word of God, the certainty of salvation is as
absolute and certain, excluding every cause or just ground for fearing the
opposite outcome, as God's promise that the world will not again be submerged
by Noah's waters, Isaiah 54:9–10, and as God's faithful covenant concerning
the succession of nights and days and the movements of the sun and moon,
Jeremiah 31:35–36.

3. Conditional certainty suggests and instills confidence, hope,
consolation, and peace of conscience in the most wicked people just as much
as in the elect and believers. For it is certain that all mortals will
persevere and be saved, provided that they practice righteousness to the end.
Cain can say, “I confidently trust that no temptations can cast me out of the
state of grace, provided that I practice righteousness in the fear of the
Lord to the end.” Then, on the other hand, this doctrine suggests terror, the
fear of hell, miserable despair, and a diabolical sorrow

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 637 | PDF p. 686 | split word joined: Diabolicam]

devoid of every consolation to true believers. For every believer can, and
indeed ought to, fear that he will perish eternally if he does not practice
righteousness to the end. Thus he can say, “Neither Christ by his
intercession and prayers, nor God the Father by his gracious keeping and
protection, nor the Holy Spirit by his supremely powerful grace can secure my
perseverance and therefore my eternal glory, if I cast off God's grace and
faith. Doing this is as free to me as it is for Peter to debate or not debate
tomorrow.” On this account, in all temptations the anchor of hope is fixed not
in grace—which, according to our opponents, cannot bestow perseverance if the
free will stands in the way—but the anchor is cast in free choice. Hope that
rests upon the creature cannot fail to put one to shame. Such faith must
vacillate and cannot be the faith of Abraham, the father of all believers,
concerning whom the Holy Spirit says in Romans 4:20, “He did not doubt God's
promise through unbelief but was strengthened in faith, giving glory to God,”
and in verse 21, “Being fully persuaded that he was able to perform what he
had promised.” But if God conditionally promises perseverance to believers in
this way, no one is persuaded that God is able to perform what he promised.

4. No one could pray for perseverance and consequently could not give glory
and thanks to God for an answered prayer.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 638 | PDF p. 687 | split word joined: gratias]

For the believer says, “Eternal God, give me grace to persevere, provided that
I pray, watch, and perform my duty.” This is the same as if he were to say,
“Give me perseverance, provided that I persevere. Grant that I constantly
pray and perform my duty, provided that I constantly pray and perform my
duty.”

5. This conditional certainty is compatible with the eternal ruin of a
person who is endowed with such certainty of faith and sealed by the Spirit
unto the day of redemption.

It is asked whether the Remonstrants rightly pass over in silence, in their
doctrine of certainty, the certainty of our election; and whether
Grevinchovius, against Ames, folio 138, rightly denies every sense of election
in this life. Reply: the Remonstrants deny that anyone in this life can know
that he is finally elected to glory, because, according to them, no one is
finally and irrevocably elected except the person who expires in faith. We,
on the contrary, contend that people ought to be certain of their eternal
election, not indeed by an *a priori* certainty—for who has known the mind of
the Lord?—but by an *a posteriori* certainty.

1. First Corinthians 2:12 says, “For we have received the Spirit who is from
God, that we may know the things which God has freely given us.” Therefore, we
may also know our election made from eternity.

2. All who are elected to glory are also predestined to conversion and
adoption as God's children, Ephesians 1:5–6. But God has given

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 639 | PDF p. 688]

us many evidences by which we may know that we are converted, and therefore
also evidences by which we may know that we are elected to glory. The major
premise is Romans 8:30 and Acts 13:48. The minor is 1 John 2:3: “By this we
know that we know him, if we keep his commandments”; and chapter 3:14, “We
know that we have been transferred from death to life because we love the
brothers”; and chapter 4:2, “By this we know that we love God's children, when
we love God and keep his commandments”; and verse 16, “And we have known and
believed the love which he has toward us.”

3. We are commanded to make our calling and election more sure through good
works, 2 Peter 1:10.

4. We know that we are called and justified through peace of conscience,
boldness, the sense of God's love poured out in our hearts, hope which does
not put us to shame, and glorying in afflictions, Romans 5:1–4; Romans
8:15–17. But those who are justified have God's express Word that they are
predestined to glory, Romans 8:30; Acts 13:48.

5. Not only the apostles but also the seventy disciples are commanded in Luke
10:20 to rejoice that their names were inscribed in the book of life.
Therefore, they knew and were bound to know that they were elected to glory.

6. Following the example of Paul and the believers, we are bound to give all
thanks and glory for our election to God, Ephesians 1:2–8; 1 Peter 1:2–4. But
no one is bound to give thanks for a merely conjectural benefit freely

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 640 | PDF p. 689 | split word joined: gratuito]

given in Christ when he does not know whether it belongs to him and is bound
not to know it.
