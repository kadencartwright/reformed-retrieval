### QUESTION V

#### Who was Ahasuerus, Esther’s husband, and to what period should Esther’s history be assigned?

Answer. There is great controversy on this matter among chronologists and
interpreters of Scripture, ancient and modern, Protestant and Roman Catholic.
Even after much reading, we do not promise to resolve it to the full
satisfaction either of ourselves or of others.

> Marginalia, printed p. 154: *The question’s difficulty*.

Some would have this Ahasuerus be Astyages the Mede, mentioned by Daniel in
chapter 9 and father of Darius the Mede; others Darius the Mede himself;
others Cambyses, Cyrus’s son; many Darius Hystaspes; more Xerxes; most
Artaxerxes Longimanus; a good many Artaxerxes Mnemon; and some Ochus. What has
created so great a variety of opinions is the profound silence about this
history among all other writers, sacred and profane alike. It is much easier
here to refute the claims advanced by others than to confirm our own.

The first three opinions may be disposed of easily. Astyages, Cyrus’s
grandfather, could not have been Esther’s Ahasuerus, for before Cyrus had
subdued Babylon and many other provinces of Asia by his arms, the Medes were
far from possessing dominion over 127 provinces, the number nevertheless
reckoned in Ahasuerus’s empire in Esther 1:1.

Nor could Darius the Mede have been Ahasuerus. Besides being plainly
distinguished from Ahasuerus as from his father in Daniel 9, before Babylon
was occupied he possessed few provinces, and after Babylon was occupied he
continued in power for only a very few years. But the third, seventh, and
twelfth years of our Ahasuerus, lord of 127 provinces, are mentioned in the
book of Esther.

Cambyses likewise did not reach twelve years in his rule, and his disposition
toward the Jews was always extremely hostile.

> Marginalia, printed p. 154: *Ahasuerus was not Astyages, Darius the Mede,
> or Cambyses*.

The last two opinions—one concerning Artaxerxes Mnemon, which Eusebius,
Jerome, Bede, and, among more recent writers, Torniellus and others defend;
the other concerning Ochus, which Capellus defends—are altogether
implausible. First, nothing resembling this history is read in the lives of
those kings. Second, according to the judgment of Josephus and most learned
men, canonical Scripture closed in the days of Ezra and Artaxerxes
Longimanus, so the book of Esther must precede that period. Further, assigning
Ahasuerus to such late centuries advances Mordecai and his ancestors to ages
unusual in those times. For even if we grant that not Mordecai himself but
his great-grandfather Kish was carried to Babylon with Jeconiah, Capellus
nevertheless admits on page 368 that Kish must have been carried away at two
years old and must not have fathered Shimei before his eightieth year; that
Shimei likewise must not have fathered Jair before his eightieth; that Jair
must have been no less than eighty when Mordecai was born; and that Abihail
likewise fathered Esther at eighty. It is exceedingly hard to assume all
these things gratuitously and without any authority.

> Marginalia, printed p. 154: *Nor Artaxerxes Mnemon or Darius Ochus*.

More writers embrace the three middle opinions. Josephus, Petavius, Salianus,
Henricus Philippi, Bellarmine, and others stand for Artaxerxes Longimanus. The
arguments they advance seem so weak to us that we do not think we should
linger over their refutation; against them we propose this single argument.
If Mordecai was carried to Babylon with Jeconiah, then Esther could not have
been Artaxerxes’s wife, for Esther was Mordecai’s first cousin and not much
younger than he. But grant that she was sixty or even a hundred whole years
younger: even then, in Ahasuerus’s seventh year she must have been not a
maiden in the bloom of youth but an old woman over a hundred, or at least
sixty. For Mordecai must then have exceeded 160 years, since many years
elapsed between Jeconiah’s deportation and Artaxerxes. Now, that Mordecai was
carried away with Jeconiah is apparent from Esther 2:5–6. When they try to
evade this passage with the interpretation that the deportation to Babylon
should be referred not to Mordecai but to his great-grandfather Kish, I reply
with three points. First, in the text a *soph pasuq*, a full stop and the end
of a verse, follows Kish. Consequently, the action mentioned in the next
verse is not referred to Kish, although he immediately precedes in the final
clause of the preceding verse, but to Mordecai, the principal substantive at
the beginning of that verse, to whom all the actions and experiences
mentioned in that place are applied. Second, if the deportation to Babylon is
applied not to Mordecai but to Kish, then the immediately connected action of
rearing Esther must be referred to him as well; but this is plainly false.
Third, Mordecai returned from Babylon to Jerusalem with Zerubbabel in Ezra
2:2. Therefore Mordecai himself, not his great-grandfather, was carried from
Jerusalem to Babylon. Nothing can be answered to this premise except that
there were several Mordecais of the same name, and that the Mordecai named by
Ezra was different from the Mordecai who reared Esther, since the latter did
not return to Jerusalem but remained in Assyria with Esther. I reply that we
should not invent multiple persons of the same

name without necessity: Mordecai could easily have gone back to Assyria after
his return to Judea with Zerubbabel.

The seventh opinion, concerning Xerxes, belongs to Scaliger, whom authors
very dear to us follow: Junius, Calvisius, Diodati, the English annotators in
both editions, Howes, and others. Yet we are driven away from it, chiefly by
Scaliger’s excessive confidence amid what seems to us the great weakness of
his arguments. He declares everyone who disagrees with him here to be men of
no intelligence—malicious, blockish, asinine, and addled. Surely someone who
wishes to persuade should mix gentle words with the strongest arguments.
Petavius affirms that Scaliger’s arguments are not only false but also foul,
absurd, stupid, and self-contradictory. This kind of abuse is peculiar to the
Jesuits; it was less becoming in Petavius here, where he offers not a single
argument against Scaliger which he did not borrow from Capellus, though he
entirely conceals the fact. In any case, Scaliger’s arguments seem by no means
compelling.

> Marginalia, printed p. 155: *Nor Xerxes, as Scaliger maintains*.

His first argument is this: the name Xerxes is the same as Ahasuerus. Answer.
The proof of this assertion supplies good grounds for denying it, for
Scaliger assumes that *Oxyares* is the same word as *Ahasuerus*. Grant that in
deriving one word from another, especially in different languages, great
variation in letters can be admitted. Nevertheless, the principal root
letters are usually retained, and new root letters are not usually added.
Grant that *heth* and *shin* are not badly changed into the Greek *zeta*,
though that is difficult enough. But why then is the root letter *aleph* at
the beginning changed into *omicron*, and the root letter *vav* at the end
changed into *eta*? Then why in the name *Xerxes* is the root letter *aleph*
entirely thrown away and a new second *zeta* added at the end? And if, as
Scaliger maintains, the reading should be *Xestes*, not *Xerxes*, another
root letter, *r*, would then be thrown away, so that Scaliger’s derivation of
*Xestes* from *Ahasueros* seems truly most tortured and deserves to be
rejected. Moreover, Scaliger derives the Persian *Ahash-shesta*, from which
he would derive Xerxes, from altogether different simple elements than those
found in *Ahasueros*. Both indeed contain the word *Ahash*, but in one there
is the conjunction *vav* and the word *resh*, leader or head, and in the
other the word *shasta*, warrior or soldier. All these are different.

Scaliger’s second argument is this: Xerxes’s wife is called Amestris by all
the Greek authors; if the first element of the compound, *Am*, is removed,
*Esther* will remain in all its root letters.

Answer. Scaliger acknowledges in this very subject how weak an argument from
names is when the facts differ. He considers ridiculous the authors who prove
Ahasuerus to have been Artaxerxes Mnemon because his beloved wife bore the
name Stateira—in which Esther’s root letters are read more clearly than in
*Amestris*—because the history differs. No less wrongly does he laugh at
those who would have Darius Nothus be Ahasuerus because his wife was named
Atossa, which is the same name as Hadassah, the name Esther bore while she
still lived in private. All the root letters in the two names are the same,
except that *d* is changed to *t*. Yet a change of this kind is legitimate
and extremely frequent in derivations in every language, since a letter
formed by one organ is changed into another formed by the same organ; and
both *t* and *d* are dental. Nevertheless, Scaliger considers this argument
worthless because the facts correspond. Now, however great the similarity
between Esther and Amestris in name, there is as much and far more—not merely
dissimilarity, but contradiction—in fact, if any trust is to be placed in
the ancient historians.

Amestris was the daughter of Otanes, a most noble Persian. Xerxes had married
her long before his reign and had fathered Darius, Artaxerxes, and other sons
by her. She was a pagan and the worst and cruelest of women. No one ever
affirmed any of these things about Esther. Ahasuerus married Esther in
complete peace at Susa in the seventh year of his reign; but in his seventh
and even his eighth year Xerxes was entangled in a most terrible war in
Greece and Ionia. Indeed, at that very time he almost engulfed himself and
his entire family in tragic disasters through his incestuous loves, and he
remained far from any new marriage to the end of his life. Nothing more is
needed against Scaliger.

There remains the eighth opinion, concerning Darius Hystaspes. It belongs to
Funccius, Broughton, Ussher, and others, and we follow it. To confirm this
opinion, it is enough that all the others have been overthrown, for it must be
laid down as an immovable foundation that Esther’s history is true and
canonical. It possesses all the marks of the canonical books—into which we
cannot now digress. We must indeed be especially wary of the grave error of
Melito, Athanasius, and Nazianzen, who strip the book of Esther of all
canonical authority. Therefore, once the truth of the history is assumed,
since Esther married some king of the Persians, but certainly married none of
those named—as we have clearly proved—it necessarily follows that the man who
married Esther was Darius Hystaspes. His kindness to the Jews, residence at
Susa, long rule, plurality of wives, and other circumstances commend this
belief. The objections made here are easier to refute than those brought
against the earlier opinions. Whether Esther was Atossa, Artystone,
