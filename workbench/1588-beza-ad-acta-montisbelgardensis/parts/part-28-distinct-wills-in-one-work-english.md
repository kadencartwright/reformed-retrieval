## Part XXVIII: Distinct Wills in One Work

As for the other examples we produced shortly before, if God’s holy work is distinguished, as it ought to be, from the work of evil agents who move themselves evilly, the matter itself will show that by acting thus God exercised some of his people for their good, instructed others toward salvation by chastising them, and brought evil people—enslaved to Satan and their lusts and rendered increasingly inexcusable—to a just destruction. Since this is so, let Andreae consider by what right he trumpets against me nothing but impieties and blasphemies in those remarkable observations of his. I certainly do not regret thinking entirely differently from him, since truth itself demands it. Augustine, so often cited by me, testifies to this truth in chapter 101 of the *Enchiridion to Lawrence*: “Sometimes with a good will a person wills something God does not will, although God wills with a will far better and more certain—for example, if a good son wills his father to live when God with a good will wills him to die. Conversely, a person may with an evil will will what God wills to be—

—done, as when an evil son wills his father to die and God also wills it. The former wills what God does not will; the latter wills what God also wills. Yet the former’s piety, although he wills something different, agrees more with God’s good will than the latter’s impiety, although he wills the same thing. It matters greatly what it is fitting for a human being to will, what it is fitting for God to will, and to what end each directs his will, so that it may be approved or disapproved. For God fulfills some of his wills, certainly good ones, through the evil wills of evil human beings. Thus Christ was killed through malicious Jews by the Father’s good will. This was so great a good that the apostle Peter, when he did not will it to occur, was called Satan by the one who willed to be killed. How good appeared the wills of the godly believers who did not will the apostle Paul to go to Jerusalem lest he suffer there the evils which the prophet Agabus predicted! Yet God willed him to suffer these things for proclaiming Christ’s faith, training a martyr of Christ. He fulfilled this good will not through the Christians’ good wills but through the Jews’ evil wills. Those who did not will what God willed belonged more to him than those through whom, willingly, what God willed was done, because they did the same thing, but God did it well through them while they did it with an evil will. Whatever the wills of angels or humans, good or evil—whether willing what God wills or something different from God—the will of almighty God is always unconquered. It can never be evil, because even when it inflicts evils it is just, and precisely because it is just it is not evil.”

Again, in Letter 48 to Vincent: “When the Father delivered the Son, the Lord his own body, and Judas the Lord, why in this delivering is God just and the human guilty, unless because although they did one thing, the cause by which they did it was not one?” Again: “That evil people sin belongs to them; that by sinning they accomplish this or that arises from God’s power, dividing the darkness as he saw fit.” These are Augustine’s words. With them I wished to conclude this very important refutation and answer the futile sophistry of some who, ignorant of this distinction, falsely conclude from our doctrine on these matters that God consents to evildoers and that those whom he nevertheless abhors as sinners obey God. Agreement or opposition must be assessed not simply from what someone wills or does not will, but from the intention of the one willing or refusing.
