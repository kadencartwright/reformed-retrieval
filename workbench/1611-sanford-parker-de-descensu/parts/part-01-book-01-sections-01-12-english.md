1. It is entirely certain that sacred writers call the place to which Christ
the Lord descended in death either *Sheol* or *Hades*. Yet people have long
asked in vain what these words denote. We have noticed why: while the subject
was still obscure, other men's disputations mixed and confused literal
meanings with figurative ones. Since a somewhat clearer truth has dawned on
us, we shall proceed by first considering the proper meaning of these names and
then their extended application. Their proper meaning must be sought in the
inner recesses of the language from which the words arose; the discipline that
studies the things to which they are transferred will show how far their
application extends. In this question, therefore, judgment belongs only to the
sacred language and sacred literature. Other literatures or languages may give
evidence, but they will have no deciding vote.

Most people—or, so far as I know, everyone—are now persuaded that the origins
of the terms before us lie hidden in several different languages. That
persuasion must be removed, not merely weakened. Contrary to the common belief,
*Sheol* does not come from a Hebrew source and *Hades* from a Greek one, as if
the first were older and the second more recent. Both come from the first
language and are equally ancient. Indeed, if we seek proofs of antiquity, the
one commonly thought later

[1611 Book I, printed p. 2 | Google Books PA2]

precedes the other. Even if we grant that *Sheol* existed earlier, it is first
read in Israel's mourning; *Hades* presents itself as contemporary with Adam
and takes us back to the world's beginning. God placed man in Paradise and
added a law of abstinence: if man broke it, he would surely die. The law was
broken, the guilty man condemned, and judgment returned—not in the exact words
that the sinner would meet death, but, and this is the chief point of our
teaching, that Man would return to the Ground. In that ground we locate the
place of the dead, and in that return the condition of death. From one of these,
or rather from both, we derive not only *Sheol* and *Hades*, but *Mahad*,
*Amenthe*, *Areimanius*, and whatever else ancient sages taught about the lower
regions.

[Marginalia: Gen. 3.]

2. The nations whose mysteries consecrated these words may through
superstitious error have invented some other abode or condition for the dead.
Yet it is clear that the fathers and founders of those rites had felt a faint
breeze of primal theology and philosophized, in both things and words, upon the
story we have drawn from Moses. It mattered greatly to humankind that the
memory of that sentence should not merely be preserved but spread abroad. It
was not enough for notions of ancient events to be impressed on minds; the
names also had to be disseminated into languages—not all languages, perhaps,
but the most widely diffused, Arabic and Greek. The Arabs have *מהד*, *mahad*,
by which they mean the place or condition of the dead, although they do not
adequately explain what or where it is. The Greeks likewise have *ton Haden* in
precisely the same sense, whether of condition or place; on all else they are
no more certain than the Arabs. But the Arabs' *Mahad* and the Greeks' *Hades*,
if one considers the word, are *Adamah*; if one considers the thing, they are
Adam's *adamah*, the Ground—that Ground to which merciful Justice pronounced
that the transgressor would one day return. The letters themselves will be the
witnesses to the word, and our eyes the judges.

[1611 Book I, printed p. 3 | Google Books PA3]

There are four letters in each. What settles the whole matter is that they are
the same four: א, ד, מ, ה. Place the third before the first and the fourth
before the second, and *Mahad* emerges; prefix the last to the first, and
*Hadam* is formed. Thus Arabs and Greeks each see in one scriptural word
something they recognize. The Arabs, being nearer the Hebrew language, see the
complete and unmistakable *Mahad*. The Greeks, according to the form of their
dialect, which admits no case ending in *m*, very lightly inflect *Hadam* as
*Haden*.

3. There seems no need even to warn the Arabs to accept this derivation. They
will embrace the demonstrated, regular metathesis of the letters, since among
them it is a settled and common rule of interpretation that a native word can
pass into a foreign one when the bond between its letters is loosened. Hence
they readily allow *machad* to be referred to *chamad*, *phachets* to
*chaphets*, *alam* to *amal*, *gadal* to *dalag*, and *dachar* to *chadar*.
Only a person destitute of both sense and shame would seek their sources
elsewhere when here he sees letters answering letters and meanings answering
meanings. All these terms have among the Hebrews the same meanings that they
possess among the Arabs. No one should suppose that this happens only rarely,
or only in the examples shown here. There are many—indeed infinitely many—of
the same kind. Grammarians chiefly for this reason hold that Arabic is nothing
other than an image of Hebrew, since the Arabs took so many words from the
Hebrews and made them their own by transposing the letters. We have therefore
easily persuaded the Ishmaelites to derive *Mahad* from *Adamah*. A denser and
more laborious dispute about *Hades* remains with the descendants of Javan.

[Marginalia: Wakefeld, Cambridge oration.]

4. Yet unless you choose to evade the matter, what can Ionia oppose when such
similar things have such corresponding consequences?

[1611 Book I, printed p. 4 | Google Books PA4]

Perhaps you will plead the nature of your language, reject Hebrew religion in
your mysteries, and produce your own witnesses who assert that *Hades* was born
at home. But what weight do these arguments have? They are so light—indeed,
nothing at all in the contrary scale—that by their own motion and weight *ho
Hades* is transferred straight to *Adamah*. First, what in our derivation is
foreign to Greek usage? Perhaps that one syllable is inflected or one letter
has changed position. I would concede the objection, had it not been customary
since Japheth to give Hebrew words Greek inflections. The Septuagint
translators inflected all Hebrew terms in this fashion, as Jerome testifies and
examples from every class of noun, not only proper names, demonstrate. From
*Mum* comes *Momos*, from *Toch* *Teichos*, and from *Cinnur* *Kinura*, to
avoid a longer catalogue. People ignorant of these languages derive them
elsewhere, but the learned easily see their origin. Moreover this whole
practice—so supposedly strange at Athens—was in ancient times not only a
necessity but a pleasure. Josephus says that the Greeks inflected the final
syllables of Hebrew names and altered them for the readers' delight. Yet are we
still reluctant, out of religious scruple about a syllable, to restore *Hades*
to antiquity? Nor should the migration of a letter detain anyone, especially
the letter at issue. The Hebrew *he* scarcely maintains among the Greeks even
the appearance of aspiration, much less the identity of a letter. Thus it
often disappears when Hebrew is rendered in Greek; sometimes it is placed
where it can stand rather than in its own place. So one man said that *Adam*
means red earth in Syriac, when אֲדָמָה carries that meaning; another, unable
to pronounce *Abraham* in the proper order of letters, moved the aspiration
and said *Habraam*. Examples of both forms survive, though the latter have not
been sufficiently noticed. We owe that largely to copyists, whose frequent
sloth and ignorance are such

[Marginalia: Jerome on Hos. 5 and to Philemon; 2 Reg. 1.16; Josephus, *Ant.*
1.12; Theodoret on Gen. 60; Suidas, s.v. Adam; Epiphanius 1.3, Haer. 38.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 5 | Google Books PA5]

that when they read words which contrary to common usage require a rough
breathing, they add no breathing mark at the very place where they read them.
If Hebrew words do not shrink either from changing cases or moving breathings
when they pass into Greek, linguistic principles can never prevent the
conclusion for which we contend: *Hades* is *Adamah*.

5. We are told next that *Hades* is counted among the Greek gods, so it is
unlikely that the Greeks took the name from the Hebrew nation, whose divine
power they deny. I accept and assume the statement, since in this whole matter
nothing truer could be said that at first offers less probability. All the
sacred rites were taken from the Hebrews; most names of the gods were formed
from Hebrew; and theologians knew the passage in which Moses declares *Adamah*
accursed. Who would readily believe it? Yet these facts stand, and since they
do, they leave no one but the obstinate in doubt that *Hades* arose from
*Adamah*. I wish some little Greek would teach me how he calls in his language
what Latin names *sacrum*. He will answer *hieron*, I suppose. From what source
does the name come? From *Hie*, of course; that is the grammarians' tradition.
But what *Hie* means your grammarians neither transmit nor understand. They
teach only that it is a sacred cry, used by the ancients when they prayed for a
deity's favor. Athenaeus, to omit others, says: “The ancients, when they wished
the divine power to become gracious, cried *Hie, Hie*.” The present reading
*ie, ie* is corrupt: Suidas, and before him the commentator on Aristophanes,
says that the word in a hymn must be aspirated. Eustathius likewise cites this
very passage from the *Deipnosophists* and reads *Hie*. The passage itself
shows that this was the written form by deriving *Hieron* from it. If temples,
priests, sacrifices, and all sacred things are named from this one little word—

[Marginalia: Athenaeus, *Deipnosophists* 8; Suidas, s.v. Hie.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 6 | Google Books PA6]

for from *to Hieron* come *hiereus*, *hiereion*, and the whole family of
terms—there can be no doubt that the ancients understood in it some notable
and eminent nature and power. Unless we take refuge in Hebrew, it can never be
found. Does not the Greeks' own ceremony compel us to do so? Their sacred hymn
is called a paean, an honor common to nearly all the gods. The paean consists
chiefly in the cry *Hie, Hie*, whose preliminary cry they make *Eleleu*, itself
a sacred word whose meaning has not been discovered. Put *Hie* after *Eleleu*,
as the rule of the paean requires, and *Eleleu Hie* gives the Hebrews' very
own *Hallelu Iah*. Among the Hebrews sacred songs are distinguished from
ordinary psalms solely by having *Hallelu Iah* before or after them, just as
the Greek paean is distinguished from other hymns by this cry. Without it the
song ceased to be a paean, as we have seen judged somewhere of a work by
Aristotle. This is clear in itself and illuminated by the Oschophoria, at which,
according to Plutarch, they cried *Eleleu Iou Iou*. The difference between
*Ie* and *Iou* is precisely that between *Iah* and *Iehovah*. That the latter
was sometimes contracted to this sound is supported, if Clement, most
grammatical of theologians, is not enough, by the pronunciation *Iaou* for the
Tetragrammaton, and at least by the Latin *Iu*, or rather *Iou*. Because its
foreign pronunciation displeased some, it was inflected into *Iovis*; others
softened it by adding “Father,” so that as they said *Marspiter* and *Diespiter*
they also said *Iupiter*. Indeed, ancient comedy shows that this father “of men
and gods” was formerly addressed as *Hie*. When Trygaeus orders, “Go now and
call Jove for me,” Mercury replies, “*Hie, Hie, Hie*.” What, I ask, is this?
The scholiast says it is an adverb of contempt. Granted—but how cold, tasteless,
and un-Attic,

[Marginalia: Jerome on Eph. 3, p. 187; Athenaeus; Plutarch; Clement;
Aristophanes.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 7 | Google Books PA7]

if it contains nothing beyond a hiss or whistle! The joke instead rests wholly
on ambiguity: the mocker of religion ridicules the ancient form of prayer as
if Jove were solemnly mocked with the very formula by which he was invoked.
Finally, whether the cry is *eleleu iou*, *eleleu hie*, or, as it apparently
should be written elsewhere, *alalai hie*, words later derived from it—
*ololuzein, elelizein, alalazein*, all equal in meaning—prove that the entire
form came from Hebrew worship. What meaning? Not the surviving one of grief or
shouting that grammarians learn from recent interpreters, but the religious
sense of *oluzai*, from the Hebrew root: celebrating divine praises and
testifying to the joyful gratitude of those who had received some remarkable
blessing from God. Thus either we must separate the festivals of branches and
wine, where this *ololugmos* was common, from Jewish festivals—which, if we
listen to Plutarch, neither should nor can be done—or admit that in common
worship the Greeks made *eleleu* from *Hallelu* and *Hie* from *Iah*, with the
aspiration leading their procession. Since one of the gods was generally
invoked by these cries, and since without explaining the names one cannot even
discern which god it was, we end this discussion of the origin of sacred rites.
We will treat it more fully in our second discussion, on the gods' names.

[Marginalia: On praying to and praising God; *El* for God; Plutarch on Theseus;
Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*.]

6. Let us next see when these names were brought into Greece, whence they came,
and under what rule they were consecrated. Herodotus, father of history, says
that for a long time the Hellenic peoples worshiped gods without proper names,
calling them by the common word *theous*, as though *thentas*, because they
governed in due measure the things and regions under their power, setting them
in proper order. When the Pelasgians at last

[Marginalia: Herodotus, *Euterpe*, p. 69.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 8 | Google Books PA8]

flourished, he says Greece heard the gods' names, which the Greeks first
received from foreigners, chiefly Egyptians, after an oracle confirmed the
act. Dionysus's name and rites came much later, not before Cadmus arrived in
Boeotia. Reduced to Hebrew chronology, this falls in Deborah's rule, 120 years
after Joshua's death, when she began judging her nation. This one testimony
establishes two points of great importance: the gods' names are not Greek but
must be traced to the Egyptians or certainly the Phoenicians; and Dionysian
rites were absent from Greek temples while Moses' discipline was famous in the
East and the name of Jehovah renowned throughout the world by Israel's
victories. It remains to discuss the deification of foreign names, which the
ancients did in more than one way. Sometimes they took a thing from foreign
literature but left its word behind; sometimes they transferred both word and
thing into religious mysteries. The latter happened in two ways: a foreign
word was enrolled among the gods in its proper letter order, so far as the
nature of languages allowed; or the letters were relocated and changed to make
a new name from the old, after which it was initiated as a deity under the
rules of the new language. Thus every divine title Greece borrowed from
foreigners falls into three classes. Some arise solely from explanation of the
thing's nature: *Apollon*, or if one prefers the sacred form *Apolluon*, from
*Abaddon*. Some arise from pronouncing the name: *Iapetos* from *Iapheth*.
Finally, some add an exchange of letters: *Adamah* first became *Hadam* and
then *Haden*, as plainly as daylight. To understand this last class better, one
must know of an ancient Hebrew discipline received from the fathers,

[1611 Book I, printed p. 9 | Google Books PA9]

by which people capable of so great a task were instructed in divine and human
knowledge through *Mascroth* and *Shemoth* in various ways. First place among
these belonged to anagramming, which we so greatly commend. The name *Iehovah*
was considered especially open to this treatment, with the singular property
that however it is rewritten it never lacks meaning, yet never means anything
but the one same reality. It is incredible how zealously Greek sages cultivated
this teaching, wherever they received it. They labored not only to express
*Iehovah* in Greek, pouring the name in various rewritten forms into their own
letters, but made other Greek and foreign names on its model, which they judged
worthy of a place in their theology. Whoever receives examples from us should
not yet demand testimonies: we think no one before our time published this
theology of letter transposition. Antiquity abounds in examples, but most
cannot be noticed unless the nature at least of the letters composing *Iehovah*
is first understood. They are *He, Vau,* and *Iod*, and each has a far wider
power than the Greek language permits its letters. *Iod* represents *iota,
eta,* and *epsilon*. Consonantal *Vau* is expressed by *beta*; otherwise it
becomes *ypsilon, omicron,* or *ou* without distinction. *He* is sometimes
rendered by *epsilon*, which corresponds in place and number, but more often at
a word's beginning serves as aspiration; elsewhere it rests in a vowel or
passes into a kindred letter. Its medial relatives are *chi*, and its final
relatives *theta, tau,* and *sigma*. Since many examples follow, we need not
offer them here.

[1611 Book I, printed p. 10 | Google Books PA10]

7. With these principles established, we may spread the sails of our discourse
and enter the sea of Hebrew antiquity so that the truth of our claims may
shine. Ten names of God immediately meet us like islands inhabited by colonies
of Greek deities. First appear *El, Elohim,* and *Elohe*, separated by so narrow
a strait that *Helios* alone occupies all three. Whence comes *Helios*? The
learned admit it is not Greek. Some call it Punic and some Assyrian; either
concedes our point. Herodotus meant Hebrews when he said Phoenicians, whose
language the Poeni used and whose own name was corrupted from Phoeni. Tacitus
also says the Jews had Assyrian origins and inhabited Hebrew lands. Thus those
who assign *Helios* to Assyrians or Phoenicians alike admit a Hebrew name.
Servius demonstrates the point by deriving the god *Helios* from the language
in which *Hel* means God. Everyone knows that Scripture often has *Hael* for
God, and even our theologians admit that the Greeks once pronounced it *Hel*.
If asked to assign the result to one of our classes, nothing is easier than to
make *Helion* from *Elohim Heliom* by the third rule. This being so, the
ancients may more boldly define the multitude of gods by the power of the one
Sun, as they did even in darkness, while our theologians will be less surprised
that this god took his name from prophetic writings, since he even appears
furnished from them with a chariot. Chrysostom, a weighty authority, says the
Greeks twisted Elijah's fiery chariot into a fable, and the Saracens' ridiculous
belief confirms it. Unless they still thought Elijah's horses identical with
the sun's, I cannot see why Zigabenus, refuting them, would insist that those
by which he was carried to heaven were angels, not horses. Next after *El*,

[Marginalia: Servius on *Aen.* 1.646; Theodoret, *Haer.* 5, final chapter;
Jerome on Jer. 25; Nicetas, *Orthodox Faith* 2; Chrysostom; Euthymius Zigabenus.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 11 | Google Books PA11]

*Heliom* seems nearest to *Elohim*. If one does not hear *Helion* in its
pronunciation, one cannot fail to recognize it in the interpretation
*Hyperion*. Later writers explained this as *hypsiston*; that has some truth,
but not the whole force. *Heliom* implies not mere superiority but a power of
overcoming, which no altitude expresses unless it also shades into the nature
of *Hyperion*.

8. Next comes *Shaddai*, a name abounding in strength and resources. When *Dis*
once fitted it to himself by proper metathesis, he readily gained rule over the
other gods. The imperial rank now held by *Zeus pantocrator* is said initially
to have belonged to *Dis*. Tradition says he was deposed because he used in
his title a syllable more familiar than royal dignity allowed. It flitted
through worshipers' mouths in so low and indistinct a sound that the king of
gods could not be distinguished from the adverb *dis*. He fell from the throne
and *Zeus* succeeded, but on condition that *dios, dii,* and *dia*, left free
by the former ruler, be admitted by the new prince as equal partners in his
realm. The fact itself shows this; the Greeks no longer say where *Dis*
withdrew. I know that Saturn sailed to Latium, where he retained not only the
old name but also its meaning, from abundance and plenty. He was worshiped as
*Zeus*, that is, God; for *Theos, Deus,* and *Zeus* once had no distinction,
since each denoted deity in general, not a particular god. Thus the Greeks got
*Theos* from the first and Latin got *Deus* from the second; the third, after
already decorating three hundred gods as an epithet, finally began to adhere
preeminently to whatever lives, flourishes, and feels, the eternal power of
gods and men.

[Marginalia: Eustathius on *Odyssey* alpha, p. 15 ff., p. 116.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 12 | Google Books PA12]

What need I say of *Ehich* or *Adonai*, except that it is now certain, and will
soon be plain, that *Heie* is found in one and *Adonin* in the other? Meanwhile
we should not conceal that the epithet of Bacchus or Apollo which we cited is
transmitted in books differently from our reading. We may follow Aristarchus,
who in Homer's line “as you may be, Phoebus,” decided against Rhodianus and
Crates that one should read *Heie*, not *Eie*.

9. That *Adoni* descends from *Adonai* might rest on the evidence of the
senses, but it will be demonstrated more fully in the Tetragrammaton, to which
we now turn. Among the Hebrews *Adonai* has long served in its place. For many
centuries Jews have read written *Iehovah* as *Adonai*, so that even they do not
know the origin or reason for the practice. Some make God its author and read
what we translate “This is my name *leolam*, forever,” as “to be concealed.”
They also force into service the Psalm, “Silence is praise to you, God, in
Zion,” as though the prophet prohibited pronouncing the secret name even in
divine praise. Others think teachers introduced the custom, either voluntarily
to prevent profane use of the holy name, or necessarily because after Simeon
the Just no one could pronounce it correctly. Many say pronouncing the exposed
name belonged to a restricted class—the high priest, not the people. Others
add times and places: even the priest might speak it only in blessings and
only in the sanctuary. Whatever the case, I do not believe the saying ascribed
to Abba Saul: “Whoever pronounces the four-letter name as its letters sound

[Marginalia: Exod. 3; Ps. 64; *Sanhedrin*, Helec.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 13 | Google Books PA13]

will have no portion in the coming age.” The supposed privilege of priest and
sanctuary is also false, unless Pharaoh was a priest and spoke in the sanctuary
when he answered, “I do not know Jehovah.” Our writers offer different reasons
why the name is called *arreton, aphaston,* and *anekphoneton*—unspeakable,
unutterable, and unpronounceable. One says this concerns not the word's sound
but the mysteries attached to it, which exceed all power of expression. This
is plainly false. Much more probable is that Hebrews ceased sounding the name
only when they realized foreign nations had transferred it to idols. The fact
is this: whatever law or custom forbade speaking *Iehovah* remained within
Hebrew boundaries and never bound other nations. Josephus deserves pardon for
saying that, as a Hebrew, he was forbidden to disclose the name's
pronunciation. But who can pardon Jerome for asserting without reason, “The
four-letter name of God can be written but not spoken”? Besides doing injury
to nature, by making the range of languages exceed the power of letters, he
also detracts from his own writings and learning, something he does not usually
do rashly. He knew that the Maker of the world whom Paul preached at the
Areopagus, inscribed “To the gods of Asia, Europe, and Africa, to the unknown
and foreign God,” was none other than the one whose name was written with four
Hebrew letters. How did he not see from Greek theology that though this God's
identity remained disputed, his name was not unknown to the ancients?

[Marginalia: Exod. 5.2; Avenarius, lexicon; Josephus, *Ant.* 2.5; Jerome on
Ezek. 16; Acts 17.23; Oecumenius there.]

10. For this is what Apollo of Claros reportedly answered when asked which god
was called *Iaos*:

[Marginalia: Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 1.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 14 | Google Books PA14]

“Say that Iao is the supreme deity of all.
In spring he is truly Jove, in summer Helius,
Dis when winter rages, and gentle Iao in autumn.”

Interpreters of oracles decided that these verses signify the sun. Why would
they not? They knew no solar name but *Helion*; the long passage of time had
erased *Hieron*, the name used for him when gods approached; and the mention of
the seasons, which continually accompany the sun's journeys, seemed to leave no
room for doubt. Yet the crafty Jovian pointed through all these twists to the
royal road of truth—a road he knew would never be entered by those who judged
*Ien* worthy of worship “from the one who sent it.” He plainly taught our very
point: *Aides, Helios,* and *Iao* are the same—that is, *Shaddai, Elohim,* and
*Iehovah*, the one supreme God to whom prayer is fitting in every season. I
could cite Apollo or his interpreter in support, but prefer Orpheus:

“Dis, Jove, Helius, and Dionysus are one and the same.
God is one everywhere; why express one with many names?”

Why, indeed, have I cited so many oracles? To show that the ancients knew the
Tetragrammaton not merely by sound but much more by its homeland and origin:
they both pronounced *Iao* and interpreted the one Hebrew name from Hebrew
sources. If anyone rejects a daemon's confession—though I maintain it neither
dared nor could lie about *Iehovah*—accept human testimony, not that of one
among many but of a most celebrated writer scarcely equaled in Greek antiquity:

[Marginalia: Cornelius Labeo; Macrobius, *Sat.* 1.18; Diodorus Siculus 3,
p. 133; Julian against the Christians; Orpheus.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 15 | Google Books PA15]

Diodorus Siculus. He says that among the Jews Moses ascribed the laws to the
God called *Iao*. Thus the one Supreme in the oracle is the God of Moses in
history, equally well known by place and name and equally incomprehensible in
power and goodness. Diodorus did not dream this through ignorance of *Iehova*,
as Feuardent and after him Pamelius suppose. They themselves sleep deeply and
fail to notice that even our most learned Greek and Latin writers pronounce
*Iehovah* as *Iao*.

[Marginalia: Diodorus, *Library* 1, p. 59; Feuardent on Irenaeus; Pamelius on
Tertullian.]

11. The form occurs in Irenaeus, Tertullian, Origen, Eusebius, Epiphanius, and
Theodoret, every one of whom says it is found in Scripture. Eusebius expressly
asserts that *Iao*, like *El* and *Saddai*, is God's name. Will anyone still
demand that I prove it is the Tetragrammaton? He shall have his demand, whoever
he is, provided he learns this: the name which Irenaeus and Epiphanius give as
the heretics' *Iao* is pronounced, by the same authors' rule, *Iaeth* or
*Iaoth*. Once this is grasped it becomes clear that in Greek the letter *He* is
sometimes separated with its marks retained and sometimes removed—as others
have observed, and as those who forged *Iao* from *Iehovah* evidently did—but
is also sometimes changed into an aspirated letter. Unable to pronounce
*Iaoh*, they unquestionably said *Iaeth* or *Iaoth*. Want examples? Ask what
form Greek gives *aluah*: you find *Eloth*. Prefer authorities? Here is
Procopius. More attentive to Greek habit than Jewish origin, he says the
ineffable name consists of four letters: Iod, Aleph, Fau, Theta. Since the last
has a double power, it adequately shows what EPIPHANIUS followed in the name
*Iehovah*,

[Marginalia: Eusebius 1.1, *Preparation* 14; *Against Celsus* 6;
*Demonstration* 10; *Heresies*, pp. 83, 88, 94; Nebrissensis 50.23; Exod. 6.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 16 | Google Books PA16]

rendering it *Iaoth*, while Irenaeus rendered it *Iaeth*. Those who derive
Irenaeus's *Iaeth* from a Babylonian root may themselves, I fear, be too
Babylonian. Nor can I grant that Tertullian received light from those who
elsewhere attached to him texts allegedly from divine Scripture in which *Iao*
appears. The cure for their obscurity which we now undertake would long ago
have been supplied much more fully by Origen, had he not suffered as badly from
copyists as they did from interpolators. In book six against Celsus, Origen
treats the same question as Irenaeus, draws the same divine names from
Scripture, and with the same answer drives away heretics who built different
gods from different Hebrew names. Irenaeus refutes Gnostics and Origen
Ophites; otherwise they closely resemble each other. The difference is that
Irenaeus has *Iaoth* and Origen *Iao*. Origen marks *Iao* by plain signs and an
illustrious description. Two passages bear on this. One calls the heretics'
second deity *Ia*; the other says that *Iao* is the one Hebrews call *Ia*. By
some misfortune *Ia*—to whom *Hallelu Iah* will be sung forever—has vanished
from both passages, swallowed by the following word in one and the preceding
in the other. The first now reads, *oiontai ton phthasanta epi ton iadein
legein Deutere Iao*. The translator renders, “They think that the one who comes
to Iadin must be told, ‘Second Iao.’” But what monster is “Iadin”? Separate the
ugly fusion and read *epi ton Ia dein legein*, with *dein* independently
meaning “must”: then one gets their intended “one must say to Ia, ‘Second
Iao.’” The other passage makes it clearer and, since it settles the whole
matter of *Iao*, deserves full quotation: “It must be known that those who
composed these things,

understanding neither magic nor divine Scripture, mixed together from magic

meaningless single word *Iadia*. If we believe Origen, then *Iao* comes from
divine Scripture, names the one God, and is what Hebrews pronounce *Iah*.
Theodoret easily supplies the remaining point, that it is the Tetragrammaton:
he says the four-letter name by which the Lord denied having been known to the
ancients is pronounced *Iabe* by Samaritans and *Ia* by Jews.

But why argue longer that *Ia* was converted from *Iehovah*, as though the
Greeks did not express the name in other ways according to the powers of their
letters? I do not yet mean anagrams; that subject comes shortly. The poets
continually sing it as one with *Iao*: one deity, and for many writers one name.
Here belong all who say that *Iove* consists of a doubled *Iah*, including
Isidore. Why will they not admit the poets' *Ieve*? By what right do they
recognize *Ievah* and *Iehovah*? Let them compare the number, order, and powers
of the letters. They will see not merely an outline of *Iehovah*, which would
satisfy me, but a far clearer and more lifelike picture than the modern
invention *Ihve*. If that is insufficient, add Philo of Byblos's report from
the Phoenicians' inner records that *Ierumbalus* was priest of the god *Iev*.

12. Its rewriting remains. The name appears in so many and such obvious forms,
even to those not searching, that while elsewhere we merely marked footprints
of Jewish learning, here we seem to have reached its very lair. The sacred
Tetractys, of which one should not speak without illumination, can assume
twelve persons: three beginning with Iod, three with Vau, and the remaining six
with doubled He. It did not descend among the Greeks in every form, so far as I
have noticed, but plainly underwent some transformation in each class. Who can
doubt that *Iahu, Iuha,* and *Huia* are sacred anagrams of the name, sounding in
Latin letters as *Iahbo, Vahbe,* and *Euie*, and identical with Greek *Iacche,
Bacche,* and *Evie*? As the Hebrew forms signify one *Iehovah*, so the Greek
forms signify one *Iao*. Their origin is not obscure: from *Iah* come *Iuh* and
*Iou*; from *Vihu, Iao*; from *Iuhi, Evoi*; and *Huis* from *Hui*. Fashioned by
the same art, they invoke the same God both among themselves and as the names
above. The surviving cries of Bacchants teach this, relics of a superstition
long destroyed. Would that more survived: proving the origins of Greek gods
would require far less labor. What remains, however, gives a specimen of the
rest. The names are cried either linked to one another or joined with other
words,

[1611 Book I, printed p. 19 (partial) | Google Books PA19]

which divine Scripture likewise consecrated for invoking the one God. Of the
first class are *Iou*, or *Io Bacche, evie bacche, evoi bacche*, modeled, I
think, on Moses, where *Iehovah* joins himself with *huih* to terrify Pharaoh.
The second includes forms mentioned above—*Hallelu-iah, eleleu Hie, eleleu ion,
ei en*. Since they answer to *haleluiah, halelu iuhh,* and *iah ahhad*, why not
boldly translate, “Praise Iah, praise Jehovah, Iah is one”? So too the taunts
hurled by one orator at another—*Evoi saboi, Huis attes, attes Huis*—need not a
Harpocration or Ulpian so much as a scholar of divine Scripture and Jewish
learning. Whence did the little Greeks get “Jehovah Sabaoth”? Whence “Jehovah,
you; you, Jehovah”? Merely write the cries in Hebrew and they teach that this
alone is their sense. Since we want our work to benefit not only experts in
Hebrew letters but beginners, and desire above all that no one hereafter miss
the meaning of things no one before has even suspected, it will be worth our
effort, while treating the rest briefly, to pursue each point here in greater
detail.
