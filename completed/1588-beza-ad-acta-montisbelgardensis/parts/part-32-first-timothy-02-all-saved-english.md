### Theodore Beza’s Antithesis

To the passage in 1 Timothy 2 we reply as both the apostle’s own purpose requires and all the orthodox fathers—especially Augustine in very many places—expressly demonstrate: “all human beings” means not every individual of each class but every class of individuals. For the apostle there discusses the various ranks of people whom the Lord calls to his church. It would be not only utterly absurd but profane to suppose that if God wills every individual to be saved, he cannot execute what he wills, or to suspend the effect of God’s will from human discretion.

### Andreae’s Observations

1. Paul’s words are open and manifest, commanding prayer for ALL human beings because God wills ALL HUMAN BEINGS to be saved. It is therefore absurd to exclude anyone from this universal promise.

2. Paul does not there discuss various ranks of people but all human beings, for whom prayer must be made, especially the magistrate, because he can greatly benefit or harm the church, to others’ salvation or destruction.

3. God did not will all human beings to be saved by an absolute will, for then all would certainly be saved. Who could resist his will? Rather, he wills them by a will restricted IN CHRIST, outside whom he wills to save no one. He offers Christ to all through the preaching of the gospel and the use of the sacraments. Whoever opposes him perishes not by God’s will but by his own impiety, against God’s will.

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to Andreae’s Observations

[Marginal note: To the replies.]

Before approaching this passage’s genuine explanation, I want the Christian reader to observe how futile is the argument drawn from the word “all” to establish this fictitious universality. If one chooses everywhere to interpret it as comprehending every individual of a class, what a multitude of utterly false absurdities will arise! “God will be ALL in ALL” on the last day, says the apostle in 1 Corinthians 15—therefore even in demons and every condemned person. “No,” you will say, “Paul there speaks only about the church gathered in heaven.” Then here too let us see what Paul discusses and not stretch the sentence farther. “I will draw ALL to myself,” says the Lord in John 12. Even the reprobate? Certainly not, since this statement by the same Christ cannot be universally understood: “No one comes to me unless the Father draws him,” spoken of those who at that time were so far from being drawn that they could not believe Christ as he spoke. “They will ALL know me, from greatest to least,” says the Lord in Jeremiah 31:34. But Christ restricts that totality to the elect: “No one knows the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son wills to reveal him” (Luke 10:22). “I became all things to—

—ALL,” says the apostle. Who dares apply this to all things or individuals without exception, any more than, “All things are lawful for me”? “From his fullness we ALL received,” says John. Does this not mean believers alone? It was said to Abraham in Genesis 18, “In you ALL nations will be blessed.” Does this mean every individual of every nation, since Ishmael was not heir even from Abraham’s own nation, and it is said of Esau and the Edomites, “I loved Jacob but hated Esau”? “God,” says the apostle in 1 Corinthians 12:6, “works all things in ALL.” Does he also work sins in the wicked, and every sin in every wicked person? “You will be hated by ALL,” says the Lord in Luke 21:17. Even the good? And Luke 6:26: “Woe to you when ALL human beings bless you”—namely those of the world, which Paul said he did not will to please.

How many such examples can and must be observed everywhere! We must therefore see what sort of universality these words concern. It is known that, by Hebrew idiom, this word is taken indefinitely no less often than universally: thus the Lord is said to have healed “every disease,” meaning every kind of disease, and the Pharisees to tithe “every herb,” meaning every kind. Everyone knows how often a part is understood synecdochically through the whole, and the whole for a part. Let us hear Augustine first testify, not only by words but by the surest testimony, that in this apostolic passage the word must be understood not of every individual of each class but every class of individuals. In chapter 103 of the *Enchiridion to Lawrence*: “It is said that he wills all human beings to be saved, not because there was no human being whom he did not will to be saved, since he refused to perform mighty miracles among those whom he says would have repented had he performed them; rather, we understand ‘all human beings’ as the whole human race distributed through every distinction: kings, private persons,” and so forth, which Augustine pursues at greater length. And shortly after: “The apostle had commanded prayer for all human beings and especially added for kings and those in authority, who could be thought to wander from faith’s humility through secular pride and arrogance”—or rather, who were enemies and persecutors of the gospel and outside its covenant, so that they seemed more worthy of prayers against them than for them—“and therefore added, ‘Who wills all human beings to be saved.’ The Lord also used this manner of speaking in the gospel when he said to the Pharisees, ‘You tithe every herb.’ The Pharisees did not tithe every herb belonging to themselves, others, and all foreigners throughout all lands. Therefore, just as ‘every herb’ here means every kind of herb, so ‘all human beings’ there can mean every kind of human being. It can be understood in any other way, provided we are not compelled to believe that almighty God willed something to occur which did not occur, since without ambiguity, if both in heaven and earth, as the truth sings, he did whatever he willed, then he certainly did not will to do whatever he did not do.”

The same Augustine writes in chapter 14 of *On Rebuke and Grace*: “It is said, ‘He wills all human beings to be saved,’ so that all the predestined are understood, because in them is every kind of human being, just as it was said—

—to the Pharisees, ‘You tithe every herb,’ and so forth. According to this manner of speech it is said, ‘Just as I please all in all things.’ Did the speaker please his many persecutors? No, but he pleased every kind of person whom Christ’s church gathered, whether already within or still to be introduced into it.” Thus Augustine confirms his interpretation both by examples of similar expressions and by the irrefutable reason that God’s will necessarily attains its outcome. Aquinas also agrees in *Summa theologiae* I, q. 19, art. 6, II–II, q. 4, and his commentary on this apostolic passage.

To confirm this by still plainer arguments, I ask from what time God can be thought to have willed every individual to be saved. Either this was perpetually his will, or he eventually began to will what he formerly did not and formerly did not will what he later willed. The latter cannot be said of God without blasphemy. The former is shown false not only by the history and experience of every age but refuted by manifest Scripture testimonies. For many ages the calling of the Gentiles was silent and unknown even to the Jews themselves, much less revealed to the Gentiles (Romans 16:25 and often elsewhere). How could God be said to have willed so many myriads of people to be saved while he allowed them to walk in their own ways? In saying this I do not accuse the severity of God, who is debtor to none. With the apostle I say there never was, is, or will be a time when God willed, wills, or will will to have mercy upon every individual. From eternity, according to his good will, which cannot be resisted, he determined both those upon whom he would have mercy in Christ and those whom he would harden in Christ.

Why should we hesitate longer about the true interpretation of this passage, since this universality is elsewhere so plainly restricted—not merely as to the event, lest a sophist intervene here, but as to God’s counsel and purpose or will to save—sometimes to those alone effectually called (Romans 8:28), sometimes to adoption and election (Ephesians 1:5, 11), sometimes to believers alone (John 3:16; Romans 1:16; Ephesians 1:13)? In this very passage the apostle joins as inseparable God’s willing that people be saved and come to knowledge of the truth. All whom God wills to come to knowledge of the truth must also be called to Christ. Flesh and blood do not reveal Christ; God alone does (Matthew 16:17), drawing his people either by a hidden inward breath alone, as happened to Paul, or by employing the outward ministry. If Andreae attempts to show—not every individual, I say, but all peoples—were inwardly called to Christ in every age, who could endure such shamelessness? To be called inwardly is nothing other than to be gifted with faith, and the matter itself proves that not even faith is common to all, much less the faith—

—which distinguishes the elect from the reprobate. But if this calling to Christ is understood of the external ministry, let Andreae show who—after the other peoples, except Abraham’s family, deserted God—were sent to every people before the apostolic age, much less every individual, to explain the coming Messiah and the prophetic writings. His claim, which I shall shortly refute, that grace given to the Israelites should have attracted all human beings to knowledge and worship of the true God is so ridiculous that it may be seen as such, as I will soon show in its place.

What more? If God certainly wills “all,” meaning every individual, to be saved, then he reveals his arm to every individual. Yet Isaiah, speaking of the elect, indeed calls them all צאן ידו, “the sheep of his hand,” but adds, “To whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed?” Therefore he also draws all. But if he draws all, why does Christ not posit the universality of grace which Andreae establishes but rather offer a remedy for the stumbling block of the multitude of unbelievers: “No one comes to me unless my Father draws him”? Therefore the Son also reveals the Father to every individual. But Christ cries the contrary: “No one knows the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son wills to reveal him.” How could Christ have willed to save even Gentiles during that time of his ministry destined only for the circumcised, when he told his disciples, “Do not go in the way of the Gentiles” (Matthew 10:5)?

To conclude this passage once for all in the words of Fulgentius, book 2, chapter 2 of *On the Remission of Sins*: “Here we must understand ‘all’ as those to whom, as Paul says, God grants repentance leading to knowledge of the truth, who before the end of the present life are freely preceded by God’s mercy, to which they are divinely predestined through gratuitous grace, so that converted they do not perish but possess eternal life.” Nor does the fact that in the same apostolic passage the command to offer prayers for all is understood of every individual of the classes obstruct this sole genuine interpretation. The Lord knows his own, whom alone he wills to be saved. We, on the contrary, cannot determine certainly whether others belong to him. Augustine says in chapter 15 of *On Rebuke and Grace*: “Because we do not know who belongs to the number of the predestined and who does not, we must be so affected by charity that we will all to be saved.” And again in chapter 16: “So far as concerns us, who cannot distinguish the predestined from the non-predestined and therefore must will all to be saved, severe rebuke must be applied medicinally by us to all so that they neither perish nor destroy others. But it belongs to God to make it useful to those whom he predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son.”
