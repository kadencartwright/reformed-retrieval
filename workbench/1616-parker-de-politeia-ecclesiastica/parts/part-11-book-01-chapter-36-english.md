CHAPTER XXXVI.

The Third Corollary: Bishops Must Yield Their Possession.

There remains the second address, which exhorts them with the weightiest
arguments to yield their possession. For what? Do they not now stir up schism
in order to retain the Episcopate, just as Novatus did in order to obtain it?
Cornelius condemned Novatus’s Episcopate because it had not been granted to
him by God. But the English Episcopate must be condemned much more, because it
has not been granted from above by God to anyone at all, as will be
demonstrated in its proper place. Therefore, by the arguments with which
Dionysius of Alexandria persuaded No—

[Marginal reference: Eusebius, book 6, chapter 42.]

[1638 control | printed p. 114 | BSB canvas 124 | bsb10507786_00124]

—vatus, we too may now properly persuade the English to renounce their
Hierarchical Episcopate. You ought to suffer anything at all rather than let
the Church be torn apart. Are you unwilling to suffer the loss of an unlawful
Episcopate? Though this loss is very small, it will be equivalent to martyrdom,
because it will conduce to the good of the whole Church. Moreover, it will
compensate for your former sin and make the reputation of your name
illustrious: that sin will not be imputed, while this loss will commend you.
Finally, you will not allow your souls to perish.

Gregory Nazianzen’s resignation is a very illustrious example, worthy of
imitation. Gregory the presbyter narrates it as follows in his life: “When he
saw that a contention had arisen among the Bishops because of his election, he
came forward boldly and wisely into the council and spoke as follows: ‘Men who
are my colleagues and fellow shepherds of Christ’s most holy flock, it would
be altogether shameful and foreign to you if you, who furnish others with
lessons of peace, should yourselves stir up an internal war among you. For how
will you bring others to concord while you yourselves differ over your
opinions and decrees? I entreat you even by the Trinity to settle your mutual
affairs honorably and peacefully. But if I am in any way the cause of your
dissension, I do not surpass the Prophet Jonah in integrity of life. Cast me
into the sea so that this turbulent tempest may cease from you. Whatever you
decide, though I am free from blame, I will willingly suffer, if in this way
your concord can be restored and repaired? Drive me from the throne, cast me
out of the city; only love truth and peace.’”

After Thomas Bilson had presented these words, he added: “As was worthy of a
Christian man, he held the unity of the Church dearer than his own safety, and
preferred a private life in quiet and solitude to so tumultuous an honor and
dignity.”

But, I say, if Nazianzen considered the Prophet Jonah an example for himself
when he was free from blame, so that a schism stirred up by others might be
extinguished, and if he conducted himself in this as was worthy of a Christian
man, how would the Hierarchists today conduct themselves worthily of Christian
men? They are as guilty of the matter itself as Jonah was, yet they do not
prefer, to their usurped honor, a life that is not private but pastoral and
holy, so that the schism stirred up not by others but by themselves might be
quieted. Most of them confess that Episcopal government is an indifferent
thing and can legitimately be changed; they grant that the reformed government
of the Church is legitimate. When these things have been duly considered,
those who obstinately defend their indifferent honor—the fan of such great
storms—against the government of the reformed Churches, legitimate according
to them and necessary according to us, can never hold the unity of the Church
dear after Nazianzen’s example.

But as Nazianzen’s example touches the Hierarchists very closely, so the
example of Hierax, approved and praised by Isidore of Pelusium, cuts the throat
of the Hierarchy itself. Hierax had been elected to the Episcopate but refused
it. When he was attacked with various calumnies, Isidore acted as his advocate
in these words: “Perhaps I may seem not to know that the dignity of ministry
and gentleness has passed into tyranny. For if you can show that the ancient
manner of tending the flock is still in force,”

[Marginal references: Eusebius, book 6, chapter 44; B., book 6, page 475;
epistle 2.23.]

[1638 control | printed p. 115 | BSB canvas 125 | bsb10507786_00125]

“it would especially be right for you to praise the man who judged himself
unequal to this business and for that reason took to flight. But if affairs
have changed into the contrary condition, no punishments at all are to be
expected from him. For then they went to meet death for the sake of everyone’s
salvation; but now shepherds bring death upon the sheep, not by
slaughtering their bodies—which would be a lighter evil—but by bringing
stumbling blocks to their souls. Then they chastised the body with fasts; but
now they contrive to exult in luxury and delights. Then they distributed their
own goods to the needy; but now they turn the goods of the poor to their own
use. Then they cultivated virtue; now they proscribe and banish those who
cultivate the virtues. But since I shall show how great the difference is
between the ancient ministry and the present tyranny, what reason remains why
you do not crown and proclaim those who love equality?”

That Isidore celebrates Hierax on this account—because he refused the
Episcopate from love of equality—belongs to another place, and therefore I do
not press it now. I note only this for the present: in Isidore’s judgment the
Episcopate cannot be retained in England with a clear conscience. There the
gentle and mild dignity of a Shepherd has passed, as he says, into ostentatious
tyranny; the ancient manner of tending the flock has ceased among the Bishops;
the Episcopate exults in luxury and delights; the salvation of souls is
hindered by the Episcopate’s stumbling blocks; God’s servants are impoverished
and stripped of their goods by the Bishops; and finally, those who cultivate
the virtues are proscribed and banished by the Bishops.

Against this, Downam asserts that he defends only the substance of a Bishop
and that personal abuses ought not to prejudice this substance. This amounts
to nothing, since Isidore thought otherwise, and rightly so. That subtle
distinction has no place when the Episcopate itself has so degenerated that it
has the abuses recalled above implanted and fixed within it, intimately and
inherently adhering to it. Indeed, not even some most holy Hierax could be free
from the faults set out above if he wished to exercise the Episcopate most
strictly according to the English Canons and without any personal abuse.

For what? Do luxury and delights not adhere so tenaciously to the English
Episcopate today that the Bishops claim their pomp and splendor are necessary
to preserve the dignity of their position? Does John Whitgift not say that he
is bound by his Episcopal oath to remove nonconforming ministers from office,
although they are learned, holy, and diligent in preaching the Gospel? Do the
Officials not declare that it belongs to their office to excommunicate either
ministers or laymen, strip them of their goods, and harass them with censures
when they find them less obedient to the most unjust decrees of their court?

Even if the see were free from these things, experience has nevertheless long
proclaimed that what someone said about a court is true of the English
Episcopal see: “It often receives good men but never makes them.” Indeed, this
must be added: when it has previously received good men, it makes them evil
thereafter. For how few there are, however learned, pious, and diligent in
preaching, who, after receiving the Episcopal mitre, have not, like Aeneas
Sylvius once he was made Pope, suddenly changed so much that they need to sing,
as he did, a palinode to their former zeal: “Re—

—ject Aeneas; receive Pius?” These matters are certainly graver than those on
account of which John of Beverley once renounced the Archbishopric of York. He
was Bede’s teacher and considered it most delightful to preach the Gospel for
the salvation of souls.
But he detested the contentions and quarrels over external ceremonies which
arose among ecclesiastics and, finally afflicted with weariness at the
disturbances they had stirred up in the Church, he left his office and withdrew
to Beverley, where he ended his life in great constancy of spirit in the year
721.

To preach the Gospel from the pulpit was most delightful to Beverley; will you
not cease to drive preachers of the Gospel from the pulpit? He detested
contentions stirred up among ecclesiastics over external ceremonies; will you
not cease to stir up contentions over ceremonies, to the most certain
destruction of the Church? He left his office when he saw that he had not been
able to settle the contentions. Are you unwilling to leave that office because
of which the English dissensions cannot be settled, but whose removal would
open the clearest way to concord?

This is clear from Gregory the Great, who dealt with a cause of the same kind
as ours, since the Episcopate of Constantinople differed from the English only
in degree, as was demonstrated elsewhere. His words therefore have a place
here: “The man who injures the Church, who swells in his heart, who seeks to
rejoice in a title of singularity, and who sets himself above even the honor of
your Empire must be restrained. Behold, we all suffer scandal over this
matter. Therefore let the author of the scandal return to the right way, and
all the quarrels of the priests will cease.”

Do you see that the shortest way to peace is for the English Bishops to cease
contending over their primacy, but that there is no hope of peace while they
injure the Church, bring scandal upon the other ministers, set themselves
above their honor, and seek a title of singularity—which Bilson presses as
applying especially to a Bishop? Therefore let Episcopal pride be gone and all
disputes will cease; let humility be preserved and peace will return. Let
Christ himself be heard. In exhorting his own to peace, he orders them to put
away their contentions over primacy, Mark 9:34, 35, 50.

When it had been made clear that the aristocratic government of Israel had
been changed by God into a monarchy, Samuel willingly laid down his
magistracy and passed into private life, 2 Samuel 12:1. Why should the English
Bishop not now strip himself of the Episcopate after this example, since the
light of truth, now risen everywhere, condemns the monarchical government of
the Church through Bishops, and it is evident that Christ loves aristocratic
government in individual ministers endowed with equal power? This, then, is
the second corollary addressed to the Bishops, arising from both the piety and
necessity of reformed discipline: the Bishops who persecute it must cease both
to contend over their possession and to yield it.
