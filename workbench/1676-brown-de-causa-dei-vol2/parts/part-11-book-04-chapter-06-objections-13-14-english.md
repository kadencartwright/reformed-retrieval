# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 13.* Although the matter of the law has not been abolished, the
law itself, insofar as it is law, is abolished.

*Answer 1.* That matter of the law either has the force of law or is wholly
devoid of any such force. If the *former* is asserted, it is not abolished
insofar as it is law, for whatever has the force of law is law. If the
*latter* is asserted, then when that matter is violated, no law is violated,
and consequently no sin is committed. For sin is transgression of the law,
and where there is no law there is no transgression, as Paul testifies.

2. If the matter of the law remains, then it remains either to bind us to
obedience or not. If *not*, what purpose does it serve? If it remains *to
bind*, then it has the force of law. For every sign of the lawgiver's will
which establishes a duty owed and binds to obedience is law. This assertion
therefore entails a contradiction. [Apollonius says in *On the Law of God*,
p. 300: *“For if believers are charged with performing duties enjoined by
law, then if they omit those duties they necessarily sin. And if they sin,
they incur the penalty and curse pronounced in the law, and are therefore
necessarily constrained by the obligation of the law. Thus not merely the
matter of the law but the law itself, as it is law and performs the offices
of law, remains with respect to them. For law as law is nothing other than
the prescribed will of the lawgiver, declaring what is honorable and what is
shameful to do, and binding a person to do honorable things and avoid
shameful ones. Thus the law remains and is in force as long as believers are
charged, by the will of God the lawgiver, with performing the duties
prescribed by the law.”*]

3. If the matter of the law remains but not the law itself as law, then it
must be said that God does not now will or command us to perform that matter
of the law—for example, that we love him

[Printed p. 65 | IA PDF 73 | IA leaf 73 | IA decausadei02brow]

above all things and worship him, and so forth. Consequently, it will be
utterly indifferent whether we love him or not; and when we do love him, we
do not perform an act of obedience.

*Objection 14.* John 1:17: *“For that law was given through Moses; grace and
truth were furnished through Jesus Christ.”* From this they conclude that
Christ has now freed his people from every law.

*Answer.* These words are understood in different ways by different people.
Musculus intends this manner of speaking to attribute to the fullness of
Christ the abundance of God's benefits lavishly bestowed upon Israel under
the Old Testament by divine grace. He therefore intends these words, equally
with the preceding ones, to be understood of the grace and truth bestowed
upon Israel under the Old Testament. For, he says, there is no reason for us
to think that the law was prior to Christ's grace and truth. Rather, grace
and truth were like a fountain from which, just as all the other benefits, so
too the law given to Israel flowed, since Christ existed even before Moses.
Thus he takes this to be the meaning of the words: although the law was given
through Moses, nevertheless it was not through Moses that the grace and truth
from which the benefit of the law flowed came, but through Jesus Christ. The
law was given through a servant, but grace and faith through the Lord
himself. Thus the law itself is not from Moses but from Christ himself,
through whom grace and faith came to Israel, so that Moses too received from
Christ's fullness. If this interpretation prevails, it is evident that
nothing can be concluded from this in favor of the abrogation of the law now
under the New Testament. For if any abrogation of the law can be inferred
from this, the inference will hold as much for the Old Testament as for the
New.

2. But let the more common opinion prevail: that John here compares Christ
with Moses and prefers him to Moses. For although Moses—as God's minister,
to whom the Jews attributed much and than whom surely no more excellent
minister ever existed in the house of God, being faithful in his whole house
(Hebrews 3:1, 5)—set forth the law of God, nevertheless no one obtained
salvation from that law. For the moral law merely pointed out the disease,
and the ceremonial law foreshadowed the mediator, but the Gospel, which is
announced through Christ, presents the remedy, namely, the mediator himself.
[Gualter says on the passage: *“For the law has precepts and ceremonies, or
sacred rites. The precepts contain the entire doctrine concerning the
worship and adoration of the one God, the proper formation of conduct, the
ordering of the whole of life, and the administration of the commonwealth
and its courts. Promises are joined to these, promising every happy thing and
a propitious God to those who observe them. External worship consisted in
the ceremonies, which contained types and seals of the promised salvation.
But if you consider both, neither of them could confer salvation. For because
the precepts require perfect obedience—indeed, the whole soul and all a
person's powers—they instead expose our impotence, convict us of sin, and
thus work wrath and death, since they teach us that we have merited God's
wrath and death, as Paul carefully shows in Romans chapters 7 and 8. The
ceremonies, however, did indeed foreshadow salvation, but were so far from
conferring it that God not infrequently brought a complaint against the
fathers because of them and rejected them together with their sacred rites.
Therefore, setting Christ over against all these things, John says that grace
and truth came through him. By the name of grace he understands God's mercy
and the free forgiveness of sins, which is conferred in Christ upon those
convicted by the law and turned to God with a contrite heart. By truth he
means the realities themselves which were foreshadowed by the legal types.
Thus in Christ alone John shows those things*

[Printed p. 66 | IA PDF 74 | IA leaf 74 | IA decausadei02brow]

*which the law could not provide.”*] What, then, follows from this? Because
grace, by which the elect are freed from the disease that the moral law
pointed out, has been furnished through Christ, as has the truth which the
ceremonies foreshadowed, does it therefore follow that the moral law itself
has been wholly removed and abrogated, so that it no longer has the character
of a rule of conduct? By what argument, I ask, will that be proved? Did the
elect under the Old Testament not become participants in this grace and
truth? Was the moral law therefore abrogated for them too? See other
commentators in Marlorat on the passage.

3. That these words are not to be understood absolutely, as though Christ
prescribed no law, is also evident from the fact that it was Christ himself
who gave the law to the Israelites on Mount Sinai. For he was the one who
appeared to Moses from the midst of the bush (Exodus 3:2). He is called the
*Angel*, namely, the Angel of the covenant. And in Galatians 3:19 the apostle
says that the law was ordained through angels in the hand of a mediator—δι᾽
ἀγγέλων ἐν χειρὶ μεσίτου—and the most learned interpreter Calvin understands
the mediator here to be Christ. And in Acts 7:30, 38 he is called the Angel:
*“This is the one who was—with the Angel who spoke to him on Mount Sinai.”*
And he was the Angel who was sent ahead to preserve Israel on the way and to
bring it into the place which the Lord had prepared and in whom was the name
of God (Exodus 23:20–21). [Apollonius says in the work cited above, p. 303:
*“He does not deny that the law was given by Christ and through Christ. For
the principal and instrumental causes are to be joined, not set against one
another. The law was given by Christ as God, and was also given through him
‘in the hand of a mediator’—not merely a typical mediator, but the true one
foreshadowed by the type. Although it is said to have been given through
Moses, it was not given by Moses; that is, it was given by his ministry, not
by his authority. And grace is not here taken away from the law absolutely,
but only comparatively, because less grace was given under the law than under
the Gospel. The ministry of the law was joined with a scant measure of the
Spirit; in the ministry of the Gospel God gives grace for grace.”*] See
Hebrews 12:26.
