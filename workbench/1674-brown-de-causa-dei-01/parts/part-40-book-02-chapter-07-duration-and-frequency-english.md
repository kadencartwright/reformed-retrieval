CHAPTER VII

The Usefulness of Determining Time for the Worship of God with Respect to
Continuation and Frequency, Separately and Jointly

We have already spoken about the usefulness and necessity of determining time
in general for worship. But since, as is clear from what was said before,
various aspects and attributes of time must be considered here—namely, *how
long*, *how often*, and *when*: that is, the *continuation* or *duration* of the
time which must be spent at once; its *frequency*, or how often the same
duration of time must recur after interruptions; and, finally, the very
*opportunity* or *season*, or when the work must be begun—it will be worthwhile
to see specifically whether it is useful or necessary that the time applied to
religion be determined with respect to these aspects.

Here, then, we will deal first with the continuation and frequency of time, or
with *how long* and *how often*, both separately and jointly; and we will show
how useful it is for these aspects of time to be determined in relation to the
worship of God.

We must first consider whether it is useful and necessary that these aspects
or attributes of time be determined in relation to any business to be
performed in time, and why. Then we will easily make manifest the usefulness
of this determination as it concerns religion. We therefore say:

1. Those attributes (or aspects) of time which affect the action itself, or
the work or undertaking itself, materially and substantially, even when they
are indeterminate—which we showed above to be *how long* and *how often*—do not
lose their force through any determination, and can, indeed ought, profitably
to be determined, whether considered separately or jointly. The reason is
that such a determination, when instituted well and wisely, makes certain the
benefit arising from observing the determinate time, which before had only
been contingent. For when no determination had been made, even those who had
this undertaking at heart could have been obstructed by many impediments from
pursuing it. Or, through ignorance of a suitable time, they could have assigned
an opportunity quite unsuitable for this undertaking. And nothing could have
been expected from those who did not have that undertaking at heart, because
they would willingly have spent no time at all upon it.

2. When an undertaking is such that it requires an ardent application of the
mind, it is necessary to determine that not some *short* time, but a *long* time
at once, be given to it, so that the length of time might in some measure
answer to the gravity of the matter and the present occasion—if, as we suppose,
it has not been determined

[1674 logical printed p. 264 | IA leaf 282 | PDF p. 282]

how frequent the repetition of the action ought to be, or if perhaps the
action is never to be repeated. Indeed, in this case it is required that the
duration be made as long as possible, nor can it be too long. For if it should
perhaps be feared that a person's strength would fail if the time were too
long, or that some urgent necessity might cause a distraction, an allowed
reservation or dispensation for refreshing one's strength in the meantime and
completing those urgent affairs can remove all this fear. That in this case the
duration of time must be made longer is clear from this: a person's mind,
recently intent upon and wholly immersed in other affairs, cannot immediately
give itself wholly to this undertaking, which is quite different or distinct
from the others. Therefore, unless the continuation is somewhat longer, we
will be called to other things before we have done what the time was determined
for us to do, and thus we will waste our labor and oil. Moreover, that
principal action perhaps has its own difficulties which no one can overcome
in the first instant or make any progress upon without a longer, serious
effort. Furthermore, what we had already learned can quickly be consigned to
oblivion unless suitable time is granted for impressing it more firmly upon
the mind. Finally, these difficulties will press more heavily when the mind is
somewhat averse from that labor, does not incline to it of its own accord, and
does not take the delight in it that it ought.

3. This determination of a *longer continuation* is also necessary when many
distinct actions are required to advance or complete the undertaking. For
each of those actions requires its own considerable or suitable time, and so
they cannot all be completed in some minimal period of time.

4. Where an undertaking is such that it cannot be completed totally and
finally at one time, however long, but always remains equally important, it is
useful and necessary that time be determined with respect to *how often*—that
is, how often the same action is to be repeated or the same labor renewed.
Indeed, that determination must be such as can advance the undertaking; but it
cannot advance it unless the same labor is repeated more often, or at least
several times.

5. If a thing is by nature perishable and can perish or be lost if it is not
advanced and sustained by continual or repeated labors, it is useful and
necessary that the renewal of labor be made as frequent as possible, or that
the occasions of performing the same work recur very often—at least as often
as other necessary affairs permit. This is so even if, because of other
necessary affairs which also require their own times and no less frequent
occasions, there is no determination of time with respect to *duration*, or at
least no *strict* one, but the determination of the duration of time is left
free to the worker according to the opportunity given. The reason is that, by
this means, the more frequently or often the labor is renewed, the greater the
progress will be.

6. To advance or complete an undertaking which is perpetually important and
requires the whole person and a vehement application of the mind, it is useful
and necessary that there be a determination of a *longer duration* and *more
frequent repetition*: that is, that the time destined for this undertaking be
both longer and recur more often. Otherwise all labor will be vain, both
because of the mind's aversion and because of other

[1674 logical printed p. 265 | IA leaf 283 | PDF p. 283]

frequently recurring impediments; we cannot meet all these evils in any other
way.

7. Where various actions requiring serious application and not easily
completed are necessary to advance an undertaking, and there are also other
affairs which must not be neglected and require their own long and frequent
occasions, it is much more useful for the *continuation* to be longer and the
*recurrence* less frequent than for the *recurrence* to be more frequent and
the *duration* short. For example, to advance one's studies, it is better to spend
ten or twelve hours at once, even if this occurs only once in a week, than to
divide those ten or twelve hours equally among seven, six, five, or four days,
and so spend only two or three hours, and so forth, on each day. The reason is
that the determinate time would end before a person was properly composed for
working or studying and had even begun all the required actions. Moreover,
other affairs will be liable to the same disadvantages: they will either be
entirely neglected or administered perfunctorily.

This determination of a *longer duration* and *sufficiently frequent
repetition* is therefore *principal* and the most useful of all when it is wisely
constituted with regard to the strength of those acting and to the other
affairs which must not be omitted. It is necessary and ordinarily sufficient
for performing that work. For no other time is sufficient, and no other
extraordinary time is equally necessary; and ordinary daily time is less
solemn. This time, therefore, when rightly determined, is *principal,
necessary, and sufficient*—ordinarily, that is.

Although it is easy to apply these things to the worship of God, we will
nevertheless explain the matter in the following propositions.

1. It is useful for the *continuation and frequency* of time to be determined in
relation to worship. For whether these aspects are considered separately or
jointly, they are in themselves and by their nature substantially useful to
religion. They have such efficacy in advancing religion, if they are well
constituted, and in hindering religion, if badly constituted, that our
progress in religion depends upon a *longer continuation* and *more frequent
recurrence*, unless an impediment is brought in from elsewhere. Thus, one who
rightly observes determinate time which is *longer in duration* and *more
frequent in recurrence* cannot fail to serve God more and advance his own
salvation more than one who observes only *shorter and less frequent* time,
unless some inequality arises from elsewhere. Hence it is clear that the
determination of time with respect both to *duration* and *frequency* is so
necessary in relation to religion that the determinate time cannot be *longer*
or *more frequent* than is fair—since religion is supremely necessary—provided
that enough time remains to perform our earthly affairs properly.

2. It is also useful that the *duration* of time to be spent at once in the
worship of God be determined, and that this proportion be made as long as
possible, provided it is not such as would be a very great burden upon a person
and an impediment to performing other things necessary for our natural life,
unless a dispensation of a short time has been allowed for completing those
necessities. And it is certain that the time thus limited for the worship of
God is more useful if it is *longer* than if it is *shorter*, because the longer
the time is, the better and more conveniently all kinds of worship can be
performed: solitary worship, domestic worship in the family, and
ecclesiastical worship in the

[1674 logical printed p. 266 | IA leaf 284 | PDF p. 284]

ecclesiastical assembly. Thus all the duties of worship can be administered
better, both for God's glory and for our salvation. Suppose, for example, that
a whole day is determined. Everyone can then worship God quite conveniently
in his own room,
in his family, and in the church. In all these exercises an opportunity will
be given for *praying, singing psalms, meditating,* and *reading*; and,
moreover, in public assemblies, for *preaching, administering the sacraments,*
and *catechizing*, and so forth. Indeed, all these things can be performed seriously,
with gravity and solemnity, without overhasty speed. Time will also be given
to prepare the heart for rightly performing all these things; to drink in the
fruits of these exercises; and to fix more deeply in the memory and impress
more effectively what is heard and taught.

Moreover, the usefulness of this *longer duration* is evident from the
following. 1. The ignorance of many who live within the visible church is
gross and palpable. 2. There are very many things necessary to be known for
faith and practice, without knowledge of which we can neither worship God
rightly nor seek and advance our eternal salvation as we ought. 3. No small
number are quite dull and incapable of instruction. 4. We are all naturally
ill-disposed toward this study, and very many are wholly occupied with earthly
things and have all their thoughts immersed in this swamp, so that they can
scarcely, if at all, be drawn away from them. 5. The worship of God requires
the whole person, all his powers, his whole heart, his whole mind, and so forth,
and a very vehement application of the mind, if the worship is to be spiritual.
6. Everything can so easily be consigned to oblivion unless it is inculcated
longer and more forcefully so as to take deeper root, and unless the truth is
firmly rooted after all objections and other impediments of that sort have
been removed. 7. An impression made upon the affections is often easily lost,
and the soul returns to its former state. Therefore, when all these things are
weighed, it will be acknowledged that it is most useful for the duration to be
longer. For in such a continuation the soul is given an opportunity to enlarge
itself in devotion, to range freely through the exercises of worship, indeed
to receive the deep impressions made by those exercises, to drink in the
truth more fully, and to advance further when the affections have been moved,
and so forth.

3. Since the solemn worship of God, or religion, is continually necessary and
always equally important, it is also useful for *frequency* to be determined and
for this repetition of divine worship to be as frequent as possible, provided
that natural and worldly necessities are satisfied. By this means the soul's
communion with God will be kept firm; knowledge of God and divine truths will
not be lost; and the divine impression, which would otherwise vanish, will
remain vivid through this frequent repetition, notwithstanding the
interposition of earthly cares. Because of our corruption, those cares are now
formed to extinguish every pious thought and erase the impression. Thus, for
example, if it were determined that we celebrate the worship of God twice
each day, as morning and evening sacrifices were offered of old, though
nothing were said about the *duration*, or *how long* on each occasion, or at
least only a loose determination of duration were made, this determinate,
frequent repetition would nevertheless preserve the impression made, which
intervening earthly affairs would otherwise quickly erase. Indeed, it would
increase it, with God blessing and other affairs sometimes presenting no
impediment to communion with God being cultivated in solemn worship somewhat
longer than usual on each or every other occasion. At least this frequent

[1674 logical printed p. 267 | IA leaf 285 | PDF p. 285]

repetition would preserve one from a total lapse, which less frequent
repetition would induce and cause.

4. It is clear from what has been said that it is supremely useful to determine
a *longer time* with respect both to *when*, or *continuation*, and to *how
often*, or *frequency*, jointly. For here two substantial aspects are joined:
*duration* and *frequency*. And this proportion of time so determined is the *principal*
and most suitable of all, in which the wisdom and goodness of its institutor
shine forth most clearly. For example, in the determination of the seventh
day, or of a whole day at once, within the recurrence or period of a week,
there appears the greatest suitability. This is so, first, with respect to the
total proportion destined for religion, for a seventh part of time can appear
to no one either greater or less than is fair. It is so also with respect to
the division of this total proportion, so that the duration of continuous time
appears neither less nor greater than is fair, and the frequency of repetition
is neither greater nor less than it ought to be. For this division is much
better than if that seventh part of time were divided equally into seven parts
and, on each day, a portion which is a seventh of that seventh part were
dedicated to religion. That this may be understood better, it should further
be noted:

5. Once an equal proportion has been given, it is much more useful for the
*duration* to be longer and the *repetition* rarer than for the *repetition* to
be more frequent and the *continuation* shorter. Accordingly, it is also more
useful for one day in every week to be destined for solemn worship than for
some shorter portion to be given to it each day, or for half a day every three
or four days to be spent upon this business. For, as was said, there are
various kinds of divine worship, and these kinds have various duties, or
different actions or parts, all of which cannot be performed in some shorter
space. Nor will suitable time be given in that shorter space to prepare
ourselves to perform our duty properly, or to ruminate upon the things which
must be fixed more deeply in the memory and reduced to practice. But if the
duration were shorter even though the frequency were greater, some part of
worship—whether solitary, domestic, or ecclesiastical—would have to be omitted
entirely. Or certain duties of worship would either be wholly neglected or
performed too hastily and perfunctorily; and so the fruit of our labors which
we can now hope for would not be expected. The time would have elapsed before
the heart was well prepared and disposed for those exercises; the impression
would easily be lost; what had been heard would be consigned to oblivion; and
all would be buried and extinguished by the worldly cares in which people
would again become entangled. But when a whole day is set apart, ample
opportunity is given for ruminating upon all these things, for all worldly
thoughts and conversations are then commanded to remain far away. An
opportunity is also given to converse with others about matters of religion.
Moreover, if that portion of time were divided otherwise, our necessary
earthly affairs would also suffer great inconvenience. For if that seventh
part of time were divided among several days, so that some portion had to be
observed solemnly by the whole church on every day, when could a journey be
undertaken to perform earthly business? Indeed, if there were to be a public
assembly of the church every day, many who live in the country and far from
the church would undertake scarcely any earthly work, because they would spend
the greater part of the day going to church and returning home again.

Hence we may conclude that the determination of one whole day within the
weekly recurrence is substantially useful to all people in every age, unless

[1674 logical printed p. 268 | IA leaf 286 | PDF p. 286]

someone thinks that a period of fourteen or sixteen hours—for we subtract from
this the time necessary to refresh our body with sleep and food—within the
recurrence of seven days is too much to be spent upon the worship of God and
the advancement of our own salvation. And if this cannot be said, and it is
not clearly enough established that this seventh part of time has now been
abolished, it cannot be said with any reason that God does not now require this
seventh part of time, or that the portion dedicated to God and his worship now
under the gospel is longer than is fair.
