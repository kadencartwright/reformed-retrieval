[Marginalia: Lecture XI. Member 2. “All things,” etc. Opinion I. Rejection in
passing. Hebrews 12:22.]

As to the second member, concerning the things which the Apostle says are
gathered here, he first designates by the general expression “all things” all
those which had previously been torn and scattered and which Christ was sent
by the Father to gather into one body. Then he explains and illustrates that
universal expression by a division based on the subjects when he adds,
“both those in heaven and those on earth.” There is no common opinion about
the things the Apostle thus describes. Some wrongly extend these words not
only to rational creatures but also to heaven, earth, and all the other
things to be renewed and restored at the end of the ages; we rejected their
opinion above. Others restrict them to elect human beings alone. On that
view, “those in heaven” means the souls of the godly already received into
heaven when the Apostle wrote, or at least before Christ’s incarnation, while
“those on earth” means the remaining believers still living in the world,
composed of both Jews and Gentiles. In the next chapter Paul will expressly
teach that they were gathered into the assembly of one Church under Christ
as head through Christ manifested in the flesh.

Finally, others—for we judged the impious heresy of Origen, that through
Christ all, even devils and reprobates, would ultimately be expiated and
saved, unworthy even to be reported, much less refuted—interpret these words
not only of elect human beings but extend them also to the blessed angels.
Elect human beings were not only gathered among themselves into one body of
the Church, but were also joined to the holy angels and admitted into their
society and fellowship, since the angels too are part of the Church
triumphant, as the Apostle teaches in Hebrews 12. Speaking there of this same
gathering of the elect and faithful through Christ, he says, “But you have
come to Mount Zion and the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem.”
Then, explaining the citizens of that city, he first adds, “to innumerable
angels, to the universal assembly and congregation of the firstborn who are
enrolled in heaven, to God the judge of all, and to the spirits of righteous
people made perfect,” and so forth. Christ too has been established as head
over them.

[Marginalia: Objection. Answer I. Luke 15. Answer II. Augustine in the
*Enchiridion to Laurence*, chapter 62.]

Someone may object that the blessed angels never fell from their grace and
happy state; therefore Christ Jesus was not sent to regather, redeem, or
restore them, and this statement of the Apostle cannot pertain to them. We
can answer in two ways. First, although they did not fall from God and their
heavenly origin, they could have fallen had they not been elect in Christ—not
as Redeemer, certainly, but as head—so that through and in him they might be
perpetually preserved and confirmed in their original happiness. Indeed,
through his coming in the flesh they were illuminated with a fuller knowledge
of the divine will and blessed with a new addition of joy, which they receive
from human salvation and conversion, as the Lord himself testifies in Luke
15. In this respect, although Christ is not the Redeemer of angels, he can
still in some sense be called their Head and Mediator, because by his
intervention they were so established and confirmed in their original grace
that they can never fall from it.

But, setting aside this consideration, about which we do not have very much
in sacred Scripture, our second answer is that the angels can be said to have
been gathered with us into one through Christ because we had been torn from
them by sin and they had been estranged from us as sinners and apostates, but
now they are reconciled to us and gathered with us into one and the same
fellowship under Christ the head. I pass over in silence the explanation by
which Augustine in the *Enchiridion to Laurence*, chapter 62, interprets the
word “restore” of the angels.—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 76 | Caput I p. 34]

—There he writes: “And surely God’s holy angels in heaven, who are blessed by the
eternal contemplation of his truth, know how great a supplement from the
human race the integrity of that city awaits. On this account the Apostle
says that all things in heaven and on earth are restored in Christ. The
things in heaven are restored in him when what fell from there among the
angels is supplied from human beings. The things on earth are restored when
the human beings predestined to eternal life are renewed from the oldness of
corruption—and all these things through Christ.”

His meaning and words are transparent in themselves. The blessed angels are
said to be restored through Christ because the fall of many diminished their
number, and that loss or numerical deficiency is as it were repaired and made
good by the salvation of elect human beings, when they are chosen or
substituted in place of the evil angels to reestablish the integrity of that
heavenly city or senate. This is certainly a subtle speculation, but since I
do not know what authority of sacred Scripture supports it, I leave it
undecided.

[Marginalia: Choice. Colossians 1:20. Conclusion of the exposition.]

After weighing the opinions of others, we would gladly apply these words of
the Apostle even to the elect angels for the reasons given, were we not
checked by the passage cited above from Colossians 1: “Having made peace
through the blood of his cross, through him to reconcile all things to
himself—through him, I say, both things on earth and things in heaven.” He
plainly appears to mean there the same “all things” as here, since in both
places he adds the same explanatory clause. Yet that reconciliation made
through the blood of Christ’s cross cannot be applied to the holy angels,
but only to the human beings whose Redeemer Christ is and for whom he shed
his blood. Therefore the *anakephalaiōsis* here must not be understood of the
angels, but only of the Church composed of human beings, unless someone
wishes forcibly to tear apart “to reconcile” and “to make peace through the
blood of the cross,” extending the former to the angels as more general but
restricting the latter to redeemed human beings as more specific. This cannot
be done without doing violence to the words, nor can angels properly be said
to be reconciled, as is plain to anyone who considers the matter.

It remains, then, that according to the Apostle’s meaning we properly
understand the *anakephalaiōsis* only of elect human beings. Nevertheless, it
also necessarily follows that those thus united to Christ and reconciled to
God through him are by the same act joined with the holy angels and, together
with them, constitute one Church, one catholic body, under the same head,
Christ.

[Marginalia: Difficulty. Hebrews 11:40. Answer. Conclusion of the answer.
Psalm 2:8.]

With these matters discussed, one further difficulty remains to be removed:
how were those who are said here to be in heaven finally gathered through
Christ’s manifestation in the flesh? The Apostle himself increases this
difficulty in Hebrews 11. After that notable catalogue of believers who lived
before Christ’s coming, he adds, “God having provided something better for
us, that apart from us they should not be made perfect”—*teleiōthōsi*—thus
showing that they were not perfected by the effectiveness of those external
rites and legal observances.

Someone may ask: were those ancient believers not reconciled to God by faith
while they lived? Were they not sanctified and cleansed from an evil
conscience of sins? For this is what we understand there by *teleiōsis*. Were
they not gathered into the body of the Church and finally received into
glory after departing this earth? Undoubtedly they attained salvation in
their own time; far be it from any godly person to doubt this. But the
Apostle’s point is that they were saved by faith in the Christ to come, not
by external observance of the law. Meanwhile their entire salvation depended
on what had not yet been performed and manifested in their time, but which
they hoped would be performed and manifested in our times, that is, at
Christ’s coming in the flesh. It follows that they were finally perfected by
Christ’s manifestation, since all their hope depended on him alone and all
their confidence was fixed on him alone. They would not have been saved by a
Christ who was to come and die if he had not come in his own time and, once
manifested in the flesh, performed and fulfilled in its time what they hoped
for.

To conclude our answer with this distinction: with respect to themselves,
they were gathered and perfected in their own time—that is, they attained
eternal salvation through faith in the Christ to come. But with respect to
the means by which they were gathered and perfected—that is, the power and
efficacy by which they were saved—they are said to have been gathered and
perfected only by Christ’s coming and manifestation and by his fulfillment
of everything he had to perform for our salvation. This is so not merely
because they were reconciled to God by Christ’s blood and sacrifice, but also
because the other believers were joined to them: both those still living on
earth who were granted to see Christ present and embrace him by faith, and
those from the Gentiles who by the preaching of the gospel were and still
daily are called and admitted by the ministry of the word into the same
communion. This accords with the prophecy in which the Father promises his
Son the Gentiles as his possession and the ends of the earth as his
inheritance—that is, a Church collected and gathered from the families of
all peoples, both Gentiles and Jews.

[Marginalia: The third member. In Christ. Comparison.]

As to the third member, the one in whom this gathering takes place, namely
“in Christ,” is already sufficiently clear from what has been said and from
the passage in Colossians cited above, where the Apostle expresses the manner
of this reconciliation: peace was made through the blood of Christ’s cross.
Just as the unity of a civil or military society—an army, for example—depends
upon the general, king, emperor, or whoever holds supreme command, so that
when the leader or head is removed the remaining multitude immediately flows
apart and falls to pieces (just as a decapitated human body must fall to the
ground), so our entire gathering and mutual fellowship, and that saving and
holy communion with our God, the holy angels, and all the believers who
preceded us into the heavenly kingdom, depend on this one head, King, and
Mediator: Jesus Christ, I say, the Son of God manifested in the flesh, who in
his own—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 77 | Caput I p. 35]

—most sacred person united our human nature with his glorious Godhead so that
by his intervention he might reunite and join us human beings to God. He does
this by first engrafting us into himself as branches into the vine. We
observed above that this is signified to us by God’s Spirit in the
phrase by which we are said to be or possess something “in Christ”—for
example, to have redemption in Christ or to be gathered in Christ.

[Marginalia: Member 4: at what time. Galatians 4:4. *Oikonomia*. 1 Timothy
3:15. Note the reason for the name.]

Finally, as to the time at which the Father determined from eternity to
gather all things in Christ, the Apostle describes it as “the dispensation
of the fullness of times.” Galatians 4 likewise says, “But when the fullness
of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of a woman, born under the
law,” and so forth. Here, however, he adds the term *oikonomia*, or
dispensation, which is taken from the care of a household. Properly speaking,
*oikonomia* is domestic administration or the method of managing a household,
when someone assumes and conducts the care of a family so as to administer
to each what is needed appropriately and at the proper time.

Since the Church is the household of God Most High and Most Great—as the
Apostle calls it in 1 Timothy 3, “the house of God, which is the Church of the
living God, the pillar and foundation of the truth,” and of which God has
taken particular care—that special providence by which God governs his
Church, dispenses and directs all the moments of the times and occasions that
pertain to it, and in his secret counsel sets and fixes the bounds of the
Church’s different periods, as of its cradle, infancy, tutelage or youth,
more mature and advanced age, and other such stages or courses of human age;
how long he willed to keep and exercise his Church under the rod and stricter
discipline of the old law, like a youth under a guardian or tutor; and when
he decreed to free it from that yoke, like one now grown and arrived at
manhood, and vindicate it into full liberty through Christ manifested—that
particular providence of God toward the Church, I say, especially in
dispensing the time at which Christ the Mediator had to be sent into the
world, is most rightly called *oikonomia*. We have been unable to express it
by any more suitable term than “dispensation” or “arrangement.”

[Marginalia: Another meaning of the word. Theodoret.]

We are not ignorant, however, that the Greek Fathers customarily use
*oikonomia* in another sense, insofar as it properly concerns Christ
manifested in the flesh. First, they call the incarnation of the Son of God
itself by that name, as Theodoret says in the *Unconfounded*: “We call the
divine condescension to humanity the *oikonomia*.” They also include under
the same word the other parts of the Mediator’s office performed by him in
the flesh: his nativity, preaching of the gospel, passion, resurrection, and
ascension into heaven. Thus the same author says in book 4: “We confess that
the Son of God completed the whole dispensation on our behalf, being God in
birth and suffering, resurrection, and ascension into heaven.”

For this reason they sometimes oppose *oikonomia*, which they refer to
Christ’s human nature, to *theologia*, by which they mean his Godhead. Thus
Basil writes against Eunomius: “It is not the manner of theology that hands
this down to us, but the account of the dispensation that declares it.” They
properly call treating God’s nature and the Lord Christ’s Godhead
*theologein*. Hence Gregory of Nyssa says that John the Evangelist “speaks
theology openly,” while the other Evangelists are mostly occupied with the
humanity. The most learned Casaubon teaches this fully in his discussion of
Gregory of Nyssa’s letter to Eustathius and Basilissa, page 107.

[Marginalia: Application. What the fullness of times means. Reasons I and
II.]

To apply this to the matter at hand: if we use *oikonomia* here in this latter
sense familiar to the orthodox Greek Fathers, the meaning will be: “in the
dispensation of the fullness of times,” that is, in executing and administering
that work of our Redemption which Christ would undertake and perform in the
flesh only in the fullness of time. Thus *oikonomia* properly signifies the
fulfillment and performance of the Mediator’s office by Christ incarnate for
us, an exposition which greatly pleases me.

But if we refer the word to that special providence of God by which he
arranged the moments of time with himself, especially for sending Christ into
the world, the sense will be the one we previously presented from Dr. Beza:
“in the dispensation of the fullness of times,” that is, at the full time
which God’s special providence had from eternity ordained, determined, and
dispensed—appointed for this very use and end—that Christ should then come
into the world and, having made peace through the blood of his cross, gather
all the elect into one body and reconcile them to God the Father. This
interpretation seems simpler than the former and less remote. We leave both
to the choice and judgment of the godly.

It remains to ask why the time of Christ’s incarnation is called both here
and in Galatians the “fulfillment of time”—for I would gladly translate
*plērōma*, which Dr. Beza renders “fullness,” in this way—or the completion of
time. First, the Apostle uses this phrase to show that it did not happen by
chance or at random that Christ the Mediator came into the world at that
particular time, revealed the Father’s counsel to human beings in the gospel,
suffered and died, rose again, ascended into heaven, sat at the right hand of
the Divine Majesty, and fulfilled everything the true Messiah, our Mediator
and Redeemer, had to fulfill. Rather, God’s supremely free will had appointed
that very time and moment for all these things. Because of God’s immutable
decree, Christ could neither be born in the world nor execute the work of our
Redemption sooner or later.

If anyone moved by human meddlesomeness and presumptuous curiosity should
dare ask why Christ delayed his coming so long, or allowed the Gentiles to
wander so long in their own ways, the Apostle may answer: because the
appointed time of his coming had not yet been fulfilled and completed. As
soon as it was fulfilled, Christ the Lord came in the flesh and entered the
world. Second, this expression has a certain force and emphasis in teaching
us that the all-wise God also had the best reasons for choosing the time to
send his Son into the world, though those reasons are generally hidden from
us. As if God were bound to give us a reason for his will, admit us into his
secret counsel, or make us, dust and ashes,—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 78 | Caput I p. 36]

—capable of that immeasurable abyss of wisdom. It was therefore the full time
or completion of time not only
because, when Christ was born, the whole span which God willed to pass from
the world’s beginning to Christ’s manifestation in the flesh had elapsed and
been completed, with none of it left to pass, but also because this moment
alone, compared with all the preceding imperfect and incomplete moments,
deserved to be called full and perfect.

[Marginalia: Reason III. 2 Corinthians 6:2.]

To understand this more fully, we say third that this time may rightly be
called full by metonymy, with regard to its subject—that is, the things which
had to be performed only then. What occurred at that time? In a word, as we
taught above when interpreting *anakephalaiōsis*, the fulfillment of all the
preceding promises, precepts, predictions, shadows, and figures. All were
perfectly fulfilled through Christ manifested in the flesh and received
their completion in him. Does not the time at which he came and fulfilled
them rightly receive the name “full,” while the preceding ages or times are
called imperfect and still lacking their end and perfection? Everything done
then was imperfect and typological and would receive its fulfillment only in
and through Christ incarnate.

Likewise, in 2 Corinthians 6:2 the Apostle, quoting Isaiah, calls the time at
which the grace of Jesus Christ was offered to the Gentiles through the
preaching of the gospel *kairon euprosdekton*, “an acceptable time,” from the
events occurring then, namely the calling of formerly unbelieving people
into Christ’s fellowship. So the time at which all things were completed in
and through Christ is rightly called by the Apostle the full time, the
completion of time, on this ground alone. Let this suffice for the exposition
of this notable passage.

[Marginalia: Observation I. Two corollaries. Conclusion.]

Before advancing, we must derive several observations in our customary way.
First, we have here the manner in which the benefits performed and procured
for us by Christ and deposited in him as their fountain—especially that
redemption by his blood which the Apostle had just mentioned—are actually
communicated and applied to us. It is through effectual calling, in which God
himself first caused his rich grace to abound toward us in all spiritual
wisdom and understanding, for thus the Apostle designated faith awakened in
our hearts. We derive two corollaries.

First, just as God first loved us in Christ when we were undeserving and
thinking nothing of the kind, and sent Christ to redeem us even before we
were born, so he first visited us from on high, shone upon us from heaven with
his saving grace, came to us, effectually called us by his gospel, and drew us
to Christ. Second, we cannot become participants in Christ and his benefits,
especially the redemption accomplished on the cross, unless we are endowed
with the true wisdom and understanding mentioned here—that is, unless we
know and acknowledge Christ crucified and embrace this immeasurable benefit
by true faith.

Away, then, from human minds and ears with that papal fiction of the work
performed merely as performed. It pretends, for example, that people who only
present themselves at that blasphemous sacrifice of the altar and merely
attend the spectacle of the Mass, without true faith, knowledge, or
apprehension of what is done, become actual participants in forgiveness,
Christ’s grace, and everything else they impiously ascribe to their Mass by
the mere act of standing by and watching. True faith is necessary to receive
Christ, and it cannot exist apart from that spiritual wisdom and
understanding in which the nature of faith itself consists.

[Marginalia: Observation II. 1 Corinthians 2:11–12, 14.]

Second, when God is said to have caused all wisdom and understanding to
abound toward us from his rich grace, we learn that just as Redemption is a
free gift of God, so the faith by which we know, embrace, and apprehend it is
an equally free gift. Indeed it is spiritual wisdom and understanding, which
sacred Scripture is so far from attributing to human nature’s powers that in
1 Corinthians 2 the Apostle excludes human nature: “Who knows the things of
a man except the spirit of the man which is in him? Likewise, no one knows
the things of God except the Spirit of God.” He then adds how we attain saving
knowledge of these things: “We have received not the spirit of the world, but
the Spirit who is from God, that we might know the things freely given to us
by God.”

We must therefore credit all knowledge of the gospel and matters pertaining
to eternal life to its sole author, the Holy Spirit who illuminates us, not
to our natural intellect. By itself and without God’s Spirit it is not even
capable of spiritual things, however discerning it may be elsewhere. In its
corrupt nature it is animal, not spiritual. No wonder it can comprehend only
animal things belonging to this animal life and not spiritual things until
the Holy Spirit’s power renews it and makes it spiritual rather than animal.
Then it becomes directly and properly capable of the things of God’s Spirit
and truly grasps, understands, and judges spiritually what the gospel offers:
it approves, apprehends, and embraces the message of Christ’s cross and our
redemption through it as God’s saving power and wisdom, which by its own
natural guidance and judgment it would have rejected as foolish and
ridiculous or shameful and dishonorable.

The act of understanding and apprehending those spiritual things, which
belongs to our intellect only when regenerated and renewed by the Holy
Spirit, must therefore be attributed to God’s Spirit as the principal cause
and to our mind as the secondary instrument. The Holy Spirit’s efficacy
cooperates toward the same act as its principal cause and chief agent.

[Marginalia: Observation III. Corollary. 1 Corinthians 2:6; Hebrews 5:14; 1
Corinthians 3:1; 1 Corinthians 2:2. Tertullian.]

Third, note the remarkable amplification by which the Apostle designates the
knowledge of faith that Christ gave us through the gospel with magnificent
words breathing a wonderful abundance and richness of grace. He not only
calls it wisdom and understanding—terms signifying the noblest and most
excellent kind of knowledge—but also uses the augmentative and emphatic word
“abound,” the universal term “all,” and the phrase “mystery of the divine
will,” to set the benefit’s—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 79 | Caput I p. 37]

—greatness more clearly before us.

This refutes the blasphemy of those who dared to assert that the gospel
contains only the rudiments of wisdom, while the Apostles possessed another
abstruse wisdom which they did not publish to all believers or commit to
public writings, but handed on orally to some imaginary “perfect” people.
They wretchedly stray from 1 Corinthians 2, where Paul says that he speaks
wisdom among the mature or adult: people so advanced in the inner man that
the message of the gospel and Christ’s cross truly tastes wise to them, and
whose senses are stronger and exercised in judging spiritual things. In the
next chapter he contrasts them with *nēpioi*, infants. In the same passage he
explains what wisdom he means, and earlier in that chapter says that he
resolved to know—that is, preach—nothing among them except Jesus Christ and
him crucified. He was very far from meaning unknown human inventions and mad
dreams never revealed in the gospel.

It is astonishing that after those ancient brain-sick and insane people who
offered their inventions and delusions under the name of wisdom, and whom
Tertullian carefully refutes in *The Prescription Against Heretics*, people
were found in our own age who dared to serve up the same rancid and stinking
dish again—to say nothing of the papists’ unwritten dogmas, whether monastic
inventions or the decrees and statutes of Roman pontiffs.

[Marginalia: First conclusion. 2 Timothy 3:16. Second conclusion. Colossians
1:10.]

When we hear the Apostle assert so expressly and magnificently that, through
the disclosure of the mystery of the divine will in the preaching of the
gospel, all wisdom and understanding flowed so abundantly toward us, we must
draw two conclusions. First, we must rest fully in the sacred writings of the
Old and New Testaments, which contain perfect wisdom abundantly sufficient to
equip us perfectly for every good work and lead us to the goal of our
heavenly calling. Nothing whatever should be sought elsewhere, sewn on, or
assumed—indeed, nothing expected or desired concerning the mysteries of
eternal salvation—outside the bounds of the divine Word revealed in the
gospel, within which all spiritual wisdom and understanding are contained
not sparingly or narrowly, but plentifully and abundantly.

Second, we must nevertheless advance daily and grow in the knowledge of God,
“bearing fruit in every good work and increasing in the knowledge of God,” as
the Apostle says in Colossians 1. The wisdom Scripture contains and offers us
is perfect and absolute in itself, but it remains imperfect and capable of
continual growth in us, who as long as we live here prophesy and know only in
part. Scripture itself has such profound recesses of this manifold wisdom
that passages which at first appear shallow to beginners always disclose
greater depths to those who advance. Lambs and children may walk safely in
them, elephants may swim, and yet not reach the bottom by the most diligent
search of their minds.

[Marginalia: Observation IV. 2 Peter 1:8.]

Fourth, we must not pass over the Apostle’s joining Wisdom with *phronēsis*
in setting forth this benefit of faith—whether we translate it as Prudence or
Understanding, which concerns the same object as Prudence, as we taught in
its place. The former concerns faith and knowledge of divine and heavenly
things; the latter clearly concerns action and the Christian life, dealing
properly with things set forth in Scripture not only to be believed and known
but also to be done, followed, imitated, and expressed throughout life. Bare,
idle knowledge is not enough unless we join Prudence to Wisdom and by its aid
bring knowledge into use and practice in a truly Christian life.

Thus in the first chapter of his later Epistle, after adding and combining
with Faith and Knowledge many other moral virtues—such as endurance,
self-control, godliness, brotherly affection, and love—the Apostle Peter
immediately gives this reason: “If these things are present and abound in
you, they will make you neither idle nor unfruitful in the knowledge of our
Lord Jesus Christ.” He indicates that without these virtues, whose root is
not philosophical but spiritual and Christian Prudence, all knowledge even
of the gospel and of our Lord Jesus Christ revealed in it will be useless and
unfruitful. No one should sacrilegiously separate what God has willed to join
by so indissoluble a bond in the work of our salvation.

[Marginalia: Observation V. Corollaries. Conclusion.]

Fifth, in the completion of time Christ gathered all things: all the elect,
not only such varied and scattered Gentiles but Jews together with Gentiles;
not only all Jews and Gentiles then alive on earth or to live later, but the
part of the Church still militant on earth together with the part already
triumphant in heaven; indeed all human beings elected to salvation together
with the blessed and glorious angels into the same catholic body, to be one
body under and in him as head. From this shines forth the unity of that
universal Church, the assembly of the elect in which no reprobate or
hypocrite has any place, part, or fellowship. Here appears the closest
communion of the elect, dependent upon the one and same head, Christ, in whom
we all are one body because we are his body, whether we already live and
reign with him in heaven or still journey away from him on earth.

Accordingly, as dear as our union with our Head is to us, so precious should
be our communion with the saints, our brothers and sisters. But on what does
our communion depend, and where should its basis be sought? The papists will
say it depends on the Roman Pontiff, the visible head of the visible Church,
to whom all who wish to enter the Catholic Church must be joined and
subjected. Our Apostle teaches no such thing. He teaches that the mystery of
the divine will revealed in the gospel is that all believers in heaven and
on earth must be joined and engrafted into Christ the head, united to Christ,
and grow together into one body in and through him. Throughout the gospel
there is not even a suspicion, much less any mention, of another head besides
Christ. Therefore all who are joined to Christ through the same orthodox
faith and joined by the bond of brotherly love to everyone who now professes
or formerly professed his truth, in whatever place or age they live or lived,—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 80 | Caput I p. 38 (through Lecture XI close)]

—have entered the fellowship of the Church and been gathered into the Lord’s fold
with all the saints, even if the synagogue of the Roman Antichrist expelled
and rejected them because they rejected its idolatries and manifold
corruption of divine worship and evangelical truth.

[Marginalia: Observation VI. Protasis. Apodosis. 2 Peter 3:3–4; 1
Thessalonians 5:3; 1 Peter 1:13.]

Finally, attentive consideration of the circumstance of time denoted by the
Apostle supplies us with singular consolation when we compare our condition
with that of those who lived before Christ’s first coming in ardent desire
and intense expectation of him. Who doubts that the day seemed long and the
ages slow to them as they eagerly awaited the fulfillment of so many promises
and prophecies, sighing for this very *anakephalaiōsis* and restoration of all
things, of which the Apostle and we are speaking, after it had in their
judgment been delayed almost four thousand years? Yet as soon as the fullness
of time came, the fulfillment of the promise came with it—a fulfillment
deferred and suspended from the first fall until that day by the most wise
decree of that secret counsel.

What, then, should we do as we await the other fullness of all times, the
final consummation of the ages? What should we do until that desired hour of
the most perfect gathering arrives, after which no other is to be expected
forever and in comparison with which the gathering accomplished by Christ’s
first coming into the world was imperfect and merely begun rather than
consummated? In that restoration the number of the elect will be completed
and all whose names were written from eternity in the book of life will be
gathered into one. Things now separated by so vast an interval—some living
and triumphing in heaven, others lying on earth dissolved into ashes—will be
restored to one another and reunited in the restored union of the same
person, never again to suffer separation.

Because of this delay which our judgment and feelings commonly imagine in
Christ’s return for the last judgment, should we lose heart, relax the force
and exertion of faith, or cast all hope to the winds? Should we join the
profane and ruined people about whom Peter warned us so long ago in the third
chapter of his second Epistle: “Scoffers will come in the last days, walking
according to their own desires and saying, ‘Where is the promise of his
coming? For since the fathers fell asleep, all things remain as they were
from the beginning of creation.’” Far be it from us to come to such madness,
lest we be caught with them by that hour unprepared, secure, and expecting
nothing less, as a pregnant woman is caught by labor pains. Rather, according
to the same Apostle’s saving admonition, let us gird up the loins of our
minds with prayer and vigilance, remain sober, and hope to the end in the
grace brought to us when Christ is revealed. If we thus prepare to meet him
and equip ourselves to welcome his return with open arms and hearts filled
with hope, faith, desire, joy, and peace, that last and glorious day will come
upon us as auspicious and blessed, at the final moment of time which the
Father fixed and ordained with himself in his immutable counsel before he
gave a beginning to time together with the world. To him, therefore, through
Christ in the unity of the Holy Spirit, be all honor and glory forever. Amen.
