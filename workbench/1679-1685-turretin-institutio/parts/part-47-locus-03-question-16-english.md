QUESTION XVI

Is the will properly distinguished into antecedent and consequent,
efficacious and inefficacious, conditional and absolute? Negative.

I. Besides the distinctions of the divine will already adduced, which are
received by the orthodox, there are others that are from time to time proposed
by our adversaries. Because these are less suitable and appear to have
something insidious (ὕπουλόν τι) within them, they are rightly rejected.

[Marginal heading: Is the will properly distinguished into antecedent and
consequent will?]

II. Such is the distinction of the will into antecedent and consequent, which
is commonly reported by the scholastics. Damascene appears to have been the
first to hand it down in book 2, chapter 29 of *The Orthodox Faith*, when he
says that one will of God is antecedent (προηγουμένην) and another consequent
(ἑπομένην). It was afterward eagerly seized by the scholastics and employed in
support of their cause by the innovators, the Arminians, the Socinians, and
other patrons of universal grace.

[Marginal heading: In what sense it is admitted by some.]

III. There are indeed also some of our theologians who think it can be
retained after the Fathers, but in a sense very different from that of the
Neo-Pelagians, namely, with respect both to commands and to decrees. With
respect to commands, they will have the antecedent will take place in the
precepts, but the consequent will in the promises and threats that are added
to the precepts by way of sanction, and in the benefits or punishments that
follow a person’s obedience or rebellion. With respect to decrees, according
to their order and our manner of conceiving, the will that is conceived as
preceding is called antecedent—for example, the will to create a human being,
which precedes the will to redeem him—whereas the will that is conceived as
following another act of the will is called consequent.

[Marginal heading: In what sense it is to be rejected.]

IV. But our theologians consistently reject it in the sense in which the
scholastics and Neo-Pelagians are found to have used it, so that by the
antecedent will is understood God’s counsel concerning

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 205 | IA leaf 259 | PDF p. 259]

the salvation of all human beings universally, but by the consequent will his
decree concerning the salvation of believers and the condemnation of
unbelievers. The former is so called because it precedes an act of the human
will, whether good or evil; the latter, because it is subsequent to the human
will and depends upon its good or evil actions.

V. Thus the Arminians will have the antecedent will to be that by which God
wills something for a rational creature before every act of that creature, or
before some act of that creature; but the consequent will, that by which he
wills something for a rational creature after some act, or after many acts,
of the creature. Thus by his antecedent will God willed to establish Saul’s
kingdom, and by his consequent will he willed to cast him down from the
kingdom. By the former he willed to gather the Jews like a hen’s chicks, but by
the latter he willed to scatter them through all nations. The former can be
resisted, but the latter cannot. So Arminius against Perkins and Corvinus
against Molinaeus.

VI. This distinction is injurious to God in many ways. 1. It ascribes
contrary wills to him: namely, that by his antecedent will God wills to save
all, but by his consequent will he does not will to save all, only some; that
from eternity he seriously willed Judas’s salvation and nevertheless at the
same time and in the same moment knew that Judas would never believe, did not
will to give Judas the faith by which he would infallibly be converted, and
indeed would permit him to remain in his unbelief and perish. Who would dare
ascribe such wills to a person of sound mind, so that he would be said
seriously and ardently to will what he nevertheless knows will never occur,
and indeed will not occur because he is unwilling to effect it, when the
effect depends upon him alone? Nor can the examples adduced here by Arminius
establish this. For example, a father desires and seriously wills his son to
obey him, yet does not violently drag him to obedience; and a merchant wills
his goods to be safe but, compelled by necessity, throws them into the sea.
Likewise, he says, God, compelled by human wickedness, condemns those whom he
had willed to save if obedient. Yet things very unlike are compared here. It
is one thing to speak of human beings who, although they very seriously will
something, nevertheless cannot attain their wish except through suitable
means that are not in their power, and therefore frequently fall short of
their hope and are frustrated in their intention. It is another thing to
speak of God, who never lacks just and suitable means through which he may
attain what he intends. Therefore, for the example to fit, it must be proposed
in this way: Can a father who knows that his son will not obey unless he gives
him this disposition and will, but who nevertheless is unwilling to give it,
be said seriously to will his son to obey? Can a merchant who knows that his
goods cannot be preserved unless he himself restrains the storm, but who is
nevertheless unwilling to do so, be said seriously to desire his goods to be
safe? If examples of this kind were produced, from which it was established
that a rational creature could seriously and ardently will what it
nevertheless knew it would never do, although it could do it and although the
event certainly depended upon it alone, the deceit would readily be evident:
the adversaries ascribe to God what could not be attributed to creatures
using right reason.

VII. Second, this distinction cannot have a place in God without ascribing to
him not only foolishness and impotence, since God is imagined seriously to
intend and by a natural affection to desire what he does not attain and cannot
attain through a human being because he himself does not will it, but also
mutability, because there can be no place for the consequent will unless the
antecedent will has first been rescinded. For how could God, in the same act
of will at the same time, will to save all human beings and to condemn the
greater part of them?

VIII. Third, the antecedent will is not so much a will as a certain velleity,
an empty and ineffectual desire that is never fulfilled. Such a thing cannot
befall the most wise and most powerful God. For how could he be called most
perfect and blessed if he is unable to attain the end and goal truly

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 206 | IA leaf 260 | PDF p. 260]

intended because of the intervention and obstacle of a creature determining
itself otherwise and independently?

IX. Fourth, in this way God is subjected to the human being himself, since
the consequent will is said to depend upon the determination of the human
will, so that no one is chosen by God who has not first chosen God through his
own faith and repentance.

X. Fifth, it conflicts with the gospel, which consistently teaches that God
wills to save through Christ not absolutely everyone, but only the elect and
believers, and that the means of salvation are not offered or conferred upon
all, but only upon some. Finally, it overthrows God’s eternal election because
it leaves election uncertain, founded not on God’s good pleasure (εὐδοκίᾳ)
but on the human will, than which nothing is more uncertain and changeable.
And it imagines an election to which no execution corresponds, that is, one
that is ineffectual and inefficacious.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XI. In 1 Samuel 13:13 no antecedent will is proposed by which God willed to
establish Saul’s kingdom forever. For how could he have willed what he knew
would never occur and whose contrary he himself had decreed? Rather, when it
is said, “Surely Jehovah would now have established your kingdom forever,” it
indicates only that Saul’s sin and rebellion were the cause of his kingdom’s
soon coming to an end and passing to another. This would not have occurred if
he had remained in his duty according to the promises made to godliness.

XII. This twofold will cannot be confirmed from Matthew 23:37. 1. It is not
said that God willed to scatter those whom he had willed to gather, but only
that Christ willed to gather those whom Jerusalem—that is, the leaders of the
people—were unwilling to have gathered. Yet notwithstanding their effort,
Christ did not cease to gather those whom he willed. Hence Augustine,
*Enchiridion*, chapter 97: “She indeed was unwilling for her children to be
gathered by him, but even while she was unwilling, he gathered those of her
children whom he willed.” Jerusalem must therefore be distinguished here from
her children, as the words themselves prove, as does the sequence of the
chapter, in which from verse 13 through verse 37 Christ addresses the scribes
and Pharisees and reproaches them because they themselves do not enter the
kingdom of heaven and do not allow those entering to enter. 2. The will in
question here is not the will of decree, which is one and simple, but the will
of precept, which relates to calling and is often repeated through the
preaching of the Word: “How often I willed.” Thus Christ speaks here as the
minister of the circumcision.

XIII. If God granted the first world a period of 120 years in order to invite
it to repentance, an antecedent will cannot be inferred from this, as though
he had decreed to spare it, because this would conflict with the eternal
decree to send the flood upon the world. Rather, only the commanding will is
indicated, which prescribed to it the duty of repentance in order to render
it inexcusable, given its stubbornness.

XIV. It is said in vain that the distinction between antecedent and consequent
will was invented to reconcile various passages of Scripture that would
otherwise seem inconsistent (ἀσύστατα): that God promised to bring the people
into Canaan and nevertheless willed to destroy them in the wilderness, Exodus
32 and Numbers 14; that he wills to save all and nevertheless to condemn very
many; and that he calls many to the wedding feast whom he nevertheless
determined to exclude from it. For there is another, far more certain and true
method of reconciliation. The promise to bring the people in does not
conflict with the will to punish because it was made under a condition,
Exodus 32:34, and was actually fulfilled—if not with respect to those
individuals who fell in the wilderness because of unbelief, Deuteronomy 1:35
and Hebrews 4:6, then at least with respect to the nation itself in general,
which at last arrived there. The will to save all human beings does not
overturn the decree of reprobation to pass over many, because it is not the
decreeing will, only the commanding and approving will, and it is not
universal,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 207 | IA leaf 261 | PDF p. 261]

regarding each and every person, but only general, extending without
distinction to any. Thus by the will of precept those can be called to the
wedding feast who nevertheless have been excluded from it from eternity by
the will of good pleasure.

[Marginal heading: The efficacious and inefficacious will.]

XV. The second distinction customarily brought forward by the adversaries is
that between efficacious and inefficacious will. If it is understood in such a
sense that the efficacious will corresponds to the will of decree and is so
called because it is always fulfilled and cannot be resisted, while the
inefficacious will coincides with the will of precept and is so called because
it often remains ineffectual on account of human rebellion, it could in some
measure be tolerated, although the expression is less suitable and open to
misrepresentation (εὐδιάβλητος). But if it is referred to the decreeing will
alone, as it is understood by the adversaries, who maintain that velleities
and ineffectual desires in which God falls short of his end and does not
attain what he intends can belong to God—a category that includes the
conditional decrees they attribute to him—it cannot be admitted. For
Scripture testifies that God’s counsel is immutable and that his will cannot
be resisted, Isaiah 46:10 and Romans 9:19. But if his will is not resisted, it
will surely accomplish what he intends necessarily. Second, an inefficacious
will cannot be attributed to God without charging him either with ignorance,
because he did not know that the event would not succeed, or with impotence,
because he could not bring about the effect that he intended. Nor should it
be said that this happens not from impotence but from good pleasure, because
he was unwilling to apply so great a power as could infallibly bring the
thing to effect. For by this very statement it is concluded that God did not
seriously will this, since one who seriously intends something employs all
the means in his power to attain it. Finally, the same reasons that teach that
there is no antecedent will prove that there is no inefficacious will.

XVI. Although every will of God is efficacious, because it always attains the
event it intends, it need not therefore be efficient. For efficiency is only
in good things, but efficacy is also in evil things.

XVII. Passages that attribute desire or a wish to God do not at once prove
that some will in him is inefficacious. Rather, things expressed in a human
manner must be understood in a manner worthy of God (θεοπρεπῶς), unless we
wish to adopt the ravings of the Anthropomorphites. If they refer to the past,
they signify nothing other than a serious disapproval of sins committed,
together with a vehement reproach of human ingratitude and a declaration of
the good things they forfeited and the evils into which they fell by their
sins. Understand in this way Psalm 81:14: “Would that my people had obeyed me,
that Israel had walked in my ways,” and so forth; and Isaiah 48:18: “Would
that you had attended to my commandments; your peace would have become like a
river.” But if they look to the future, as in Deuteronomy 32:29, “Would that
they had such a mind that they might fear me and keep my commandments, that
it might be well with them,” and so forth, they indicate only a serious
command supported by its promises and threats, by which God declares what he
approves as honorable and holy and how greatly those who obey him please him.

XVIII. God’s testimony in Ezekiel 18:23 that he does not will the death of the
sinner, but his conversion and life, does not support an inefficacious will or
velleity in God. For the word חפץ that occurs there denotes not so much desire
as delight and complacency, so that God is said not to delight in the
punishment of the wicked insofar as it is the destruction of the creature,
although he wills it insofar as it denotes the exercise of his justice. Thus
he is said to will the repentance of sinners approvingly and preceptively, as
a thing most pleasing to himself and expressed in his commands, although he
does not will it decretively and effectively with respect to all.

[Marginal heading: The absolute and conditional will.]

XIX. The third distinction is that of the will into absolute and conditional.
The former is said not to depend on anything else, especially on an external
condition; the latter is

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 208 | IA leaf 262 | PDF p. 262]

suspended upon a condition outside God—for example, when God is conceived to
will to save Judas if he believes. This distinction, although it might be
admitted less properly by some with respect to the will of precept because
promises and threats are proposed conditionally, is nevertheless rightly
rejected if it is understood of the will of decree and good pleasure, as the
adversaries will have it. For it is unworthy of God, since it conflicts with
his independence as well as his wisdom and power, and because it remains
doubtful and uncertain, being suspended from the changeable human will, and
thus is inefficacious and futile, through which God must often fall short of
his end.

XX. Again, the condition under which God is conceived to will something will
either certainly be future from God’s decree, or it will certainly not be
future and indeed not even possible. If the former, the will shall no longer
be conditional but absolute, since God is assumed to will the condition—not,
of course, conditionally, because otherwise there would be an infinite
regress, but absolutely. If the latter, God will be held seriously to intend
something under a condition that will never be future and indeed is
impossible, which God himself does not will to give, although he alone can
give it. If this cannot be said of a wise human being, it cannot be attributed
to God without the greatest injury to the most wise and most powerful divine
Majesty.

XXI. Meanwhile, lest we go astray here, the conditional will must be viewed
in two ways: either *a priori* and antecedently, when it is suspended upon a
condition, or *a posteriori* and consequently, when its execution takes place
upon the intervention of some condition in the creature. In the latter sense
the will can be called conditional, though less accurately, because God’s
decrees ordain the means along with the end by which they are put into
execution. Thus he who decrees salvation for us also decrees faith and
repentance as the means for obtaining it. But in the former sense, which is
the one at issue, it cannot be admitted, because in this respect all God’s
good pleasure is absolute.

XXII. It is one thing for the condition to stand on the side of the internal
act, or of the volition itself; it is another for it to stand on the side of
the external object, or of the thing willed. In the latter sense we grant that
the things willed are conditional, but not in the former, as though the
volition itself depended upon a condition. For example, the salvation
decreed for us by God can be called conditional because it is not given
except through the intervention of faith. But the decree of salvation itself
cannot be called conditional, for it is absolute both with respect to the
end—that is, salvation—and with respect to the means that God determined to
bestow absolutely and without condition upon all his elect.

XXIII. Therefore the proposition “God wills salvation for human beings if
they believe” can have two senses. Either it signifies that God wills or
determines that salvation should come to us under the condition of faith,
that is, provided we believe. This is certainly true, but here only our
salvation becomes conditional, not God’s will. Indeed, God’s will establishes
both the condition and salvation. Or it signifies that, once the condition of
faith has been posited or at least foreseen, a will to confer salvation upon
human beings arises in God. In this sense the proposition is false, because
nothing temporal can be the cause of what is eternal. Thus the will in God is
not to be conceived as suspended antecedently upon a condition, but as
positing the condition itself consequently as a means. He does not will to
save human beings if they believe; rather, he wills them to believe in order
that they may be saved, or he wills to save them through the faith that he
determined to give them. But these matters will have to be discussed more
fully later, when the decrees are treated.
