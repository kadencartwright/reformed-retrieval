QUESTION XIX.

ON THE PRIMACY OF THE POPE.

Is the Roman pope Peter’s successor in the monarchy, or ecumenical
pontificate? We deny.

[Marginalia: Is the pope Peter’s successor?]

I. It is well known that the primacy of the pope rests upon this foundation:
that Peter obtained it from Christ and would transmit it to his successors,
who are none other than the Roman pontiffs. But just as we have already proved
that no such monarchical primacy was given to Peter, so now we will prove that
the pope cannot be Peter’s successor in whatever privilege Peter may have
obtained, because it was extraordinary and special and could not pass to
others.

[Marginalia: The position of the adversaries.]

II. Bellarmine, in book 2, chapter 12, *On the Pontiff*, makes various
observations about this question. First, he distinguishes “between succession
itself and the ground of succession. The succession of the Roman pontiff to
Peter’s pontificate is from Christ’s institution and therefore rests upon
divine law. But the ground of the succession—namely, that the Roman pontiff
rather than someone else, for example the bishop of Antioch, succeeds—had its
beginning in Peter’s action, because Peter fixed his see at Rome and held it
until death, though he could have placed it elsewhere. (2.) Although it may
perhaps not be by divine law that the Roman pontiff, because he is the Roman
pontiff, succeeds Peter in the governance of the whole church, nevertheless,
if someone asks without qualification whether the Roman pontiff is by divine
law the pastor and head of the whole church, it must certainly be affirmed.
(3.) Although it is not by divine law that the Roman pontiff, as Roman
pontiff, succeeds Peter, nevertheless this pertains to the catholic faith,
because for something to be a matter of faith and for it to be by divine law
are not the same.” But the same man, being little consistent with himself,
asserts the contrary in book 4, chapter 4, near the end: “Since, therefore, it
is not established how Christ commanded Peter to locate his see at Rome, it is
not a matter of faith that the see was established at Rome by a divine and
immutable command; nevertheless, as we have said, it is highly probable and
is piously to be believed.”

[Marginalia: It is proved that the pope is not Peter’s successor.]

III. We deny that the pope was or can be called Peter’s successor in the way
they allege. These are our reasons. First, if he is Peter’s successor, it will
be either with respect to the common and ordinary ministry of preaching the
word, administering the sacraments, and exercising discipline, or with
respect to the special and extraordinary office of the apostolate. In the
former sense, however, succession does not pertain to one particular person,
but generally to all true ministers of Christ and pastors of the church, who
are therefore commonly called by the Fathers “successors of the divine
apostles.” In the latter sense, no one could succeed the apostles, because
their office was temporary and extraordinary, appointed for laying the
foundations of the Christian church, and therefore had no successors in the
same office. This can be gathered even from their distinguishing marks. For
these things were peculiar to the apostles: (1) the sight of Christ, so that
they could be witnesses of Christ’s resurrection (Acts 1 and 1 Cor. 9:1); (2)
an immediate call (Gal. 1:1); (3) infallibility in doctrine; (4) an ecumenical
ministry; and (5) the gift of miracles. Since these were granted to them alone
as apostles, they would be sought in vain among successors.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 152 | IA leaf 186 | PDF p. 186]

IV. Second, no one succeeded the apostles in their personal apostolic
preeminence. Therefore, no one succeeded Peter in it either, for the same
principle had to apply to them all. Nor should Peter’s privilege with respect
to ecclesiastical monarchy be raised as an objection, because this is the
*first falsehood*, which was refuted above.

V. Third, the pope boasts no less in Paul’s authority and succession than in
Peter’s, yet no one ever dreamed of Paul’s primacy. Hence the pontifical seal
displays the image of each, and in former times those who came to Rome for
religious devotion were said to come *to the thresholds of the apostles*, for
each is reported to be a founder of the Roman church, as Irenaeus has it in
book 3, chapter 3. Epiphanius also calls Peter and Paul bishops of the Roman
church in Heresy 27. To this pertains Arnauld’s French book *On the Greatness
of the Roman Church*, where the author intends to prove that “the authority of
the Roman church was founded upon Peter and Paul, who were equals.” This book
was badly received at Rome because it made, so to speak, a diarchy out of a
monarchy and assigned a two-headed head to one body.

VI. Although Peter had been bishop of Rome, the Roman bishops would have
succeeded him only in the episcopate of that city, but not in the apostolate
or primacy, which were peculiar to Peter’s person—no more than the bishops of
Antioch, who are no less Peter’s successors than they are. Nor should it be
said here with Bellarmine in book 1, chapter 12, *On the Roman Pontiff*, that
“the bishop of Antioch succeeded Peter while he was living, but the bishop of
Rome succeeded him when he was dying.” For (1) why does the right of
primogeniture not belong to the bishop of Antioch rather than the Roman? (2)
They are not yet agreed among themselves about Peter’s first successor,
whether it was Linus or Clement. If Linus is named, as most maintain, he
succeeded Peter while Peter was living at Rome no differently than Evodius
did at Antioch. But if Clement is said to have been Peter’s first and immediate
successor, so that they may derive Peter’s primacy from the dying Peter to the
Roman pontiff, what then will become of Linus and Anacletus, whom the common
consensus of the best authors testifies to have been Roman bishops before
Clement? Will they say that “Linus and Anacletus were only rural bishops” and
that, notwithstanding them, Clement was Peter’s immediate successor in the
episcopate? But the absurdity of this invention is evident from the fact that
rural bishops were appointed not in cities but *in rural districts*, that is,
in fields subject to a city or in regions of the same diocese remote from the
city. A rural bishop is “a bishop over a rural district,” or “of the rural
district,” who could do nothing of greater importance without the bishop of
the city, to whom both he and the region are subject, as Apostolic Canon 39
says. Will they say that Peter, with his suffering imminent, did indeed leave
the episcopal see to Clement, but that Clement did not immediately succeed
Peter; rather, Linus and Anacletus were supreme pontiffs before Clement,
because Clement, out of modesty and humility, declined the episcopate while
Linus and Cletus lived, since they had been Peter’s assistants in the
episcopal office, as Bellarmine says in book 2, chapter 5? Or was it so that
the example of this nomination would not pass to posterity, as Genebrard says
in book 3 of his *Chronicle* under Linus? But is it humility to evade an office
that has been imposed? Did Clement have to fear falling under suspicion of
pride when the episcopal office at that time was not one of authority but of
ministry? Why did he not decline while Peter was alive, so that Peter himself
might designate someone else? Or, if there was some reason why he wished
Linus and Cletus to be preferred to himself, why did Peter not see it and
entrust the episcopate to Linus instead? Again, if Linus was not bishop of Rome
before Peter’s death, but Clement was ordained bishop by Peter when death was
imminent and was left by him as his lawful successor, then Linus and Anacletus
obtained that episcopate by no right but were usurpers. Finally, will it be
added that the senators of the Roman church did not accept Clement but elected
Linus, recognizing that such a

[1847 vol. III printed p. 153 | IA leaf 187 | PDF p. 187]

form of nomination could gravely harm the church in the future, as the *Book
of Ceremonies*, book 1, section 11, has it? But in this way either impiety or
folly is imputed to Peter, because in a matter of such great importance he did
something that would be an evil and destructive example.

[Marginalia: It is proved that the pope was not the ecumenical pontiff. 1.
From the equality of the patriarchs.]

VII. Besides these arguments, which establish that the Roman bishop did not
succeed Peter, there are not a few others proving that he was anything but the
ecumenical pontiff. First, from the equality of the patriarchs, because he was
one of the number of patriarchs who were mutually equal in authority, although
some were earlier or later than others in order. This is evident (1) from the
Council of Nicaea, Canon 6: “Let the ancient custom prevail in Egypt, Libya,
and Pentapolis, so that the bishop of Alexandria has authority over them all,
since the same custom applies to the bishop of Rome.” From this it is evident
that equal authority is attributed in each place to the bishop of Alexandria
and to the Roman, whatever the papists may say, as will be shown more fully in
the following question. (2) It is evident from the Council of Constantinople,
in which, because Constantine had removed the seat of the empire from Rome and
transferred it to Constantinople, the second place after the Roman was given
to the bishop of Constantinople. Canon 3 says: “Let the bishop of
Constantinople obtain the prerogatives of honor after the bishop of Rome,
because it is New Rome.” (3) It is evident from Canon 28 of the fourth
ecumenical council at Chalcedon and Canon 36 of the Trullan Council, reported
by Gratian in distinction 22. By them it was decreed that the see of
Constantinople would enjoy equal prerogatives of honor with the Roman see and
be exalted like it in ecclesiastical affairs, yet so as to be second after it.

[Marginalia: 2. From the convocation of councils.]

VIII. 2. From the convocation of councils, because if the Roman bishop was the
ecumenical pontiff and monarch of the church, councils would have been
convened by his authority and command, since a monarch allows no assemblies
to be held in his empire without public authority. Yet nothing of the kind can
be said either about particular councils or about ecumenical ones. For we read
of no particular councils convened by the Roman bishop except in his own
province, and Bellarmine admits that diocesan councils had to be convened by
the bishop, provincial councils by the archbishop, and national councils by a
patriarch or primate. As for ecumenical councils, it is certain that none was
convened by the Roman pontiff until after the diminution of the empire and the
confirmation of his tyranny. It is established that no bulls or letters were
heard of or seen, such as in more recent times have been prefixed with great
pomp at the beginning of councils to declare that their convocation was made
by the authority and command of the Roman bishop. On the contrary, it is
established that the emperors convened synods by their letters, and that the
synods testify that they were convened by the emperor’s authority, but never
by the Roman pontiff’s. Indeed, the Roman pontiffs more than once earnestly
pleaded with the emperor for the convocation of a synod, and sometimes it was
denied to them, as is evident from ecclesiastical history. That the first
Council of Nicaea was convened by Emperor Constantine is so well known that it
needs no proof. Eusebius, an eyewitness, declares it in book 3, chapter 6, *On
the Life of the Emperor*; Socrates in book 1, chapter 5; and Theodoret in the
place cited, chapter 9. The second was Constantinople I in A.D. 383, which
Sozomen in book 7, chapter 7, and Socrates in book 5, chapter 8, teach was
convened by Theodosius; and the bishops themselves write to the emperor that
they had been convened according to his rescript. The third was Ephesus in
A.D. 434, which, as often as it says in its acts that it was assembled, so
often acknowledges the emperor’s authority, but nowhere that of the Roman
bishop. See Evagrius, book 1, chapters 3 and 4. Chalcedon in A.D. 454 was
convened through the emperors Valentinian and Marcian, as is proved by the day
and place appointed by the emperor, even though Pope Leo petitioned in Epistle
43 to Marcian, and by the acts, which teach that the synod was held by decree
of the most pious emperors Valentinian and Marcian. The fifth was the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 154 | IA leaf 188 | PDF p. 188]

Second Council of Constantinople in the year 532, called by Justinian, as
Evagrius testifies in book 4, chapter 37: “Justinian convened the fifth
council,” and Nicephorus in book 17, chapter 37. The synod confirms the same
thing in its petition to the emperor, as do Justinian’s own letters placed at
the beginning of the synod. In them, not only is its convocation decreed, but
the emperor also supports his right with preceding examples, so that all
might know he was usurping nothing. The sixth is the Trullan Council, which in
Act 1 is said to have “assembled according to the imperial decree.” The same
is read about Nicaea II in the year 781, assembled by the emperors Constantine
and Irene, and about Constantinople IV, which is the eighth ecumenical council
and is said in the preface to have been called by Emperor Basil. From these it
is plainly evident that the right of convening councils belonged not to the
Roman pontiffs but to the emperors. Hence Socrates rightly says in the preface
to book 5 of his *Ecclesiastical History* that he mentioned the emperors “since
from the time they began to be Christians the affairs of the church appeared
to depend upon their will, and indeed the greatest councils both were convened
by their judgment and are still convened.” And Cardinal Cusanus, book 2,
chapter 25, *On Catholic Concord*, admits that “all eight universal councils
were convened by the emperors, and the Roman pontiff, just like the other
patriarchs, received sacred commands to come or to send someone to the
council.” From this it is gathered how vain Bellarmine’s escape is: “Custom
can here be set against custom; if the emperors called four or five councils,
the Romans called more than twelve.” For the most ancient custom, such as we
allege, extending through the eighth century and beyond, is far preferable to
the custom of the most recent centuries, in which everything began to rush
toward the worse through the ambition of the Roman bishops. Add that the
councils said to have been convened by the pontiffs were not so much
ecumenical as national, composed only of Latins, except for the Council of
Florence, which a few Greeks attended.

[Marginalia: 3. From the prohibition of appeals to the Roman bishop.]

IX. 3. From the prohibition of appeals to the Roman bishop. For if he was
always acknowledged as the ecumenical pontiff and monarch of the church, it
was lawful to appeal to him as the supreme judge, from whom there could be no
further appeal. Yet it can be gathered from various sources that this ought
not to have been done. (1) From the authority of those councils which wished
ecclesiastical cases to be judged and concluded within their own province.
Canon 5 of Nicaea says: “He is to be regarded as excommunicated who has been
condemned in a provincial council.” This could not have been said if he had
the right to appeal elsewhere. This is confirmed by Canons 6 and 15 of the
Council of Antioch and by Justinian’s Novella 123. (2) Because appeals to the
Roman bishop are often condemned. In Epistle 55 to Cornelius of Rome,
concerning Fortunatus and Felicissimus, who had been condemned in Africa and
fled to Rome, Cyprian sharply rebukes him for such an act: first, because each
person’s case is to be heard where the crime was committed; then, because a
portion of the flock has been assigned to individual pastors to govern, as
those who will render an account of their conduct to God, so that the Roman
can no more reconsider the judgment of the Carthaginian than the latter can
reconsider his; and finally, because the authority of the Africans is not less
than that of those to whom they had fled. (3) The eastern bishops testify the
same thing. According to Socrates, book 2, chapter 11, in reply to Julius, who
had written to them about the restoration of Athanasius, they said: “If some
had perhaps been cast out of the church by their judgment, the matter ought
not to be decided by him, for when he drove Novatus from the church, they did
not contradict him at all.” Although they are not to be excused in the
condemnation of Athanasius, it is not credible that, if it had been lawful to
appeal to Rome, they would have dared so openly to contradict this right or to
accuse Julius of acting against the canons of the church, as Sozomen notes in
book 3, chapter 7, while Julius would have said nothing in reply about this
right of appeals in so certain and just a case. (4) Damasus, bishop of Rome,
writing

[1847 vol. III printed p. 155 | IA leaf 189 | PDF p. 189]

to Theophilus and Anysius in Epistle 79 among the epistles of Ambrose,
acknowledges that he cannot judge concerning Bonosus after the decree of a
particular synod, which had appointed other judges. (5) The Council of Milevis,
chapter 22, condemns all overseas appeals: “Those who think they ought to
appeal overseas are to be received into communion by no one within Africa.”
But to Africans Rome was overseas. Nor should it be said that the words
“unless perhaps an appeal is made to the apostolic see” were added to the same
canon, as it is found in Gratian, cause 2, question 6, because Bellarmine
admits that it was enacted chiefly on account of Rome, so that it would not be
lawful to appeal across the sea. Nor, as Bellarmine wishes, does it deal only
with appeals by presbyters and lesser clergy, while those by bishops were
permitted. For the Romans also approve and admit the appeals of presbyters, as
was seen in the case of Apiarius, an African presbyter whom the Roman pontiff
wished to restore after he had been condemned in Africa. Then the canon,
copied from the Latin codices, was truncated by Bellarmine, for in Greek it
reads: “They appeal only to African councils or to the primates of their
provinces, as was also often decreed concerning bishops.”

[Marginalia: 4. From appeals from the Roman bishop.]

X. 4. From appeals from the Roman bishop. For if an appeal could be taken
from the judgment of the Roman bishop, by that very fact he was not
acknowledged as the ecumenical pontiff, since it is lawful to appeal from
inferiors to superiors, but never from the supreme. Kings permit appeals to
themselves, but never from themselves. This is established as having happened
in the case of Donatus of Casae Nigrae, who appealed from Melchiades, bishop
of Rome, and was judged a second time by the bishop of Arles. Appealing from
him again, he was judged by the emperor. See Augustine, Epistle 162, who
traces this whole history at length. To this pertains the fact that the Roman
bishops themselves, when they met opposition, referred the judgment to a
synod. They would not have done this if they had recognized themselves as
supreme judges. This was seen in Liberius, who, when Emperor Constantius
opposed him in the case of Athanasius, nowhere boasted of ecumenical authority
but asked for a council to be called, according to Theodoret, book 2, chapter
16. In the case of Chrysostom, Innocent says in Sozomen, book 8, chapter 26,
that only a synod could provide a remedy. In the case of Anatolius, Leo writes
to Emperor Theodosius in Epistle 33 that he should convene a council to judge
the man.

[Marginalia: 5. From disagreement with the Roman bishop.]

XI. 5. From the disagreement of various persons with the Roman bishop. For if
he had been regarded as the ecumenical pontiff, no one would have dared to
disagree with him. Yet this was done by the Asian churches, which freely
disagreed with Victor in the Easter controversy and were therefore
excommunicated by him. But this scarcely pleased the bishops, who thought it
lawful not only to disagree with him but also dared to rebuke him sharply, as
Irenaeus of Lyons did—something anyone today would not get away with doing.
See Eusebius, book 5, chapter 24. The same thing is evident from the example of
Cyprian, who writes against Stephen, the bishop of Rome, concerning
rebaptism. There he refutes Stephen’s letter on the matter in a sharp and
rebuking style. Augustine discusses this in book 5, chapter 25, *Against the
Donatists*, where he notes that Cyprian dealt with Stephen as a brother, not
as an ecumenical prince or lord, and thought he committed no absurdity if he
not only disagreed with Stephen but rebuked him.

[Marginalia: 6. From the subjection of the Roman bishops to the emperors.]

XII. 6. The same thing could be shown from the subjection of the Roman
bishops to the emperors, who often deposed them. Thus Constantius sent
Liberius into exile; Justinian sent Silverius; King Theodoric cast John into
prison; Otto I deposed John XII; and Henry III deposed Gregory VI. The Roman
pontiffs acknowledged them as their lords. Gregory I, book 3, Epistle 61,
“calls the most serene emperor his lord, but himself the emperor’s unworthy
servant, who

[1847 vol. III printed p. 156 | IA leaf 190 | PDF p. 190]

owes him obedience,” as he does also in book 4, Epistles 31 and 34. Nor should
it be thought that Gregory said these things out of feigned humility, in the
way the pontiff calls himself the servant of servants while actually
conducting himself as lord of lords. The very words testify to the contrary,
and the same thing is gathered from book 2, Epistle 61, in which he calls
himself the emperor’s servant by the law of the commonwealth. Shortly
afterward he introduces God speaking thus to the emperor: “I entrusted my
priests to your hand.” There Gregory also admits that he is subject to the
emperor’s commands. The same emperors appointed pontiffs as delegates under
imperial authority in ecclesiastical cases, as Constantine did with respect
to Melchiades, the Roman pontiff, in the case of Caecilian against Donatus of
Casae Nigrae. When they convened ecumenical councils, they were accustomed to
summon the Roman bishop just as they did the other bishops, and indeed by
authority, not only by entreaties. This is evident from the words of Theodosius
and Valentinian to Cyril concerning the convocation of the Synod of Ephesus.
Therefore, in Epistle 13 to the empress Pulcheria, Leo humbly excuses himself
because he could not attend the synod in accordance with the emperor’s
letters. So also, when the Roman bishops dealt with ecclesiastical matters,
they petitioned humbly. See Leo’s Epistle 24 to Emperor Theodosius and Epistle
26 to the empress Pulcheria. Finally, by their authority the emperors
confirmed or rejected the elected Roman bishop, as is evident from Platina
under Pelagius II: “Nothing done by the clergy in electing a pontiff was valid
unless the emperor approved his election.” The same is confirmed by Sabellico,
Ennead 8, book 5. But now it is far otherwise: the pontiff alleges that the
emperor’s election is invalid if he himself has not confirmed it.

[Marginalia: 7. From the equality of the titles by which the bishops are
designated.]

XIII. 7. From the equality of the titles by which the other bishops designate
themselves along with the Roman bishop. Not only does the Roman bishop himself
call the others colleagues, brothers, and fellow bishops—for those who are
over others are sometimes accustomed to lower themselves in this way, as when
emperors call their soldiers fellow soldiers—but the other bishops also
addressed the Roman this way from the beginning as a sign of equality.
Cyprian, addressing Cornelius the bishop of Rome, Lucius, and Stephen, writes,
“Cyprian to his brother Cornelius, greetings,” and likewise throughout
Epistles 41, 48, 58, and 67. He is never read to have used those magnificent
titles which afterward gained currency. But could the pontiff today tolerate
bishops or cardinals addressing him in this way? To say here with Pamelius
that “the ancient bishops used that mode of address which belonged more to
gentleness and benevolence than to profane domination” is to admit that the
proud and ambitious names “most blessed and most holy father and lord,” which
were afterward introduced and are used today, belong to profane domination,
not to gentleness and benevolence, and savor of the disposition of antichrist,
not of Christ and his servants.

XIV. Finally, it can be evident from various considerations that the pope is
not Peter’s successor either by right or in fact. First, by right. (1) If such
a successor had been given by Christ, Christ would have informed the apostles
about him, just as such explicit mention is made of the Jewish high priest in
the Old Testament. But it was proved above that this was not done. (2) If a
worthy successor had to be sought for Peter, this would with far greater right
have been owed to the apostle John, who outlived Peter by almost thirty years.
(3) One who does not perform Peter’s duties, such as feeding Christ’s sheep,
teaching by the word, and other things of that kind, cannot by right arrogate
Peter’s succession to himself. Nor can it be said either that “he is detained
by legitimate hindrances,” for there can be none that should call him away
from exercising his office, or that “the pope performs it through others,”
for Peter and the other apostles did this themselves after setting other
business aside. Nor can a deputy transfer his office to someone else. Nor can
one be called the vicar of Christ who either refuses or fails to discharge the
duties of Christ entrusted to him in preaching the gospel.

XV. Second, in fact. (1) Nothing is more certain than that the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 157 | IA leaf 191 | PDF p. 191]

Roman bishops were formerly chosen by the votes of the Roman people and
clergy. But who gave the Roman people this power and right to provide the
universal church with a head? (2) The papists do not agree about the truth of
the succession, since some place Linus and then Clement after Peter, others
instead put Clement next, some make Cletus next after Linus, others again
substitute Anacletus for Cletus, and some affirm that Linus occupied the see
while Peter was alive and looking on. This diversity easily proves that
whatever is handed down about this matter is of doubtful credibility. Again,
they contend sharply among themselves over whose possession it was: the see’s
or its occupant’s. Was the ecumenical pontificate annexed to the Roman see, so
that it passed from there to the bishop, or rather did the bishop take Peter’s
place, and from him did that see receive its prerogative over all others? Then
was that succession a matter of divine or ecclesiastical law? Some allege that
this privilege was made peculiar to the Roman bishop by divine law, while
others say it was only by ecclesiastical law, as Bellarmine observed above. On
the latter account, nothing would prevent the succession from being
transferred from the Roman bishop to others. Finally, from the arguments
already presented, it is certain that for many centuries no trace of that
ecumenical pontificate appears.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XVI. Succession is necessary in the church, but it is the succession of
doctrine and of the common pastoral office, not of place or persons and the
extraordinary apostolic office. Pastors succeed the apostles in the former
way, but no one could succeed them in the latter. Nor, if the bishop or pontiff
exists for the sake of the church, does it follow that the same form of
government always remains and that the same office with the same authority is
transmitted from one person to another. The unity of the church does not
depend upon an external form of polity, for it has been one from the
beginning, though it has not been governed in one way. Its beginnings must be
distinguished from its progress. There are some who must perpetually
accompany the church, such as pastors and teachers, but others who were
appointed to found it, such as apostles and evangelists, had to remain only
for a time.

XVII. Succession in the Old Testament cannot pertain to this, because
Scripture clearly teaches that divine law provided for succession to Aaron.
But the New Testament says nothing about Peter’s successor. The apostles
indeed had to be succeeded, and they actually have been succeeded in teaching
with respect to the ministry; and a perpetual succession of pastors must be
granted in it. But this succession is not bound to one see or to any particular
person. Then there was a very great difference between the priesthood of the
Old Testament and the ministry of the new, especially in this: the high priest
was a figure of Christ, not of Peter or the pope. He had to be succeeded
because he was mortal, but Christ does not, because he is eternal.

XVIII. The primacy sometimes attributed to the Roman church is not founded
upon Peter’s succession. Rather, it is given by reason of the city, because he
was bishop of the city which held rule over the world, as the Council of
Chalcedon explicitly says: “because that city was the imperial capital.” For
the same reason, the second place among the patriarchs was assigned to the
bishop of Constantinople—not because he was the successor of the apostle
Andrew, but because Constantinople was New Rome, that is, because the seat of
the empire had been transferred there. “For,” as the Fathers of that council
say, “the Fathers reasonably gave the prerogative of honor to the see of old
Rome because that city was queen of the world, and, aiming at the same end,
the 150 bishops most beloved by God gave equal prerogatives of honor to the
most holy throne of New Rome.”

XIX. 1. The right of appeals to the Roman see cannot be proved from the canon
put forward as a canon of the Council of Nicaea, namely: “Let all bishops who
are assailed or accused in certain weightier cases, whenever necessary,
freely appeal to the apostolic see and flee to it as to a mother, etc.” For
that canon is proved to be spurious and adulterated. (1) Because the Synod of
Nicaea issued only twenty

[1847 vol. III printed p. 158 | IA leaf 192 | PDF p. 192]

canons, no more, among which this one about appeals is not found. Theodoret,
speaking about the Fathers of the council in book 1, chapter 8, says: “They
wrote twenty laws concerning ecclesiastical administration.” Nicephorus
confirms this in book 8, chapter 19. And Pope Stephen, according to Gratian,
distinction 16, says: “Only twenty chapters of the Synod of Nicaea are held in
the holy Roman church.” The sixth Council of Carthage lists only twenty
canons. (2) Because, if it were not spurious, the Roman bishop Zosimus would
not have been forced to cite other canons from the Synod of Sardica under the
name of Nicaea, from which he was convicted of falsehood and driven off. But
Bellarmine absurdly and gratuitously invents in book 2, chapter 25, as a means
of washing away this fault, that the Sardican canon was reported as Nicene
because those two councils were regarded as one and the same. (3) Because the
canon conflicts with Canons 5 and 6 of Nicaea, which are acknowledged as
genuine. For the fifth permits appeals from a bishop, not to a bishop but to a

divides ecclesiastical jurisdiction among the Roman, Alexandrian, and
Antiochene patriarchs and makes the latter two equal to the Roman. 2. Nor is
it better proved from the canon of the Council of Chalcedon proposed by the
papists, by which a bishop is given the freedom to appeal to the most blessed
bishop of old Rome, who is called the vicar of the apostolic throne, for it
contains various indications of its own falsity. Who could believe that those
Fathers, who made the bishop of Constantinople equal to the Roman even against
the will of Leo’s legates and wished him to be second only in order but “to be
equally magnified” in ecclesiastical affairs, intended to decree such appeals?
Indeed, Canon 9 establishes the contrary: “If a bishop or cleric has a dispute
with the metropolitan of his province, let him approach the exarch of the
diocese or the throne of the imperial city of Constantinople and litigate
before him.” 3. Nor can it be proved from the Council of Sardica, which was a
particular, not an ecumenical, council, and whose authority therefore cannot
be set against the Council of Nicaea defining the contrary. Besides, those
canons were abrogated at the Council of Chalcedon when the bishop of
Constantinople was made equal to the Roman.

XX. Appeals, if any were made, were either made by wicked and ill-disposed
men who attempted to set the churches against each other and sought to be
absolved from certain censures they had incurred among others. They did not so
much seek a lawful reconsideration of the judgment as a testimony of
repentance and communion, so that, after making their repentance acceptable
to this or that bishop, they would be received by him into communion and then
carry letters to others. In this way they hoped to find some supporters for
themselves, or at least trusted that they could deceive those to whom they
were not well known, as was done by Marcion, Fortunatus, Basilides, and
others. But if appeals were made by pious men, they were not so much appeals
to a supreme judge who would command others by his authority as recourse to
an honorary arbiter who above others could assist them with counsel and aid.
This was not because of any judicial authority but because of the friendly
fellowship of the same communion: the Roman bishops admitted them into their
communion as confessors of the same faith and then undertook the defense of
their cause. This was done by Athanasius, Chrysostom, and Flavian.

XXI. Whatever is brought forward from councils to establish the primacy of
the pope is either fictitious and distorted, or concerns only *good order*, or
is said on account of the eminence of the see, as the Council of Chalcedon has
it, or on account of the piety and patience of the earlier pontiffs; and this
was conferred upon them not by right but at discretion. For it is certain that
an authoritative primacy was first obtained through wicked arts by Boniface
from Phocas the emperor-killer. Previously little regard was paid to the Roman
church, as Aeneas Sylvius, later Pius II, admits in book 1, Epistle 288. Nor,
if they convened or confirmed certain councils,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 159 | IA leaf 193 | PDF p. 193]

and presided over them either personally or through legates, were they
therefore furnished with legitimate authority. For the question concerns
right, not fact; nor did the other churches approve this deed, but for the
sake of public peace they yielded to Roman ambition and tyranny because they
could not safely resist. Again, councils can be confirmed in two ways: by
edict or by assent. They were confirmed by edict when, by a law enacted, the
churches were commanded to receive and preserve the acts of the councils,
even with a penalty imposed upon rebels; by assent, when someone testified
that he agreed with the determinations and added his own vote. The popes
cannot be said to have confirmed councils in the former way, nor can any
edicts of the pontiffs be produced by which they confirmed any acts of
councils, as was done later when their tyranny gained strength, for this
belonged to the emperors. But they could confirm them by their assent, which
signifies no power on the part of the one who consents over those who made the
decree, for in this way all bishops confirmed councils by their assent.
Dissent also was common to all bishops. Nevertheless, this was exceptional in
the patriarchs: the bishops over whom they presided usually followed their
judgment, so their weight in confirming or invalidating was very great. But
this also must be noted: the dissent of the Roman bishop did not prevent
decrees from being valid. This was seen in the decree of the Council of
Chalcedon by which the bishop of Constantinople was preferred to the bishop
of Alexandria. That decree was not abrogated but confirmed at the Trullan
Council, although Leo had testified to his dissent.

XXII. The papists have no support for constructing their primacy from the
First Council of Constantinople, because it allegedly says in its letter to
Damasus, bishop of Rome, that it assembled at the command of the pontiff’s
letters sent to it through the emperor, and acknowledges the Roman church as
the head of the others. For no mention is made of a command in that letter, as
it is read in Theodoret, book 5, chapter 9, but only of Damasus’s letters,
which he wrote to Theodosius to convene the council. Nor is the Roman church
there called the head of the others; on the contrary, the church of Jerusalem
is called the “mother of all,” while the bishops acknowledge themselves to be
“proper members,” not of the Roman church but of the whole church or council.
Nor do they have support from the First Council of Ephesus, in which Nestorius
is said to have been condemned at Celestine’s command. For mention is indeed
made of Celestine’s letters to the council, by which he offered his assent,
but not of a command. Indeed, when the Fathers call Celestine a “fellow
minister,” they make sufficiently clear that they do not acknowledge him as
head and lord. Nor do they have support from the Council of Chalcedon, which
commonly calls Leo a bishop of the universal church. For it is one thing to be
a *bishop of the universal church*, which belongs to all bishops upon whom
the care of the universal church rests, as Chrysostom speaks of himself, and
another to be the *universal bishop of the church*. The former is said of Leo,
not the latter. Then it is well known that the name “catholic” was conferred
on account of orthodoxy, to distinguish such men from schismatics and
heretics, who belonged to particular assemblies. Finally, the name
“ecumenical” is read to have been conferred more than once not only upon the
Roman patriarch but also upon the patriarch of Constantinople and all the
others. Nor do they have support from Constantinople V, assembled in the case
of Anthimus, because it calls the Roman church apostolic and says that it
follows and obeys it. For we too obey one who gives good counsel, although he
does not have supreme authority over us. Add that it is evident from the
history of the pontiffs that he was not regarded as supreme judge, because
when Agapetus deposed Anthimus and the latter complained, the emperor
assembled a council. Finally, the ancient usage was that all those churches
which an apostle founded by his living voice were called apostolic, such as
the churches of Jerusalem, Antioch, and Rome. And because among the Latin
churches there was no such church except the Roman, it was therefore called
the apostolic see by preeminence. What Bellarmine brings forward from the
three councils of the Lateran, Lyons, and Florence has no

[1847 vol. III printed p. 160 | IA leaf 194 | PDF p. 194]

greater weight, because it is evident that they are very recent and were held
after the Roman tyranny had been confirmed.

XXIII. The various names attributed to the Roman bishop cannot prove his
ecumenical pontificate. (1) They are either common to others and not peculiar
to him. Thus the name “pope” was common to all bishops for many centuries,
was then given to the patriarchs, and finally, through a degenerate
innovation, was taken from all and abusively reserved for the Roman alone, as
Onuphrius notes in his addition to Platina under the life of Boniface III. The
name “father of fathers” is also read to have been attributed to John of
Constantinople at the fifth ecumenical synod. It is nothing other than a
circumlocution for an archbishop and patriarch, who presides over bishops who
are called fathers. Thus Sidonius Apollinaris, book 6, Epistle 1, calls Lupus
the bishop of Troyes by this name. The name “supreme pontiff,” or “high
priest,” was at first common to bishops who have presbyters, or “priests,”
beneath them. But as the church’s original simplicity gradually perished, the
name passed into abuse and was conferred upon the patriarchs, and finally it
was assigned to the Roman bishop alone. Undoubtedly carried away by this
abuse, Anselm and Bernard, who lived after the tenth century, gave this name
to him. The name “prince of priests” can be used in three ways: either for the
episcopate, because a bishop is a “high priest”; or for order of dignity,
since the Latins use “prince” for “first”; or for authority and power over the
other bishops. The first sense is common to all bishops, each of whom is a
prince in his own church. Thus Nazianzen, in his oration in praise of Hero,
calls Athanasius “high priest of the priests,” or “prince of priests,” though
he was never ecumenical. The second is indeed common to the Roman, but not
uniquely, for councils command that the bishop of Constantinople be made equal
to him in ecclesiastical affairs. The third was never used until after the
introduction of the tyranny. Or the names are abusive and were received only
in later centuries from flattery, not from the truth of the matter. Such are
the names “vicar of Christ, head of the church, father and teacher of all
Christians, bridegroom of the church, universal bishop, our most holy lord,
and most blessed pope,” and others of that kind, which demonstrate his
intolerable pride and his tyrannical and sacrilegious arrogance.
