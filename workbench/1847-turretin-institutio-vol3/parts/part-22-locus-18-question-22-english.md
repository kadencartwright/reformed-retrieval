QUESTION XXII.

ON THE CALLING OF PASTORS.

Is it necessary that there be a public ministry and a calling to it in the
church? We affirm against fanatics and enthusiasts.

I. Since God willed to lead people through the Word into communion with Christ
and through it into participation in salvation, it was necessary that there
be men selected for this office by a lawful calling, to whom the right and
power to preach the Word and administer the sacraments belonged. Concerning
the necessity of this ministry, our question is with fanatics and
enthusiasts.

[Marginalia: State of the Question.]

II. For the state of the question, observe: 1. The question does not concern
the usefulness of the ministry. Our opponents cannot deny that it is manifold,
and its most salutary effects—namely, the conversion and salvation of
people—sufficiently prove it. Nor does it concern its dignity, which is also
great. This is demonstrated not only by the honorable titles with which it is
adorned throughout Scripture, but also by the nature of the office itself:
whether we consider its efficient cause, namely God, the author of this order;
the subject matter with which it is occupied, namely divine mysteries and
things which eye has not seen, ear has not heard, and which have not entered
the human heart; the object about which it is engaged, namely human souls; the
remarkable effects which it produces, the renewal and happiness of people; or
the associates who have discharged it, namely Christ, the prophets, and the
apostles. Rather, the question concerns its necessity: whether it was
necessary that some such office be instituted in the church. Our opponents
deny this; we affirm it.

III. 2. The question does not concern an absolute and simple necessity. We do
not deny that God could, had he wished, have converted and saved people
immediately by himself without human ministry. It concerns instead a
hypothetical necessity

[1847 vol. III printed p. 179 | IA leaf 213 | PDF p. 213]

relative to God’s own good pleasure and decree. We assert this against modern
enthusiasts, Schwenckfeldians, and other fanatics of that kind who boast only
of divine inspirations and enthusiasms.

[Marginalia: The necessity of the ministry is proved. 1. From God’s
institution.]

IV. The reasons are as follows. First, there is God’s express institution. In
both the Old and New Testaments he called sacred ministers, some ordinary and
some extraordinary, to discharge the sacred office of preaching the Word and
teaching the people. This is seen in Moses, Aaron, and the Levitical
priesthood, and in the prophets whom he repeatedly raised up outside the
ordinary course in the Old Testament; and in the apostles, evangelists, and
other men of God in the New Testament to whom Christ committed the ministry of
the Word. The calling of the apostles pertains here (Matt. 28:19–20): “Go,
teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, the Son, and the
Holy Spirit, etc.” The following confirms the apostles (Eph. 4:11): “Christ,”
he says, “having ascended into heaven, gave some as apostles, others as
evangelists, others as pastors and teachers, for the gathering together of the
saints, for the work of ministry and the edification of the body of Christ,
until we all attain to the unity of faith, etc.” Here, together with the
institution of the ministry, its necessity, use, and perpetual duration to the
end of the age are clearly confirmed.

[Marginalia: 2. From God’s counsel.]

V. Second, there is God’s counsel to call people to salvation through the
preaching of the gospel. Since the natural person does not receive the things
of God (1 Cor. 2:14) and cannot be self-taught, he needed teaching and
instruction, which could be obtained from no other source than the ministry of
the Word. Although from the beginning God called humanity also through his
works, yet because that means became insufficient after the fall, he
instituted another through the Word of the gospel. This appears foolish to the
flesh, although it is in reality the wisdom of God in a mystery. “Since, in
the wisdom of God, the world did not know God through wisdom, it pleased God
to save believers through the foolishness of preaching.” Hence elsewhere Paul
derives the necessity of preaching from this counsel of God (Rom. 10:14–15):
“How will they call upon him in whom they have not believed? How will they
believe in him of whom they have not heard? How will they preach unless they
are sent? Faith, therefore, comes from hearing, and hearing through the Word of
God.” Here he uses a most beautiful progression: just as invocation requires
faith beforehand, faith the hearing of the Word, and hearing preaching, so
preaching requires a divine mission, without which no one can lawfully preach.
It is futile to reply that Paul was speaking only of his own time, “when the
teaching of the gospel was still new and unheard of, and therefore the matter
itself required those who were to announce it to be able to demonstrate their
mission. But since in our time nothing new is brought forward and the ancient
teaching of the gospel is presented, there is no longer a need for a
particular calling. Thus Paul is speaking there of the apostles, and this
should not be extended to all who teach others.” For the apostle’s statement
is general and extends to every sacred minister and every age, no less than
the other steps of the progression which concern invocation, faith, and the
hearing of the Word. The nature of the matter also teaches this, because the
ministry cannot be discharged without the will and commission of the Lord and
master of the household.

[Marginalia: 3. From the nature of the ministry.]

VI. Third, the nature of the ministry requires this because pastors are
ambassadors (2 Cor. 5:20). Yet no ambassador undertakes the duties of that
office without the prince’s special calling and command. They are stewards and
dispensers of the mysteries in God’s house (1 Cor. 4:1). Yet no one takes this
office upon himself unless the master of the household has placed him in
charge. They are soldiers of Christ (2 Tim. 2:3), who must perform the duties
of commanders and officers in this warfare. This cannot be done without the
calling of the heavenly

[1847 vol. III printed p. 180 | IA leaf 214 | PDF p. 214]

emperor. They are laborers in the Lord’s field, who must wait until they are
sent to the work by the Lord of the harvest and vineyard.

[Marginalia: 4. Because God condemns the self-called.]

VII. Fourth, God condemns the self-called and inveighs gravely against them as
false prophets and deceivers (Jer. 23:21): “I did not send the prophets, yet
they ran; I did not speak to them, yet they prophesied.” And in verse 32 the
Lord says, “Behold, I am against the prophets who dream lies, who led my people
astray by their lie, although I neither sent them nor commanded them.” Here the
lack of a calling and mission is presented as proof of deception. Although
Jeremiah speaks specifically in this passage about the Israelite false
prophets, who were neither sent nor called by God but put forward the
deceptions of their own hearts, a no less sound argument and general rule
against the self-called is drawn from it: no one who has not been sent by God
ought to usurp the teaching office in the church, whether a new or an old
doctrine is proposed. For it always remains true that no one ought to take
upon himself the part of an ambassador or minister unless he is sent by the
Lord. All who teach in the church without being called or sent are said to
“come in their own name,” not Christ’s (John 5:43); that is, they have not been
sent by God but have thrust themselves in by their own authority. They
therefore deserve the name of thieves and robbers, not true pastors (John
10:8). “In those who come,” says Jerome in the prologue to Matthew, “there is
the presumption of rashness; in those who are sent, the obedience of service.”
And Theophylact on this passage says, “All who came—that is, the false
prophets—not all who were sent, such as the prophets.”

[Marginalia: 5. From Hebrews 5:4.]

VIII. Fifth, from Hebrews 5:4: “No one takes this honor for himself, but he who
is called by God as Aaron was; so also Christ did not glorify himself, but the
one who said to him, You are my Son, etc.” The apostle establishes a
comparison between Aaron, the high priest of the Old Testament, and Christ, in
order to teach that Christ was truly appointed the high priest of the New
Testament. That is, just as Aaron did not thrust himself by his own authority
into the priestly office but waited for a divine calling, so Christ did not
glorify himself by arrogating this honor to himself apart from the Father’s
will. Rather, he is said to have been glorified by the one who spoke to him,
saying, “You are my Son”—namely the Father, who acknowledged him as his Son.
The apostle proves the first member of the comparison by the general axiom that
“no one takes this honor for himself”; that is, no one ought to take it for
himself, because verbs which signify action often denote duty and obligation,
but only “he who is called by God.” Although the priestly office is discussed
here, it does not follow that this cannot or ought not be extended to every
other public office, whether ecclesiastical or political. The special example
of Aaron does not restrict or limit the general rule under which it falls, but
explains and illustrates it. Nor is the case different for the office of
teaching others, in which there is more labor than honor, although undertaking
some labor has always been considered praiseworthy. For labor and honor in the
teaching office are not opposites, but subordinate things that accompany one
another, both in the pastoral and priestly office. Although it is always
praiseworthy to undertake labor to which each person is privately bound, the
same cannot be said of public labor, which requires a preceding calling.

[Marginalia: 6. From the practice of the church.]

IX. Sixth, from the practice of the church and from examples, because no one
undertook a sacred office without a calling. This is evident in the priests,
prophets, Christ, and the apostles. Hence the prophets repeatedly appeal to
their calling (Isa. 6:8; Jer. 1:4; Hos. 1:1), as do Christ (Luke 4:18, from
Isa. 61:1–2; John 20:21) and the apostles (Rom. 1:1; 1 Cor. 1; 2 Cor. 1; Gal.
1:1; 1 Pet. 1:1). Those who succeeded them in the teaching office did not
undertake it without a solemn calling.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 181 | IA leaf 215 | PDF p. 215]

In Acts 14:23, “When Paul and Barnabas had appointed presbyters in each church
and prayed with fasting, they commended them to the Lord.” And in 1 Timothy
4:14, “Grace is said to have been given to Timothy through prophecy with the
laying on of the hands of the presbytery.” Other passages of this kind also
belong here (2 Tim. 1:6; 1 Tim. 5:22; 2 Tim. 2:2; Titus 1:5). Since this is the
perpetual practice of the church, it is rightly derived from the divine
institution which demonstrates its necessity. Thus our opponents wrongly say
here that custom should be opposed to necessity.

[Marginalia: 7. From the causes requiring a calling.]

X. Seventh, from the weightiest causes requiring a calling, which concern the
honor of God, the salvation of the church, and the peace of consciences. With
respect to God, it is required so that his institution, will, and order may be
satisfied (Rom. 10:15; Heb. 5:4). With respect to the hearers, it is required
so that they may be assured of the lawful calling of the ministers and of the
truth of the doctrine which they will present, and may be persuaded that God
wills to attend lawfully called ministers with the efficacy of his Holy Spirit
and to bless their labors. This arouses reverence and obedience in their
hearts. With respect to the pastors themselves, it is required so that from
their calling they may be certain that God wills to use their labor in the
ministry and may conceive firm confidence in God’s blessing upon their
ministry and his unconquerable protection against the hatred of the world and
the dangers threatening them. The prophets often sustain themselves by this
argument (Isa. 49:1–2; 51:16; 59:21; Jer. 1:7–8).

[Marginalia: Sources of Solutions.]

XI. It is one thing to teach from charity and the general calling of
Christians, which belongs to everyone without a particular calling; another
to teach publicly and with authority, which belongs only to those lawfully
called. Thus a work good in itself, if not rightly done, turns to evil for the
doer, as when Uzzah supported the ark. Nor should a good work immediately be
performed indiscriminately by anyone. To exercise the magistracy is a good
work, since the magistrate is God’s minister (Rom. 13:4), but it is not
therefore lawful for anyone to exercise it without a special calling. To serve
as an ambassador is a good work, but no one would say that everyone can and
ought to undertake it without a calling. Christian charity must be exercised
in such a way that the divinely established order is not violated and distinct
offices and ways of life are not confused.

XII. The case of a disturbed church or one being established is different
from that of a church established and well ordered. In the former, charity
seizes any opportunity to propagate the gospel. In the latter, care must be
taken that all things are done decently and in order (1 Cor. 14:40). Many
things tolerated in the former cannot be borne in the latter.

XIII. When the apostle says, “If anyone aspires to the episcopate, he desires
a good work” (1 Tim. 3:1), by *aspiring* and *desiring* he does not understand
an administration of the sacred ministry undertaken at one’s own discretion
without a calling, a violent intrusion into this office, or an unlawful
calling obtained by evil arts. All these things oppose the divinely instituted
order. He understands instead the orderly and pious desire by which someone
freely devotes his labor to the church, commits the judgment of himself to
others, and awaits a lawful calling. Thus an internal calling is denoted which
does not exclude the external one.

XIV. Although by the general command all Christians can and ought to celebrate
God’s praises, offer spiritual sacrifices to God, and exhort and comfort one
another (Eph. 5:19; Col. 4:16; 1 Thess. 4:18), it does not follow that a
special command is not required for the public work of the ministry of the
Word and the administration of sacred things, which it is not lawful to
handle without being called.

XV. Those scattered people discussed in Acts 8:4, who preached the gospel
after Stephen’s death, were not wholly without a calling, because among them
there could have been evangelists, presbyters, and deacons, such as

[1847 vol. III printed p. 182 | IA leaf 216 | PDF p. 216]

Philip (Acts 21:8). Add that the matter concerned a church being established,
not one already established.

XVI. It is one thing to devote oneself to ministering to the needs of the
saints, which is said of the household of Stephanas and Fortunatus, the first
fruits of Achaia (1 Cor. 16:15); another to appoint themselves as presbyters
in order to preach the Word and administer the sacraments to others. If the
passage is extended to the ministry of the Word, it implies that they freely
offered their labor from charity but were lawfully called, or that by a
definite vow and purpose they set themselves apart for this sacred office,
without excluding a lawful calling.

XVII. The prophets whom Paul discusses in 1 Corinthians 14:29, who spoke in
the church, are not all believers indiscriminately, but either the ordinary
teachers of the church, who are often designated by this name, or those who,
equipped with a special gift of prophecy, submitted their teaching to the
judgment of others.

XVIII. From the delivery of the keys made to the church (Matt. 16:19), it is
rightly concluded against the papists that the right of calling ministers
belongs to the whole church, to which the keys were delivered. Yet it cannot
be inferred from this that all who are in the church can teach publicly even
without a calling. The keys were indeed given to the whole church—that is,
for the use and benefit of the whole church—and in such a way that every order
of the church has some right in a calling. But the administration of those
keys is entrusted by the church, and by the force of the divine institution
ought to be entrusted, to certain persons whom God has appointed as stewards
in his house, to whom he gives the power to close and open, to retain and
remit sins.

XIX. If believers are called “taught by God” (Isa. 54:13; John 6:45), and if
under the covenant of grace God was to inscribe the law upon the hearts of
believers so that they would no longer need anyone to teach them (Jer. 31:34),
with the sacred anointing teaching them all things (1 John 2:20, 27), this
should not be understood simply and absolutely so as to exclude the necessity
of the gospel ministry, as if God wished to teach believers immediately by
himself. Otherwise there would have been no need for Christ to send the
apostles to preach the gospel and to give pastors and teachers for the
edification of his body and the gathering together of the saints. Nor should
the apostles have appointed presbyters in each town to shepherd and teach the
churches they had founded. And John would have written to believers vainly and
needlessly. Rather, these statements should be understood comparatively and
in a qualified sense, concerning the knowledge of the New Testament, which
was to be far fuller than the knowledge of the Old. Under the Old Testament,
believers were still immature minors in constant need of teachers and guides
to lead them by the hand and gradually instill the law, line upon line, as it
is written (Isa. 28:13). But now, in the fullness of light and the Spirit,
believers who have come of age can perceive for themselves far more clearly
and easily the gospel mysteries disclosed to them in the Word. Or the Holy
Spirit intended to teach that the external ministry is insufficient to
produce faith unless the internal operation of grace is added, effectually
bending people to receive the gospel. This is a benefit of the gospel covenant
above the legal covenant. In this sense the Spirit is said to lead us into all
truth, not immediately through enthusiasms but through means ordained by him,
namely the preaching of the Word and human study. Through these, faith is not
only conceived and formed but nourished and increased, while that Word of
life, which is the incorruptible seed of our regeneration (1 Pet. 1:23), is
also the pure spiritual food and milk by which we are nourished and grow in
grace (2:2).
