Objections

Objection 1. James 1:15. The apostle does not call concupiscence—that is, the
first movement—sin. He calls its imperfect offspring, that is, a delight not
fully deliberate, sin, but not mortal sin. Finally, he defines its perfect
offspring, that is, desire with full consent, as plainly mortal sin.
Therefore.

Response 1. It is false that the apostle calls delight which is not fully
deliberate a venial sin, since every sin by its own nature is mortal, as will
be taught later.

Response 2. Although he does not call concupiscence sin in this passage, it
does not follow that it is not sin, because elsewhere he calls it sin, as was
proved above.

Response 3. It does not follow: he calls the offspring of concupiscence sin;
therefore concupiscence, its root, is not sin. On the contrary, fruit and tree
are of the same nature.

Response 4. There is an equivocation in the word “sin,” because in the cited
passage it signifies actual sin.

[1610 printed p. 101 | Google Books PA101]

Concupiscence, however, is original sin.
Therefore the conclusion does not follow. “It is not actual sin; therefore it
is not sin” is a fallacy of passing from a qualified statement to an
unqualified one. Moreover, it is a parent sin, not an offspring, but it is sin
nonetheless.

Instance. Actual sin is fully deliberate and consummated. But that sin
differs from consummated sin, as is clear from the text. Therefore.

Response 1. It is false that every actual sin is fully deliberate, for sins
of ignorance and omission are not of that kind.

Response 2. It is false that by the consummated sin which gives birth to
death the apostle means some one actual sin. He speaks instead of the sinner's
whole course, that is, of all his sins collectively. When that course is
finished, nothing remains except punishment.

Objection 2. Romans 7:17. The apostle says that the movement of concupiscence
is not his work and, for that reason, cannot be imputed to him, and that this
evil is not in himself but in his flesh. But the flesh is not capable of sin
properly so called. Therefore concupiscence in a reborn person is not properly
sin.

Response 1. This is a fallacy of passing from a qualified statement to an
unqualified one, because he does not deny simply that the movement of
concupiscence is his work, but that it is voluntary, as is clear from verse
15. Moreover, he denies that it is his work only insofar as he is spiritual,
and he asserts that it is his insofar as he is carnal, that is, not reborn,
in verse 14 of the same chapter.

Response 2. We assert that original sin cannot be imputed to the reborn;
therefore it is altogether absent from them. The inference is denied. The
subject of sin is neither the soul alone nor the body, but the whole human
being, as was proved above.

Response 3. He distorts the text, for the words are:

[1610 printed p. 102 | Google Books PA102]

“I know that it does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh.” Bellarmine,
however, says that Paul declares it not to be in himself but in his flesh.

Response 4. This is a fallacy arising from the equivocation of “flesh,”
because it does not signify the other part of the composite—as Bellarmine
wishes—but the whole human being insofar as he is carnal and corrupt, as in
John 1:13; 3:6; Romans 8:7; Colossians 2:13, 18; and the whole of Romans 7.
Bellarmine concedes that the word is often so used in that same passage.

Objection 3. Romans 8:1. There is nothing of condemnation—that is, nothing
worthy of condemnation or a cause of condemnation—in the reborn. Therefore,
although believers feel concupiscence resisting the law of the mind, what
remains of it in them is nevertheless not sin by its own nature.

Response 1. I deny the inference. Although the reborn are not condemned and
their sins are not material for condemnation because they are remitted in
Christ with respect to guilt, they nevertheless remain sins by their own
nature and merit death in themselves.

Response 2. Bellarmine's gloss—“There is nothing of condemnation,” that is,
nothing worthy of condemnation—is false. The fact that there is nothing of
condemnation does not arise from the nature of sin in itself, but from Christ,
who causes what in itself is sin, and therefore a cause of condemnation, not
to be so for believers. It is therefore a fallacy of treating what is not the
cause as the cause. In chapter 7 the apostle confessed that he carried the
material of death about with him and that no good dwelt in his flesh, and so
forth, but he said in the last verse of that chapter that because of Christ he
was not condemned.

Instance 1. If guilt is removed in Christ in the reborn, then nothing of sin
remains, because when a thing's form is removed, the thing does not remain.
But guilt is the form of sin. Therefore.

[1610 printed p. 103 | Google Books PA103]

Response. It was said above that sin properly has no form, since it is a
privation, and that guilt is put in the place of a form for the sake of
teaching. Moreover, although guilt could be called a consequence of sin, it is
nevertheless not a bare consequence, because sin is not a simple defect but a
defect with a relation or obligation to punishment on account of the
transgression or deficiency of the law. Finally, guilt is removed with
respect to imputation, but not with respect to sin itself. Thus Scotus, book
2, distinction 32, and others err when they hold that the formal element of
original sin is the absence of original righteousness and its material
element is the obligation to possess that righteousness.

Instance 2. Guilt is altogether inseparable from what by its own nature is
worthy of death, because guilt is a relation whose proximate foundation is
iniquity. But a relation cannot be separated from its proximate foundation.
Therefore.

Response. Guilt is not separated from iniquity if iniquity is considered as
it is in itself, but when iniquity is given, guilt is assigned. Thus, when a
homicide has been committed, the killer is guilty, but in such a way that the
judge can pardon him—that is, not impute the guilt. So too, when iniquity is
given, we are guilty before God with respect to that iniquity. But insofar as
we are in Christ, guilt is not imputed to us. Thus in Psalm 32:1–2 the sins of
the reborn are called covered and not imputed, but they are not said not to
exist.

Instance 3. God never remits guilt while its foundation remains, Psalm 5:6;
Romans 2:2. Therefore.

Response. I deny the antecedent, and the passages prove nothing. It does not
follow that, because God truly judges and hates iniquity, sin does not remain
when guilt has been removed. Because God judges truly, he does not impute sins
to us on account of Christ's satisfaction; and because he hates sins, he
punished them in Christ.

[1610 printed p. 104 | Google Books PA104]

Instance 4. That opinion is deduced from the preceding statements in chapter
7. But above the apostle did not say that God pardons concupiscence or guilt;
he showed instead that involuntary movements of concupiscence are not sins,
Romans 7:16, because they occur in us but do not arise from us.

Response. It is true that those words are deduced from the preceding
statements, but Bellarmine falsely says that it is not stated that God pardons
our guilt, for it is stated in verses 24–25 and elsewhere. Moreover, he
falsely says that the apostle declares the movements of concupiscence not to
be sins, for he plainly calls them sins, as was proved above. Finally, it is
false that Paul says he does not do them. In verse 15 he says that he does do
them. He says he does not do them, not simply, but insofar as he is reborn.

Objection 4. Micah 7:19; Ezekiel 36:25. Through baptism we are regenerated
from every sin. Therefore no sin remains in the baptized.

Response 1. In the cited passages the words concern not only sanctification
but properly justification. Yet this does not follow: sins are remitted in
baptism; therefore they do not exist in the baptized. Remission does not
remove sins so that they do not exist, but so that they are not imputed unto
condemnation. Sanctification follows justification and removes corruption
itself and sin's inherence, but gradually; it does so fully only after this
life.

Response 2. Through baptism we are regenerated and sins are removed, not by
the work performed or by the power of baptism—as the Papists maintain—but
these and similar expressions must be understood sacramentally, as will be
said in its proper place.

Objection 5. Titus 3:6. The Holy Spirit is poured out abundantly upon us.
Therefore the remnants of sin are removed altogether in the reborn.

[1610 printed p. 105 | Google Books PA105]

Response 1. I deny the inference, because the Holy Spirit is poured out
abundantly upon us not so as to remove sin completely in this life, but so as
to overcome it, prevent it from reigning, and renew our inner person from day
to day, 2 Corinthians 4:16.

Response 2. This is a fallacy of passing from a qualified statement to an
unqualified one, because the Holy Spirit is poured out abundantly not simply,
but in comparison with others. Believers have only the firstfruits of the
Spirit in this life, Romans 6:23. The complete destruction of sin and integral
righteousness will come after this life.

Objection 6. First Corinthians 6:11. The reborn have been washed and
sanctified. Therefore nothing of original sin exists in them.

Response. I deny the inference. When the apostle says, “You have been washed,”
he signifies the perfect remission of sins in Christ's blood. When he says,
“You have been sanctified,” sanctification by Christ's Spirit has begun and
is to be completed through its stages, rather than already being perfect.
Moreover, it follows only that no sin exists with respect to guilt, not with
respect to inherence, because the inherence of sin remains in us in part.
Scripture therefore exhorts the reborn to strive more and more after holiness.
Augustine correctly interprets this passage in book 6, chapter 8, *Against
Julian*: “When Paul says, ‘Such indeed you were, but you have been washed, but
you have been sanctified,’ he says that they were changed for the better, not
that they lacked concupiscence, which cannot happen in this life, but that
they did not obey it,” and so forth.

Objection 7. Second Corinthians 6:14. Light has no fellowship with darkness,
nor righteousness with unrighteousness. Therefore original sin does not
remain in the reborn.

Response. I distinguish the antecedent. Reigning unrighteousness has no
fellowship with righteousness: this is true, and the passage speaks of it.
But that nonreigning unrighteousness has no fellowship with righteousness is
false, as the apostle teaches in his own person in Romans 7.

[1610 printed p. 106 | Google Books PA106]

Objection 8. Ephesians 5:27. Christ cleansed the church so that it might be
without wrinkle and blemish. Therefore original sin does not remain in the
reborn.

Response 1. The passage speaks chiefly about justification, by which guilt,
not the existence of sin, is removed.

Response 2. It says that Christ cleansed the church so that he might present
it before God without wrinkle, even with respect to the inherence of sin, but
not fully in this life; for that reason the apostle uses a verb in the future
tense. Finally, the church is without wrinkle in God's sight insofar as the
Father beholds it in Christ and does not impute its sins but covers them.

Objection 9. Hebrews 9:26. Christ died to abolish sins. But sins whose remnants
remain have not been abolished. Therefore.

Response. Christ abolishes sin perfectly in justification with respect to
remission and the imputation of his righteousness, and he also abolishes it
perfectly—but gradually—with respect to the mortification of sin and the
restoration of righteousness. Although guilt has been perfectly removed, the
defect or inherence nevertheless remains, according to the saying, “If we say
that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves,” 1 John 1:8.

Objection 10. Luke 10:30. The man who is said in the parable to have been
stripped and wounded represents Adam's state after the fall. Adam was stripped
of original righteousness and received wounds in his nature because it was
corrupted. But in that man's case, to have been wounded and stripped was not
sin but a wretched condition. In the same way, for Adam and us to have been
stripped of heavenly gifts and to have received a wounded nature is not sin
but a wretched and unhappy condition.

Response 1. The passage is a parable and therefore must not be drawn beyond
the sense of the one who proposed it. But Christ's purpose was not to say that
after the fall there is no

[1610 printed p. 107 | Google Books PA107]

sin in us, but that love must be shown toward everyone, as is clear from the
passage.

Response 2. His conclusion is against the Papists themselves, for it implies
that the corruption of nature is not sin in any person, even one who is not
reborn. This was the heresy of Pelagius.

Instance 1. God either hates concupiscence in the reborn or does not hate it.
If he does not hate it, it is not sin, because he hates every sin. If he hates
it, he has not remitted it. Therefore.

Response. These claims do not contradict one another. Something remains in
the reborn which, considered absolutely and in itself, God hates, and yet he
has remitted it in Christ. He hates it with respect to the nature of sin, but
remits it with respect to Christ's merit. Thus a father loves his son but
hates the faults in his son, which he has also forgiven.

Instance 2. God loves the reborn and baptized. Therefore he hates nothing in
them.

Response. I deny the inference. It is a fallacy of treating what is not the
cause as the cause, for God does not love the reborn because they are
altogether pure, but because of Christ, whom they have put on. In Christ their
sins are remitted, and with his righteousness Christ covers the sin that
still inheres in them.

Instance 3. If guilt remains in the reborn, then the reborn will be both God's
friends and his enemies: friends by grace, enemies because of guilt. But the
latter is absurd. Therefore so is the former.

Response. The proposition would be true if guilt remained with respect to
liability, that is, if a reborn person were guilty before God. But although
vice exists in the reborn, liability is pardoned on account of Christ's
merit. Indeed, the vice itself is partly mortified and partly removed in the
reborn. Thus the reborn are no longer God's enemies but are reconciled to God,
Romans 8:1.

Objection 11. Through baptism we were buried in Christ, Romans 6:4. Therefore
everything that has the character of sin is removed in baptism.

[1610 printed p. 108 | Google Books PA108]

Response. The antecedent is true, first, with respect to guilt, which is
removed in Christ; and second, with respect to the dominion of sin, because
it does not reign in the reborn. Instead, the reborn resist it, putting off
the old person day by day and putting on the new. Augustine therefore says
correctly in book 2, chapter 8, *On the Merits and Remission of Sins*: “If
perfect newness came to be in the inner person at baptism, the apostle would
not say, ‘Although the outer person is wasting away, the inner person is
nevertheless renewed from day to day.’ For the one who is renewed from day to
day has not yet been wholly renewed, and insofar as he has not yet been
renewed, he remains in the old state.”

Instance. God's works are perfect. Therefore regeneration is perfect as well.

Response. I distinguish the antecedent. They are perfect in their own order
and degree; otherwise, they are not. Thus creation was a perfect work, but not
on the first day. In the same way, regeneration occurs by degrees.

Objection 12. Privatively opposed things do not exist in the same subject.
But sin and righteousness are of that kind. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is true with respect to the same thing and in
intense degrees, but false with respect to lesser degrees. Sin and
righteousness do not exist in the reborn in intense degrees, but in lesser
ones.

Objection 13. Bellarmine, book 5, chapter 9, *On the Loss of Grace*: Baptism
does not free a person from concupiscence.
Therefore either concupiscence is not original sin, or baptism does not free a
person from original sin.

Response. That baptism does not free a person by the work performed, as the
Papists maintain, is true. But that it does not free a person sacramentally
from guilt and dominion is false.

Objection 14. Concupiscence is an effect and punishment of original sin.
Therefore it is not original sin itself.

Response. I deny the inference. Bellarmine concedes in book 5

[1610 printed p. 109 | Google Books PA109]

of *On the Loss of Grace*, chapter 9, reason 2, that one and the same thing can
be sin, the punishment of sin, and the cause of sin. Moreover, although
concupiscence, which is inherent original sin, is the punishment of imputed
original sin, it is nevertheless sin. But if original sin is taken as a whole,
so as to embrace
both parts which Bellarmine explains near the end of book 2, chapter 17, *On
the Loss of Grace*, the first part—namely, imputed original sin—is the cause
of inherent original sin, while inherent original sin is the punishment of
imputed original sin, is at the same time sin, and remains inherent.

Objection 15. If a person were not born from Adam and nevertheless had the
concupiscence he now has, concupiscence could not properly be called sin in
him. Therefore concupiscence is not sin in itself and by its own nature.

Response. There is an equivocation in the word “concupiscence,” because in the
antecedent it means concupiscence as a natural law, that is, as it signifies
an ordered appetite for objects. In that sense the claim is true, for
everything natural—that is, everything created by God in nature—is good. But
if it speaks of concupiscence as a disordered appetite for objects, it is
false that concupiscence is not properly called sin, because it is condemned
by God's law and called sin in Scripture, as was proved above.

Objection 16. If concupiscence is taken as an involuntary act, that is, as the
first movements which the mind resists, it is not sin. Much less, therefore,
will it be sin if it is taken as a propensity to act.

Proof of the antecedent. Concupiscence in children, the insane, and those who
are asleep is not sin, because it is not in their power not to desire, just as
it is not

[1610 printed p. 110 | Google Books PA110]

in the power of the wise and wakeful not to have those first movements of
concupiscence, as experience teaches. Therefore they are not sins.

Response 1. I deny both the antecedent of the first argument and the
antecedent of the prosyllogism, because insofar as concupiscence signifies the
first disordered movement, it is a sin condemned by the law, as was proved
above.

Response 2. The argument rests upon the false foundation that every sin is
voluntary. This maxim is true only in civil matters and of actual sin, and not
even of every actual sin, as was said above.

Response 3. The necessity of sinning does not remove sin. Finally, infants
and the like do not lack the capacity to will. Although they do not will in
act, they nevertheless will by inclination and therefore sin.

Instance. God's law is not impossible but sweet and easy for the reborn,
Matthew 11:29; 1 Corinthians 10:13; 1 John 5:3, and so forth. But it is
impossible for the reborn not to have movements of concupiscence resisting the
law of the mind, Romans 7; 2 Corinthians 12. Therefore those movements are not
sins.

Response 1. I deny the proposition, for perfect fulfillment of the law is
impossible for the reborn in this life, as was demonstrated in controversy 12
in the discussion of justification.

Response 2. God's commandments are not called burdensome for two reasons.
First, because the reborn, cheerfully and willingly moved by the Holy Spirit,
render the law as much obedience as they can, and because their delight is in
the law, Psalm 1:2. Second, because in Christ everything that was burdensome
has been removed.

Response 3. The passage in Matthew speaks of the gospel, whose doctrine
concerns faith and the obedience begun in us by the Holy Spirit, who causes us
to believe and gladly obey God's commandments, although our obedience is

[1610 printed p. 111 | Google Books PA111]

not perfect in this life. Finally, the passage in Corinthians speaks of
afflictions which God does not impose upon believers beyond the strength
supplied to them to endure those afflictions patiently.

Objection 17. If concupiscence is truly sin and always remains in a person,
then injury is done to Christ and his merits. The inference is proved because
it follows that Christ has freed us from sin not truly but imputatively. But
the latter is absurd. Therefore.

Response 1. I deny the proposition, because in justification full remission
of all sins is given on account of Christ, and in sanctification the abolition
of every sin is begun. As to why God works in the reborn in this manner and
order, see Augustine, book 4, chapter 3, *Against Julian the Pelagian*: “As if
we denied that God's help is powerful enough for us to be able today to have
no evil desires against which we must fight, even most reluctantly; yet you
do not deny that this does not happen. But who has known the mind of the Lord
as to why it does not happen? Still, I know no small thing when I know that,
whatever the reason may be, neither is iniquity absent from the righteous nor
does weakness belong to the Almighty. There is therefore something hidden
and deep in his counsel that explains why, for as long as we live in this
mortal flesh, there is something in us against which our mind must fight;
there is also a reason for us to say, ‘Forgive us our debts.’” And a little
later: “Therefore it was done in the place of this weakness lest we live
proudly and without the daily remission of sins. But whether
this is the reason, or another which is far more hidden from me, I nevertheless
cannot doubt that, however much progress we make under the burden of this
corruptible body, if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves,” and so
forth.

Response 2. He ineptly

[1610 printed p. 112 | Google Books PA112]

opposes true righteousness to imputed righteousness, as though imputed
righteousness did not truly justify and free from every sin.

Instance 1. If sin remains in us, then the devil and Adam have accomplished
more by their iniquity than Christ by his righteousness, because the former
were able truly to defile us, whereas Christ cannot truly cleanse us. But
grace abounds beyond sin. Therefore.

Response. I deny the proposition, because Christ's righteousness is far more
powerful than sin with respect to remission, since all sins are remitted to
believers on account of Christ. Second, it is far more efficacious with
respect to healing, because remission is always joined with the operation of
the Holy Spirit, who begins and perfects renewal. Thus, just as the twofold
evil of corruption and guilt befell us through sin, so through Christ comes
twofold grace which removes both evils, namely, the remission of sins and
regeneration. Consequently, we are no less truly cleansed in Christ than we
were defiled by sin in Adam. Finally, it is inept to say that we are not truly
cleansed in Christ because we are cleansed imputatively, as was shown at
greater length in the discussion of justification.

Instance 2. If sin remains in us, then these testimonies will be false: John
8:36; Romans 5:19. For those who are in sin have not been truly freed or made
righteous, just as through Adam they were truly, and not only imputatively,
made unrighteous. But the latter is absurd. Therefore.

Response. I deny the connection. Moreover, in the proof he falsely says that
the reborn are in sins, since this belongs only to unbelievers, who have not
been freed in Christ. Finally, the reborn are made truly righteous both by
imputed righteousness and by inherent righteousness, although by degrees,

[1610 printed p. 113 | Google Books PA113]

just as through Adam they were made unrighteous by both imputed and inherent
sin.

Objection 18. If sin remains in the saints, then they cannot love God with
their whole heart, and all their works are imperfect. But believers have often
said that they loved God. Therefore.

Response. I deny the proposition. We love God with our whole heart, though
not wholly. Moreover, this does not follow: the works of the saints are
imperfect; therefore they do not love God. They truly love God with perfection
of parts, though not of degrees. Finally, when the saints say that they love
God, it is understood in three ways: first, comparatively, as when they are
compared with enemies on account of the cause they uphold; second, according
to the part by which they are reborn; third, in Christ, in whom they are
perfectly obedient, just as in Adam they were perfectly disobedient.

Objection 19. The fathers teach that the baptized lack every sin and that sin
is wholly destroyed in baptism, as do Augustine in tractate 4 on John and book
2 of *The City of God*, Jerome, and others. Therefore.

Response. The fathers teach only that no guilt of sin remains in the reborn,
but they teach that vice remains, as is clear from the testimonies of
Augustine and others cited above. To these add the statement of Augustine in
book 2 of *The City of God*, where he attributes innocence to infants, but
only from actual sin, not original sin. When he says that sin does not exist
in the reborn, he does not speak simply, but says that reigning sin does not
exist, as in tractate 4 on John and elsewhere.
