## Lecture XV: The Spirit’s Earnest and the Consummation of Redemption

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 92 | Caput I p. 50 (from Lecture XV opening)]

Thus far we have completed the first of those two members which we set out
above in these two verses concerning the calling of the Gentiles. It remains
now to explain the second member in the following verse. Taking occasion from
the immediately preceding words, in which he had mentioned the Holy Spirit of
promise, the Apostle describes this same Spirit from another use and office
of his toward believers when he calls him “the earnest of our inheritance
unto the vindication of redemption”—that is, until we are vindicated into
liberty—“to his glory.” Here three members can likewise be distinctly
observed. First, the end and use of the Holy Spirit given to us, who seals the
promise in us, namely, that he should be the earnest of our inheritance.
Second, how long this use or effect of his toward us endures, namely, until we
are vindicated into liberty. Third, the end to which both this full
vindication of us and the other benefits preceding it are directed, namely,
the glory of God.

[Marginalia: Lecture XV. Transition. The latter part. Subdivision.]

As to the first, in order that we may better grasp this most striking and
delightful metaphor which the Holy Spirit uses here, the force of the word
“earnest” must be weighed. We see our Apostle use it elsewhere too in the same
sense, as, for example, in 2 Corinthians 1: “Who also sealed us and placed the
earnest of the Spirit in our hearts.” Likewise in chapter 5 of the same
epistle: “Moreover, it is God who fashioned us for this very thing,” namely,
the blessed immortality and glory even of our bodies, “who also gave us the
earnest of the Spirit.” It is well known, even from the grammarians, that this
word passed from the Hebrews to the Greeks and from them to the Latins. For
the Hebrew word ערבון properly signifies a pledge, as is evident even from
that one passage in Genesis 38, in the shameful compact made between Judah and
his daughter-in-law Tamar, where the word is repeated twice in the same
sense. It is, moreover—

[Marginalia: Member 1. Earnest. 2 Corinthians 1:22 and 5:5. Genesis 38:17.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 93 | Caput I p. 51]

—evidently derived from the very widely extending root ערב, which has many
meanings. Among other things, it signifies to pledge, give surety, contract,
and transact business. Thus ערבון appropriately signifies the pledge or
earnest of an undertaking in Greek and Latin as well as in Hebrew.

[Marginalia: “If you will give a pledge until you send it.” Note in passing
the distinction.]

There is indeed a distinction between a pledge and an earnest or arrabo.
These latter two words, to say this in passing, signify the same thing for
Latin writers. But although the word *arrabo* was used by the ancient Latins,
it later began to be regarded among base and obsolete words, so that, as
Aulus Gellius testifies, *arra* succeeded in its place, perhaps by dropping
the final syllable for the sake of neatness and elegance. We read that the
Hebrews themselves did this with the word *kikajon*: the plant called
*kikajon* in Jonah’s age was afterward called *kik* by more recent writers.
Hence the *kiki* of the Egyptians and Greeks. It is likely that the same thing
happened when the Latin word *arrabo* was cut down to *arra*. But, to return,
the distinction between a pledge and an earnest is that the latter is given
as part of the future price in a contract between a buyer and seller, and is
given to confirm the purchase so that there may be no room for withdrawal or
fraud, as is evident to anyone. A pledge, however, is customarily given to a
creditor when money is borrowed, as a hostage for payment or for the return
of the sum. Accordingly, once the creditor has been repaid and the loan
returned, the pledge in turn is customarily given back. Jerome likewise wrote
this on the present passage and therefore rightly reproved the common
Translator for always rendering this word “earnest” as “pledge.” “For a
pledge,” he says, “is set against borrowed money, but an earnest is given as a
kind of testimony and bond for a future purchase.”

[Marginalia: Recapitulation. Jerome.]

Hence, to note this too in passing, I had thought I had gathered an argument
not to be despised by which, from this passage among very many others, I could
prove that Jerome was by no means the author of this common translation. Yet
the Papists customarily assert this against us shamelessly, recommending
their worthless scraps to the common people in tunics by covering them, as it
were, with a certain venerable soot of antiquity. But I had indeed forgotten
that I had long ago read the same observation in the most learned Whitaker.
Although in our Apostle this word should be translated “earnest” rather than
“pledge,” as we will soon show from his purpose, we deny that this distinction
was always observed in the word’s use either by Hebrews or Latins. We have
already shown above that in Hebrew it is used for the pledge which Judah,
after sending that uncertain payment, namely, a nursing kid, later took back
from his daughter-in-law. Aulus Gellius also cites a passage from Marcus Cato
who, speaking about certain hostages, said that there was only an *arrabo*
among the Romans, using this word for “pledge” for the sake of greater
gravity. To apply these matters to our purpose, the reason for the present
translation is evident from what has been said.

[Marginalia: Corollary. In the controversy concerning Scripture. Application
to the present purpose.]

For just as, when a purchase is contracted, the person who has received an
earnest is assured by it, as a pledge and guarantee of the whole sum, that he
will receive from the buyer all the remaining price when he has delivered the
thing sold—something which perhaps is not yet finished and prepared for the
buyer’s use, or is not yet present and at hand so that it can be delivered—so
too the gift of the Holy Spirit, and therefore our regeneration begun through
him, is and ought to be for us a most certain earnest that at the appointed
time we will receive whatever has been promised to us in that eternal and
gracious covenant of the gospel. Indeed, another correspondence with the
spiritual matter which the Apostle illustrates by this comparison can be
observed in the same analogy. For just as an earnest is not only a pledge of
the future completion of payment but also a part of the whole price, which,
when the full price has been paid, is customarily credited as payment and
reckoned by the buyer, so too our sanctification begun here is, as it were, a
certain part and beginning of that perfect sanctification—that is, eternal
life—which is contained in the promise and will be paid in full only in the
age to come. What has been begun here will neither perish nor be taken back,
as customarily happens with a pledge for borrowed money, but will be perfected
in that glorious life.

[Marginalia: Exposition.]

Someone might object that what the Apostle says in 1 Corinthians 13—“after
that which is perfect has come, then that which is in part will be
abolished”—works against the doctrine which we have just derived. It is as if
the Apostle said that when the entire sum is paid and the full price is
counted out in the life to come, the earnest given to us in this life will
then be abolished, and consequently what I was speaking about will not be
perfected. This statement must be understood rightly and soundly, not so much
of the thing itself as of its imperfection and its imperfect mode, which has a
place only in this life. It is as if the Apostle said: when that perfect
knowledge, that is, the consummated knowledge of God, has come, what will be
removed is not so much the knowledge itself which we have in this life as all
of its imperfection. Those means and instruments which God saw fit to use in
teaching and instructing us in this state of imperfection will also cease. In
that place there will be no use for schools, the gospel ministry, teachers,
or books. But we will know God in a very different manner and degree from
that in which we have known him until now, namely, face to face, in that
glorious and beatific vision—that is, in a far more perfect mode. Therefore
this remains true: in future glory the thing itself will not be removed but
perfected.

[Marginalia: Anticipation of an objection. 1 Corinthians 13:10. Answer.]

Let us now examine the second member: how long the use of this earnest will
endure, namely, “until we are vindicated into full liberty, unto the
redemption of the possession.” There can be three interpretations of these
words, although, as will be evident, they all come to one thing as regards the
sense and the thing itself. Some posit a Hebraism like the one we observed in
the immediately preceding verse, where “Spirit of promise” means the promised
Spirit. Thus they expound “redemption of acquisition” as “acquired
redemption,” that is, the redemption which Christ acquired for us. Others,
using a metonymy of the adjunct for the subject, take “redemption of
acquisition” to mean the redemption of the acquired possession, that is, of
his Church, which he purchased with his own blood, just as the Apostle
commonly uses “circumcision” for the circumcised and “foreskin” for the
uncircumcised. I would prefer this to the former, not only because it better
fits the phrase, but also because I see him use the same word “possession” of
the Church in Acts 20, although both interpretations seem somewhat harsh.
Third, then, Master Beza’s interpretation expounds this expression of the
redemption of assertion or vindication, that is, full, perfect, and
consummated redemption.

[Marginalia: Member 2. Threefold exposition. Acts 20:28.]

To understand this, we must hold briefly that our deliverance or redemption
can be taken in two ways. First, as it is only begun and is still imperfect
with respect to us, that is, insofar as it is applied to us. For although with
respect to merit Christ our Redeemer has perfectly and absolutely—

[Marginalia: Twofold redemption.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 94 | Caput I p. 52]

—paid the price of our redemption, and in this respect can be said already to
have redeemed us perfectly, that same redemption is not yet perfectly applied
to us in this life. We are still oppressed by a certain slavery to sin, and,
burdened with this body of death, we sigh and groan under our load until we
are finally stripped of it plainly and fully. Indeed, death itself will keep
our bodies bound and restrained by its bonds in the grave until that day of
the supreme judgment on which Christ will lay his liberating hand upon us as
those redeemed by him. He spoke of this redemption above in verse 7 and makes
extended mention of it in Romans 7.

Second, redemption may be taken as altogether perfect and consummated. By it
we will obtain perfect liberty, absolute in every respect, being far removed
not only from every fear of and slavery to death and sin but also from every
conflict, ambush, proximity, and commerce with them, and exempt from all the
temptations and weaknesses to which we remain liable in this life and mortal
body. Romans 8 speaks of this when it says that we await adoption, that is,
the redemption of our body. Thus both here and below the Apostle calls the
final day of judgment the day of redemption. Therefore these two different
forms of speech do not conflict with each other. In one we are said already
to be redeemed and to have redemption in Christ’s blood, already exempted
from slavery to sin and Satan and manumitted from them by the merit and
benefit of Christ’s death. In the other we are said to hope for and await the
same redemption, and to have been given this earnest of the Spirit of
adoption to nourish and seal this hope in us. Having been pledged by our Lord
with this earnest, we who have already been manumitted through faith may be
more certain that at last we will also be vindicated from the power of the
possessor in bad faith into rightful liberty. For teaching’s sake, the former
deliverance, or redemption considered in the former way, can be called “the
redemption of deliverance,” but the latter “the redemption of possession,” as
the Apostle calls it here and as Beza learnedly annotates on this passage.

[Marginalia: Ephesians 1:7. Second sense. Romans 8:23. Ephesians 4:30.
Coinage of names.]

As to the final member, namely, the end of this perfect deliverance of ours,
which is God’s own glory, there is nothing for us to say at present, since it
is the same subject mentioned so often above. For above, when speaking of his
own effectual calling and that of the other believing Jews, the Apostle says,
“so that we who first hoped in Christ may be for the praise of his glory.” In
the same way here, while he continues to apply the same grace of adoption,
the same benefit of the eternal inheritance, and glorious redemption to the
Ephesians and believing Gentiles, he repeats the expression “for the praise
of his glory.” He does so not only to make them equal to the Jews in this
respect, but also to remove from both groups every ground of glorying in
themselves in the cause and matter of their salvation. He openly testifies and
insists that all these things came to both groups freely, so that all the
praise and glory of their salvation might be owed to his grace alone. For in
themselves both groups were alike sinners and entirely destitute of God’s
glory, so that whoever glories should glory in the Lord, and in him alone.

[Marginalia: Member 3. Passing over a familiar subject. Reason for the
repetition. Romans 3:23.]

Indeed, because throughout this passage the Apostle directs every good of
ours to God’s glory, and so often presses the same statement, we must
certainly draw this immediate inference: God’s glory ought to be not only the
end of all our actions and of our entire life, as we observed above and
therefore need not repeat here, but also the rule of all the goods we ought to
wish and seek for ourselves. Thus, in asking them from God, we should always
consider not so much our own advantage as the divine glory. We should finally
both desire and pray that they be granted to us if they can be consistent
with God’s glory and can serve to display it, and not merely if they will be
of use to us, although they may otherwise be advantageous. For this regard
for the divine glory ought so to rule and dominate in our soul, so to prevail
and outweigh all other things, that we set up nothing at all to counterbalance
it, much less anything of equal weight. When it enters the mind, every other
consideration should cease, and it alone should hold and guide the helm and
shaft of our reason and will. So dear and precious to us ought that glory to
be, which God himself set before himself as the end and target toward which he
would direct all his works, including our salvation through Christ and even
the just destruction and condemnation of the devil and the ungodly. It
remains now for us to derive one or two observations from this in our
customary manner.

[Marginalia: Corollary. Colossians 3:15.]

First, from both these most striking comparisons—sealing and the earnest—which
the Apostle uses in these two verses, even a blind person can surely perceive
how carefully divine kindness has provided for us, not only for eternal
salvation against spiritual death, but also for spiritual security against
carnal fear and distrust. He did this so that our souls would not be
perpetually suspended and tossed about, but, built upon a solid foundation,
would retain a living and firm hope to the end, namely, an immovable and
unshaken certainty of our adoption and eternal inheritance. He willed not
only to confirm his most certain and true promise by an oath, but, with the
blood and death of the Testator intervening, to make that new and gracious
covenant ratified and authentic like an inviolable testament. Nor this alone:
he willed also, through true and living faith, effectually to inscribe that
same covenant on the tablets of our hearts. Nor only this: by the Spirit of
regeneration impressed upon us as a seal, he willed to seal that same
promise. Finally, not content even with this, he willed the character of that
Spirit impressed upon us, and therefore the Spirit of adoption himself
remaining and dwelling in us, to be a pledge and earnest by which we might be
fully persuaded concerning the inheritance to be received at its appointed
time, namely, on that final and glorious day of our redemption. Who does not
therefore see that this was God’s very design: to produce full confidence and
unconquerable assurance in believers’ souls, so that we might have the
strongest foundation of consolation, on which our faith would always rest
and remain unconquered in life as well as in death?

[Marginalia: Observation 1. Further insistence. Conclusion of the
observation.]

From this it is proved more clearly than the noonday light that nothing at all
conflicts more directly with God’s counsel in dispensing our salvation to us
through the gospel, and nothing disagrees more with his will, than that
teaching—plainly diabolical, to state it in one word—by which people are
taught to doubt their salvation, adoption, God’s grace, and fatherly love.
For, as we have just perceived abundantly, God, the most merciful Father, does
everything to the contrary: he removes from us every occasion and from our
souls every scruple for doubt; heals this disease of distrust and unbelief
which is so natural and so deeply rooted in us; utterly tears out and
eradicates its—

[Marginalia: Corollary.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 95 | Caput I p. 53]

—roots from us; and makes us most certain of his promise by giving us an
earnest so dear and precious to himself, namely, his own Holy Spirit. O most
precious earnest of salvation! Have those people ever felt it, have they ever
received it from God, who teach that it is pleasing to God and fitting for
Christian souls to be uncertain about their salvation and adoption, to doubt
and waver? Far be it that so great a crime, so impious a thought, should ever
enter our souls! For the Holy Spirit of promise, who is given to believers and
is the earnest of that inheritance of ours, is the Spirit of certainty, full
assurance, and confidence. He is the sealing Spirit and the very seal by which
we are sealed by God’s own hand, not the Spirit of distrust, doubt, or
uncertainty.

[Marginalia: Amplification.]

For consider this. If you have received an earnest from a man of good faith,
from a buyer whom you know to be upright, honest, and constant and who,
moreover, has ample means to pay, you will not doubt that he will pay you the
price of the thing sold. Henceforth secure, you will rest even on his word
alone, apart from any earnest. But if the buyer with whom you are dealing has
given you not merely some earnest of any sort, but, in place of an earnest, a
precious ornament worth many talents, entirely golden, jeweled, and set with
diamonds, which you know very well the buyer would not wish to lose at any
price, then whatever your buyer’s faithfulness may finally be, once you have
received an earnest of this kind you will be far more secure concerning the
price for your goods to be paid by him.

[Marginalia: Heightening by a comparative argument. Application.]

But then—O blind treachery of the human mind!—why do you not reflect on what
sort of person you are dealing with: not a buyer, but the gracious giver and
promiser of salvation; and what an earnest of salvation you have received
from him? Will you still doubt that God, the best and greatest, reserves the
most certain salvation for you in heaven, after he has pledged you as a
believer with the most precious gift of his own Spirit—than which, so to
speak, he has nothing more precious in his possession—using Ambrose’s word,
and by this betrothal earnest has designated and sealed you together with the
other believers as a bride for his Son Jesus Christ?

Meanwhile I do not deny that scruples of doubt sometimes assail us, that our
ship even frequently strikes the rocks of the gravest temptations, and that
our faith, although true and living, can be tossed and troubled by various
winds of distrust and storms from an evil consciousness of sins. What mortal
would dare deny it? But I say that these disturbances of conscience, these
whirlwinds and storms of temptations, these scruples of distrust and dreadful
rocks of doubts are the very root of that accursed flesh and the fibers of
native unbelief still remaining in us. We must struggle with them from time
to time throughout life, and they must be torn out and eradicated daily, not
nursed and nourished according to that satanic doctrine. For although they
conflict diametrically with true faith, assaulting and opposing it more
gravely in some and more lightly in others, as God has seen fit to exercise
some more and others less with warfare of this kind, in true believers they
can never conquer it or extinguish it entirely.

[Marginalia: Objection. Answer.]

For doubts and temptations of this sort are no small part of the gates of hell
of which Matthew 16 speaks. Against the true Church as well as its true
members they have some strength, agitating, troubling, tempting, and
assaulting them, but they will never prevail against them by conquering and
overcoming them. A certain pagan historian once said of the Roman Empire that
the Romans were often defeated in battle but never in war, as long, of
course, as that ancient Roman state stood. How much more truly and correctly
will we say this of the true Church and each of its members? Believers can be
defeated in this battle or that one, but never in the war as a whole, whose
end and outcome always proves joyful and happy for them.

[Marginalia: Note the confirmation in Matthew 16:18. A note in passing.
Florus, unless I am mistaken.]

Furthermore, to fortify us with the full assurance of faith against all this
opposing force, whether inborn—such as the remnants of sin and the fibers of
native unbelief, which, whatever those natural-minded Papists babble to the
contrary, is the bitter and detestable root of our inborn sin—or assailing us
from elsewhere, such as Satan’s fiery darts, our heavenly Father, who has the
clearest knowledge of our weakness, gave us this earnest, so certain and so
precious, without which our faith would have been undone.

[Marginalia: Exposition.]

From this emerges the second observation on this passage. Since we plainly
see that God’s will and very counsel is that we should be most certain of our
adoption and heavenly inheritance and well fortified and defended against
all the power of hell, yet our nature is so far removed from and opposed to
this certainty—as both that already refuted carnal doctrine of merely carnal
people, that is, the Papists, sufficiently indicates and the weakness of
almost everyone’s faith attests—where does this full assurance of faith come
from for us? What must we do to become possessors of that certainty so saving
and desirable? The Apostle teaches us precisely this in the same passage:
namely, that we must seek that earnest of the Spirit, which alone will produce
and bring forth that blessed certainty of faith in our hearts. To state it in
one word, throughout our lives we must strive and contend with the highest
zeal to perceive Christ’s Spirit residing and dwelling in us.

[Marginalia: Observation 2.]

But, again, by what practical working out will we attain this? How will we
perceive the Spirit of God dwelling in us, cherish him as present, and invite
and allure him, so to speak, to establish his seat in us? Namely—to avoid
detaining you with long circumlocutions—if, denying ourselves and the world,
we devote ourselves to true holiness. For he will engrave his image and the
character of holiness upon us, by no means as an idle guest, ineffective like
a dead medicine in the stomach. He himself will daily purify, enlighten, and
soften our hearts and conform us to the image of Christ, until he finally
completes in our heavenly homeland the work of remaking us which he began
here. Therefore, as dear as the certainty of eternal salvation ought to be to
us, so earnestly must we devote ourselves to repentance and newness of life,
and so earnestly must Christ’s footsteps be set before and pressed upon us.
But whatever obstructs this arduous and divine work of our regeneration and
sanctification must be shunned more than every plague and death. Such things
call away, grieve, disturb, and, as it were, compel the very Spirit of God,
who strives and labors at that work in us, to desist from what he has begun.
They compel him not only to withdraw his hand from that tablet of our heart
upon which he had been placing that image of his holiness with such care, but
also, provoked by our sins, to depart and, with his presence, to withdraw from
us all spiritual peace, light, life, and joy. Behold the things which a devout
soul ought continually to meditate on and earnestly and constantly ask from
God in prayer, if it wishes to obtain this earnest, so holy, precious, and
saving, and retain it throughout life!

[Marginalia: Conclusion.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 96 | De Prædestinatione p. 54]

Now, to conclude with this general observation the whole treatment of the
causes and means of our salvation contained in this first part of the
chapter, we may gather briefly from all the preceding discussion the order of
our salvation, consisting in certain stages, and reduce it to the following
summary and synopsis. The first stage, rightly laid beneath the others in the
first place by our Apostle, is our eternal election in Christ to life,
adoption, and holiness, for the praise of God’s glorious grace, an election
which depends on his mere good pleasure and gracious benevolence. The second
is our gracious redemption in Christ, accomplished for us on the cross,
together with those other spiritual benefits which he merited and obtained
for us by his death, even while we were unaware and had not yet been brought
forth into this light. The third is the dispensation or communication of
God’s grace and of this salvation laid up for us in Christ and acquired for
us through Christ. By it we are effectually called into fellowship with him
through the voice of the gospel, are given faith, are sealed with the Spirit,
and receive the earnest of that heavenly inheritance to confirm its hope and
certainty in us. Finally, the last stage of this salvation is “the redemption
of the possession,” that is, assertion or vindication into full and perfect
liberty, consummated redemption, and the full possession and enjoyment of the
eternal inheritance itself, which will make us eternally blessed in the glory
of the life to come. To God through Jesus Christ be all honor and glory for
ever and ever. Amen.
