But Arminius professes candor. He certainly professes it in words, yet in reality he vigorously drives it away from himself. By following his tracks, we have repeatedly exposed his crafty and slippery disposition, his remarkable dissimulation, and his crudely sophistical art. Here, however, we shall explain briefly how far this very profession is from candor. For he professes that he set himself to read this book of Perkins with the intention that Perkins might by this labor remove the scruples which had long troubled his mind. Let us consider briefly what he means by those “scruples,” for the removal of which he pretends that he undertook to examine that treatise diligently. Scruples are surely nothing other than certain arguments opposed to the opinion we embrace, arguments which we are unable to answer precisely but which are nevertheless not important enough to make us change our opinion at once, only to make us rest in it less firmly. But what sort of scruples were those which moved Arminius to have recourse to Perkins, in the hope that perhaps they might be removed by his labor, if that were possible? It plainly appears necessary that they were objections to the very opinion which Perkins had undertaken to defend and to vindicate from the accusations burdening it.

For no one could expect him to remove scruples of another kind in this work. It follows that Arminius must at that time have professed one and the same opinion as Perkins when he took up this book to remove what he calls those scruples. I say that this must have been so, provided that he states simply and candidly what the fact is, rather than falsely and with artful pretense putting forward as real what is not so. For if he had held a different opinion opposed to Perkins, those “scruples” which he pretends he wished to have removed should not be called scruples that he wished removed. They would instead be just so many arguments confirming his own opinion, or just so many accusations aggravating the opposing opinion.

What, then, are we to judge? Did Arminius approach the reading of Perkins's book with the very disposition he professes, as one who had until then shared Perkins's faith in the matter of predestination, though perhaps his faith was somewhat weakened by those scruples? Or had he already embraced an altogether contrary opinion, so that all his talk about scruples troubling his mind is the feigned, theatrical fiction of a masked actor? His countrymen, fellow citizens, and fellow initiates know best whether it was so or otherwise. Yet neither are we without evidence from which to gather something about this matter. In 1613 the conference of Jacob Arminius with Francis Junius on predestination was published. In the dedicatory epistle the editor reports that Arminius attempted, in a short writing sent to Junius, to demonstrate by selected arguments that both opinions—the opinions of Calvin and Junius—besides other disadvantages introduced a necessity of sin, and that it was therefore necessary to arrive at a third opinion which presupposed creation and the fall. Had he achieved this, he intended to advance further and at last propose a decree to save believers and condemn unbelievers. After Junius answered this writing, Arminius composed a reply and sent it to Junius. Dr. Junius kept that reply with him for six full years before God called him from this life, as the same editor testifies. Since Arminius succeeded Junius in his position in 1603, it follows that Arminius wrote that reply before 1597. It can be established from the reply what opinion Arminius embraced at that time. Perkins's book, however, appeared in 1598. At that time Arminius pretended that he shared Perkins's opinion on the article of predestination, though not without scruples troubling his mind. Behold the man's candor, even when he makes the greatest possible profession of candor!

Anyone can easily perceive where these stratagems tend: namely, to enjoy more favorable winds for advancing their cause, at least among the common run of theologians who are led more by a falsely reported success in events than by the weight of reasons. This is especially so when they have heard that a teacher of so great a name was trained from his youth in the school of Beza or Calvin, and imbued with their doctrines both on other heads of theology and on the subject of predestination; but at last, when the arguments for the contrary opinion outweighed them, he departed from those teachers almost against his will and embraced the opposing opinion as resting on better foundations.

The more pompous narrative of Bertius in his funeral oration on the death of Jacob Arminius tends to the same end. He says, namely,

[Praefatio; printed p. 19; BSB canvas 27]

that Arminius was urged by Dr. Martin Lydius—who had previously served as pastor in the Amsterdam church and was then professor in the new academy of the Frisians—to write a refutation of a certain booklet written in the church at Delft, whose title was *A reply to certain arguments of Beza and Calvin, from the treatise on predestination concerning Romans 9*. Arminius therefore equipped himself for this work. “But while he was laboring at the refutation,” Bertius says, “while he weighed the arguments on both sides, while he compared the Scriptures, while he ranged about and wearied himself, he was conquered by the truth. First he adopted the very opinion which he had been opposing; later, by the divine rod and the leading of the Holy Spirit, he was carried into that opinion which he constantly asserted to the end of his life.” That is, God's eternal decree in predestination is not to choose precisely and absolutely certain people whom he had not yet decreed to create, as Dr. Beza held; nor, once a decree of creation and a foreseen fall were posited, to choose certain people apart from the antecedent consideration of Jesus Christ, as the Delft brethren held; but from among created and fallen people to choose those who would answer the calling God with the obedience of true faith, as was maintained by the most learned Melanchthon, Nicolas Hemmingius, and very many other theologians. From these facts one may also gather what that opinion was toward which Arminius was being carried not merely almost, but under full sail, before he arranged to publish his book on Perkins. Yet here he was not ashamed to profess that he had consulted Perkins—where was his modesty? where his candor?—about certain scruples which had troubled his mind, as though he still cherished the same opinion as Perkins.
