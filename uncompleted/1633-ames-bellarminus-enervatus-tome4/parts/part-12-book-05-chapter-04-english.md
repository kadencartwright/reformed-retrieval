### Chapter IV

The second question concerning faith is whether it alone justifies. The
Pontificians deny it; we affirm it.

This question is treated in reverse order by Bellarmine, before he had
estab-*

[Printed p. 308 | Google Books PA308 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*lished what it is to justify or in what thing justification properly
consists. He therefore could not but dispute about it in a thoroughly confused
way—which perhaps he also did deliberately.

#### Bellarmine's Arguments

Bellarmine. 1. *Faith does not so much justify as it justifies as the beginning
and first root of justification.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine here plays a perplexing sophistical game while
parading a proof. For I. The statement is ridiculous and devoid of sense:
*faith does not so much justify as it justifies as the beginning.* II.
“Beginning” can be understood in two ways: it denotes either the beginning of
a movement *toward producing a thing,* or the beginning or first degree *in a
thing produced.* And in the first sense it can be a beginning either together
with other things of the same kind or alone in its own kind. Bellarmine does
not indicate what he wished to prove.

Bellarmine. 1. Hebrews 11:6. *The first movement toward God is attributed to
faith, through which the man who was far away now begins to draw near.*

Protestant reply. If by *begins to draw near* Bellarmine understands something
other than *coming* to God in Christ itself, then he contradicts Christ
himself, who in John 6:35 marks these phrases as having the same meaning: *he
who comes to me; he who believes in me.* If he understands the same thing, he
does not contradict us.

Bellarmine. 2. Romans 10:13. *Faith, as the inception of justification,
precedes invocation and the other things.*

Protestant reply. I. Nothing is defined there about the order of faith in
relation to justification. II. Justification itself precedes spiritual and
effectual invocation and all the other things of the same kind. Therefore
this objection contains no appearance of any proof.

Bellarmine. 3. John 1:12. *As many as received him, to them he gave the power
to become the sons of God. Here John plainly teaches that those who receive
Christ through faith are not yet sons of God, but can become sons if they
proceed further, so that they also begin to hope and love.*

Protestant reply. John teaches no such thing either openly or obscurely. For
I. He does not say that believers have power, δύναμιν, but dignity or right,
ἐξουσίαν, to be sons of God. II. He says nothing about their *not yet* being
sons or about their *having to proceed further* for this to occur. III. In
express words he affirms in verse 13 that they were *born of God.* IV. This is
John's whole syllogism: *Those who have the Son have life,* 1 John 5:12. Those
who believe in Christ have received (or have) him. Therefore they have life.
V. According to Bellarmine's doctrine of grace, that sufficient assistance is
given to each and every human being according to place and time, by which, if
they proceed fur-*

[Printed p. 309 | Google Books PA309 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*ther, they can become sons of God. This, then, is no privilege of those who
believe in Christ.

Bellarmine. 4. *Faith is the foundation of hope and love.*

Protestant reply. So too is justification. Romans 5:3; 1 John 4:10–11.

Bellarmine. 5. *In bodily illnesses, the beginning of health is for the sick
man to believe and to have faith in the physician who wishes to cure him; yet
that faith alone is not complete health.*

Protestant reply. Nor can a similar faith justify, but rather that faith by
which the medicine that alone removes the guilt of sin is received and
applied.

2. Bellarmine. *Fear concurs in justification in almost the same way that
faith itself concurs.*

Protestant reply. This can in no way occur. For the proper object of fear is
evil, not good, much less that good by which we are justified in Christ.

Bellarmine. 1. Ecclesiasticus 1:27. *He who is without fear will not be able to
be justified.*

Protestant reply. I. Nothing of the kind is found in the Greek text, but, as
Arias Montanus translates it: *the irascible man (θυμώδης) will not be able
to be justified.* II. If Ecclesiasticus had said so, it would no more follow
from it that fear justifies than it follows that agriculture or some similar,
more honorable occupation in life justifies, since the same Ecclesiasticus in
chapter 26 denies that an innkeeper is just or is justified from sin.

Bellarmine. 2. *The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom,* Psalm 110
and Proverbs 1; *by wisdom perfect justification is understood, and by fear,
servile fear.*

Protestant reply. Neither of these is true. Yet even if both were granted,
nothing else would follow from the words except that men are aroused by fear
to seek justification through faith.

Bellarmine. 3. *Fear causes us to approach God.*

Protestant reply. Just as the law, which does not justify, does this, so also
does the fear struck into us by the law—namely, as an antecedent occasion.

Bellarmine. 4. *Fear is a fountain of life.* Proverbs 14:27.

Protestant reply. Not with respect to the acquisition of the right, as
justifying faith is, but with respect to the way of obtaining possession
itself. Yet this is not servile fear but religion itself in its entirety.

Bellarmine. 5. *The fear of the Lord drives out sin.* Ecclesiasticus 1:21.

Protestant reply. It wards off future sins; it does not remove the guilt of
past sins.

Bellarmine. 6. *It is the nature of fear to flee evils and seek remedies.*

[Printed p. 310 | Google Books PA310 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. This is the nature of filial fear, not servile fear. But for
justification there is required that which removes the evil, not merely flees
it, and which finds such a remedy, not merely seeks it.

3. Bellarmine. *The hope of obtaining pardon is also a disposition toward
righteousness and the remission of sins.* Proverbs 28:25; Psalm 37:40; Psalm
91:14; Matthew 9:2.

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine cared little that, in order to make trouble
for us, he contradicted himself. For earlier, in chapters 4 and 10, he
maintains that *trust is hope and cannot justify, because it presupposes
justification and depends upon it, and because an object cannot be produced by
the very act whose object it is.* Yet here he wishes the hope of obtaining
pardon to be the cause of obtaining pardon or justification. He cannot excuse
it there as a general, conditional, uncertain hope, for by that means he will
not escape the force of the argument, nor is that the sort of hope praised in
the passages he cites. II. None of these passages except the last treats the
remission of sins; yet even there nothing is said about *the hope of pardon,*
but about confidence or trust in a miraculous healing. III. Hope is named in
none except the second, where, however, the Hebrew word *(casa)* means the
same as *(batac)* in the others—namely, a firm and trustful expectation of
salvation.

4. Bellarmine. *Some love is prior to the remission of sins—prior in time if
it is imperfect love, or by nature if it is perfect—and disposes one toward
that remission.*

Protestant reply. I. Something can be prior to remission as a prerequisite
disposition without being its cause. II. No love precedes remission as a
causative disposition.

Bellarmine. 1. Luke 7. *Her many sins were forgiven her because she loved
much.*

Protestant reply. I. The proof from ὅτι, “because,” can be drawn no less from
a consequent effect than from an antecedent cause. II. That love is noted in
this passage as the effect of remission is plainly apparent from the whole
discourse: *The debtor to whom the creditor showed more favor will love more.
But it was not Simon the Pharisee, but the sinful woman, who loved more.
Therefore God the creditor showed more favor not to Simon but to the woman.*
The argument is from the effect. III. In express words faith alone is named as
the cause, verse 50: *Your faith has saved.*

Bellarmine. 2. Galatians 5:6. *Faith avails which works through love.*

Protestant reply. The quality and causality of faith, not of love, are
declared here.

[Printed p. 311 | Google Books PA311 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Bellarmine. 3. John 3:14. *We have passed from death to life because we love
the brethren.*

Protestant reply. This is not so much sophistry as the utmost impudence: to
mutilate the sacred text in this way. John's words are: *We know that we have
passed from death to life because we love the brethren.* Love, as the effect
of the passage from death, is the reason or argument for the knowledge we have
of that passage. But Bellarmine, by removing the thing being proved, wishes
his novel axiom to be John's.

5. Bellarmine. *Repentance (which is grief over and detestation of sin) is
granted by God unto life,* Acts 11:18; *it works repentance unto salvation,*
2 Corinthians 7:10; *and it is said to make the soul alive,* Ezekiel 18:27;
*therefore it justifies.*

Protestant reply. I. Repentance, insofar as it is legal humiliation, does
indeed precede justification as a disposition prerequisite in the order, but
not as a cause. II. Evangelical repentance either denotes the whole of
conversion, whose principal part is faith, as in Acts 11 and Ezekiel 18, or
is the effect of justifying faith and thus of justification itself. The
repentance unto salvation in 2 Corinthians 7:10 was of this kind. III. However
it is understood, grief over and detestation of sin cannot be a justifying
cause, because it has no power to apply Christ's righteousness to us. The
acquisition of such a good does not consist in aversion from evil. The
difference between repentance and faith is indicated in Acts 20:21:
*Repentance toward God and faith in our Lord Jesus Christ.*

6. Bellarmine. *The sixth disposition is the purpose and desire actually to
receive the Sacrament.*

Protestant reply. The desire to seek grace and salvation through the means
instituted by God for that end is virtually contained in true faith. But that
desire, insofar as it is directed toward the outward Sacrament, does not
justify, because justification is not bound to the Sacraments.

7. Bellarmine. *The seventh disposition is the purpose of a new life.*

Protestant reply. This purpose is a part of repentance, which indeed
accompanies justifying faith, but without any power to justify. A purpose is
effectual only toward that which it purposes.

8. Bellarmine. *If faith is separated from hope and love, and so forth, it
will not be able to justify; therefore faith alone cannot justify.*

Protestant reply. The consequence is denied, because the faith which
justifies has hope and love joined to it by its nature, so that if it is
actually separated from them, it is at the same time separated from itself,
or ceases to be such a faith.

Bellarmine. 1. *If the entire power of justifying were in faith alone, faith
itself would justify just as much when they were absent as when they were
present.*

[Printed p. 312 | Google Books PA312 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. The consequence is denied, because the faith which
justifies has hope and love joined to it by its nature, so that if it is
actually separated from them, it is at the same time separated from itself,
or ceases to be such a faith.

Bellarmine. 1. *If the entire power of justifying were in faith alone, faith
itself would justify just as much when they were absent as when they were
present.*

Protestant reply. It does not follow, because faith itself cannot be present
where the other virtues are entirely absent.

Bellarmine. 2. *The seed of faith is said (by Calvin) to remain amid the most
grievous falls; therefore it can be separated from love.*

Protestant reply. Where the seed of faith remains, the seed of love is also
present; therefore they are not separated.

Bellarmine. 3. *Nevertheless, we do not say that faith is alone, but that if
it were alone, it would accomplish this.*

Protestant reply. The supposition is absurd. Let the man who supposes such
things see what would follow from it. It is just as if someone were to suppose
hope or love without faith and then inquire what they would accomplish if
they were alone.

Bellarmine. 4. *Faith will justify prior at least by nature, and will be
understood as joined with righteousness before it has brought love and the
other virtues.*

Protestant reply. We concede this.

Bellarmine. *It will follow that a man is just and not just, and so forth.*

Protestant reply. It will follow only that a man is just by nature prior
through the remission of sins and divine acceptance in Christ before he is
made just through sanctification in himself. This is most true.

Bellarmine. 5. *True faith can actually be separated from love and the other
virtues.*

Protestant reply. Some faith can, and Pontifical faith is of this kind. But
the faith to which we attribute the power to justify cannot be separated from
Christ's life-giving and sanctifying Spirit, since it brings about our union
with Christ. Thus Cardinal Contarini, in his refutation of Luther's articles,
article 1: *This faith is not merely that by which we believe that God exists
and believe that the things God says are true, for this faith is found even
in demons and the most abandoned men; but it is that by which we tend toward
God, and from this faith good works must necessarily proceed.* Therefore,
while Bellarmine strives with great effort to prove that some true faith,
though unformed, can be separated from love, he beats the air with the windy
fan of an empty disputation.

6. Bellarmine. *Nowhere in the sacred writings can that particle “alone” be
shown when justifying faith is treated.*

Protestant reply. The question is not about that little word but about the
sense. Since Scripture, in Romans 3 and 4 and Galatians 2 and 3, re-*

[Printed p. 313 | Google Books PA313 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*moves everything from justification except faith—*not by works, without
works, without law, but by faith, that it might be by grace*—it teaches
plainly enough that by faith it means faith *alone.* To this is also added
what Cardinal Contarini has on Romans 4: *It must be noted that Scripture
attributes this imputation of righteousness to nothing other than faith.* It
therefore follows that Scripture attributes justification to faith alone.

Bellarmine. 1. *Not all works and all laws are excluded. For faith itself is
a work,* John 6, *and faith is the law of faith,* Romans 3.

Protestant reply. I. Toledo replies concerning faith on Romans 3: *Note that
faith has no efficacy from itself, as some act of ours, for remitting and
reconciling; rather, the whole power proceeds from the object itself, namely
Christ, whose power and merit God ordained to be applied to the sinner for
justification through faith in him.* II. Cajetan replies concerning the law
of faith in the same place: *If you ask in what manner Paul distinguishes the
law of faith from the law of works, even moral works, since faith too is
comprehended in the genus of works—for believing is a work of the
spirit—the answer is that believing in him who justifies the ungodly rests
upon another's righteousness, namely God's through Christ; but the other
works rest upon one's own righteousnesses, such that every work is good in
itself and renders its possessor good.* III. It must be observed that faith
is called a *law* not properly but metaphorically, just as sin too is called
a law in Romans 7. Bellarmine therefore quite foolishly places the law of
faith among the species of law, just as Thomas too is ridiculous when he
wishes the law of sin to be a species of law, 1. 2, question 91.

Bellarmine. 2. *Grace excludes merits which are ours from ourselves, but not
the gifts of God.*

Protestant reply. The gifts of God are indeed communicated, not excluded, by
grace so that they exist and are approved by God. Nevertheless, they are
excluded from the act of justifying. Otherwise this consequence would not be
sound: *therefore it is of faith, that it might be by grace,* Romans 4:16.
Indeed, this excludes faith itself from justifying insofar as it is a gift of
God and our act or habit, as Toledo rightly observes on Romans 3: *If faith,
insofar as it is a certain act, produced righteousness from itself,
righteousness would not be given freely. God has not used works for
justifying as he has used faith, lest men should boast by attributing
righteousness to the power or merit of works.*

10. Bellarmine. *It did not please God to grant justification with the sole
condition of faith.*

Protestant reply. This pleased God most of all. Romans 3:25: *God set forth
Christ to be a propitiation through faith.* John 3:16: *He gave his only
begotten Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal
life.* The apostle also plainly testifies in Galatians 3:11–12 that faith
stands in the Gospel as “*do this*” stands in the law.

Bellarmine. *Scripture more expressly requires for justification the condi-*

[Printed p. 314 | Google Books PA314 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*tion of repentance and of the Sacraments than that of faith itself,* Ezekiel
18:27; Luke 13:4; John 3:5.

Protestant reply. The falsity of this assertion is sufficiently manifest from
what has now been said. *Scripture* (Contarini says on Romans 3) *attributes
justification to nothing other than faith.* When the apostle discusses
justification in the Epistles *to the Romans and Galatians,* he requires no
condition except faith for someone to become a partaker of it. In the passages
cited by Bellarmine, there is no mention of justification. Yet in the first
two repentance denotes conversion, whose principal part is faith, and
regeneration in John 3 consists principally in the production of faith, whose
seal is the Sacrament.

11. Bellarmine. *Faith alone does not possess the ability to apprehend
justification, apply it to us, and make it ours.*

Protestant reply. If the matter stood thus, Scripture would have to be charged
with error, for in John 1:12 it teaches that *receiving Christ and believing
in his name* are one and the same.

Bellarmine. 1. *Faith is not properly said to apprehend. Apprehensive
knowledge is indeed presupposed for faith, but is not properly faith.*

Protestant reply. This skirmish is futile. For Bellarmine was not ignorant
that by apprehension we understand something far different from the
speculation of the intellect. Contarini correctly says in his treatise on
justification: *We receive justification through faith,* Galatians 3:14.
*Thomas calls this reception application. Protestants call it apprehension,
not in the signification which pertains to the knowledge of the intellect,
but in the sense in which we are said to apprehend that at which we arrive and
which we attain after our motion.*

Bellarmine. 2. *To trust is not to apprehend but to adhere firmly to a thing
apprehended.*

Protestant reply. To adhere firmly to something so that we are sustained by
it in attaining the desired end is to trust and to apprehend, in the sense in
which we employ those words in this matter.

Bellarmine. 3. *Grant that justification is apprehended in some way through
faith. Certainly it is not apprehended in such a way that it is already
actually possessed and inheres, but only so that it is present in the manner
of an apprehended object. But love and joy also apprehend in this way. Love
especially makes another's things ours, for lovers have all things in common.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine foolishly confounds these two things: *that it
is actually possessed, and that it inheres.* Many things are possessed which
do not inhere. II. The apprehension of justification through true trust is
not simply in the manner of an object, but in the manner of an object given to
us. For Scripture testifies in express words that God has given believers
Christ and all things with him, Romans 8:32. Yet it must be observed here
that, speaking precisely, the apprehension of Christ and his righteous-*

[Printed p. 315 | Google Books PA315 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*ness is justifying faith, because our justification arises from the
apprehension of Christ; but the apprehension of justification as our present
possession is the fruit and effect of the prior apprehension. III. Joy in our
righteousness through Christ does not make us just but presupposes us to be
just; it flows from the apprehension of justification and has its foundation
in the trustful apprehension of Christ. IV. The love of friendship toward God
cannot be possessed by us before we understand that God has been reconciled
to us and made, as it were, our friend through faith. The act of love is
exercised by being sent outward from us toward God, not by being sent inward
from God to us. Finally, our love toward others does not make what belongs to
them ours, but makes what is ours theirs.

Bellarmine. 4. *The Sacraments apply the promises and make them ours;
therefore faith alone does not justify in the manner of an applying
instrument.*

Protestant reply. Nevertheless, of the things that are in us or are directed
by us toward God, faith alone receives. For the Sacraments are from God toward
us, and they apply the promise as instruments of giving, not of receiving.

Bellarmine. *This makes little difference, for each is an instrument of God.*

Protestant reply. It makes a very great difference. Although in some sense the
Sacraments can be called our instruments, insofar as through them as means we
attain our end, properly speaking they are instruments of God. So too faith
can be called an instrument of God, because God justifies us *from faith and
through faith,* Romans 3:30; properly speaking, however, it is our instrument.
God baptizes and feeds us, not we ourselves; we believe in Christ, not God.

12. Bellarmine. *True and justifying faith is not the bare and sole
apprehension of righteousness, but is a cause and has the power of justifying.*

Protestant reply. This opposition between the apprehension of righteousness
and a cause of justifying is absurd, because apprehension itself is in its own
manner a cause. Contarini correctly says in his treatise on justification:
*We are justified by faith not formally, as whiteness makes a wall white or
health makes a man healthy, but efficiently, as whitewashing makes a wall
white and medication makes a man healthy. Thus, or in a not dissimilar way,
faith makes a man just and justifies him.*

Bellarmine. 1. *When the apostle denies that a man is justified through works
or from works, through law or from law, he does not deny that righteousness
is apprehended by law or works. Nor was that the opinion of those whom he
refutes; rather, they thought that the gift of justification was owed to them
by the merit of their works. Therefore, since the same apostle attributes to
faith what he denies to works and law, saying that a man is justified through
faith and from faith, he does so without*

[Printed p. 316 | Google Books PA316 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*doubt, attributing efficacy and some merit to faith with respect to
justification.*

Protestant reply. I. If faith is here understood as designating its object,
Christ, by metonymy—which is the apostle's meaning in many places—then the
whole conclusion can be conceded. II. If faith is considered as our act by
which we are borne toward Christ, it can also be conceded once the word
“merit” is struck out. III. Bellarmine is inconsistent in all his arguments.
Earlier, in chapters 14 and 15, he proved that faith alone does not justify
because it can be in a man who is not just; but now he wishes faith to justify
in the same way the observance of the whole law would justify if it were
present. IV. He exposes his grossest error in wishing justification to be the
reward of faith as merit, in precisely the same way that those against whom
the apostle disputes wished it to be the reward of observing the law. V. The
foundation of this objection is utterly vain: namely, that in an opposition
of this kind—*not from this, but from that*—the same genus of cause is
denoted on either side. Romans 9:8, 11, 16 and 11:6: *Sons not of the flesh
but of the promise; not from works but from the one calling; not of the one
running but of the one showing mercy; from the grace of election, not from
works.* Is anyone among the Pontificians so senseless that he would dare to
affirm that precisely the same genus of cause is denoted on either side in
these oppositions?

13. Bellarmine. *Faith is the beginning of righteousness and thus is the
inchoate formal cause of justification.*

Protestant reply. Faith is the beginning of righteousness just as looking at
the bronze serpent was the beginning of healing, and thus it is not the
inchoate formal cause of justification but an instrumental cause.

Bellarmine. 1. Romans 4:5. *Faith is reckoned for righteousness; therefore
faith itself is righteousness.*

Protestant reply. Toledo the Jesuit replies on that passage: *Faith is not
said to be reckoned for righteousness as though it itself were the
righteousness produced, but because it introduces righteousness into a man
before God—not insofar as it is an act of man, for then grace would be from
works, since believing is a certain work, but from the divine will, by which
God willed righteousness to be given to man through faith and the power of
Christ, in whom one believes, to be communicated through faith to the
believer. This is to reckon faith for righteousness before God.*

Bellarmine. 2. 1 Corinthians 3:11. *Faith is the foundation, and a foundation
is a part and beginning of a building. Therefore faith is a part and
beginning of justification.*

Protestant reply. I. Faith is the foundation only by reason of its object,
Jesus Christ. II. There are four terms in this sophism, for the whole
spiritual building is not one and the same as justification.

[Printed p. 317 | Google Books PA317 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Bellarmine. 3. Acts 15:9. *Faith purifies the heart; it does this formally, as
light entering drives away darkness.*

Protestant reply. This is to beg the question, not to prove it.

14. Bellarmine. *Through faith the remission of sins is obtained, and by this
a man is justified from faith—not because faith merely apprehends the
promise, but because it obtains remission and even merits it in its own way.*

Protestant reply. I. This objection overthrows the previous one, for if faith
obtains and merits justification, then it is not itself formal righteousness.
II. That faith in some way merits remission is shamelessly affirmed against
Scripture without the slightest appearance of proof. III. To obtain remission
and to apprehend Christ unto the remission promised in him to all who
apprehend him are one and the same. Bellarmine therefore childishly opposes
them, and the proofs which he adduces to prove obtaining—which is not in
doubt—are entirely idle.

#### Our Arguments

15. Protestant argument. Scripture everywhere teaches that a man is justified
*without works, not from the law of deeds, not from works, not from law, but
from faith.* Therefore he is justified from faith alone.

Bellarmine. 1. *It must be noted, first, that in* Romans 3 *the law of deeds
in the apostle is that which commands what is to be done, but that the law of
faith is faith itself, which obtains the grace of doing what the law of deeds
commands.*

Protestant reply. This is indeed one difference between the law and the
Gospel, but it is neither the whole difference nor the one which the apostle
intends. For I. The apostle says nothing in that passage about obtaining
strength. II. He places the justifying power of faith in the relation which it
has to the propitiation of Christ's blood, verses 22 and 25. III. In Abraham's
example nothing at all is said about obtaining through faith the ability to
perform the law, but about the imputation of faith for righteousness. IV. In
David's testimony, obtaining the remission of sins is established as the
proper effect of justifying faith, which is far different from obtaining the
grace to perform the law. V. If there is no other difference between faith
and the law of works except that faith adds strength to act, then Christ's
death and propitiation certainly had no other use than to procure strength
for us so that we might do the works of the law. Cajetan saw this, and
therefore, seeking another interpretation, candidly declares the truth of the
matter: *If you ask in what manner Paul distinguishes the law of faith from
the law of works, even moral works, the answer is that believing in him who
justifies the ungodly rests upon another's righteousness, namely God's
through Christ; but the other works rest upon one's own righteousnesses.*

[Printed p. 318 | Google Books PA318 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Bellarmine. 2. *It must be noted, second, that the righteousness of the law is
the just works which the law prescribes as to be done, which righteousness
the apostle nowhere rejects as useless. But righteousness from the law or in
the law consists in those works which men do without the grace of faith. Paul
rejects this righteousness as useless and opposes it to the righteousness of
faith—that is, to good works which are done from grace and faith.*

16. Protestant reply. I. This distinction between *the righteousness of the
law and righteousness in or from the law* has no foundation either in the
Scriptures or in reason. II. Although the apostle does not reject the just
works prescribed by the law as simply useless, he nevertheless rejects them
from justification before God, as we shall afterward prove. III. If only
those works which are done without the grace of faith are excluded from the
act of justifying, then certainly the apostle had no need to labor much on
that question. For a man who thought himself previously justified without
grace and without Christ would not be counted among the faithful, nor could
he seek or receive grace. IV. Nothing more absurd can be imagined than what
Bellarmine dares maintain: that *the righteousness of faith is good works done
from grace and faith.* For the righteousness of faith is *without works,
imputed, consisting in the remission of sins,* Romans 4:6, 8. Who would affirm
these things of any of our works?

Bellarmine. 3. *It must be noted, third, that when the apostle says a man is
justified without works, he understands all works done before faith, not all
works absolutely.*

Protestant reply. This is plainly refuted in the person of Abraham, Romans 4
and Galatians 3, and also in the apostle's own person, Philippians 3:9.

Bellarmine. 4. *From these things it is answered concerning* Romans 3:27 *that
not all boasting is excluded, but only that which arises from works done by
the powers of free choice alone. For he does not say, “Where is boasting?” but
“Where is your boasting?”*

Protestant reply. The word *your* is superfluous in the Latin version (as
Cajetan observes), because it is not in the Greek text. Yet a man who earns
righteousness by his works, although they arise only in part from the powers
of free choice, has his *own* boasting at least from that part, in which he
can boast in himself, because he can arrogate to himself from that part the
praise for an effect of which he was the cause.

Bellarmine. 5. Romans 4:2. *It is answered that the works excluded are those
which Abraham could have done not from faith.*

Protestant reply. The apostle therefore excludes only those works which the
adversaries against whom he disputes could not have included.

Bellarmine. 6. *In the Epistle to the Galatians a twofold question is treated,
one*

[Printed p. 319 | Google Books PA319 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*particular—whether Christians can be saved without the legal
ceremonies—and the other general—whether justification can be obtained
through the law without grace and faith in Jesus Christ.*

Protestant reply. This latter question could scarcely have had a place among
the believing Galatians. If they had held any such thing, the apostle's
denunciation among them in Galatians 4:5 would certainly have been empty:
*You have been severed from Christ,* because they would have expected
justification and salvation without Christ.

Bellarmine. *The apostle's arguments—“If righteousness is through the law,
then Christ died in vain”; “therefore it is not from the promise”; “you have
fallen from grace”—are exceedingly weak and accomplish nothing if someone
wishes by them to exclude works which are done from grace and faith.*

Protestant reply. On the contrary, they are exceedingly strong, because by
the nature of the matter those who seek justification in themselves or their
works deny Christ.

Bellarmine. 7. *In* Philippians 3 *the apostle calls works done from knowledge
of the law by the powers of nature alone righteousness from the law.*

Protestant reply. Cajetan answers on the passage: *In contrast to one's own
righteousness and that of the law, he adversatively places the righteousness
which is from faith in Jesus Christ, or from God in faith. The righteousness
of the law and one's own righteousness is the observance of the commandments
of the law; but the righteousness of faith is the remission of sins. This is
said to be from faith in Jesus Christ believed, and it is said to be from God
in faith, in distinction from legal righteousness, which is indeed from God
but in a work. For this is the righteousness which was imputed by God to
Abraham in his faith. Thus it appears that this righteousness is not my
righteousness but faith's; nor is it from the law but from Jesus Christ
believed.*

17. Protestant argument. Scripture everywhere attributes justification to
faith alone, passing over all other works or dispositions: Matthew 9:5; Luke
7:50 and 8:48; John 1:12 and 3:36; Romans 5:1.

Bellarmine. 1. Luke 8:50 *treats the performance of a miracle, not the
justification of an ungodly man.*

Protestant reply. I. Earlier, in chapter 4, Bellarmine maintained that the
faith of miracles and justifying faith are one and the same faith. II.
Miracles of this kind were singular benefits of God by which the blessing of
justification was foreshadowed, Luke 4:18. III. These benefits were often
joined with justification, Matthew 9:3, 6.

Bellarmine. 2. *Other virtues alone or the Sacraments are also sometimes
named. For an effect toward which many causes concur is attributed now to one
cause, now to another. But justification is more frequently attributed to
faith because faith is the beginning of justification.*

Protestant reply. I. Contarini answers on Romans 4: *It must be noted that
Scripture attributes this imputation of righteousness to nothing other than
faith.* II. Faith (according to Bellarmine) is such a beginning of justifi-*

[Printed p. 320 | Google Books PA320 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*cation that it can be in a man who remains unjust. No reason is contained in
this for justification to be attributed more frequently to faith than to
love. III. Justification is so attributed to faith that the other things
Bellarmine calls contributing causes are declared to be effects, Romans
5:1–2, and so forth. IV. Even after he is just, the just man is said to live
by faith; and even when Abraham was already just, faith is nevertheless said
to have been imputed to him for righteousness.

Bellarmine. 3. *Life is continued through faith, but not through faith alone.
By a certain new act of faith the just Abraham was made more just.*

Protestant reply. Therefore, by Bellarmine's concession, Scripture attributes
to faith both the beginning and the continuation of justification. But since
faith alone is the beginning, and continuation is attributed to faith in
Scripture in the same way as the beginning, it follows that justification is
begun and continued by faith alone.

18. Protestant argument. To be justified freely or by grace is to be
justified by faith alone, because grace excludes all works from being causes
with it of the same effect, and specifically of justification: Romans 3, 4,
and 11; Ephesians 2; and so forth.

Bellarmine. 1. *The word “freely” can be understood in one way as opposed to a
debt from justice, or to merits of condignity. In this sense gratuitous
justification does not exclude works which are said to concur in
justification as dispositions or as merits of congruity. According to this
sense, to be justified freely is not the same as to be justified by faith
alone.*

Protestant reply. I. The apostle says expressly: *If it is from grace, then it
is not from works; if it is from works, then it is not from grace.* Bellarmine
will not concede this except with a limitation which has no foundation in
Scripture but only in the Pontificians' audacious fiction. II. Toledo the
Jesuit admits on Romans 3:24: *If faith, insofar as it is a certain act,
produced righteousness from itself, righteousness would not be given freely.*
Bellarmine wishes not only faith but also many other dispositions, which are
acts, to produce righteousness. III. A little later, explaining what he
proposes obscurely here, Bellarmine says that *merit of congruity is based
more upon some dignity of the work than upon a promise, but merit of
condignity is based upon a promise with the condition of a work.* But if this
is so, then merit of congruity, which is based on the work alone, is certainly
more opposed to grace than merit of condignity, which is based in part upon a
promise. Therefore, if grace excludes merits of condignity, as Bellarmine
admits, much more does it exclude merit of congruity. IV. The apostle in
Romans 4:16—*from faith, that it might be by grace*—plainly declares that
nothing of ours except faith can have a place in the act of justification by
grace.

Bellarmine. 2. *In another way the word “freely” can be understood as opposed
to a work done without any grace, and in this way gratuitous justification
does not*

[Printed p. 321 | Google Books PA321 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*exclude merits absolutely, but one's own merits, which are ours from
ourselves and not from God.*

Protestant reply. I. It is entirely unreasonable that “*freely* by his
*grace*,” δωρεὰν τῇ αὐτοῦ χάριτι, in Romans 3:24 should be opposed only to
merits which occur without any grace. Toledo the Jesuit refutes this in the
place cited: *We will not escape the difficulty by saying that faith itself
and repentance are gifts of God; for although they are gifts of God, they are
at the same time works of choice. I further confirm this same point: it is
certain that the works of the law too are of God, for they do not occur
without the gratuitous assistance of God,* and so forth.

2. The apostle shows plainly enough that by grace he understands the gracious
will of God, not a gratuitous gift inhering in us, as Bellarmine wishes:
*grace through the redemption in Jesus Christ, whom God set forth as a
propitiation through faith in his blood,* Romans 3:24–25. This is also
perfectly apparent from the objection in chapter 6: *Shall we remain in sin
that grace may become more abundant?* The apostle would surely have raised a
ridiculous objection against himself if by grace he understood the gift of
righteousness inhering in us.

19. Protestant argument. Scripture teaches everywhere that all who believe
are justified and saved. This would not be infallibly true unless faith alone
justified.

Bellarmine. 1. *“Everyone” can rightly be explained negatively: everyone who
believes is justified—that is, no one is justified without faith.*

Protestant reply. This negation is indeed contained in that affirmation,
because it declares justification to be proper to faith and believers; but
the man who affirmed that nothing more is indicated by this statement would
be insane. To interpret *all who believe are justified from faith, through
faith, without works* only negatively would be nothing other than mocking
God.

Bellarmine. 2. *“Everyone” can refer to every class of men.*

Protestant reply. It indeed can and must be so referred, but at the same time
also to every most particular species of believing men. So in the law,
*Whoever does these things shall live through them* must necessarily refer
both to every class of men and also to every individual man who does them.

Bellarmine. 3. *It is sometimes referred to individual believers, signifying
that faith, for its own part, always justifies and saves, provided the other
things which are required together with it are added.*

Protestant reply. By this sophistry someone could diminish the miracle of the
bronze serpent: *The one who looks at the bronze serpent will receive
health,* Numbers 21—that is, he who looks at the serpent and adds the other
things which, according to the rules of medicine, are effective for curing
such a disease. But the Lord himself teaches in John 3 that faith has the same
relation to justification as looking at the bronze serpent had to healing.

20. Protestant argument. That alone by which the Gospel is received
justifies; but the Gospel is received by faith alone. Therefore.

Bellarmine. 1. *The word of God must not only be apprehended by faith but also
fulfilled by love.*

Protestant reply. It is the word of promise which justifies, and this is
properly fulfilled by the one who promises.

Bellarmine. 2. *The word which is apprehended by faith is not salvation
itself.*

Protestant reply. I. Just as Christ is our righteousness and salvation, so
that which is apprehended by faith in the word of the Gospel is salvation
itself. II. It is sufficient for us if justification follows immediately from
the reception of the Gospel.
