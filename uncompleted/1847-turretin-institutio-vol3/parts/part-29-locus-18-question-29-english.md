QUESTION XXIX.

ON ECCLESIASTICAL POWER.

Does the church possess any spiritual power distinct from political power? We
affirm.

I. Since God, who is not a God of confusion but of order, instituted sacred
government and order in the church by which it might be duly gathered and,
once gathered, preserved and governed, he therefore willed pastors to be
equipped with some legitimate power and authority so that they might discharge
the office entrusted to them in a holy manner. But because this power is
variously abused both by excess and by defect, careful consideration must be
given to the limits within which it ought to be confined, lest it be either
extended too far or restricted too narrowly.

II. The papists err by excess, converting this power into dominion and an
intolerable tyranny over consciences. They confer it as supreme and absolute
upon the Roman pontiff as the vicar of Christ and successor of Peter. The
Erastians and Libertines, however, err by defect, along with other agitators of
that kind. They acknowledge no spiritual power given to the church, but hold
that mere preaching and persuasion have been left to pastors and that, if any
power belongs to them, all of it is derived from the magistrate. They contend
that every other power or jurisdiction of the church, whatever it may be, is
mere tyranny and indeed part of the mystery of iniquity. Thus, “while fools
avoid vices, they run into their opposites”: while wishing to remove tyranny,
they fall into

[1847 vol. III printed p. 233 | IA leaf 267 | PDF p. 267]

anarchy, and because of a thing’s abuse they abolish its legitimate use. Louis
du Moulin, son of the venerable Pierre du Moulin and doctor of medicine, joined
them not long ago. Following in Erastus’s footsteps, he maintained that
ecclesiastical power and the excommunication that depends upon it are a mere
human invention, indeed a part of the mystery of iniquity and the ladder by
which the pope climbed to the summit of his intolerable tyranny, through which
he impiously brought emperors and kings themselves under the yoke. He held that
no means more suitable for overthrowing the whole papacy at a single stroke
could be found than the abolition of ecclesiastical power. With great effort he
tries to prove this in his *Admonition to the Builders of an Empire within an
Empire*, in *The Throat of the Cause*, and in various letters and treatises
which he published on this subject. In them he rails violently and most
unjustly against the doctrine received in our churches, the practice hitherto
successfully employed with great benefit, and the blessed memory of the great
men and faithful servants of God who instituted this holy government in the
church. He is not ashamed to disparage our Calvin above all, as though these
men had still retained remnants of popery and had wished to erect the church’s
tribunal above the magistrate’s political tribunal. The vanity and falsehood
of his invention have already been exposed and soundly refuted by various
writers, most recently by the celebrated theologian Pierre Jurieu, his nephew,
in the learned treatise *On the Power of the Church*.

III. Holding a middle course between the two extremes, the orthodox
acknowledge that a power and authority distinct from political power has been
given to the church, but one that is only ministerial and subordinate, not
supreme and sovereign. Just as they confess that the tyrannical power of the
papacy, introduced by human agency, has rightly been abolished, so against the
disorder of the Anabaptists and Libertines they judge that the ecclesiastical
order divinely sanctioned must be retained from the Word of God.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

IV. For the state of the question and the nature of this ecclesiastical power
to be better understood, observe: (1) The question does not concern lordly,
royal, monarchical, supreme, and unaccountable power, which belongs to Christ
alone as head and king of the church, but the inferior and ministerial power
which belongs to his servants. (2) Nor does it concern extrinsic ecclesiastical
power, which is such only indirectly, objectively, and by external designation.
Although this power is occupied with the church in relation to outward things,
it does not formally inhere in the church or directly subordinate it to itself;
such is the magistrate’s power concerning sacred things. Rather, the question
concerns power that is formally and intrinsically ecclesiastical with respect
to spiritual things which properly pertain to faith and religion. (3) Nor does
it concern the extraordinary power of prophets and apostles, taken formally
and specifically as such, which was peculiar to them and did not pass to
others, but the ordinary and common power which Christ gave to the church and
all its governors. (4) The question does not concern power occupied with the
internal and invisible state of the church. In that state the church is subject
to Christ alone, and no mortal obtains any right and power over it in that
respect. Rather, it concerns the power occupied with the church’s external and
visible state in the communion of the same profession of faith and use of
sacred rites. This is nothing other than the sacred ministerial right granted
by Christ, the head of the church, to govern itself and its affairs externally
for mutual edification and salvation.

V. This power is distinguished in various ways. Some divide it into two parts:
dogmatic power and judicial or disciplinary power. To the former are referred
the preaching of the Word, administration of the sacraments, and the remaining
ecclesiastical acts, together with the things that serve them as means: the
gathering and constitution of churches, the election of ministers, the
convocation and holding of assemblies, and the like. The exercise of discipline
belongs to the latter. To these pertains the twofold key of knowledge and
government. Others more suitably establish three parts: (1) dogmatic, (2)

[1847 vol. III printed p. 234 | IA leaf 268 | PDF p. 268]

ordering, and (3) judicial and disciplinary. The first is occupied with the
dogmas of faith, the preaching of the Word, and the administration of the
sacraments. The second is occupied with ecclesiastical laws and constitutions
and the government of the church from them. The third concerns the exercise of
discipline and includes both the right to mark sinners by censure and to
excommunicate them—whether by minor excommunication through suspension from
the communion of sacred things or by major excommunication through exclusion
from the church—and the right to reconcile those so excommunicated when they
have repented.

[Marginalia: Ecclesiastical power is proved: 1. From the keys.]

VI. That such power is given in the church is proved: 1. From the keys given
to the church. Since it is agreed that keys are an emblem of power and
authority, there can be no doubt that a power distinct from political power was
given to pastors, for Christ expressly testifies that he gave them the keys of
the kingdom of heaven: namely, the power to close and open heaven, to bind and
loose sinners, and to remit and retain sins (Matt. 16:19–20; 18:18; John
20:22–23). Although the use of the keys is related to the preaching of the
Word, it is not contained within preaching but must be extended to the exercise
of discipline which Christ instituted in his church and which ought to be
exercised not only separately by individual pastors but jointly by the whole
council.

[Marginalia: 2. From the nature of a society.]

VII. 2. From the nature of a society, because no society can remain whole and
intact without some order and government, and government cannot subsist
without some power rightly and suitably to arrange all things that concern the
common good and to remove and ward off evils which threaten the society with
ruin. So the church of Christ, which is the most excellent and best-ordered of
all societies, could not preserve its integrity in this world unless it were
bound to a definite government and suitable laws by which its order and good
arrangement might be preserved inviolate against the deceits and assaults of
the flesh, the world, and Satan. Therefore Christ, who never fails to provide
his church with necessities, entrusted it with this power to be exercised
through its governors.

[Marginalia: 3. From the nature of an office.]

VIII. 3. From the nature of an office, because every function, office, and
ministry implies power. Whoever is entrusted with an office is given the right
and power to act according to it and is even placed under the necessity of
acting. Thus if someone is entrusted with the office of prætor, quæstor,
secretary, consul, and so forth, a corresponding power is understood by
necessary consequence to have been given to him. But sacred ministers are
charged with an office (Eph. 4:11–12; Matt. 16:19; Rom. 12:6; Acts 20:28).
Therefore they are given the power and right to exercise it in all its parts.

[Marginalia: 4. From the titles given to pastors.]

IX. 4. Because the titles by which pastors are distinguished denote this
power. They are called “those who preside” (1 Tim. 5:17), “those who are over
you” (1 Thess. 5:12), “leaders” (Heb. 13:7, 17), “overseers” (Acts 20:28),
“stewards” (1 Cor. 4:1–2; Titus 1:7), and “governments” (1 Cor. 12:28). All
these titles involve some authority and power over those committed to their
care. It is objected in vain that all these must be understood spiritually, of
the authority exercised in the administration of the Word, and not of that
which is concerned with discipline. The authority designated is that which was
then exercised by governors, and this did not concern only the preaching of
the Word but discipline also, as various examples teach. Moreover, the word
“governments” denotes a far different power from one that is merely doctrinal;
otherwise there would be no difference between those who rule well and those
who labor in the Word and doctrine (1 Tim. 5:17), whom Paul declares worthy of
double honor.

[Marginalia: 5. From passages of Scripture.]

X. 5. Because Scripture establishes this power in more than one place. “For
even if I should boast somewhat more of our power, which the Lord gave us for
your edification and not for your destruction, I shall not be ashamed” (2 Cor.
10:8). There he speaks not only of the power to proclaim the gospel but also of
the power to exercise discipline, as is clear from verses 4–6. Likewise: “For
this reason I write these things while absent, lest when present I use sharp
severity according to the authority which the Lord gave me for edification”
(2 Cor. 13:10). In both places the word *exousia*, which denotes power, occurs.
Other passages of this kind in which Paul asserts the authority of his ministry
belong here. Although the authority and power of pastors today falls far below
the apostolic authority that obtained in the primitive church, it must not
therefore be considered nonexistent. Pastors’ authority and power must always
have their own weight, however much less it may be.

XI. 6. Because the exercise of such power occurs more than once, as in the
Jerusalem council (Acts 15). The church not only convened it by the power
granted to it, but also exercised this power in several ways in the decision
itself: dogmatic power, by determining the disputed truth and rejecting error;
ordering power, by commanding the Gentiles to abstain from certain things for
the sake of peace and edification; and judicial power, by censuring the authors
of that schism and error and regarding them as liars and subverters of souls
(v. 24). It also pertains to this that the council’s decrees are called
“ordinances” and “ordinances that had been decided” (Acts 16:4), which
necessarily implies power and jurisdiction. Although the apostles who took
part in this council were endowed with the extraordinary gift of
infallibility, it does not follow that the decrees of the council were peculiar
to them and cannot pertain to other churches. It is agreed that not only the
apostles but also the presbyters and the whole council were given the force of
a decisive vote, as is gathered from Acts 16:4. The same is clear from the
apostolic practice toward the incestuous Corinthian, whom Paul with the whole
council judges ought to be delivered to Satan (1 Cor. 5:3–5). They could never
have done this unless they understood spiritual authority and power to have
been conferred upon them for it. More will have to be said about this passage
later when excommunication is treated. Third, it is clear from the practice of
the primitive church, which repeatedly assembled synods and made various
decrees and enactments concerning both doctrine and discipline.

[Marginalia: 7. From the Old Testament church.]

XII. 7. Because the Old Testament church had its own particular polity and
form of sacred government, in the exercise of which sacred ministers had a
power distinct from political power by the institution of God himself.
Therefore, by parity of reasoning, the same must hold for the New Testament
church, so that it too may have its own power and form of government to avoid
confusion and preserve good order.

[Marginalia: 8. From 1 Corinthians 14:32.]

XIII. 8. Because “the spirits of prophets are subject to prophets” (1 Cor.
14:32), namely, with respect to the examination of doctrine and with respect
to correction and censure if something worthy of rebuke should occur either in
doctrine or morals. From this it is clear that the prophets taken collectively
were granted power to judge prophets individually—not by a political judgment
or the merely discriminating judgment of private persons, but by an
ecclesiastical and authoritative judgment. If this had a place among
extraordinary prophets, how much more ought it to have a place among ordinary
ministers, so that no one should claim unaccountable power for himself, as
though he had the right to judge everyone while being judged by no one. Also
relevant is Paul’s prohibition elsewhere: “Do not receive an accusation
against a presbyter except under two or three witnesses” (1 Tim. 5:19). Here
government and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 236 | IA leaf 270 | PDF p. 270]

the power of government clearly shine forth, for receiving accusations under
two or three witnesses is an act of power.

[Marginalia: Ecclesiastical power is distinct from political power with
respect to origin.]

XIV. This power, whatever it may be, is not the same as political power but
differs from it in its whole genus. (1) With respect to origin, political power
was instituted by God the Creator and supreme ruler of the world, but
ecclesiastical power by Jesus Christ, the supreme head of the church and its
Lord and governor. The political magistrate as such does not properly and
formally serve to advance the kingdom of Christ, nor does he discharge his
office in Christ’s name, as is clear in the case of a pagan magistrate. But
ministers are sent by Christ to establish his kingdom and act in his name.

[Marginalia: The subject.]

(2) With respect to the subject, political power can reside with pagans and
strangers to the covenant, but ecclesiastical power cannot. The former can be
exercised by women; the latter cannot.

[Marginalia: The form.]

(3) With respect to form, political power is architectonic and sovereign,
which implies dominion and command. But ecclesiastical power is service,
stewardship, and ministry, consisting in ministry alone without any command or
dominion properly so called. Although the magistrate is God’s minister in
relation to God and his power is a ministry (Rom. 13:4), in relation to the
subjects over whom he presides he exercises dominion and command (1 Pet. 2:13;
Luke 22:25). Ministers, however, are only ministers and not lords, not merely
in relation to God but also in relation to the people over whom they preside (1
Cor. 4:1; 2 Cor. 1:24). The magistrate has the power to enact laws properly so
called for governing the commonwealth and, when necessity demands, to rescind
and change them. But ministers do not possess this legislative power. They can
and must proclaim Christ’s laws and apply them to particular cases; they can
make canons for maintaining good order; but they can enact no laws properly so
called. Christ alone is the legislator, and his laws can neither be changed nor
rescinded by ministers. The magistrate has coercive power over goods and bodies
(Rom. 13:4–5), but pastors are not armed with the sword and have no coercive
power to inflict bodily punishments.

[Marginalia: With respect to the end.]

XV. (4) With respect to the end. The principal and supreme end of the political
magistrate as such is the glory of God as Creator, preserver of the human race,
and ruler of the world. The subordinate end that concerns the community itself
is public peace and tranquility, the external and temporal good of the
commonwealth. Although the magistrate as a Christian ought also to advance
Christ’s kingdom, he does not do so as magistrate. But the supreme end of the
ministry is not only the glory of the Creator but also the glory of Christ the
Mediator, the king and head of his church. Its subordinate end is the peace and
salvation of the church and the preservation and propagation of the kingdom of
Christ the Mediator.

[Marginalia: The object.]

(5) With respect to the object. Political power has for its object things,
actions, and persons insofar as they pertain to physical life and public
coexistence, or to the outward person. Ecclesiastical power, however, has for
its object sacred things, persons, and actions as such, under this formal
qualification, insofar as they are and can be ordered toward union with God in
Christ and eternal salvation. It must not be denied that political power is
also occupied with sacred and ecclesiastical matters, for their care, although
not their administration, pertains to it as well. Yet it is related to them
only extrinsically, not intrinsically. It has outward things, not inward
things, as its object, and in this respect political power must not be called
formally and directly ecclesiastical, but only objectively and indirectly.

[Marginalia: The effects.]

(6) With respect to the effects. Just as the magistrate deals with his object

[1847 vol. III printed p. 237 | IA leaf 271 | PDF p. 271]

in a political manner and has a political end, he also has corresponding
effects: namely, political effects, civil laws, civil punishments and fines,
and through these means civil peace and tranquility. But because ecclesiastical
power is occupied with a spiritual object in a spiritual manner and toward a
spiritual end, it also has spiritual effects, such as the determination of
controversies of faith, the establishment of canons concerning good order, the
administration of the keys by which sinners are bound and loosed, and other
things of that kind.

[Marginalia: With respect to the manner.]

XVI. (7) With respect to the manner. Political power is worldly and natural,
reaching only the outward person. Ecclesiastical power, however, is spiritual,
concerning the inward person and the conscience. It is regulated not by
natural reason, political laws, and human statutes by which commonwealths are
governed, but solely by the Word of God and the canon of Scripture dictated by
the Holy Spirit. It is exercised not in a political and worldly manner but in
a spiritual and mystical manner through the keys of the kingdom of heaven, the
sword of the Spirit, and other weapons of that kind—not carnal weapons, but
weapons mighty through God for demolishing strongholds and taking every
thought captive to the obedience of Christ (2 Cor. 10:4). It administers
sacraments which are seals not of some worldly and civil privilege, but of
mystical communion with Christ and his spiritual benefits. Nor does it look to
any other end than the edification of Christ’s body, the establishment of
Christ’s kingdom, and the salvation of sinners.

XVII. Therefore, since this ecclesiastical power is spiritual and differs from
political power in so many ways, it follows that it is not formally and
specifically civil and secular but specifically distinct from civil power.
Consequently, it does not reside with the magistrate as the first and proper
receptive subject from whom it is derived to pastors, as the pseudo-politicians
and Libertines, enemies of ecclesiastical power, contend.

XVIII. The falsehood of this invention is clear. (1) On this account the keys
of the kingdom of heaven would have been given to the magistrate as such, which
is nowhere recorded. They would have been given to every magistrate, which
cannot be said of pagans who are not members of the church. They would have
been given to the magistrate alone, because whatever includes a thing as such
includes it in itself; therefore they would not have been given to apostles
and evangelists, pastors and governors of the church, whether ordinary or
extraordinary, because they are not magistrates. (2) Thus the apostles and
those who followed them could have had no right to exercise this ecclesiastical
power which Christ would have given to magistrates as such. (3) Thus the church
would be confused with the commonwealth, because the magistrate as such
presides over the commonwealth and has the commonwealth as his correlate. (4)
Thus the church would neither be the church nor possess any ecclesiastical
power absolutely necessary for the edification of Christ’s kingdom and the
salvation of human beings except in dependence upon the magistrate and when the
magistrate is within the church. But the church does not cease to be the church
or to possess that ecclesiastical power even when the magistrate is outside
the church. Indeed, with respect to its spiritual state it sometimes fares
better then, as in the time of the apostles and the apostolic men who followed
them. (5) It would follow that there is some sovereign power within the church
and that someone on earth besides Christ can be called master, contrary to
Matthew 23:8. (6) Thus ministers would no longer be called ministers of
Christ in the church and the work of Christ in the distinctive sense, as the
apostle wishes (1 Cor. 4:1; 2 Cor. 5:19–20; Gal. 1:10), but ministers of human
beings or deputies and vicars of the magistrate, just like any other envoys and
prefects, because the magistrate would employ their labor in ecclesiastical
affairs by the same right and manner as he employs the others’ in political
affairs. (7) It would follow that the magistrate himself could personally
exercise all ecclesiastical power if it were not expedient to use the labor of
others, for what someone executes through another as minister or delegate he
can execute himself. Thus he will constitute the council and synod, feed the
people with the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 238 | IA leaf 272 | PDF p. 272]

Word and sacraments, excommunicate, and use the keys of the kingdom of heaven,
which everyone sees to be utterly absurd. (8) Because the church is the kingdom
of heaven and is not of this world, magistrates, or the lords of this world,
therefore have no intrinsic, direct, and proper power over it. For there is no
proportion between the object, which is purely sacred and spiritual, and the
power, which is in itself secular and human.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XIX. Ecclesiastical power cannot prejudice political power, as though an
empire within an empire were being erected, because they are powers differing
in their whole genus, as has already been said. Paternal and marital power no
more prejudices political power, because they belong to a different order. I
grant that the ecclesiastical and hierarchical power of the papacy, which
erects dominion and tyranny over consciences and withdraws sacred ministers
from the jurisdiction of the civil magistrate, can rightly be called an empire
within an empire. But the legitimate and ministerial power which we maintain
has a different character and is utterly foreign to that power.

XX. The magistrate’s power concerning sacred things ought not to abolish the
power belonging to governors of the church. Although both powers are occupied
with the same object materially, they do not have the same object formally.
The magistrate’s power is external, while the pastors’ is internal. The former
is coercive and bodily, being armed with the right and power of the sword. The
latter is spiritual, restraining and convincing with spiritual arms—that is,
the Word of God and censures applied to the conscience. The former includes
dominion; the latter only ministry. The former concerns the church and sacred
things in relation to outward things; the latter is within the church itself
and concerns inward things. The former is called ecclesiastical only
objectively and improperly; the latter formally and properly.

XXI. Although the apostles did not speak as masters and lords or arrogate
Christ’s authority to themselves, it does not follow that they had no authority
at all or gave no commands to the faithful. The contrary is clear from Titus
2:15; 1 Thessalonians 4:11; Philemon 8; Acts 15:28; Colossians 4:10; and 1
Timothy 4:11; 5:7; and 6:17. Hence obedience to them is commanded (Heb. 13:17;
2 Cor. 2:9). Nor is this authority peculiar to the apostles, but although in
an unequal degree, it is common to all pastors, to whom these passages also
extend (Titus 2:15; Heb. 13:17; 2 Tim. 2:2).

XXII. Our doctrine has nothing in common with papal tyranny, because just as
it gives pastors the power to command, so it grants the faithful the ability
to test all things and retain what is good (1 Thess. 5:21). It wishes an appeal
to be made to the judgment of the faithful themselves (1 Cor. 10:15; 1 John
4:1) and the blind obedience of the papists to be rejected.

XXIII. A thing’s use must not be abolished because of its abuse. Nor, because
papal tyranny must be avoided, should we therefore pass into the other extreme
of Anabaptist confusion and anarchy. Rather, the middle course of legitimate
and ministerial ecclesiastical power must be held between these two extremes.

XXIV. It is one thing to speak of intrinsic and proper power, which is purely
spiritual and formally ecclesiastical and ministerial. It is another to speak
of secular power, which is formally political, supreme, and ruling. This power
is extrinsic, not intrinsic, to the church as such and can therefore be called
ecclesiastical only objectively and by external designation; but it is
intrinsic to the commonwealth and the supreme powers established in it. The
former power, which is spiritual and ministerial, belongs only to the church as
such; the latter only to rulers and magistrates.

XXV. Political power is architectonic: all other powers are subordinate to and
depend upon it, including ecclesiastical and spiritual power. But subordination
is twofold. One is essential, or insofar as a power is spiritual; this we deny
in sacred matters and concede in civil matters. The other is accidental, or
insofar as a power is

[1847 vol. III printed p. 239 | IA leaf 273 | PDF p. 273]

bodily and secular or joined to a secular thing or action; this we acknowledge
in sacred matters.
