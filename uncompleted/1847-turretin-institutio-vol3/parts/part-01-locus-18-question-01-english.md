EIGHTEENTH LOCUS

ON THE CHURCH

QUESTION ONE

On the Necessity of Treating the Church, and Whether Knowledge of the Church
Ought to Precede Knowledge of Doctrine

[Marginalia: The necessity of treating the church is proved.]

I. Although a treatment of all the Articles of Faith is most useful and of the
greatest importance for salvation, scarcely any other discussion, amid the
controversies between us and our adversaries, seems more momentous and
necessary in this most wretched age—so exceedingly prolific in disputes—than a
discussion of the church. Under divine auspices, we now undertake to carry it
out.

II. The controversies about the church were not among the primary ones that
occasioned our separation from the Roman church. They were only among the
secondary controversies that arose from others, insofar as the papists,
distrusting the strength of their cause in other heads of doctrine, retreated
to the authority of the church so that they might hide more safely beneath its
shield. Nevertheless, with the passage of time they became principal
controversies, in which our adversaries seem to place the prow and stern of
their cause. Hence arises the many-sided necessity for this inquiry, whether
it is considered absolutely and in itself or in relation to our adversaries.

III. First, the church is the chief work of the Holy Trinity, the object of
Christ’s advent and mediation, and the subject to which his benefits are
applied. Indeed, he came into the world and discharged the office of Mediator
for no other reason than to acquire the church for himself and, having
acquired it, to call it into the communion of grace and glory. Therefore, once
the offices and benefits of Christ have been explained, order requires a
discussion of the church, for which alone they are intended and to which they
come to be applied.

Second, since there is no salvation outside the church any more than there was
outside the ark, and since no one has God as Father in heaven whose mother is
not the church on earth, nothing should be closer to our heart than that we
know that mother in whose bosom God has willed us to be reared, at whose
breasts we must be nourished, and under whose care we must be governed until
we mature and reach the goal of faith. We must also know which assembly is
that true church to which, by God’s command, we are bound to join ourselves so
that we may attain salvation (Acts 2:47). Third, this doctrine is placed in the
creed among the principal articles of faith, which we are bound to know and
believe.

IV. Fourth, everyone claims for himself with great zeal, though not with equal
truth, the holy name of the church that we confess in the creed. In former
times the Jews boasted that they were the true church of God and children of
Abraham, yet nothing could have been further from them than imitating his
faith. For this reason John calls them a brood of vipers, and Christ calls them
children of the devil. Heretics likewise claimed the name of the church for
themselves. Hence Tertullian, book 4, *Against Marcion*: “Wasps make
honeycombs, and Marcionites make churches.”

[1847 vol. III printed p. 4 | IA leaf 38 | PDF p. 38]

Cyprian, *On the Simplicity of Prelates*, says, “The devil devised a new fraud,
so that under the very title of the Christian name he might deceive the
unwary.” And Leo, letter 83 to the Palestinians: “They arm themselves with the
name of the church and fight against the church.” So it is with the papists
today. Although nothing could be further from them than being the true church
of Christ, they nevertheless boast of the name of the church alone and are not
ashamed to display the insignia of the very church they attack. By hiding in
this way beneath the imposing title of the antiquity and infallibility of the
catholic church, they think they can, as if with a single blow, repel and
dissolve the charge brought against them concerning the various and most
grievous corruptions they have introduced into heavenly doctrine.

V. Nothing can be called more unjust than this manner of proceeding, because
the very thing in question is imposed upon us as a principle of faith that
must be believed. For when the question concerns whether the Roman church is
the true church of Christ, the head and mistress of all the others, they think
they can settle the matter with one word by presenting as an undisputed
principle the very thing that is most disputed. And lest they be charged with
error, they shamelessly cry out with those scribes in Jeremiah 8:8 that the
church is infallible, that it is found among them, and that they alone are
wise. Thus, in defining the church—the source from which they derive their
positions—with the mad deceit of the false apostles, “measuring themselves by
themselves and comparing themselves with themselves, they do not understand”
(2 Corinthians 10:12). Then, as if the matter were settled, they condemn as
schismatics and heretics all those who withdraw from obedience to the church
that they dress up with deceptive artifice.

It is not without reason that those who at times wish to seem more liberal in
other matters are obstinate on this point. “Believe whatever you like,” they
say, “provided that you subject yourself to the church and surrender your
faith as a captive.” For these shrewd men know that they will immediately
recover whatever they had previously seemed to concede, if only they can once
subject people’s very minds to the decree and dominion of their church.

VI. These devices of our adversaries impose upon us the necessity of this
inquiry, so that we may distinguish the church’s genuine face from masks and
not allow ourselves to be deceived by those imposing and splendid names,
which are nevertheless devoid of the reality of the thing. They habitually
repeat them with an incessant cry and enormous clamor so that they alone may be
regarded as the heirs and all others driven from that possession. In the same
way the Jews, advertising themselves as the people of God and repeatedly
crying, “The temple of the Lord,” persecuted God’s pious servants the prophets,
and in their fury even cast out the Lord of the vineyard himself after
cruelly killing him.

[Marginalia: Whether knowledge of the church ought to precede knowledge of
faith.]

VII. Although knowledge of the church is especially necessary for us, we must
not therefore suppose that it ought to precede the examination and knowledge
of doctrine itself, as though faith or doctrine ought to be known from the
church rather than the church from doctrine and faith. Cardinal du Perron
asserts this far too confidently in his *Reply to the King of England*, book
1, chapters 4 and 5, when he contends that faith is known from the church
rather than the church from faith, and that we can be secure about the faith
provided that we are in the church.

The author of the *Book of Prejudices* intends nothing else. In his preface he
labors mightily to prove that, in order to discern which sect among Christians
is the true one to which we ought to join ourselves, we must follow the way of
the church’s authority and not the way of discussing and examining doctrine.
The latter way, he says, is long, uncertain, and dangerous, whereas the former
is short and certain. From this prejudice he concludes that we who refuse to
follow the first way by submitting ourselves in blind obedience to the Roman
church can justly be condemned without even being heard—as if what is not
known could be condemned, or what is not examined could be known.

[Marginalia: The negative is proved.]

[Marginalia: 1. From Scripture.]

VIII. But the falsity of this fiction is easily uncovered from various
sources. 1. From Scripture itself, which ordinarily places the examination of
faith and doctrine before the knowledge and communion of the church. When
Christ sends the apostles to gather the church, he presupposes the necessity
of prior

[1847 vol. III printed p. 5 | IA leaf 39 | PDF p. 39]

instruction in and knowledge of doctrine: “Go, teach all nations, baptizing
them” (Matthew 28). Those who had first been taught by the apostles were added
to the church (Acts 2:41), and those in Samaria who believed were baptized
(Acts 8:12). Therefore, just as faith in adults ought to precede baptism, which
is entrance into the church, so the examination and knowledge of faith ought
to precede knowledge of the church.

[Marginalia: 2. From the nature of the thing itself.]

IX. 2. From the nature of the thing itself. Since the unity of a society
necessarily presupposes some principle in which its members are united, the
unity of the church presupposes a prior unity of faith in which the faithful
agree. In a state, which is an aggregation of citizens, one must possess the
right of citizenship before sharing in the community of citizens in the
commonwealth. In a family, which is a society of siblings born from the same
father, birth from that father must be presupposed before the fellowship of
the siblings. In the same way, since the church is the city and family of God,
into which no one is admitted unless faithful, faith must precede that
communion, since the communion is constituted through faith. Otherwise, to
wish to enter the church before we have faith and knowledge of the truth is to
wish to be in God’s family before we are born from him, to be admitted into
the commonwealth before we possess the right of citizenship, and to wish to
know a thing while remaining ignorant of its form, which is what doctrine is
in relation to the church (Ephesians 2:20).

[Marginalia: 3. From the Fathers.]

X. 3. From the statements of the Fathers. Jerome, on Psalm 138: “The church
does not consist in walls, but in the truth of its doctrines. The church is
where the true faith is.” Ambrose, *On Luke*, book 6, chapter 9: “We are
therefore commanded to seek the faith of the church first. If Christ dwells in
it, it should undoubtedly be chosen; but if a faithless people or a heretical
teacher disfigures the dwelling, communion with the heretics is to be avoided
and the synagogue is judged one from which we must flee.” Augustine, *On the
Unity of the Church*, book 10, chapter 3: “Let us not hear, ‘I say this; you say
that,’ but, ‘The Lord says this.’ There certainly are books of the Lord whose
authority we both accept, both believe, and both serve. Let us seek the church
there; let us discuss our cause there.” And in chapter 2: “The question between
us and the Donatists is where the church is. What, then, shall we do? Shall we
seek it in our own words, or in the words of its Head, our Lord Jesus Christ?
I think we ought to seek it in the words of him who is the truth and knows his
own body best.” He pursues the same point elsewhere in chapter 16.

Nor can it be said with du Perron that Augustine is not speaking about the
examination of doctrine, but about an inquiry into the external marks by which
the church is recognized. The contrary is evident to anyone reading the words
themselves. Furthermore, if the church’s marks must be sought in Scripture,
how much more must doctrine itself be sought there, since it is far more
necessary?

[Marginalia: 4. From reason.]

XI. 4. From reason. The authority of the church cannot be admitted unless a
twofold examination has first been made. 1. Has God willed that some such
infallible authority be given to the church, to which the faithful must submit
absolutely and without examination? For how could we be moved by such an
authority unless we were persuaded that it had been given? 2. If such
authority has been given, does it belong to the Roman church? It would be
useless for me to be certain in the thesis about the church’s infallible
authority unless it were established for me in the hypothesis that this
church is the Roman church. Neither point can be known unless knowledge of the
word of God is presupposed, since it is from the word that knowledge of each
point must be sought.

XII. 5. It is gratuitously assumed that the way of authority is far shorter
and easier than the way of examination, although it is evident, on the
contrary, that it is much longer, more intricate, and more uncertain. The way
of examination, which is founded upon the authority of God’s word, rests upon
this twofold hypothesis: (1) whatever God teaches us is true; and (2) whatever
Scripture reveals to us is the word of God. Christians agree upon these two
hypotheses. But the way of authority, which advances the church’s authority
and infallibility as the rule of faith, likewise

[1847 vol. III printed p. 6 | IA leaf 40 | PDF p. 40]

rests upon two hypotheses, but ones far more obscure and uncertain—indeed,
utterly false: namely, that there is in the world an infallible visible church
which teaches nothing but the truth, and that this church is none other than
the Roman church. Examining both points requires a long series of arguments
and a careful investigation of histories, since this position presupposes the
truth of an uninterrupted antiquity and succession. That cannot be discovered
without reading the whole of church history, the councils, and the Fathers.
This is a task of immense labor that very few of the learned can scarcely
accomplish even with great study, much less can it be understood by the
uneducated and common people.

[Marginalia: Sources for the solution.]

XIII. Although the examination of faith and doctrine cannot take place
without study and labor, it does not follow that it is impossible or dangerous
for the unlearned and inexperienced. The issue is not a distinct examination
and knowledge of all the doctrines that can enter into controversy and are
ordinarily debated in the schools. It concerns only the doctrines necessary
for salvation, which are contained clearly enough in Scripture to be
understood by any of the faithful who are endowed with a spirit of
discernment. Hence Paul appeals to the judgment of the faithful and commands
them to test everything and retain what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21), and
John wishes the faithful to test the spirits to see whether they are from God
(1 John 4:1). This surely could not be said if such an examination were
impossible or dangerous for them.

XIV. Whatever difficulty arises in the way of examining doctrine is no less
present in the way of the church’s authority. There always remains the
necessity of examining both that authority and the church which claims it for
itself, lest we be deceived in the judgment that must be made about each. Since
this cannot be done except through Scripture, it always requires a prior
knowledge of doctrine. No one of sound mind will ever submit himself to the
authority of any church unless he is persuaded that it is legitimate and
instituted by God.

Add that if it is difficult to grasp the meaning of Scripture, it is far more
difficult to grasp the meaning of the church. First, one must inquire which
church it is: the ancient or the present church? If it is the present church,
where is it found among the various sects of Christians: among the Protestants
or the papists? If among the papists, where does its authority reside: in the
pope separately, in a council separately, or in the two conjointly? Second,
the decisions and sanctions of councils and pontiffs which are put forward as
the church’s faith can be interpreted in very different ways. They are not
seldom entangled in innumerable obscurities and contradictions that even the
most keen-sighted among them cannot unravel and reconcile.

XV. It is one thing to know the church with a general knowledge as an assembly
of people professing the same doctrine and using the same sacred rites. It is
another to know it with a distinct knowledge as an assembly of the faithful
who retain the true faith and doctrine of Christ. In the former sense, we
acknowledge that knowledge of the church precedes knowledge of faith; but in
the latter sense, we deny it, because the truth of the church rests upon the
truth of the faith.

XVI. No one can be in the true church as its true member if he does not hold
its faith, at least as regards the things necessary for salvation, since it is
through faith that he is received into it and becomes its member. In the same
way, no one can have true faith without by that very fact being in the church,
outside which there is no salvation. Du Perron therefore reasons wrongly when,
in order to teach that knowledge of the church precedes knowledge of doctrine,
he says that someone who is in the church is certain about the faith even if
he does not hold its articles distinctly, while someone who has faith but is
not in the church cannot hope for salvation. He also gratuitously assumes that
someone can have faith and not be in the church, although faith inserts us
into Christ and his mystical body. It is indeed true that someone who is
certain he is in the true church is certain he has true faith; but

[1847 vol. III printed p. 7 | IA leaf 41 | PDF p. 41]

it is false that he knows true doctrine because he knows he is in the true
church. On the contrary, he knows that he is in the true church because he
knows that the church in which he is has true doctrine.

XVII. It is one thing not to acknowledge the church and its legitimate
authority; it is another to deny that knowledge of the church precedes
knowledge of faith. We do not assert the former, but the latter, because there
can be no knowledge of the church unless the doctrine and faith which make the
church a true church are first known. Otherwise, how could someone join
himself to the assembly of the faithful if he does not know what it is to be
faithful? And how could he know what it is to be faithful unless he first
knows the doctrine of faith? How will it be discerned which of several
churches contending with one another is the true one unless the rule of truth
and purity is first known?

But the papists, reversing the order, wish the church to be believed before
they teach what the church ought to believe, and the people to follow their
leaders before knowing whether they teach the true faith and the way of
salvation. Their purpose is not obscure: namely, to subject the people in
blind obedience to their own authority and to call them away from the study
and reading of Scripture, which alone can make them wise for salvation, so
that they may reign in darkness more easily and their errors may not be
uncovered by the light of truth.
