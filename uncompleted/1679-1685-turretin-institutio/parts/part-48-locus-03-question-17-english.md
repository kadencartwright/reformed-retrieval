QUESTION XVII

Can any cause of God’s will be given? Negative.

I. This question depends upon the preceding matters, and it is necessary
because of the Pelagians, who in the matter of predestination seek causes of
the divine will outside God in the foresight of faith or of the good use of
free choice. We, however, judge with the orthodox that no properly so-called
cause of God’s will should be sought outside God.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. For the state of the question, note that it is one thing to inquire into
the reason for God’s will and another to inquire into its cause. It is not
asked here whether any reason for God’s will can be given, for God’s will
cannot be irrational (ἄλογος), since he made all things most wisely, even
though that reason is very often hidden from us. Augustine says in Letter 59
to Paulinus, “The cause of God’s will can be hidden; it cannot be unjust,”
where he takes “cause” for “reason,” because elsewhere, in book 1, chapter 2
of *On Genesis Against the Manichaeans*, he holds it absurd to seek a cause of
God’s will: “They seek the causes of God’s will without end, although God’s
will is itself the cause of all things that exist.” Rather, the question
concerns a properly so-called cause that would move God’s will to will this or
that. The adversaries hold that such a cause can be given; we deny it.

III. Just as God’s will is the cause of all things, so it itself can have no
cause of its own. It is as certain that no cause of God’s will outside God can
be given as it is certain that nothing is prior to God. For if his will has a
cause, there is something that precedes it, and so it would be a second cause,
not the first; something regulated, not the rule. Hence Lombard, book 1,
distinction 45: “One who seeks a cause of the divine will seeks something
greater than it, although nothing is greater.” Add that since God’s will is
eternal, it can have no non-eternal cause outside itself.

IV. It is one thing for a cause to be given on the side of the act of willing;
it is another for one to be given on the side of the thing willed. In the
former sense no cause of God’s will can be given, but in the latter it can,
because among the things willed by God some can be causes of others. Thus
Christ can be called the cause of decreed salvation, because salvation is
given to us on account of Christ, but not the cause of the decree of
salvation, because that decree flowed from God’s good pleasure alone. This is
what the scholastics mean when they say that God wills this on account of
this, but does not on account of this will this—for example, that he wills
salvation on account of Christ, but does not on account of Christ will
salvation. Thus Christ, who is the meritorious cause of salvation, stands on
the side of the salvation he acquired for us, but not on the side of the
decree itself by which it is appointed for us. Accordingly, there is order
and causality among the things willed by God, so that one is for the sake of
another, but not in the divine volition, which acknowledges no cause outside
itself.

V. Just as some of God’s properties are absolute, resting upon God’s absolute
right and his supreme dominion—such as his might and autocratic power
(αὐτοκρατορικὴ)—while others are relative, resting upon right tempered by the
virtues and requiring for their exercise some condition or quality in the
object with respect to which they must be exercised—such as justice, which
presupposes sin, and mercy, which presupposes misery—so we must reason about
the acts of the will that operates according to those properties. For when it
operates from the absolute properties, as the supreme Lord and arbiter of all
things—for example, when he wills to create or to choose one rather than
another—not only is there no cause of his will apart from his good pleasure,
but neither is there any condition or quality in the object by which he is
moved. For power does not presuppose its object but constitutes it, and since
election is among equals, it sees nothing in one rather than another by which
among

them they may be distinguished; hence it acts from its mere good pleasure. But
when it acts from the relative properties, as from justice to punish or from
mercy to save, although some quality is always presupposed in the object,
namely, either sin or misery, this cannot take the place of a properly
so-called cause. For then either all would be condemned because they are
sinners, or all would be saved because they are miserable. It is only a
quality in the object requisite for the exercise of those properties. And if
some cause or motive is to be sought, it must not be sought outside God but
only in his justice and mercy, by which his will is incited to act.

VI. Although among the effects of the divine will some have the character of
causes with respect to others, since God willed there to be a certain order
both in nature and in grace by which they would be connected with one another,
they nevertheless do not have the power to move the divine will to elicit its
act, since they are effects of God as the first cause. No other cause can be
given for why the Lord made this or that in this way than that he willed it.
But if you go farther and ask why he willed it in this way, you seek something
greater and higher than God’s will, which cannot be found. Human rashness must
therefore restrain itself and not seek what does not exist, lest perhaps it
fail to find what does exist.
