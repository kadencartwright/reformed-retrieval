# Questions on Marriage

*A fresh translation of the complete Latin consilium in CO X/1 231–233.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 231]

The degrees of blood relationship within which God does not permit marriage
to be contracted under his law are sufficiently well known. A brother may not
marry his sister, nor a paternal or maternal uncle his niece or even his
great-niece. Likewise, a paternal or maternal aunt cannot marry her nephew or
great-nephew. A male cousin on either the father's or mother's side, however,
is not forbidden to marry a woman in the corresponding cousin-degree.

As far as prohibitions are concerned, it is not lawful to call into question
what has been commanded by God's law: the marriages condemned there must be
accounted incestuous. But because a long-established usage bars a cousin on
either the mother's or father's side from marrying his corresponding female
cousin, we have taken account of offense and have not granted liberty for
such marriages. Therefore, if someone asks about strict right, we will not
answer simply that a man who takes his cousin as wife sins. But because this
liberty would expose Christ's gospel to many reproaches, Paul's admonition
must be observed: “Let not our liberty become a stumbling block to anyone”
(1 Cor. 10:23). We must also abstain even from lawful things unless they are
expedient. Nor should the servitude by which believers are mutually bound to
one another in the duty of love be feared, because their consciences remain
free before God.

The principle is similar with respect to degrees of relationship by marriage.
Some debate—or rather litigate—whether a widower may marry his deceased
wife's sister. They seize a pretext from Moses' words: “You shall not take a
wife together with her sister, to cause grief or distress, so as to uncover
her nakedness while she is alive” (Lev. 18:18). But their error is refuted by
these same words. Moses there condemns not so much incest as the cruelty of a
husband who would wish to bring his wife's female relative in as an
additional wife. This is therefore an exception within polygamy. Since the
Jews had been permitted, because of the hardness of their hearts, to have
several

### [CO X/1, p. 232]

wives at the same time, God wished to add the exception that two sisters—or
female relatives, which amounts to the same thing—should not be set against
one another in rivalry or jealousy.

**II.** Because adulteries have not until now been punished as severely as
they ought, so that the lives of those who violate marital fidelity are
spared, it would be harsh to bar from marriage for their whole lives either a
man whose wife divorced him on grounds of adultery or a woman repudiated by
her husband, if they struggle with incontinence. Thus one indulgence must
necessarily draw another after it. Yet it does not seem prudent to put it in
the discretion of the party who was condemned to fly immediately to another
marriage. Liberty should therefore be delayed for a time, whether one chooses
to prescribe a fixed period or to wait until the innocent party enters
another marriage.

**III.** Christ expressly excludes from marriage impotent men and eunuchs to
whom virility has been denied. And certainly, since nothing is more contrary
to consent than mistake, the pledge of a woman who thought that she was
marrying a man cannot be binding. Add that this frustration entirely
overturns the nature and end of marriage. For what is marriage other than the
union of male and female? And for what was it instituted, except for two
ends—either for begetting offspring or as a remedy for incontinence?

There is therefore no doubt that a woman who was deceived ought to obtain a
divorce once the case has been heard and properly proved. Nor is there any
need to dissolve a marriage which was null from the beginning. It will be
enough to declare that the man who was not fit for marriage wrongfully and
fraudulently deceived a woman whose husband he could not be, and thus that an
undertaking in which there was nothing mutual was invalid and entirely null.

**IV.** It has always rightly been judged that marriage is lawful only between
those who have reached puberty. Therefore,

### [CO X/1, p. 233]

it is a childish game, yet at the same time a frivolity worthy of correction,
if a boy betroths a wife to himself. First, it must be established that all
who are under the authority of parents or guardians are not free or legally
independent, especially in this matter. But although the consent of parents
or guardians is added, or even though they are the principal instigators,
undertakings made before the proper age do not bind minors unless, after they
have grown up, they remain of the same mind and freely testify that they
ratify what was previously done at an improper time.

If some parents betroth their children before the proper time so as to bind
themselves and their property, they nevertheless cannot cast a snare upon
children not yet mature enough for marriage. And although a contract of this
kind is a certain profanation of marriage, if anyone has rashly imposed a
penalty upon himself, let him bear the punishment he has deserved. There is,
however, no action to compel the marriage, because children, when they reach
puberty, are free to retract whatever their parents transacted badly
concerning them.
