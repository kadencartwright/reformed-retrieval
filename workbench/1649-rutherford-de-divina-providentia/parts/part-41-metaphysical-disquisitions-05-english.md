QUESTION V. On the hypothesis that the first cause did not exist, would the
possible or the impossible therefore exist in the nature of things?

I would not dare raise this question—resting as it does upon so execrable and
monstrous a hypothesis of the atheists—had I not seen it raised by the
Scholastics. I do so to expose the Jesuits’ impiety more easily and to assert
that, through his own free good pleasure, God is the parent and author of all
created moral rectitude. Those who posit the possible and the impossible in
themselves and prior to every power—indeed, who remove the first cause and yet
maintain such propositions as true (*the possible is possible*; *the impossible
is impossible*)—seem to me to favor the patrons of middle knowledge far too
much. For just as the latter maintain that conditional futures are determinately
true or false prior to GOD’s decree—that is, they posit futures that are not
future, and imagine merely possible things, which are indifferent to being
future or not future, nevertheless to be determinately future or not future—so
the former dream of possibles and impossibles prior to omnipotence and the first
cause.

Many approve the proposition, *If God did not exist, the possible would still
be possible intrinsically*, and much more this one: *If God did not exist, all
things would be intrinsically and simply impossible.* Yet that first
proposition does not please me. Ripalda says: *Although created things truly
have existence from another, they do not have their intrinsic non-repugnance
to existing from another; rather, they are non-repugnant to existing from
themselves.*

[Marginalia: Nyssenus, book *On Fate*, chapter 4. Those who posit the possible and impossible prior
to omnipotence err just as much as the patrons of middle knowledge who maintain
that things are future or not future prior to GOD’s decree. Martinez de
Ripalda, *On Supernatural Being*, book 2, question 7, number 35.]

Nor does this statement of Francisco de Oviedo please me: *The causality we
signify when we say, “Because a creature is possible, GOD can produce it,” is
quasi-formal.* On the contrary, omnipotence in first act precedes this formal
cause of the possible, because the formality of a quasi-possible being is
wholly in the power of the first cause and his omnipotence. It implies a
contradiction for this thing to be possible and yet for the omnipotent one, by
reason of whom it is formally possible, not to be presupposed. Nor, if the
first cause were set aside—if that spotless glory of beings, namely God, did
not exist—would an external thing remain possible, or a thing remain formally
non-repugnant to being. This is proved because if what does not conflict with
being were actual, no contradiction or impossibility would follow; but if the
possible were actual in that case, something impossible and repugnant would
follow, while nevertheless no omnipotence would exist. Just as a contradiction
would follow if something were rational but not human, so in this case a being
would exist without the first being, which involves a contradiction.

2. If there were no first power, a possible would be the same as a chimera or
stag-goat, for it would be a purely negative being remaining in the state of
non-beings. But if there were no first cause, a non-being that remained a
non-being could become a being. This is proved as follows: a possible is not a
being in act; yet if the possible were possible when the first cause had been
removed, nothing absurd would follow if that possible became a being in act
with the first cause removed. It would thus be a being in act when the first
being had been removed, which is monstrous.

3. It cannot be said that GOD is neither the impossible nor possible matter
and form: for GOD is not the internal cause of a thing; therefore a thing can
be materially possible when the first efficient cause is removed. I answer as
follows. On that account the internal causes of heaven, the sun, humanity, and
all creatures could actually exist if that most beautiful first beauty, GOD,
were absent. For he is no more the internal cause of existing creatures than
of possible ones. But this must not be said, because no internal causes can
exist without the first and infinite external cause. Second, God as efficient
cause creates the whole being and everything belonging to being. Therefore
internal causes are produced by God; therefore beings are not even possible
with respect to their internal causes when the first cause is removed.

It seems to me, moreover, that no impossible can be posited or exist if there
is no first cause. First, because nothing is possible of itself if there were
no first power and omnipotence. It involves a contradiction for a possible to
exist without there being some actively-*

[Marginalia: Oviedo, *Metaphysics*, controversy 10, point 5, section 2, number
10. Execrable
supposition: that the first cause does not exist; then nothing would be

*—powerful, or omnipotent, cause in whose power that possible effect is
contained; for it would be possible and not possible. Therefore, if nothing is
possible, neither will anything be impossible, because nothing possible will
exist to which that impossible could be opposed.

2. Remove the first being and you simultaneously remove every truth, because
you remove the first truth. Consequently, even this will not be true: *It is
impossible for the same thing not to be the same thing*, because you remove the
first identity. Remove every truth, and you will remove every falsehood. For if
there were no first identity and first being, not even this would obtain its
truth in the first being: *It is impossible for the same uncreated being to be
simultaneously a being and a non-being*, because you suppose that there is no
first entity. Therefore truth would not exist in any proposition, not even
fundamentally and objectively, because there would be no intellect and no
uncreated being, as is supposed. Therefore there would be no essence, whether
uncreated, derived from it, or an essence by participation.

You will say: either this would be true in itself—*Nothing exists*—because this
is supposed true, *GOD does not exist*; or this would be false—*Nothing does not
exist*—because one part of a contradiction is necessarily true and the other
false. Therefore, if the first cause did not exist, this would be necessarily
true in itself and intrinsically: *Nothing exists; all things are negatively
impossible*, because on the hypothesis no first being is given.

I answer: there are two kinds of non-being and non-future. One is purely
negative, a non-being and simply impossible, which is negatively non-future.
The other is a non-being of such a kind—a privative non-being denying either a
created or an uncreated being. These two non-beings are distinguished in that
the former is purely negative, the latter privative. Second, the former
non-being would obtain even if God did not exist, but *obtain* is used
improperly here for pure non-being. This non-being would be neither prior nor
posterior to GOD, because formally it is purely non-being and simply nothing.
Third, the former non-being is capable of futurition and non-futurition, and is
indifferent to becoming future or non-future through GOD’s decree; it is also
capable of positive or privative entity. The former non-being is most
improperly said to exist or to be prior to GOD. But the latter non-being
originates from God, as was said above.

With Alvarez and the Dominicans, we rightly maintain that before God’s free
decree nothing is determinately future and nothing determinately non-future.
And we maintain that the following is false at every rational stage prior to
GOD’s decree: *From eternity Peter either was going to dispute, or Peter was
not going to dispute.* For just as every determinate entity, and by the same
reason every determinate non-entity, so also-*

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Question V; printed page 548; IA PDF page 605; BSB

*—every determinate truth and falsity, is as the thing itself is. Aristotle
says most rightly, ὁμοίως οἱ λόγοι ἀληθεῖς, ὥσπερ καὶ τὰ πράγματα: *As the
things are or are not, so the statement is true or false.* But before GOD’s
decree there is no determinate entity, no futurition, and no non-futurition.
For otherwise so many determinate entities would be future, and so many
determinate non-entities future, apart from and independently of the will of
the first efficient cause and first end—than which nothing is more absurd.

And if God has counted all future raindrops and all determinately future
things, he has counted all possible dew, hail, and snow that will never be
determinate futures. He both contains them in number and in a numbering decree,
and knows them exactly. For since the number of possible things that will be
determinate futures and those that determinately will never be futures is not
even but odd, God—who knows whether the stars are even or odd in number—knows
exactly, decreeing by his own decision that only one world will be future,
while three, four, or a thousand possible worlds will never be future. Thus God
knows, by comprehending omnipotence as regulated by a free decree, so to speak,
in acting and not acting, two possible worlds that will never be future, and
also three, and also four. In this way he knows a number increasing to infinity
beyond the single world: because he positively decreed to create one, he
therefore positively decreed not to create two others, nor three; and he has
counted how many beings are merely possible but never future.

For since possibles as possibles are non-future, they do not leap by themselves
and intrinsically out of the state and class of possibles and non-futures on
the ground that this has been true from eternity—*Every future from eternity is
future*—or its contradictory—*Every future from eternity is not future*. We
answer that, at the rational stage prior to the decree, through which alone and
through no other imaginable cause all things are future or non-future, it is
just as impossible for the future to be future or non-future of itself and
intrinsically as for a being by participation to exist without the first being
and being through itself; or for a being to be present or past and yet not to
have been future; or for something hot to exist without the first hot thing,
or something luminous without the first luminous thing. In the same way every
possible being and every non-possible or impossible being would neither be in
itself nor not be in itself; and neither part of a contradiction would be true
or false, if you suppose that most beautiful and delightful being not to exist,
namely, the first being of beings, dwelling in-*

[Marginalia: Aristotle, *On Interpretation*, chapter 10. By a positive decree
God knows all things that will not be future.]

*—inaccessible light. If he did not exist—O abominable hypothesis!—the eternal
darkness of contradictions would close in.

But you will say: negatively, all things would be purely non-beings and purely
impossible; for in order to be non-beings they do not need a being, and
therefore they do not need the first being.

I answer: neither would non-beings, if we are arguing about metaphysical
falsehood as we now are, exist or obtain metaphysically fundamental truth apart
from the most beautiful first being, namely, the existing Jehovah. For, third,
if there were no first being, neither would there be a first good. But a first
evil, or the destruction of infinite good, is not possible. I now ask why some
created objective goodness would not exist—for example, *a created good is
good*, or *a created good is not good*—if you suppose that the first good does
not exist, rather than that other affection of being, namely truth. But it must
not be said that created goodness exists or is possible, or that malice exists,
if the first good did not exist. Hence good, true, false, possible, impossible,
or that which is not possible through the first power, would be banished; and
those negative non-beings and negative things that will never be future would
be defections from being objectively considered.

But if you remove the first being from which they would defect—when you suppose
that there is no being at all—then, if there were no first being, what is
negatively a non-being would not negate created being, because nothing of that
kind exists or is possible when you suppose that the first being and the first
power in whom alone all beings are possible do not exist. And that non-being
would by no means negate uncreated being, because you suppose it does not
exist. If you suppose it does not exist, you suppose it never exists and cannot
exist. And if God is a being that is never possible, non-being would not negate
such a being, because if there were no prior affirmation, every subsequent
negation would be impossible; and if you remove affirmation, you also remove
negation. Therefore, if you remove all being, you must remove non-being; but if
you remove the first being, you remove every being. Does not that saying of the
Philosopher obtain here: ἑνὸς ἀτόπου δοθέντος, ἀνάγκη τἆλλα συμβαίνειν—*Once
one absurdity is granted, many must follow?* If the first being were removed,
this too would be overthrown—*It is impossible for the same thing to be and not
to be*—and the chaos of contradictions would return.

4. The adversaries teach that it involves a contradiction for a human being
not to be a rational animal and yet to be a human being. In the same way, for
GOD not to be—for God not to exist—is, by reason of the immutability and
firmness of his being, for God not to be GOD, and is an absolute
contradiction.-*

[Marginalia: If GOD did not exist, there would be nothing true, nothing false,
neither being nor non-being.]

*—For whatever God can be, he actually, essentially, and necessarily is,
because God cannot be anything potentially unless that potential is either in
God or in another being outside GOD. It cannot be in God, because he would then
properly be the cause to himself of actually being what he is potentially, and
thus formally more imperfect than himself; and then he would not be the
absolutely necessary and first being. Nor is that potential in another, because
that other would be prior to God, and thus either a creature or God. But it is
repugnant for a creature to be prior to God, and in that case the creature
would be a cause actualizing GOD. Through a power prior to himself God would be
in potentiality to be made by a creature actually something other than what he
is now potentially. But if that potential were God and an uncreated being, the
hypothesis would now be destroyed, and a God prior to this GOD would exist.

5. Bradwardine argues similarly: *If GOD does not exist, this proposition will
be true: “GOD does not exist.” Therefore some truth exists, and the first truth
does not exist.* This truth, I say, is either created or uncreated truth, or a
creatable and objective truth or non-repugnance. It is not created truth prior
to the first and uncreated truth, which nevertheless is supposed not to exist.
If it is uncreated truth, then God exists. Therefore this will be true—*God
exists*—while this impious hypothesis simultaneously stands—*God does not
exist*—which is contradictory. Creatable truth and non-repugnance are not
possible while this hypothesis stands; indeed they are simply uncreatable,
because the Creator, who is the first cause, is removed. Thus it would be
repugnant for something to be a being, because there is no first being; and it
would not be repugnant, because the adversary says there have been from
eternity, prior to GOD, so many eternal non-repugnances and infinitely many
possibles, without, apart from, and prior to the first infinite being.

And if there are so many non-repugnances and objective beings—neither created
nor uncreated but creatable—then by parity of reasoning all would also be
intrinsically and objectively future or non-future prior to God’s decree, just
as there are so many non-repugnances prior to omnipotence. The reasoning is the
same in both cases. There is no reason why the intrinsically future and
non-future should not be future and non-future in themselves and prior to God’s
will, by reason of the created contingent cause and of matter intrinsically
future apart from God’s will, as the Jesuits imagine—just as infinitely many
non-repugnances and repugnances, possibles, impossibles, truths, and falsehoods
exist prior to and apart from the first being. For this proposition is just as
true—*What will never be future*, for instance a thousand times a thousand
worlds, *will never be future apart from God*—as this one: *Nothing is nothing-*

[Marginalia: Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 24. God is
actually whatever he can be from eternity. If there is no first being, there
would be no non-repugnances apart from God.]

*—apart from and prior to God.* Yet outside this hypothesis and speaking
simply, future and non-future things are causally such by GOD’s free will,
because from eternity all future and non-future things were future and
non-future. For in time these statements are true—*Present things now exist*;
and the breaking of CHRIST’s bones and the raising of children to Abraham from
stones are not now present. As Aristotle rightly says, εἰ δὲ ἀεὶ ἀληθὲς ἦν
εἰπεῖν, ὅτι ἓν τῶν γινομένων ἦν ἢ ἔστιν, οὐχ οἷόν τε τοῦτο μὴ εἶναι, οὐδὲ μὴ
ἔσεσθαι: *If from eternity it was true that something now exists or would be
future, certainly it cannot fail to happen or to be future, and it is
necessarily true in the present.*

But from eternity possible things do not become future from themselves and by
reason of intrinsic matter, because then all possibles would be future, which
is absurd; for we see that some possible things will be future and others will
not. Nor are they future or non-future from GOD’s omnipotence, because from
eternity they are merely possible or non-possible from GOD’s omnipotence alone;
nothing else can be imagined.

2. If things were not future and non-future from eternity by GOD’s free will,
but intrinsically and prior to God’s will, then by his omnipotence God could not
freely have created more worlds and more people than those actually created.
For GOD’s free will cannot make more things future and present if all things
are future intrinsically in themselves and prior to his free will. By the same
reason God could not freely have brought about fewer future things and fewer
people than those which actually exist. Nor could God freely have brought
about the breaking of his Son’s bones, or his deliverance from death by more
than twelve legions of angels, because, intrinsically and prior to GOD’s free
will—as the mad Jesuit philosophers suppose—the non-breaking of his bones and
that non-deliverance would have been future. Indeed, all things would be so
against GOD’s will.

If there is no first being, nothing was future from eternity. Yet it involves a
contradiction that nothing in the present order of things was future from
eternity, because for the past not to be past and the present not to be present
implies a contradiction. And if you now suppose that the first being does not
exist, you suppose the contradiction that no created beings exist; yet it is
impossible for created beings to be uncreated. Therefore, if so impious a
supposition must be imagined, you must suppose that the first being never
existed; and since the necessity of existing belongs to God’s essence, the
supposition involves a contradiction.

3. Present things will exist and will occur-*

*—in time because intrinsically and prior to God’s free will they were future
and going to occur from eternity. It is not easy to determine who sins more
grievously: the abominable Jesuits, or the impure Socinians, whose numerous
troop is now ravaging England. For the Jesuits teach that apart from providence
all contingent futures are determinately future. The Socinians claim that no
future things, especially contingent ones, are known or knowable by God, or
have determinate truth. So Volckelius says in *On True*—rather, false—*Religion*,
chapter 24, columns 200–201, against Isaiah 41:22, 23, 26; 44:7–8; 48:3, 5, 6,

division of the holy land in Genesis 48 and Deuteronomy 33, which afterward
occurred by lots in Joshua 15–16; the destruction of Ahab, the Assyrians, the
Chaldeans, and others; and CHRIST’s death in all the circumstances that came
about through the ministry of free causes. For on that impious view, second
causes produce their effects from absolute dominion if God does not prevent
them, while God’s providence lies dormant.

Furthermore, these objective non-repugnances to being are mere fictions, for
they are negative beings and pure nothings. Insofar as God can make a being
from them and elicit it omnipotently, a human being considered objectively and
potentially therefore necessarily and essentially expresses a relation to the
omnipotence in whose power this potential is contained. Now remove
omnipotence: a thing which is in potentiality to be a human being is no more
potentially human than it is potentially a stag-goat, stone, mountain, or
donkey. Indeed, it is impotence, or is in impossibility and repugnance to
becoming human.

Hence I argue: what is intrinsically and of itself repugnant to becoming human,
prior to omnipotence, does not essentially or formally possess
non-repugnance—whatever essence is understood, it does not matter—to becoming
human. But all beings, by reason of objective potentiality, are repugnant to
becoming creatures prior to and apart from the first omnipotent cause. For just
as things of themselves are repugnant to being future or non-future prior to
GOD’s decree, the first cause of future and non-future things, so all objective
beings of themselves are repugnant to being prior to the first omnipotent
cause. For those things to which impossibility of being belongs do not possess
objective non-repugnance, or possibility of being. But impossibility of being
belongs to all possibles prior to and apart from the first supremely powerful
and omnipotent cause.-*

*—Two objections are raised here.

*Therefore, if impossibility of being belongs to possibles prior to the first
being and apart from God, then if the first cause were removed all things would
be impossible; and apart from GOD these propositions would truly hold:
“Nothing exists; nothing can exist; all things are impossible.” Indeed, they
would not be creatable.*

I answer: if there were no first cause, what intellect would form this
proposition—*Nothing exists; all things are impossible*? Not an uncreated
intellect, which does not exist on the supposition; and even if it did, it
would behold things without composition and division. Nor would a created
intellect do so, because no created being would exist. If someone says, “But
perhaps fundamental truth, or objective non-repugnance, will exist even if the
first being does not,” I answer that repugnance is either incomplex or complex.
It was said above that nothing simple and incomplex is repugnant, because all
things are possible to God except those which imply a contradiction. But if
there is no first cause—as is impiously supposed—nothing will be possible,
there will be no being; nothing will be true, nothing false; nothing will
exist, and nothing will not exist. Like a stag-goat, it will be neither white
nor black, living nor dead. What, then, will it be?

It is one thing for uncreatability, impossibility, or non-possibility of being
to belong to possibles of themselves, even if there were no first cause; and
another for uncreatability or non-possibility to belong to creatable things in
themselves, yet originally from the first cause. For if we suppose there is no
first cause, such propositions would contain neither beings properly speaking,
nor non-beings properly speaking, nor anything true or false, nor anything
possible or impossible. They would be sheer nothings—neither true nor false,
and perhaps both true and false and contradictory—because in supposing there
is no first being you suppose an infinite contradiction, and there is nothing
absurd in infinitely many contradictions following from so notable a
contradiction.

Yet if the first cause’s existence in itself is presupposed, these would be
true: *Nothing exists; nothing can exist; all things are possible.* They would
not, however, be true without every relation to the first being. For although
nothing is nothing in itself and the impossible is objectively impossible in
itself, it does not follow that they are beings in potentiality or impossibles
apart from God and the existing first being. They are deviations in the genus
of being from him, and by his mode of not freely producing they have it that
they are non-beings and nothings. For it is not repugnant for non-being to be
nothing in itself formally and intrinsically if every consideration of every
efficient cause is removed, just as a human being is a rational animal apart
from every-*

I answer: on so impious a hypothesis, in which there were no first cause, this
first principle would be overthrown—*It is impossible for the same being
simultaneously to be and not be*—because the first identical being, namely GOD,
would simultaneously be God—for nothing is more contradictory or blasphemous

animal, nor would the same created thing be identical with itself, unless the
same uncreated being were prior, infinitely identical, and most gloriously
coidentified with himself.

upon the composite and upon matter, form, and all internal causes. For God
