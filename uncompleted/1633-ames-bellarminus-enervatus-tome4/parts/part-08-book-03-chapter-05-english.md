### Chapter V

Certain arguments of our own against Bellarmine's fifth and seventh
propositions, in which he taught that there is no one who does not have
sufficient help, according to place and time, for conversion from sins
committed, and always for avoiding sins.

1. Protestant reply. Scripture testifies that certain men are hardened and
blinded by God.

Bellarmine. *We believe that God, although not always, nevertheless according
to place and time extends even to them help by which they can repel blindness
and hardness from their own heart—namely, when they cannot avoid sin without
conversion.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine himself, in *On the Loss of Grace*, book 2,
chapter 14, describes blinding and hardening in such a way that he says they
occur “so that, by the just judgment of God punishing past sins, a man is so
deprived of divine help that he cannot morally avoid falling.” Can it be
piously believed that God at the same time withdraws and extends the help by
which someone may repel blindness and hardness from his heart? II. If
hardening consists in the penal deprivation of help, how can the hardened
person, while he remains such, have sufficient help for repelling hardness,
unless the person who by God's judgment is deprived of help has help for
repelling that judicial deprivation? III. If sufficient help is present to
the hardened when they cannot avoid sin without conversion, then it is always
present; for those who are hardened are hardened in sin, and hardening itself,
as Bellarmine admits in *On the Loss of Grace*, book 2, chapter 14, is a sin
and a cause of sin. IV. Mercy and hardening are opposed to each other, so that
when one is affirmed the other is denied. Romans 9:18. The consequence is
therefore firm: this man is hardened; therefore he is destitute of the helps
of grace and mercy.

2. Protestant reply. Acts 14:16 and verse 17: *God allowed all nations to walk
in their own ways.*

Bellarmine. 1. *That is, God's grace did not flow as abundantly as it did
afterward, but on that account there was never lacking that measure of grace
which seemed sufficient and necessary to divine providence.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine disguises the question and answers something
else. The question concerns grace sufficient for conversion from sins
committed and for avoiding other sins; he answers about grace sufficient for
divine providence. II. The grace which now flows among many nations

[Printed p. 277 | Google Books PA277 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

is certainly not more than sufficient for conversion and for avoiding all
sins. Therefore, the lesser grace which Bellarmine acknowledges was not
sufficient to produce these effects.

Bellarmine. 2. *God did not leave himself without testimony.*

Protestant reply. That is, as Acts 14:17 says, “he gave rains and appointed
seasons for bringing forth fruits, filling our hearts with food and
gladness.” Is this grace sufficient for rising again from sins and for
avoiding sins?

Bellarmine. 3. *The apostle adds in Acts 17:27 that they were able to seek and
find God.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine adds this. The apostle's words are “that they
should seek the Lord, if perhaps they might feel after him and find him.”
There is nothing here about an ability—at least, an ability to rise again from
sins and avoid all sins.

Bellarmine. 4. *The apostle confirms in Romans 1:20 that these same Gentiles
were without excuse.*

Protestant reply. This was because, namely, they did not glorify God according
to their knowledge or even according to their ability, but knowingly and
willingly indulged in sins.

3. Protestant reply. Romans 10:14: *How will they believe without a preacher?*

Bellarmine. *Those to whom the gospel is not preached can be awakened by God
to believe in God, and from such faith they can be awakened to obtain from God
through prayers and alms a greater light of faith. We have an example of this
in Cornelius, Acts 10. They therefore have help by which they can be converted,
not immediately but mediately.*

Protestant reply. I. This evasion is manifest. The question concerns what
actually occurs—whether sufficient grace is given to all—but Bellarmine
answers concerning what could occur through God's unlimited power. II. In
order to mock the cited words with his own invention, he directly contradicts
other words of Paul placed in the same passage. For he affirms that a man
without Christian faith and grace can efficaciously invoke God to obtain a
greater light of faith. But the apostle connects these things: “How will they
call? How will they believe? How will they hear?” Thus he makes Christian
faith depend upon hearing, and invocation upon faith, not faith and hearing
upon invocation, which is Bellarmine's perverse order. III. If this is the law
and common course by which grace is communicated to the greater part of the
human race, it is remarkable that no example of such a process of grace can
be produced except in one centurion. IV. The example of Cornelius does not
fit. No circumstance suggests that he was then an unbeliever and unconverted
when he prayed and gave alms any more than it suggests this of that other
centurion whose faith Christ himself is said to have marveled at, in Luke

[Printed p. 278 | Google Books PA278 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

7:9. Moreover, the Carthusian says on Acts 10: “The Doctors say concerning
Cornelius that before the apparition he had faith which is a theological
virtue, faith formed and adorned with charity, faith concerning Christ, at
least implicit and indistinct.” Thomas himself, whom alone Bellarmine praises
here as the authority for his judgment concerning Cornelius, contradicts him
in express words and agrees with us. 2–2, question 10, article 4, 3; and 3,
question 69, article 2.

4. Protestant reply. John 6:44–45: *No one can come to me unless the Father
has drawn him; and everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes to
me.* No one can come unless he is drawn, but not all are drawn, because not
all come; therefore not all can come.

Bellarmine. 1. *All are drawn, for to be drawn is to hear. Yet not everyone
who hears comes, but everyone who hears and learns; and this is efficacious
grace.*

Protestant reply. Nothing could have been said more contrary to Christ's
words. I. Christ gives the reason why not all came to him: namely, because not
all were drawn. Bellarmine answers, “All are drawn.” II. Christ manifestly
distinguishes among his hearers: some of them were drawn by the Father and
some were not. Bellarmine answers, “To be drawn is to hear.” III. Christ
places drawing in the most efficacious action of God: “The Father draws.”
Bellarmine places it in an inefficacious action: “To be drawn is to hear; not
everyone who hears comes.” IV. When Christ explains what it is “to be drawn,”
he says that it is “to be taught by God”; and when he explains what it is “to
be taught,” he affirms that it is “to hear and learn.” Bellarmine, on the
contrary, wills “to be drawn” to mean to hear, and “to be taught” and “to
learn” to mean I know not what else, which Christ either was unwilling or was
unable to explain. V. Christ says that those “to whom it is given by God” are
drawn, verse 65, and by this gift he distinguishes some from others among his
hearers and disciples, verse 54. Bellarmine, on the contrary, says that “all”
are drawn, so that by this gift not a single person can be distinguished from
another.

Bellarmine. 2. *It may be said that the Lord speaks of efficacious grace. The
person who is not drawn by efficacious grace is said to be unable to come,
not because he is absolutely unable, but because he will infallibly not come.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine's second thoughts, therefore, are that the person
who is not drawn by the Father, is not taught by God, does not hear and learn,
or to whom it has not been given by the Father, is absolutely able to believe.

5. Bellarmine. *The eighth proposition is this: Although sufficient grace is
given to all, no reason for divine predestination can be assigned on our part,
not even a condition without which the person who is predestined would not be
predestined.*

Protestant reply. Enough was said previously about the former part of this
axiom. The latter part is most true as it stands. But

[Printed p. 279 | Google Books PA279 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

I. it should be observed that by this opinion Bellarmine condemns of a most
serious error not only various scholastics but also a great many more recent
papists, among whom are Jesuits of great, though not good, reputation. Among
others is Becanus, a most bitter slanderer of our theologians, who in *On
Predestination*, part 2, chapter 3, lays down this foundation as a certain
rule: “If we show that this or that effect of predestination is given to us
from merits, it will already have been shown that predestination with respect
to that effect was made from foreseen merits.” In the same place he assumes
concerning the grace of justification that “most scholastics think that merit
of congruity rightly coheres with gratuitous justification; the merit of
faith and love does not conflict with gratuitous justification.” But
concerning glory he pronounces more boldly: “It is evident from Scripture that
eternal life is given from merits of grace that are worthy of it. Therefore,
it cannot be doubted that it was prepared from eternity upon the foresight of
merits. The reason why these have been elect from eternity and not those is
that these did good works and those did not—namely, in the foreknowledge of
God. The eminent theologians Camerarius, Molina, Osorius, Turrianus, Vásquez,
and others think in this manner.” II. It should also be observed that this
opinion cannot in any way be reconciled with what Bellarmine previously taught
about efficacious grace, unless, just as he placed the efficacy of grace in
its being urged upon a person in the manner, place, and time in which God
foresaw that this or that person would embrace it through free choice, so he
also places election to grace in God's decreeing to offer it with such
circumstances. On this account, however, that free activity of free choice by
which someone consents to the helps of awakening grace will not be the effect
of election. Thus no one would be elected to consent to God as he calls, or
elected to efficacious grace. Therefore, this very plausible profession of
Bellarmine concerning God's gratuitous predestination removes its primary
part.

8. Bellarmine. *According to our manner of understanding, this appears to be
the order of predestination in the divine mind. 1. God foresaw that if he
created man, man would fall together with all his posterity; and at the same
time he saw that he could by various means deliver either all or some,
according to his own choice. 2. He willed to create man and permit him to
fall, and mercifully to deliver some from the number of the fallen, while
justly leaving others in the mass of perdition. 3. He devised remedies suited
to saving the elect, among which the incarnation and the Savior's passion had
the first place. 4. He approved the remedies, and then chose Christ and us in
him before the foundation of the world. 5. He disposed, ordained, and in a
certain manner commanded that it should so occur.*

Protestant reply. Since these signs, instances, or moments, earlier and later,
do not belong to the divine acts in themselves but only to our manner of
understanding, their order is therefore variously as-*

[Printed p. 280 | Google Books PA280 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*serted according to the various ways in which men understand and judge the
divine acts. According to the common opinion of Thomas and the Thomists, the
order should be understood as follows: I. “Through the knowledge of simple
intelligence, God knew all possible creatures.” II. “From all possible
creatures he decreed to create those which were made by him, according as
they were created in time.” III. “He decreed to permit sin, toward that end
to which it is ordained in time.” IV. “He willed the incarnation of the Word
as a remedy for sin, and predestined Christ.” V. “He chose the heirs of
salvation in Christ and rejected the rest.” VI. “He decreed to confer upon the
elect, and not upon others, efficacious helps for salvation.” The Scotists,
speaking more distinctly about these signs, establish one order with respect
to intention and another with respect to execution. “With respect to
intention,” they say, “God I. willed himself and his own glory. II. He willed
Christ with the elect. III. He willed the goods of grace. IV. He willed the
goods of nature and this sensible world. V. Foreseeing the fall, he willed for
the elect a remedy through Christ's passion. But with respect to execution,
I. he willed the goods of nature. II. He willed the goods of grace, and among
them, upon the occasion of the fall, redemption through Christ. III. He willed
the grace of the elect and of Christ. IV. He willed his own glory.” There are
other more recent theologians, chiefly Jesuits, who are not particularly
difficult in other matters, but in all those which properly concern the
predestination of individual men they will the primary hinge to be a certain
*middle knowledge* between those forms of knowledge which are called “simple
intelligence” and “vision.” By this knowledge, they say, before every act of
his will God knew toward which alternative the choice of this or that person
would turn itself by its innate liberty, upon the hypothesis that it were
placed with such helps in such circumstances. According to this foreknowledge,
they will that God both conceived the decree of predestination from eternity
and committed it to execution in time. Bellarmine in fact agrees with these
latter theologians. But, restrained by some shame from professing the same
thing in words, he expresses this order so as to turn it into confusion and a
covering for his errors. For I. the greatest confusion lies in the proposition
that “God foresaw that if he created man, man would fall with all his
posterity.” There is no connection between man's creation and his fall. At
least temptation, its outcome, and the ordination of its end intervened. But
Bellarmine preferred to be silent about these things rather than confess the
truth. II. In the second sign Bellarmine does not say toward what end “God
willed to permit man to fall,” nor whether “the certain persons whom he willed
to deliver” were considered collectively or as certain individual persons.
III. In the third sign, when he affirms that the other remedies as well as
“Christ's passion were devised for the elect,” he refutes what he previously
taught in chapter 5, that “Christ paid the price for all,”

[Printed p. 281 | Google Books PA281 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

“and grace is given to all by which they may be made partakers of Christ's
merits.” IV. In the fourth place, he absurdly says that men were elected after
remedies suited for saving the elect had been devised in the third sign. For
the suitability of means with respect to the elect cannot be considered
before election. V. In the final sign, where he says that “God disposed,
ordained, and in a certain manner commanded that it should so occur,” under
the uncertain designation of “so” he leaves everything uncertain. It is also
sufficiently evident from the things treated previously that Bellarmine
subtracts from divine predestination and decree the application of the will
to accept offered grace. According to Bellarmine, therefore, predestination
is not certain and infallible by its own power, but by reason of human consent
which is indeed foreknown but not predestined. VI. These instances are plainly
devised at pleasure. The fifth instance, to omit the others, can according to
Bellarmine's opinion be distinguished in like manner into five others. For
example, he could have said: I. God foresaw that these and those elect persons,
with such help and at such a place and time, would turn themselves to God. II.
He decreed to offer such help to them at such a place and time. III. He
decreed to concur with them in perfecting that act. IV. He decreed to give the
elect suitable help for perseverance. V. He decreed to glorify those who
persevered.
