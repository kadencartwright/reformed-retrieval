As for the second, let us hear the judgment of Francis Bacon, who was afterward
made chancellor of England. *There are two circumstances,* he says, *in the
administration of bishops in which I confess that satisfaction has never been
given me. One is the singular exercise of authority; the other, its delegation.
As for the first, the bishop confers orders alone, excommunicates alone, and
judges alone. This seems to be without*

[1623 printed p. 311 | IA leaf 323 | PDF p. 323]

*almost any example in government, and it is therefore likely that it crept in during
degenerate and more corrupt ages. We see that supreme kings and monarchs have
their own senate of counselors. Nowhere is there a temporal court of a higher
nature in which authority resides in one person. The King’s Bench, the Common
Bench of Common Pleas, and the Exchequer*—I shall use the lawyers’ forensic
style in translation, as Camden does in his *Britannia*—*are benches, or courts,
with a fixed number of several judges. The chancellor of England has an array
of twelve Masters in Chancery. The master general of the Court of Wards has the
senate of his court, as does the chancellor of the Duchy. In the Exchequer the
lord treasurer is joined with the chancellor and barons. The Masters of Requests
are always more than one. The justices of assize are always two. The lord
president in the border region and the northern district has a senate. The Star
Chamber is an assembly of the king’s counselors interspersed with Lords
Spiritual and Temporal. Thus in every court the chief officer always has
colleagues or assessors. The like can be found in other well-administered
kingdoms abroad, where jurisdiction is distributed still more widely, as in the
Courts of Parlement in France and in other places. No one will deny that the
acts exercised through a bishop’s jurisdiction are of no less importance than
those exercised in civil courts. For human souls are of greater worth than
bodies, and so too is reputation. Bishops have their infirmities and are not
exempt from that common curse pronounced against all who live: “Woe to him who
is alone, for if he falls,”* etc. *Indeed, the authority first granted in
spiritual causes is entrusted to several: “Tell it to the church,” which is not
done in temporal causes. And in common causes of ecclesiastical government
there are assemblies of the whole clergy in councils, just as there are of the
estates in parliaments. From where, then, comes that singular exercise of
jurisdiction? I certainly judge, and not without reason, as I think, that it was
not so from the beginning; and that the dean and chapters were councils set
beside the bishops’ sees and cathedrals, and were presbyteries and consistories
for them; and that they involved themselves not only in the administration of
revenues and goods but also, and much more, in ecclesiastical jurisdiction. But
it is probable that in matters pertaining to profit and the world, the dean and
chapter adhered more tenaciously to the bishop and were unwilling to lose their
right. But as for jurisdiction, because they considered it a matter full of
labor and constant oversight, they allowed the bishop to creep in and usurp it;
therefore one endured and the other perished. And we see the Roman pontiff*—it
is right to be taught by an enemy, and without doubt the first institutions of
that church were most excellent—*exercise all ecclesiastical jurisdiction as
if in consistory. And of whom does that consistory of his consist, if not the
parish presbyters of Rome, who call themselves cardinals, from the hinges of the
world, because he vaunts himself as universal bishop of the whole globe? And
again we see certain shadows of it endure, inasmuch as the dean and chapter pro
forma elect the bishop, which is the highest act in jurisdiction; and when the
bishop confers orders, if any*

*presbyters happen to be present, he summons them to impose hands together with
him, and certain other particular things are
done. Therefore it seems to me pious, equitable, and consonant with the first
institution that in weightier causes which call for spiritual adjudication—such
as ordination, suspension, deprivation of ministers, excommunication restored
to its genuine and proper use, as will be indicated afterward, pronouncing
sentence on the validity of marriage and legitimations, and the trial of
criminal causes such as simony, incest, blasphemy, and the like—the bishop ought
not to proceed alone and without assistance. This article of reform is of such
a kind that it can be instituted without commotion; and it is a matter of the
kind that will add strength to the bishops, gain the favor of the prelates of
lower ranks, and bring a more successful prosecution and determination of the
causes that will be heard. And just as I would wish that strength to be added
to your bishops in the Senate, so it is not unworthy of Your Royal Majesty’s
consideration whether you judge it fitting that the general council of the
clergy, the House of Convocation, should be strengthened. Its power was
restrained when part of the kingdom was suspect because of the homage paid to
the Roman pontiff not many years ago, but that order will now yield to none in
fidelity and obedience toward Your Majesty.* When it is said here that the dean
and chapter were originally the bishop’s counselors, this must be understood of
the time when the dean and chapter were first established, not when the
episcopate was first instituted, much less when everything was conducted by the
common counsel of all the presbyters without a bishop. Presbyters existed before
the bishop, and when bishops were first placed over presbyters they were simple
presidents and, as it were, deans. There was no dean other than the bishop as
president, nor for many ages was there a difference between cardinal and
non-cardinal presbyters, or between canons and non-canons, so that the former
rather than the latter were the bishop’s counselors. We demonstrated this from
Bellarmine himself in the preceding chapter and from Field the hierarchist.
Instead, everything was conducted by the counsel of all the presbyters of the
whole city, as if now at London all the parish ministers together with the
bishop constituted an ecclesiastical senate, and that for the entire parish of
the city, not for the territories of rural bishops. So far were the first
institutions of the Roman church from being most excellent—even if they were
very splendid with respect to the pontifical monarchy—that this selection of
certain men stripped other lawful pastors of the government of their own
churches, which were dearer to them than others, excluded them even from common
government, and enlarged the dignity of the cardinals. The most excellent
institutions of government are not to be expected or sought from that mystical
Babylon, the seat of Antichrist. Nevertheless, those cardinals were parish
presbyters and deacons of the city of Rome, not canons like our prebendaries in
cities that are episcopal sees. But even that common government of the chapter
eventually became obsolete. How dangerous, then, it is to depart from that form
of government prescribed in the divine tablets and to devise

[1623 printed p. 313 | IA leaf 325 | PDF p. 325 | ends before `Quantum ad tertium`]

other foreign forms of government! We accept what the statesman concedes, and
let the English bishops be ashamed that the Roman pontiff administers
everything as if in
consistory and has a senate of cardinals whose counsel he employs, while they
have no use of a senate except when the church’s goods are being alienated and
the chapter’s consent is required. And what sort of form of government, I ask,
is one that is not approved even in a well-constituted commonwealth? *Penalties,
suspensions, and excommunications in the bishop’s consistory proceed from the
authority and judgment of one bishop, if he is present, or from the sentence and
power of the vicar-general or of one commissioner, if he is absent. Indeed, do
not all censures of this kind in the archdeacon’s consistory proceed from the
authority of one archdeacon, or, if he is absent, from the sole authority of his
official?* But the like cannot be found in a commonwealth where justice in
criminal causes is administered by the ministry of any one subject. In the
court of law they call the King’s Bench, because the king used to preside in it,
the chief justice of England cannot by himself, without three or four associate
justices, interpret a law or statute when a controversy arises over its
interpretation. But the other three, if they come to a different opinion, can
draw the court to their interpretation; nor can he alone pronounce punishment
against the accused unless the man has first confessed or been examined by the
inquest of twelve men, whom they call jurors. In the court they call the Common
Bench, because common pleas between subjects are heard in it under what is
called common law, the chief justice likewise has three or four justices as
assessors. *And is it not sufficiently established,* Downham himself says, *that
the judges in the King’s Bench or Common Pleas, who are the attendants of the
chief justices, are joined to each of them both to assist in administering
judgment rightly and also to restrain him from judging alone according to his
own pleasure?* In the Court of Wards, or of wards under age, in addition to the
master general they have the surveyor of liveries, the receiver general, the
attorney or advocate general, and others. So too in the courts of equity, as in
Chancery and the Court of Requests, where the causes of the poor and of royal
servants are heard, there are several judges. The defender of Christian polity
runs through every court of law and equity, down to the lowest in the English
kingdom, and shows that in each of them law is administered by several, not by
one. Therefore the singular government of a bishop or of his chancellor,
archdeacon, or official came from elsewhere—namely, from Babylon. For it does
not accord with the government of the English commonwealth. I do not say this
because I would have the government of the church conformed to civil
government, but because ecclesiastics ought to be ashamed to use a form of
government which well-constituted commonwealths reject, so far as can be done.
