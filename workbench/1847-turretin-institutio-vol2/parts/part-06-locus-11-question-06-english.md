## QUESTION VI

On the rules for explaining and observing the Decalogue.

What rules must be maintained in explaining the precepts of the Decalogue and
in observing them?

[Marginal heading: The first rule for explaining the law.]

I. Two matters are investigated in this question. First, there are the *rules
for explaining* the Decalogue, so that the genuine sense of its precepts may
be maintained against the perverse judgments of people who interpret it
wrongly. Second, there are the *rules of obedience*, so that we may know what
kind of obedience the law requires from us. With respect to the first matter,
the following rules must be observed in explaining the law.

II. First, *the law is spiritual: it regards not only the external actions of
the body but also the internal movements of the soul.* This is evident both
from Paul, who testifies in Romans 7:14 that the law is spiritual, and from the
nature of the Lawgiver himself. He is not a human and earthly lawgiver, who,
because he has bodily eyes, attends only to external deeds that fall under the
senses and does not regard purposes and wills except insofar as they have
emerged outwardly. Hence such a lawgiver's prohibitions are not violated
unless crimes have actually been committed. Rather, he is the divine and
heavenly Lawgiver, from whose eye nothing escapes and who does not regard the
outward appearance so much as purity of heart. Therefore, he speaks no less to
the soul than to the body and demands internal obedience no less than
external. Christ teaches this plainly in Matthew 5, when he extends murder to
hatred of one's brother and adultery to desire and the looking upon a woman.

[Marginal heading: Rule 2.]

III. Second, *negative commands are contained in affirmative precepts, and
affirmative commands in negative precepts.* Just as Scripture contains more
things than words, so the precepts and prohibitions always contain more than
their words express. This is not because the law should be treated as a
Lesbian rule by which we may make anything out of anything, but so that we may
inquire more deeply into the Lawgiver's meaning and include those things that
are connected with one another or depend upon one another. Since the good
prescribed by the law cannot be performed unless the opposite evil is
avoided, and the forbidden evil cannot be avoided unless the opposite good is
performed, negative commands are contained in affirmative precepts and
affirmative commands in negative precepts. Thus, things that are expressly
negative are implicitly affirmative, and conversely, and virtues are
commended when vices are forbidden. This does not mean that virtue should be
located in abstinence from vice alone, as is commonly said, for we must
proceed further to the contrary duties and deeds. Thus, in the precept “You
shall not kill,” reason sees nothing beyond the necessity of abstaining from
every injury. Yet it is certain that love is commended in addition, so that we
should assist our neighbor's life in whatever ways we can. Reason requires
this because God forbids our brother to be injured or harmed unjustly since he
wills his life to be dear and precious to us, so that we should render
whatever services we can toward its preservation. In the same way,
beneficence is commanded when theft is forbidden. If God commands children to
honor their parents and promises a reward to those who honor them, by that
very fact he forbids anyone to injure them and indicates that whoever does the
contrary will not go unpunished.

[Marginal heading: Rule 3.]

IV. Third, *synecdoche must be recognized in all the precepts*: when one
species is set forth, all the species under the same genus are understood;
and when one vice is forbidden, other vices of the same kind and analogous to
it, and whatever tends or leads to it, are forbidden. Thus, by the precept
against

[Printed p. 33 | Internet Archive PDF 61 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

committing adultery, every affection toward another man's wife that tends to
desiring her is prohibited (Matt. 5:28), and every kind of unlawful lust is
forbidden. All movements of anger are included under murder. Moreover, what
is most shameful in each kind of sin is forbidden, as are the principal sins,
to which all the others are connected, whether because they flow from those
sins, ultimately lead to them, or—though they seem very small to people—are
condemned with a more severe judgment at God's perfectly exact tribunal.
Therefore, this is not done to excuse or exclude lesser sins, but so that a
greater detestation of sin may be impressed upon our minds. The flesh attempts
to dilute the foulness of sin and cover it with plausible pretexts. For
example, anger and hatred are not thought so utterly detestable when they are
called by their own names. But when they are forbidden to us under the name of
murder, we understand better how abominable they are before God.

[Marginal heading: Rule 4.]

V. The fourth rule, which pertains to the same matter, is this: *the cause is
included in the effect, the species in the genus, and the correlative in the
relative*, because one who wills or prohibits something also wills or
prohibits that without which it cannot or does not usually occur. Thus, in the
prohibition of adultery, every unlawful lust is prohibited, as are its
fountain, intemperance, and all its incitements. Likewise, when the law
commands chastity, it also requires its nurse, temperance, and moderation in
food. When children are commanded to honor their parents, parents in turn are
commanded to nurture their children in a fatherly manner and bring them up in
the discipline of the Lord. Under the name of parents are understood all
superiors—magistrates, masters, teachers, and pastors—to whom in turn are
prescribed the duties by which they are bound toward their inferiors.

[Marginal heading: Rule 5.]

VI. Fifth, *the precepts of the first table take precedence over those of the
second with respect to necessary internal and external acts when the two
cannot both have place at the same time.* Thus, love of one's neighbor must be
subjected to love of God, and we are bound to hate father and mother for the
sake of Christ (Luke 14:26) when love for parents is incompatible with love
for Christ. Human commands must be disregarded where they are opposed to the
commands of God (Matt. 10:37; Acts 4:19). Conversely, however, “the ceremonial
requirements of the first table yield to the moral requirements of the
second, because God wills mercy and not sacrifice” (Hos. 6:6)—that is, he
commands moral worship chiefly and primarily, as more excellent and necessary
in itself, but ceremonial worship only secondarily for the sake of moral
worship. Hence we ought not to be as concerned about the latter as about the
former.

[Marginal heading: Rule 6.]

VII. Sixth, *some precepts are affirmative*: they command things that are to
be done, and are sinned against by omission. *Others are negative*: they
prohibit things that are to be avoided, and are sinned against by commission.
*The former bind always, but not to action at every moment; the latter bind
always and to abstention at every moment.* The virtues and duties commanded by
affirmative precepts cannot all be exercised at once at every moment, and
they presuppose certain conditions without which there is no occasion for
them. For example, parents are not always alive or present so that we may
render them due honor. But the vices and crimes that are forbidden cannot be
lawfully committed at any moment. The general affirmative command to love God
seems to be excepted here, because it binds always and to action at every
moment. There is no time, place, or condition in which a person can be exempt
from the duty of loving God.

[Marginal heading: Rule 7.]

VIII. Seventh, *love is the principle and end of all the precepts.* Hence love
is called the end and fulfillment of the law (Rom. 13:10; 1 Tim. 1:5). Love
discharges every function of God's beneficence and of human obedience. Just as
all God's benefits flow from love and are contained in it, so all human duties
are included in love. God's love is the fullness of the gospel; human love is
the fullness of the law. God is love, and the badge of the children of God is
nothing other than love (John

[Printed p. 34 | Internet Archive PDF 62 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

13:35). Now just as love has a twofold object, God and one's neighbor, so a
twofold love is commanded: love of God in the first table of the law, and love
of one's neighbor in the second. The former is called “the first and great
commandment,” and the latter “the second, like it.” Love of God is rightly
called the *first* commandment because, just as nothing is prior to God, so
worship of him must be our first aim, so that all things may begin from him
and end in him. It is called *great*, first, with respect to its *object*,
because it concerns the greatest and infinite object, namely God; second, with
respect to its *subject*, because it requires every faculty and power in a
person, so that we love God with the whole mind, the whole heart, and all our
strength; third, with respect to its *breadth and extension*, because it
embraces everything within its scope and influences all human actions, for
nothing can be pleasing to God unless it is done for God's sake. The second is
called like the first, not with respect to *dignity*, but first, with respect
to *quality*, because sincerity and purity of heart are required in both love
of God and love of one's neighbor; second, with respect to *authority*,
because both are commanded by God and tend toward his glory; third, with
respect to *punishment*, because violation of either table incurs guilt of
eternal death; fourth, with respect to *connection and dependence*, because
they are so closely connected that neither can be performed without the
other. God cannot be loved unless one's neighbor, who was made in the image of
God, is also loved; nor can one's neighbor be loved unless God, who created
him, is loved. “If anyone says that he loves God and hates his brother, he is
a liar. He who does not love his brother, whom he sees—how can he love God,
whom he does not see?” (1 John 4:20).

[Marginal heading: The first rule for observing the law.]

IX. The rules of the obedience that must be rendered to the law are also
various. First, *the whole law must be fulfilled by the whole person.* Just as
God prescribes the whole law to us with respect to all its precepts without
any division among them, so he requires the whole and not a divided person to
observe it, not only externally with respect to the body, but also internally
with respect to the soul and all its faculties. Just as none of the precepts
may be omitted by us, but we must continue in all things that are written, so,
because the whole person is under law to God, no part of the person can or
ought to be withdrawn from obedience to him. Whatever is in a person—whether
in the mind, the will and affections, the soul, or the body—must worship God
and be subjected to him. This is required both by the perfectly exact justice
of the *Lawgiver* and by the nature of the *good*, which ought to be complete
in every respect, as well as by the form of the *legal covenant*.

[Marginal heading: Rule 2.]

X. Second, *a fourfold perfection is required in obedience.* First, with
respect to its *principle*, it must be *true and sincere*, consisting not
merely in speech or in an external movement of the body, but having its seat
in the soul and arising from a purified heart and unfeigned faith. Paul
indicates this in 1 Timothy 1:5 when he says that *the end of the commandment
is love from a pure heart, a good conscience, and unfeigned faith*. Second,
with respect to its *object* or *parts*, it must be *universal*, not with
respect to this or that commandment but with respect to *all* of them, because
whoever sins in one is guilty of all; nor is anyone regarded as having
fulfilled the law unless he has continued in all the things that are written.
Third, with respect to its *degrees*, it must be *intense* and complete in
every respect, so that nothing can be added to it. Hence Bernard says that the
measure of loving God is to *love him without measure*. Fourth, with respect
to *time and duration*, it must be perpetual and constant from beginning to
end. We must not apply ourselves to the law in only one or another act, but
must *continue* throughout our whole life in the things that are written.

[Marginal heading: Rule 3.]

XI. Third, *just as internal obedience far excels external obedience, and
moral obedience ceremonial, so the latter is pleaded in vain without the
former.* Hence the prophets' grave complaints: they reproach the people for
their impiety because they separate things that God joined by an indissoluble
bond, being content with external and ceremonial worship and wholly
unconcerned about internal and moral worship. See

[Printed p. 35 | Internet Archive PDF 63 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Isaiah 1:15–18; 58:1–4; 66:1–4; Micah 6:6–7; and passages throughout.

[Marginal heading: Rule 4.]

XII. Fourth, *just as the precepts do not have an equal degree of dignity and
necessity, so obedience is not considered to have the same rank and value in
every case.* Nor does every act of disobedience have the same demerit, but it
contracts greater or lesser guilt. Omission of a precept is one thing; active
opposition to it is another. Forgetfulness is one thing, neglect another, and
contempt another. Neglect arises from the weakness of sloth, but contempt from
the swelling of pride. Hence the haughtiness of the person who shows contempt
increases the fault and turns a blemish into a crime. In easier matters,
contempt is more damnable and the act of obedience less praiseworthy. In more
difficult matters, obedience is more pleasing, just as transgression is more
serious.
