On the Power of God

Power is a communicable attribute by which God is signified to be effectually
endowed with strength and ability to act, simply and infinitely, 2 Samuel
33:3,[^p22-1] 1 Chronicles 29:12, and Hebrews 9:4.[^p22-2] He is therefore
called אל and אלוה. Power is attributed to him most simply because it is not
an accident in God but God's very essence; therefore he can accomplish
whatever he wills without any difficulty. It is attributed infinitely because
he has power to act from himself, not from another; he does not begin to be
able to do something he could not do before, nor does he cease to be able to
do what he can, Romans 1:20. He can do neither more nor fewer things than he
could from eternity. God's power is therefore infinite:

1. By reason of his essence, because he is infinite act.

2. With respect to its objects, because he never acts without being able to
do more.

3. By reason of its effect, because he does not extend the effects of his
power among creatures without being able to extend them further.

4. By reason of its duration, because he neither begins nor ceases to be able
to do what he can.

Power in general is twofold, passive or active. Passive power is not only that
by which someone undergoes something, but also that by which something that
does not exist can come to be; accordingly, it is related equally

[1620 Tome I | col. 206 | BSB frame 00119]

to nonbeing and to being. This in no way applies to God, because it is an
imperfection and defect, whereas God is most perfect act.

Active power is that by which someone is ready to act. This is not properly
attributed to God, because properly speaking there is no action in God, since
he is pure act; nor is he said to be able to act by reason of himself, but by
reason of creatures, which can receive God's act.

Active power, however, is twofold:

1. Truly one's own and not received from elsewhere.

2. Communicated. This, however, belongs not to God but to creatures. God's own
power is twofold according to its different relations: (1) immanent and (2)
emanent.

Immanent power concerns those things which God can always perform within
himself and does perform; it is twofold:

1. The power common to the three persons, by which they can understand and
love themselves, and so forth. This differs in no way from the essence itself.

2. The power particular to the persons, such as the Father's power to beget,
and so forth. This power is not really distinct from the essence itself.

Emanent power, of which Scripture speaks, concerns things outside God. It is
twofold. Absolute power is that by which God can do absolutely everything
that is possible, although he does not do everything; Matthew 19:26, Luke
1:3,[^p22-3] and Philippians 3:21 speak of it. Thus God could have created
more worlds and done many other things, and could have acted otherwise if he
had willed, Matthew 26:53 and 3:9, and Ephesians 3:20. Actual power is that by
which God actually does the things he freely wills, Psalms 135:6 and 115:3,
Ephesians 1:11, and John 5:21. When the Scriptures speak of this power, they
join it with promises. Furthermore, when God is said to be unable to do things
that involve imperfection, God's power is not removed or denied but rather
established. Thus he is said to be unable to suffer, eat, drink, sin, or will
contradictory things, and so forth. He cannot do these very things because he
is omnipotent; if he could do them, he would not

[1620 Tome I | col. 207 | BSB frame 00120]

be God. Chrysostom therefore says correctly in Homily 14 on John, “God can do
all things, but in such a way that he remains God.” Augustine likewise says in
book 1, chapter 1, *On the Creed*, “God is omnipotent precisely because he
cannot do evil; if he could do it, he would not be omnipotent.” The things,
therefore, on account of which God is said not to be omnipotent are things
which by their own nature and in themselves are not impossible, but are simply
impossible in the case of God—such as those which conflict with God's perfect
and omnipotent nature.[^p22-4] There are two kinds of impossibilities with
respect to God:

1. Things that are impossible in themselves and by their own nature, such as
contradictories being true at the same time.

2. Things that can indeed be done by creatures but have no place in God,
because they involve impotence and their nature consists in defect. In this
sense it is impossible for God to sin. God is then said absolutely to be
unable to do certain things in two ways:

1. By reason of the object which is called possible or impossible. This occurs
when what is predicated of some subject cannot cohere with some subject. For
example, if you say, “Being does not exist,” this is impossible because it
removes the whole nature of being. In this way God is said to be unable to do
contradictory things.

2. By reason of God himself, and this in two ways: either with respect to
power or with respect to will. With respect to power, the statement that God
cannot do something indicates power, although in its form of expression it
may seem to indicate impotence—as when you say that God cannot lie or deny
himself, and so forth.[^p22-5] With respect to will, he is said to be unable
to do things that he cannot will, such as sin. Excepting these kinds of
impossible things, which involve impotence, God can do all things.

Finally, just as God's will, so also his power receives different names
according to diverse modes of acting, ob—

[1620 Tome I | col. 208 | BSB frame 00120]

—jects, and effects, and is called truth, goodness, and so forth.

Uses of the doctrine. 1. This serves to confirm our faith, so that we do not
doubt the fulfillment of God's promises, Romans 4:20.

2. Therefore we must pray with confidence, because he can give what he has
promised.

3. Therefore adversities must be borne with courage, and in the midst of
death we must hope steadfastly, because he has promised to be present with us
and is able to be present and come to our aid.

4. Since he could have disposed of us as a potter disposes of clay, thanks
must be given that he has chosen, called, and justified us in Christ unto
eternal life, and so forth.

5. Therefore we must not despair of salvation even for those who seem lost,
because God can call them when he wills.

6. Therefore, apart from God's will, nothing either prosperous or adverse can
happen to us or to others. Consequently, let us be grateful to God in
prosperity and patient in adversity.
