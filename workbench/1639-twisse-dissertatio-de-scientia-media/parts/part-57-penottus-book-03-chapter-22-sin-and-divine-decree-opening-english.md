ON

GABRIEL PENOTTUS’S

*Defense of Human Liberty*

BOOK THREE, CHAPTER XXII

In which the second doubt is resolved: namely, whether morally evil acts, or sins, are known in the divine
decree.

PENOTTUS.

It is the opinion of certain more recent theologians—especially of those who say that all contingent effects
were predefined from eternity and that all contingent causes are physically predetermined in time toward
each of their acts, even those morally evil, with respect to their natural entity but with their wickedness
excepted—that all effects which are also morally evil and are called moral sins are sufficiently known by God
in his eternal decree. By that decree he resolved that those acts would come to pass with respect to their
natural entity and resolved to permit their wickedness. Certain of those cited in the preceding chapter think
so in the account of the first opinion, Alvarez especially in disputation 10 and expressly in disputation 11.
We shall bring forward and resolve their arguments below.

RESPONSE.

In this argument, I acknowledge, the adversaries are carried as though upon a favorable current, and it
appears to defenders of human liberty that their cause sails as though upon the Pacific Ocean. For no one at
all denies that man’s choice is free to perpetrate evil, and it appears hateful to ascribe to divine
providence not only the good things that come to pass, by whomsoever they come to pass, but evil things too.
On the other hand, it is also beyond controversy that evils of guilt cannot be ascribed to divine providence
as a blameworthy cause. Even according to our opinion, they are by no means ascribed to God as their
efficient cause—although Penottus deliberately thrusts this upon the divine will as their effective principle,
and does so from Augustine wrongly understood in the preceding chapter, number 4, where his words are these:
“The divine decree is the principle of being of all future contingents.” Rather, God is ascribed only the
permitting of the act’s wickedness. Nevertheless, Scripture everywhere teaches that a more hidden providence
must be attributed to God even in evil.

It testifies that Joseph was sent into Egypt by God’s will, although this was accomplished only through the
fratricidal crime of his brothers; that God hardened Pharaoh’s heart so that he would not release the people
of Israel, and Sihon also so that he would not permit the Israelites to pass through his land; and that God
sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the Shechemites so that they would destroy one another in mutual
hatred and thus the slaughter of Gideon’s sons would return upon their own heads. It testifies too that he
hardened the Canaanites so that, to their own preservation, they would not make a treaty with the
Israelites; that he likewise commanded Shimei to assail David with abuse; that he armed Absalom against his
father’s life and for his brother’s murder; and that he also brought it about that Absalom would defile his
father’s concubines by incestuous intercourse. God openly professes that the secession of the ten tribes from
the royal tribe also came about by his ordering.

The Apostle likewise teaches that the impure nations were handed over by God to a reprobate mind so that
they would do what was unfitting, and that by this very fact they received the reward of their error. He
teaches that Christians too were handed over to the efficacy of error so that they would believe a lie, and
this because they had by no means received the love of truth.

Finally, Peter testifies that Herod and Pontius Pilate, together with the nations and the people of Israel,
were gathered to do what God’s hand and counsel had foreordained to come to pass. These things were said
concerning Christ’s ignominious treatment and his delivery to death, namely death upon the cross. In the
Apocalypse Christ also reveals to John that God put it into the hearts of the kings to do his will and to
hand their kingdom over to the beast. All these, I say, are so many illustrious testimonies establishing
God’s hidden providence in evil.

But when Scripture is set aside and the matter is recalled to the tribunal of merely human judgment, it is
easy to range widely through all these matters in deference to human choice. The Jesuits, therefore, and with
the Jesuits this Penottus of Novara, gladly range over this field. But if the contest were decided by the
authority alone of the more recent scholastics, what, I ask, can the Jesuits despise in the Dominicans?
Whenever I compare the Jesuits with the Dominicans in learning and scholastic method, I appear to myself to
see as great a difference between them as between a disposition and a habit. And what, I ask, has Penottus
produced that can be compared with Alvarez’s learned labors? Let a fair appraiser of things judge whether he

who is named alongside Alvarez on the same day is worthy of it. For he conducts himself as a mountebank
rather than a scholastic, and carries on his case by
trickery and crafty guile rather than by arguments. As proof of this, without going any farther afield,
observe, I ask, with what good faith he says that this is the opinion only of “certain more recent
theologians.” He represents Alvarez alone as an advocate of this opinion. Yet who with even a moderate
acquaintance with scholastic matters does not know that this is the very opinion of Scotus, who expressly
teaches it? For in book 1, distinction 41, § “Against this,” and so forth, he says:

“God foreknows that he will cooperate with Lucifer toward the substance of that act which will be sin. He
foreknows this because he wills to cooperate with him if it is a sin of commission; or he foreknows that he
will not cooperate toward some act if he does not will that act, and this if that first act were a sin of
omission. And by knowing that he will cooperate toward such a substance of an act without the due
circumstances, or that he will not cooperate with him toward a necessary act and consequently, because he
will omit it, he knows that Lucifer will sin. Thus God knows that this man will sin not only because he
knows that he will permit it, but also because he knows that he will cooperate with him toward the substance
of an act not duly circumstanced, and consequently the man will commit it; or that he will not cooperate with
him toward a necessary act, and consequently the man will omit it.”

What sort of cooperation Scotus acknowledged—namely, the predetermination of the creature’s will toward
acting—he had already taught in distinction 35. There, having removed the other opinions concerning the mode
in which God foreknows future contingents, he substituted his own, namely from the determination of the
divine will. And in distinction 47 he asks “whether divine permission is some act of the divine will,” and
argues for the affirmative side in this manner:

“Otherwise God does not appear to know with certainty that a man will sin. For he does not know a future
contingent without a determination of his own will, but with respect to the sinner he does not appear to have
any act except permitting him to sin. For he has neither willing nor nilling with respect to that man.
Therefore, if permitting is not some act, God will not be certain of that man’s future sin, because there will
be no determination of his will for knowing that future contingent with certainty.”

He responds to this in the following words: “To the first argument it was said in distinction 41”—the response
from that distinction has already been recited above—“how God’s foresight of a future sin does not arise merely
from his knowing that he will permit this man to sin finally and thus finally to be damned. Along with this,
however, it is required that he know that he will cooperate with the man in the act of sinning, or that he
will not cooperate toward that act whose omission will be a sin of omission.”

The argument proceeded concerning determination, but Scotus responds concerning cooperation. Scotus’s
meaning is that the root of divine knowledge of future contingents is the determination of the divine will.
That determination of the divine will cannot be directed only toward cooperating with a free creature, but
must also be directed toward determining the creature’s will to operate freely. Otherwise it is manifest that
Scotus has by no means assigned a sufficient ground for knowing future contingents with certainty.

The same Scotus also says near the end of book 4, distinction 49, q. 6, § “I say, therefore”: “It is contrary
to the nature of the will to be determined by an inferior cause, because then the will itself would not be
superior. But it is not contrary to its nature to be determined by a superior cause, because this is
consistent with its being a cause in its own order.” He then imagines an opponent arguing thus: “On the
contrary, if a superior cause determines it, then the will is determined in its own order of causing.
Therefore it is not contingent in its own order of causing.” He responds in this way: “Because of its
determination there is equal contingency in its own order. But the fact that the effect proceeding from the
will and other causes does not arise altogether contingently from the will’s contingency is because another,
prior cause has been determined toward that effect.”

Luis de Molina too, in the first part, q. 14, art. 13, disputation 11, as Penottus reports in book 4, chapter
6, writes that “God’s influx”—according to Scotus—“or general concurrence with secondary causes is an influx
into those secondary causes themselves, imposing upon them a necessity to operate, even upon the human
will.” He says that Corduba too attributes this to Scotus. Nevertheless, Penottus adds that it is difficult
to discover where Scotus said this. Yet he acknowledges that Scotus appears to savor this doctrine in book
4, distinction 49, q. 6, § “I say, therefore.” “Nevertheless,” he says, “in book 4, distinction 1, q. 1, reply
to the last, he not obscurely intimates the opposite and openly teaches that, when the first cause concurs
with a secondary cause possessing the power of causing in the manner of something permanent, the first
cause infuses nothing new into the secondary cause.”

It will therefore be a labor by no means contemptible to examine that passage too, in order to discover
Scotus’s true opinion on this side. In the passage cited Scotus says: “It must be understood that nothing can
act in any order of acting except by its own power. For just as it was said above that a thing cannot act at
all unless it has an active and intrinsic form, so too it cannot act unless it has its own intrinsic power,
because that form is a power. If it did not previously possess a form or power and now acts in some order,
it must now possess anew a form and power in that order. For the same nature, remaining without any change,
can in no way be now active and now inactive. Nevertheless, a dependent agent is said to act in the power
of another, because the power of the dependent agent is insufficient without that power upon which it
depends. An instrument, however, is said to act in the power of another more than a secondary cause does,
because that which does not have an active form in a state of rest but receives it only in actual motion
depends more upon the superior agent than does that which has an active form in a state of rest.

“For a secondary cause which has an active form in a state of rest to act in the power of another is not for
it now to receive anything from that other, but only to have an inferior order toward that other agent,
acting simultaneously in its own order. But for an instrument—that is, something which receives an active
form in motion—to act in the power of another is for it then actually to receive from the other the form by
which it acts. From this it is evident that the influence of the first cause upon a secondary cause properly
so called, when they act simultaneously, is not a new influx, as though it were the creation of something
inhering in the secondary cause. Rather, the influx there is the determinate ordering of those causes in
producing their common effect. From these points the meaning of the major premise is evident. For it is
indeed true that a secondary cause and an instrument can do more in the power of another than properly”—supply
“by itself”—“when the other’s power is prescinded from, because its own power is diminished and subordinated
to the other’s power, so that without that other power acting, its power cannot issue into an effect.
Nevertheless, in the power of another it cannot do more than it can in its own power—that is, something to
which its power in no way extends within its own order of causing. Now if the minor premise takes its own
power while prescinding from the power of the superior cause, it is false, for the creature can do nothing
without the power of God acting principally. Therefore it does not hold.”

[Book I, printed p. 136; BSB canvas 00162; upper section]

When Scotus denies that the first cause infuses anything new into a secondary cause, he proceeds by a
comparison. For he compares the secondary cause with an instrument. He says that an instrument, when it acts
in the power of another, receives the active form by which it acts, but that this is not so with a secondary
cause. For the secondary cause receives no active form anew from the first cause when it is said to act in
the first cause’s power. Yet nothing in Scotus is better known than that, in his opinion, a secondary cause
does not move unless moved by the first. And it is moved by the first in such a way that if the first cause
moved necessarily, no effect would be contingent, even with respect to secondary causes.

Again, not only Scotus but Thomas’s school too sings in harmony with the same opinion, as is manifest from
the writings of the Dominicans. Augustine also professed long ago that “nothing comes to pass unless the
omnipotent wills it to come to pass.” It is evident from the words which follow that not only good things but
evil things too are embraced in this generality, for he adds: “either by permitting it to come to pass or by
doing it himself.” Nevertheless, I would not conclude from this that God’s will is the effective principle of
all future contingents. Penottus does so when he gathers from this that the divine decree is the principle of
being of all future contingents. We say that it is indeed the effective principle of good things, but only
the permissive principle of evil things: although the things which God permits come to pass with God
willing, they do not come to pass with God effecting them. Penottus himself readily allows that all
contingent effects, even those morally evil, are defined and determined by God, but by no means allows that
they are predefined and predetermined by God.
