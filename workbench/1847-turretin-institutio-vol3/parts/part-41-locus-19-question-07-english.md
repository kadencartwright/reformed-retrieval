QUESTION VII.

ON THE MINISTER’S INTENTION.

Is the minister’s intention, at least the intention of doing what the church
does, necessary for the essence and efficacy of a sacrament? We deny against
the papists.

[Marginalia: The papists’ opinion on the minister’s intention.]

I. This weighty question was raised in the Council of Trent and debated with
great contention of minds. Its decision is set forth in Session 7, Canon 11:
“If anyone says that, when ministers confect and confer the sacraments, there
is no requirement that they have at least the intention of doing what the
church does, let him be anathema.” This was taken from the decrees of Eugene
IV to the Armenians at the Council of Florence, which contain these words:
“All sacraments are completed by three things, namely, by things as the matter,
by words as the form, and by the person of the minister conferring the
sacrament with the intention of doing what the church does. If any of these is
lacking, the sacrament will not be completed.”

II. Yet the meaning of the words “the intention of doing what the church does
is required” is ambiguous, for they can be understood either “from

[1847 vol. III printed p. 305 | IA leaf 339 | PDF p. 339]

the duty of the one ministering” or “from the necessity of the sacrament.” The papists
do not understand them in the former sense, which we readily admit. Every
minister administering a sacrament ought to have the intention of doing what
the church does according to Christ’s institution, and if he lacks this
intention, he sins gravely. Rather, they understand them in the latter sense,
by which the intention is declared necessary for the integrity, efficacy, and
validity of the sacrament. This is evident (1) because the synod follows the
preceding decision of Eugene IV, which conveys this; (2) because the anathema
is directed against the Protestants, who attack the latter sense, not the
former; and (3) because this is the received opinion among the papists.

III. The papists, however, are astonishingly perplexed about the nature of
this intention and even oppose one another. Some require an actual intention,
others only a habitual intention. Still others, declaring the first
unnecessary and the second insufficient, urge a third that they call virtual:
“by which a person, because of the mind’s wandering, indeed does not have the
intention to baptize actually in the present, but had it before and is disposed
to have it,” as Bellarmine says in book 1, *On the Sacraments*, chapter 27.
Again, they laboriously dispute about material and formal intention: is it
restricted to the will to perform this action as though it were material, or
is the will to make a sacrament also required? See Becanus, chapter 5, *On the
Sacraments*, question 3. They further distinguish particular and general
intention: “It is indeed better for a person to have a particular intention,
that is, to confect the sacrament of baptism, but meanwhile the general
intention of doing what the church does is sufficient.” Bellarmine says in the
book cited that “there is no need to intend what the Roman church does, but
what the true church does, whichever it may be, or what Christ instituted, or
what Christians do.” Indeed, Bellarmine advances so far as to maintain that
“the intention to do what some particular and false church does, which the
person himself thinks true, such as the Genevan church, is sufficient; for,”
he says, “the person who intends to do what the Genevan church does intends to
do what the universal church does.” Thus, having nothing certain, he plainly
cuts down his own vineyard when he maintains that transubstantiation is
effected by a petty priest even when he does not intend to effect it, just as
it is acknowledged that it is neither effected nor intended by our church.

[Marginalia: State of the question.]

IV. From this the state of the question is gathered. 1. The question does not
concern the things necessary for the minister to discharge his office rightly:
whether he ought to be attentive to the things he must do, lest through
carelessness or profanity he do or intend to do something other than what he
ought. No one denies this. Rather, is this intention required for the integrity
and validity of the sacrament? The papists maintain that it is. 2. The question
is not whether a pastor who lacks a proper intention is guilty of a grave
crime for which he is bound to give an account to God. We acknowledge this.
Rather, does the sacrament that he administers therefore cease to be a true
and lawful sacrament? We deny it. 3. The question is not whether an external
intention is required, that is, one that regards the act itself of conferring
the sacrament according to the form of the institution and denotes the will
to perform all the sacramental acts according to Christ’s order. Rather, is an
internal intention required, one that regards the end of the sacrament, so
that the pastor has the mind and will to act for the end that Christ wills to
be regarded? In the administration of a sacrament, supposing that nothing is
lacking except the minister’s internal intention, is the sacrament invalid
because that intention is lacking? The papists affirm; we deny, relying on
these arguments.

[Marginalia: It is proved that the minister’s intention is not required for
the essence of a sacrament. 1. Because its absence is a personal sin.]

V. 1. One person’s personal sin does not harm another who does not share in
it. But the absence of the minister’s intention is the minister’s own personal
sin. Therefore

[1847 vol. III printed p. 306 | IA leaf 340 | PDF p. 340]

it does not harm another who does not share in it, namely, the person who
partakes of a sacrament rightly celebrated. Yet it would harm him if the
sacrament were invalid because that intention was absent.

[Marginalia: 2. Because the principle of the sacrament is the same as that of
the Word.]

VI. 2. The principle of the sacrament is the same as that of the preached
Word, since each is an instrument of God: the former is the visible Word, the
latter the audible Word. But the efficacy of the preached Word does not depend
on the minister’s intention, but on the power itself that accompanies the
Word. Thus Paul shows in Philippians 1:16–17 that it makes no difference
whether the gospel is preached from sincerity or from strife, provided that it
is preached. He indicates that these sins of the preachers do not harm the
hearers who embrace it with the obedience of faith. Bellarmine wrongly wishes
to distinguish the Word and the sacrament on the ground that the former only
signifies grace and can therefore arouse faith whatever the intention, whereas
the sacrament effects grace, which it does not do if it is not pronounced with
the intention of effecting it. For it is gratuitously supposed that the
sacrament is the kind of practical sign that effects the thing itself, as will
be proved below. The Word of God is no less efficacious than the sacrament,
but the efficacy of both the Word and the sacrament depends upon the Holy
Spirit, not upon the instrumental cause that only dispenses the external means.

[Marginalia: 3. Because the sacramental action would depend upon a human
being.]

VII. 3. The sacramental action would be effective not according to the
intention of the principal agent, namely God, but according to the intention
of the inferior cause. Thus the minister’s part would be more powerful than
God’s, and human unbelief would render the truth and faithfulness of God void,
contrary to Paul (Rom. 3:3).

[Marginalia: 4. Because a lack of uprightness does not hinder the sacrament.]

VIII. 4. According to their way of speaking, a lack of uprightness and mortal
sin in the minister do not prevent a true sacrament from being confected. This
accords with Canon 12, where anathema is pronounced against those who deny
that “a minister who exists in mortal sin, provided that he has observed all
the essentials that pertain to confecting the sacrament, confects or confers a
sacrament.” Therefore neither will a lack of intention, which is the greatest
wickedness and a mortal sin, prevent it. For what is said of the genus can also
be affirmed of the species.

[Marginalia: 5. Because there would be no certainty about the effect of the
sacraments.]

IX. 5. If the truth and efficacy of the sacraments depend upon the minister’s
intention, no certainty will be given about the saving effect of the
sacraments, and thus all true consolation expected from their use is removed.
For that knowledge is unsearchable, and no mortal can be infallibly certain
about the truth of that intention. Thus, apart from an extraordinary
revelation, no one can be certain that he has been truly baptized. It is
answered in vain here that metaphysical certainty, which comes from divine
revelation, indeed cannot be had, but moral certainty, which rests upon human
arguments, both suffices and can be had very easily. The reason given is that,
since it is very easy to have the intention, there is no cause to doubt it
unless the minister betrays its absence by some sign, and we are compelled to
rest in that certainty. For when we are baptized in infancy, we cannot know
that we have been baptized in any other way than from the report of parents
and friends, which is moral certainty. Yet since this moral certainty is
merely conjectural and is presumption rather than certainty, it is in no way
sufficient to quiet the conscience. The basis of the certainty that we can
have about our baptism is different, because baptism is an external act that
falls under the senses. Indubitable certainty about it can be had because it
rests on sense and the most certain human testimonies. But the minister’s
intention is internal and hidden, and no one can testify about it as a matter
certainly known to himself. “For what person knows the things of a person
except the spirit of the person that is in him?” (1 Cor. 2:11).

[Marginalia: 6. Because it renders the whole papacy doubtful.]

X. 6. The same necessity of intention renders the whole papacy doubtful and
always exposes Christians to the danger of idolatry. For suppose that a priest
lacked the intention to baptize or consecrate even a single time. There will
be no sacrament, and therefore the person who was dipped in water will not be
truly baptized. If that person later becomes a priest or bishop who administers
sacraments daily and ordains priests, those sacraments will all be nothing,
because a person who was not rightly baptized could not be ordained or
consecrate others. Hence infinitely many nullifications of sacraments arise,
which deservedly render the whole papacy uncertain and doubtful, as Ambrosius
Catarinus urged at the council. But no less an inconvenience arises from the
most immediate danger of idolatry, namely, in the adoration of the host. Due
to a lack of intention not only in the presbyter consecrating it but also in
the bishop who ordained him, neither the presbyter nor anyone else can know
whether the bread has been transubstantiated into Christ’s body or even could
have been transubstantiated. Now if there is no sacrament, the most manifest
idolatry will result, because something will be adored that will neither be
God nor have anything divine.

[Marginalia: 7. Because the fathers favor us.]

XI. 7. The fathers repeatedly confirm this, continually asserting that “the
truth of the sacraments rests not upon the morals, faith, and quality of the
minister, but upon God’s institution; whatever the outward minister may be,
God is nevertheless the inward worker. A bad minister does no harm where the
Lord is good. The sacraments are confected not by the faith of the one using
or administering them, but by the words of their author.” In book 7, *On
Baptism*, chapter 53, Augustine asks whether baptism bestowed in mimicry is
baptism, and he does not dare to decide. Therefore he did not know that the
minister’s intention was required for its essence. In book 1, *Against the
Letters of Petilian*, chapter 7, he opposes this rule to the Donatist error of
regarding the conscience of the giver: “The human conscience is often unknown
to me, but I am certain of Christ’s mercy.” And around the middle of Letter
166, against the Donatists: “If the religious validity of baptism is judged
weak in those who were first initiated because those from whom it is received
are thought sinners, it will be necessary to renew the sacrament delivered as
often as the administrator of the baptism conferred is found unworthy, and our
faith will depend not upon the choice of our will or the grace of the divine
gift, but upon the merits of priests and the quality of clerics.” And later:
“It is always God’s grace and God’s sacrament, but only a human ministry. If
the minister is good, he cleaves to God; if he is evil, God works through him
the visible form of the sacrament, but himself gives the invisible grace.”
What he says about the minister’s uprightness must also be understood of his
intention. Indeed, antiquity was so far removed from the papal dogma that
Alexander, bishop of Alexandria, wished the baptism administered by the boy
Athanasius to other boys to be held valid. Athanasius had administered it in
playful imitation of what he saw done daily in the church, and Alexander’s
sole reason was that the external rite had been administered according to the
church’s order, as Sozomen reports in book 2, chapter 16, and Nicephorus in
book 8, chapter 40. I do not now say how rightly this was decided, since the
playful administration of baptism cannot be regarded as lawful or as performed
by a lawfully called minister. I only infer from it that they did not believe
intention to be necessary.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XII. Although ministers administering the sacraments are instruments that use
reason, it does not follow that an intention related to the effect in question
is required in them. (1) By the same reasoning it would be proved that the
minister’s preaching without his intention is useless to the hearer, because
ministers are instruments using reason no less in preaching the gospel than
in administering the sacraments, which the adversaries nevertheless do not
maintain.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 308 | IA leaf 342 | PDF p. 342]

(2) Servants are their masters’ rational instruments, yet a master does not
consider with what mind and intention they execute his commands, provided it
is evident that his commands are truly fulfilled. (3) The analogy sought from
civil judges appointed by a king is dissimilar. A king confers a certain
dominion with respect to the actions they must perform, but ministers are only
stewards of those things whose dominion belongs to Christ alone, who is always
most closely joined to the church by his grace and Spirit. Therefore, when
the minister’s intention is lacking, the intention of Christ the Lord is not
lacking, for Christ himself is present through the minister to baptize and
confer the Eucharist. Thus, just as a servant’s negligence or hatred does not
prejudice the Lord’s liberality, neither does the corrupt intention of a
minister administering according to the institution render Christ’s promise
void.

XIII. The words of the sacraments are not practical so as to effect the thing
itself, but only to signify and seal it in lawful use, which is accomplished
even if they are pronounced without the minister’s intention. Acting persons
must be distinguished. Some are principal, acting and speaking from
themselves, and then an intention related to executing what is commanded is
required. Others are less principal and speak from another, as when a herald
or interpreters announce a ruler’s commands. The words have their force from
the ruler even without the intention of the instrumental cause. Intention is
required in the primary cause, not in instruments. Thus the practical force of
the sacraments depends not upon the intention of the one speaking, but upon
the intention of the one who instituted them.

XIV. Intention makes a person a minister so that he wills to exercise the
ministry, but it does not always make him exercise it sincerely and for the
end that he ought to regard. Thus the minister is required to will to perform
the external sacramental acts according to Christ’s prescription. Yet for the
sacrament to be valid, the further intention of doing what either the universal
or a particular church does is not required.

XV. Although the minister’s intention is not necessary for the integrity of a
sacrament, it does not follow that a father washing his son in a bath while
adding the words, “I wash you in the name of God the Father, etc.,” or a priest
at the table reading the Gospel and reciting the words, “This is my body,”
effects a true sacrament. Such a baptism and Eucharist are not true sacraments,
not because the administrators’ operative intention is lacking, but because
Christ’s institution is not observed. According to it, the sacraments must be
administered solemnly and with due reverence by a lawfully called minister in
a public assembly.
