CHAPTER V. On the certainty of a sentence pronounced by a council.

The fifth question is whether a general council confirmed by the Pope can err,
either in explaining the faith or in handing down precepts of morals common to
the whole Church. The Pontificians deny this; we affirm it. Book 2.

*In this position of the Pontificians, however, nothing is really ascribed to
the council; rather, all that is asserted tends to exalt the Pope. For they do
not say that a council—even one convened by the Pope and held with his legates
presiding—cannot err. They say instead that the error of a council cannot be
confirmed by the Pope. Yet according to their position the same thing can be
affirmed of a diocesan council, as Bellarmine shows in chapter 5, or of the
advice of any single Doctor.*

Bellarmine’s arguments.

THESIS I.

1. Bellarmine. *Matthew 18: Where two or three are gathered in my name, there
I am in the midst of them.*

Protestants. These words contain nothing peculiar to those who frame canons
about faith or morals common to the whole Church; nothing about decrees
confirmed by the Pope; and, finally, nothing about any privilege or
prerogative of a general council above a parochial assembly, or even a smaller
one. *He did not say “if all”* (as Cajetan observes on verse 19), *lest it be
understood only of the universal Church. He did not say “if many,” lest the
statement seem restricted to the favor of large churches. But he said “if
two,” so that it might be explained how great the power is of even the smallest
number of believers.*

2. Bellarmine. *Whether few or many, whether private persons or bishops, those
gathered in Christ’s name obtain what it is fitting for them to obtain: the
former in small and private matters, but bishops in great and public matters.*

Protestants. I. Nothing has yet been found about the infallibility of a general
council, unless assemblies of a few private persons are also immune from every
error; for Bellarmine here expressly concedes that this promise pertains to
them. II. Less than nothing is said about the infallibility of a council
depending upon the Pope’s confirmation. III. If those gathered in Christ’s
name obtain only what it is fitting for them to obtain, then a council does not
obtain infallibility of judgment; for this is not fitting for men unless they
are immediately inspired or moved by the Holy Spirit. IV. Something is said
to be *fitting* for believers either from an absolute necessity of the means to
salvation, or from the necessity of a command imposing this or that duty upon
them. The infallibility of this or that council cannot be reckoned of the
former kind; and Bellarmine teaches that not all and every matter of the latter
order

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n237 | printed p. 20]

is always granted either to councils or to the Pope himself when ruling from
the Chair, since he admits that the Pope can err in particular questions,
especially those concerning fact.

3. Bellarmine. *John 16: The Spirit of truth will teach you all truth. This is
understood of the successors of the Apostles in perpetuity, but not when they
exist separately; therefore at least of all when gathered into one. Moreover,
the Church’s Chair, through which God teaches us, is especially the Chair of
the Supreme Pontiff joined with the consent of a general council.*

Protestants. I. This passage does not speak of teaching the Church from any
external chair, much less from the Papal Chair. II. Nothing greater or more
certain is promised concerning teaching than concerning the other acts *of
leading, directing, and strengthening* (says Cajetan), *concerning inward
leading, which signifies more than teaching. For teaching terminates in the
understanding, but leading into all truth extends to all the powers of the
soul—memory, will, and affection—together with the capacities that serve
them.* Therefore bishops are no more promised here that they will be immune
from error of judgment than from error of life and conduct. III. Although
promises of this kind, given to the Apostles, pertain to their successors as
regards their substance, they did not pertain to the Apostles and their
successors in the same manner and degree. IV. This promise was equally valid
for the Apostles when they were apart and when they were together. Therefore
nothing peculiar to councils can be concluded from it. V. Even if it were
understood of councils and of all truth without exception, it still would not
follow that this always happens, and in every assembly, however legitimate.
VI. The Spirit taught and led the Church for three hundred years without that
chair. VII. The Pope’s Chair is brought in here all too beggarly, as if through
the back door, without any semblance of justification.

4. Bellarmine. *Acts 15: It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us. All
legitimate councils which received their pattern from that first council can
assert the same. For the presence of the Holy Spirit is no less necessary at
other times than it was then.*

Protestants. I. That council in Acts 15 had something more august in itself
than the Council of Trent, or even than any legitimate and general council
whatever. The Apostles, who were moved by the Holy Spirit, could pronounce
what pleased him far more certainly than ordinary bishops can. It did not seem
good to the Holy Spirit because it so seemed to the Apostles; rather, it seemed
good to the Apostles because it seemed good to the Holy Spirit, by whose
illumination they were taught. Let councils show us, by the proofs through
which this was evident in the Apostles, that their decrees proceed from the
Holy Spirit, and this question will be settled. II. That assembly of the
Apostles was as much a pattern for subsequent councils as Apostolic preaching
is a pattern for ordinary preaching,

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n238 | printed p. 21]

which, namely, we ought to follow but cannot equal. III. The infallible
assistance of the Holy Spirit is less necessary for other, more recent
councils, because the Church has now been instructed more abundantly by the
infallible writings of the Apostles. Nevertheless, judgment about matters of
this kind ought not to be rendered from what seems necessary to men, but rather
from the revealed will of God.

5. Bellarmine. *The Church cannot err, either in believing or in teaching;
therefore neither can a general council, because the Church’s whole authority
is formally in the prelates, and the whole Church is represented by a
council.*

Protestants. I. This inference is not necessary unless delegates of each and
every particular church are present at the council, and unless all of them
also bring the judgments of their churches and follow them in every respect,
without subtraction or addition. Bellarmine, book 1, chapter 17, admits that
no general council of this kind has ever been held, nor is one to be expected
hereafter. II. The immunity from error which the Church enjoys does not arise
from the Church’s authority, nor does it bring authority to her; rather, it
depends upon Christ’s authority and brings consolation to the Church. III. The
power of believing and teaching rightly is not formally in all prelates, for
then they would all be true believers and faithful pastors. IV. The Church’s
whole authority is not in the prelates, as is evident from the authority which
the Church exercises in appointing or removing prelates. V. Not every
representation of the Church causes a council to possess the same certainty
of direction as the Church herself. The Pope’s legates represent him, yet they
do not possess that power of judging infallibly which the Pope is said to
possess, because it cannot be subdelegated to others. *Báñez, on II-II,
question 1, article 10, page 60.* Nor in truth can the Church delegate to
bishops the judgment of her faith. VI. The decrees of councils are framed by
the majority; error can therefore be in them even though neither the whole
Church nor the whole council together errs. VII. The whole militant Church
herself can err in matters which are not necessary to salvation by a necessity
of the means.

6. Bellarmine. *The Roman Pontiff cannot err; therefore neither can a council
confirmed by him.*

Protestants. I. This begs the question; it does not prove it. II. Nothing
peculiar can be concluded from the Pope to a general council any more than to
a provincial, diocesan, or even parochial council confirmed by him.

7. Bellarmine. *We are commanded to follow bishops as governors, pastors,
teachers, watchmen, and fathers; therefore at least when all are together they
cannot deceive us by erring.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n239 | printed p. 22]

Protestants. Not all who are called bishops are—even with respect to their
office—of the sort whom God commands us to follow. Nor are the very men whom
God commends as guides to be followed absolutely in all things, but only
insofar as they carry before us the torch of God’s word, which is the one pure
and perfect lamp for our feet and light for our path, Psalm 119:105. When they
lead by this light they are to be followed even without a council, just as the
primitive Church followed its pastors for three hundred years. But the
Pontificians have that custom which Canus severely censures in book 12,
chapter 4: *It is the custom of Saracens, pagans, and heretics to embrace the
dogmas of their blind and rash sect without judgment, and to receive without
any investigation the very things which most require investigation. Believe
me, these people are not educated but constrained; they are not taught by a
way and method, but are driven into madness as if by sorceries and charms.*

8. Bellarmine. *The Fathers affirm: 1. that the sentence of a general council
is the Church’s final judgment in a cause of faith; 2. that all who do not
acquiesce in plenary councils are heretics and are to be excommunicated; 3.
that the decrees of legitimate general councils are divine and issued by the
Holy Spirit; 4. that one ought to die rather than depart from the decrees of
general councils.*

Protestants. I. In all these assertions there is no mention of that distinction
between decrees common to the whole Church and particular decrees, or between
those which concern law and others which concern fact. Yet if this distinction
is removed, Bellarmine himself will not defend these assertions. II. Not one
word—not even an obscure intimation—is offered by any of the Fathers cited by
Bellarmine that the authority of councils depends upon the confirmation of the
Roman Pontiff; yet from this confirmation he makes the whole infallibility of
councils depend. III. The Church’s final judgment is not free from error.
Appeal is afterward made to the judgment of God. IV. The Fathers regard one
who does not acquiesce in councils as a heretic, just as one who did not
acquiesce in the Samaritan woman’s speech, John 4:42, would in some manner have
had to be called a heretic—not because of her authority, but because of
Christ himself. V. For the same reason, and no other, the principal decrees of
godly councils were divine and to be confirmed even at the peril of death, as
the words of that woman were divine and to be held most firmly. Meanwhile, we
do not deny that the force of the testimony of an ecclesiastical council is
greater than that of a private person, yet the difference is not properly in
the persons themselves, but in their knowledge and truthfulness, which are
presumed to be much greater in a council than in a private man.

9. Bellarmine. *If general councils could err, there would be no firm judgment
in the Church; condemned heresies could be called back into doubt; some books
of Scripture would have to be doubted; and all councils would also have
admitted intolerable error when they issued new creeds or new propositions as
matters of faith.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n240 | printed p. 23]

Protestants. These consequences are nothing. For I. the firm judgment is that
of Christ and the Holy Spirit in Scripture. II. By that judgment all heresies
which ought to be condemned are condemned. III. The books of Scripture are
received not because of councils, but because of themselves. IV. A dreadful
injury is inflicted upon godly councils when they are accused of impiety in
this fashion, as though they had issued new articles of faith.

10. Protestants. All the priests of the Old Testament are often accused not
only of wickedness but also of ignorance and error, Isaiah 56; Jeremiah 6 and
14; Ezekiel 22; Hosea 9; and so forth. Therefore councils composed of such men
can err.

Bellarmine 1. *All are reproved because of some bad men.*

Protestants. Not because of a few, but because of many—that is, nearly all,
if not simply all. A council, however, is said to err when the majority errs in
making a determination, although a few may dissent.

Bellarmine 2. *These things are said against priests when dispersed, not when
gathered in council; for all are sent to a council of the priests,
Deuteronomy 17.*

Protestants. Gathering does not remove error and wickedness; it unites them and
thereby makes them stronger. Thus *companies of priests* are also accused,
Hosea 6:9; their full assembly is charged with error, 2 Samuel 6:3; and even
with criminal wickedness, Jeremiah 26:7–9. Yet the Jews were sent to them by
Moses even outside a general council, just as they were sent to the same men
by Christ, while at the same time being warned to beware of the leaven of their
doctrine, Matthew 16:6, 12.

Bellarmine 3. *The Church of the New Testament has surer and greater promises
than that of the Old Testament.*

Protestants. With respect to immunity from error in judging, the Old Testament
Church in some manner had greater promises and privileges while it had a
priest with the *Urim and Thummim*, especially if Bellarmine’s interpretation
is admitted, by which he concludes, *On the Pontiff, book 4, chapter 3, that by
sign and word the Lord promised that doctrine and truth would dwell in the
breast of the high priest, and therefore that he would not err when he taught
the people.* By no sign and no word is this promised to any bishop of the New
Testament.

11. Protestants. It was also foretold that there would be false teachers in
the New Testament Church, just as there were false prophets of old, 2 Peter 2;
Acts 20; and so forth.

Bellarmine. *These things are not understood of councils; indeed, councils are
gathered against such false men.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n241 | printed p. 24]

Protestants. I. They are understood of bishops, of whom councils consist. II.
Bellarmine himself bears witness, book 1, chapter 6, that various councils,
even general ones, have consisted of false teachers of this kind. III. The
Roman Pontiff, to whom Bellarmine ascribes all authority in convening,
directing, approving, or rejecting councils, can be a false teacher, by the
confession of the most learned among the Papists. Nothing therefore prevents
him, just like Caiaphas, from gathering his men against the Lord and his
Christ, Acts 4:26–27.

12. Protestants. The council of the Jews, presided over by the high priest
Caiaphas, erred shamefully and ruinously in rejecting and condemning Christ.

Bellarmine. *The Pontiffs and councils of the Jews could not err before Christ
came, but they could when he was present. For just as it is not necessary that
the Pope’s vicar cannot err when the Pope himself governs the Church and
defends her from error, so also it was not necessary that the Jewish Pontiffs
could not err when Christ, the supreme Pontiff of the whole Church, was
present and administered the Church by himself.*

Protestants. I. It is well indeed that Bellarmine still had enough shame not to
dare make his own that detestable response of Hosius, Peter de Soto, Melchior
Canus, and Harding, by which they defend as right, pious, and proceeding from
the Holy Spirit that very sentence of the Jewish council by which Christ our
Lord was condemned. For Harding, drawing from Hosius and Peter de Soto, says
this in Jewel, *Apology*, chapter 6, division 3: *Some acts of that council
were impious and criminal, but its sentence was true and good.* Canus likewise
says in *Theological Places*, book 5, final chapter: *Some acts of the ancient
priests were indeed contrary to Christ, but the sentence of otherwise very
wicked men was not only most true, but also most useful to the commonwealth.
From this it follows that the life and works of our Pontiffs may perhaps be
contrary to the Lord Jesus, but their judgments—namely, those approved by the
Supreme Pontiff—come out true and useful to Christians.* Bellarmine, however,
was in some measure ashamed of this so infamous and monstrous position. Yet he
shows clearly enough that he departed from them and others of the same stamp
only under compulsion and fled to this new invention, and that he did so very
timidly and hesitantly: *Therefore* (since, that is, he could not sustain the
other answers) *perhaps one must answer that they could err; it was not
necessary that they could not err.* II. What Bellarmine asserts here—that
Christ’s presence was the cause of the Jews’ ability to err—is utterly false.
For Christ himself testifies that they had often committed precisely the same
error before, in condemning, killing, and slaughtering God’s holy servants,
Matthew 21:34–35,

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n242 | printed p. 25]

36, and so forth. Stephen, Christ’s first martyr, left the same attested by his
own blood, Acts 7:51–53. Christ also expressly testifies, Matthew 5, 15, and
23, that they had long been blind guides and authors of various errors. III.
This too is false: that Christ administered the Jewish Church visibly by
himself as her high priest. IV. Even if this had been true, every consideration
would nevertheless require that the Jewish Pontiffs—who had always previously
been immune from error so that they might direct men to Christ—not lose this
privilege through Christ’s presence before they had pronounced by their
infallible judgment that Christ had now come, that Jesus was he, and therefore
that henceforth a certain judgment was to be expected from him alone and not
from themselves. It certainly belonged to divine providence to make known to
all when the Jewish Pontiffs ceased to possess a certain and infallible
judgment concerning the faith. V. If the scribes and Pharisees had suffered
such a diminution of headship and authority through his presence, Christ
himself would not have sent men to them as still sitting in Moses’ chair,
Matthew 23:2—especially if Bellarmine’s argument in chapter 2 is true, *Those
whom God commands us to hear cannot deceive us,* or if what Bellarmine affirms
in *On the Word of God*, book 3, chapter 5, is sound: *That by these words
Christ wished to teach that the Pontiffs and priests of that time were judges
of all controversies, that they were to be believed, and that their doctrine
was to be followed by all.* VI. The comparison of the Pope and his vicar with
Christ and the Pontiff of the Jews is plainly futile. For all admit that the
Pope’s vicar can always err. The Pope’s presence is therefore not the cause of
his ability to err, although in reality it is the cause of manifold and deadly
errors for an infinite number of men; and those from whom the Pope is farthest
removed err less. It would be pointless to dispute incidentally with Bellarmine
about particular errors of councils approved by the Roman Pontiff. For that is
what is at issue in nearly every individual controversy, and one should not
expect a man easily to acknowledge an error approved by the Roman Pontiff when
he openly professes that, *if the Pope erred by commanding vices or forbidding
virtues, he would be bound to believe that the vices were good and the virtues
evil. Bellarmine, On the Roman Pontiff, book 4, chapter 5.*

13. Protestants. In every assembly of a multitude it often happens that the
larger part defeats the better. Therefore councils, in which decrees are made
according to the votes of the majority, can err.

Bellarmine. *This happens in human assemblies, but in a council of the Church,
where the Holy Spirit presides and Christ is in their midst, the larger part
never defeats the better.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n243 | printed p. 26]

Protestants. I. Bellarmine himself has just confessed that this happened in a
council of the Jewish Church. II. A council of the Church has no privilege
above other human assemblies except by virtue of a promise made to it; but no
promise exists by which councils are secured against every error. III. If the
Holy Spirit’s presidency and Christ’s presence in the councils themselves are
the reason councils cannot err, then they cannot err before the Pope’s
confirmation or approval, because these are present before it, not only for
the first time afterward. Indeed, they cannot always be present in the council
at that later point, because the Pope’s approval very often used to follow the
dissolution of councils. IV. Bellarmine must teach us where the Holy Spirit
resides. He does not always reside in the larger part of a council, since, by
Bellarmine’s admission, they can resist the truth. Indeed, Bellarmine will say
that the Spirit is present in no one individual member of a council so that
the member cannot err. How, then, or by what reasoning can the Spirit be found
in the whole? Christ’s presence admits of various degrees; therefore the
inference from any degree of his presence to infallibility of judgment is not
valid.

14. Protestants. It is not certain by divine faith that this or that council
was legitimate and legitimately decreed this or that; therefore the decrees of
councils cannot produce a firm, infallible, and divine faith.

Bellarmine. *That this or that council is legitimate is evident to those
present; to those absent or to posterity, however, the same thing is known by
human faith, but a most certain one.*

Protestants. I. Various Pontificians deny that the very evidence of some
particular fact can make the assent depending upon it an assent of divine
faith. Thus Báñez, on II-II, question 1, article 10, page 152: *It is not
certain, nor a matter of faith, that a proposition which follows from one
proposition of faith and another known by the natural light is itself a matter
of faith. Indeed, it is certain that it is not a matter of faith and is not
even a theological conclusion, as some think,* and so forth. II. Although we
freely concede that point, we nevertheless deny that it is evident to those
present at this or that council that the council is legitimate—that is, that
those conditions concur in it which the Pontificians require for a legitimate
council: namely, that it consist of legitimate bishops, and that it be
convened, directed, and confirmed by a legitimate Pontiff. For this cannot be
discerned by them either by the natural light or by sense. III. Neither those
absent nor those present can know these things by that most certain human faith
which could in some manner be compared with natural evidence, because few or
none are competent witnesses of these matters.

Bellarmine. *That Cicero was consul, that Julius Caesar was dictator, and so
forth,*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n244 | printed p. 27]

*depend upon human faith and yet are so certain that they seem to possess a
kind of evidence.*

Protestants. Certain facts of this sort can be certain, but their
circumstances—especially those further removed from the senses—cannot possess
evidence from human testimonies. That Cicero was consul and Caesar dictator
seems evident, but concerning the legitimacy of their election according to
Roman law, nothing is held so certain that it cannot be called into doubt. So
it is sufficiently well known that this or that council—for example, the
Council of Trent—was held. But that it was legitimately convened, that the
bishops who appeared there were legitimate, and that they legitimately made
the decrees there—these matters are not so evident.

Bellarmine. *See, if you please, Melchior Canus on this matter, On Theological
Places, book 6, final chapter, response 10.*

Protestants. I. We have read Canus and observed that he proves nothing which
serves Bellarmine’s cause, and that he establishes no other certainty about a
legitimately held council than he does about the priest’s intention in
consecrating the sacrament. II. Azor read Canus and thus pronounces upon him in
*Moral Institutes*, part 2, book 5, chapter 5: *Canus appears to teach that it
is not certain by divine faith that this or that man is the Supreme Pontiff.
For although that which is inferred by sound reasoning from one proposition
certain by divine faith and another certain by the evident natural light is
believed by divine faith, this is not so for that which is inferred from one
proposition believed by divine faith and another known only by human faith.*
Therefore, even if we were to concede as true by divine faith the proposition,
*A council legitimately gathered in Christ’s name cannot err,* nevertheless,
because the assumption, *The Council of Trent was legitimately gathered in
Christ’s name,* rests only upon that human faith which can admit falsehood, the
conclusion, *The Council of Trent did not err,* cannot be certain by divine
faith, nor can any decrees of such a council, whose credibility depends upon
that conclusion. Azor pronounces still more clearly in chapter 12 of the same
book: *Canus says that the Councils of Nicaea, Chalcedon, and Ephesus were
legitimately assembled is not believed from divine revelation, though it is
believed from human faith,* and so forth. Báñez says precisely the same in his
commentary on II-II, question 1, article 10, doubt 2, response 2. From this
hypothesis Azor concludes: *If we believe only by human faith that these
councils were duly held and confirmed, I do not see how their decrees in
causes of faith ought to be believed by divine faith. For the argument against
Canus has great force: a conclusion depends upon its premises and therefore
cannot be more certain than the premises. Therefore, if one of the premises is
certain only by human faith, the conclusion cannot be certain by divine
faith.* Nor does the plaster which Azor applies to this wound help: *By the very
fact that we believe by divine faith—*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n245 | printed p. 28]

*—that the definitions of the Council of Nicaea are true, we also believe by
divine faith that it was a legitimately gathered council.* For the very fact
that it was legitimately gathered is the reason why we believe its definitions
to be true, and therefore it must be believed before the definitions
themselves.
