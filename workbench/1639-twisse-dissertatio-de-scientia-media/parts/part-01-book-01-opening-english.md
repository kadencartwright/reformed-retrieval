For the resolution of this difficulty, I thought that two distinctions must
necessarily be set down first. The first proposition—that future contingents are
present to GOD from eternity according to their real existences—can be understood
in two ways.

1. In one way, that future contingents themselves, according to their real
existences, coexist with GOD from eternity, in eternity itself.

2. In another way, that although future contingents themselves do not coexist
with GOD from eternity according to their real existences, nevertheless the gaze
of the divine eye, ranging immovably over every difference of time and over all
that occurs in any difference of time, reaches from eternity not only the future
being of contingent things, but the very present being of their actual existence,
which they will have when they have actually been produced in the nature of
things.

It will be worthwhile to discuss these points, both because the device appears to
be new and because, when the other ways of asserting middle knowledge have been
driven out, Penottus retreats to this subtlety as to a sacred anchor. For in
chapter 14, no. 4, he acknowledges that propositions concerning a contingent
future do not contain determinate truth by reason of that future being which
contingents have in their causes, nor insofar as they are formally about the
future, but by reason of the present being which a future contingent will have in
itself outside its causes. He also adds: “That the ground of this certainty must
be taken from the infallibility of the divine intellect and light and from divine
knowledge—no Doctor ever doubted this.”

“But in this alone,” he says, “lies the difficulty: granted an infinite intellect,
infinite light, and infinite divine knowledge, nevertheless, if a future
contingent is not of itself intelligible, such infinity is not sufficient for
understanding it, just as the infinity of divine power is not sufficient to make
what is not possible to be made.”

These words have the force of a true refutation, provided that they are rightly
understood of future contingents considered materially—that is, of things
considered merely as capable of occurring or not occurring, as all contingents
are by their nature. For in this way they are not intelligible as future any more
than as not future. They are neither determinately future nor determinately not
future, and consequently they are not determinately true in the category of the
future, but only in the category of the possible; and only in that way are they
intelligible. Hence, when Penottus philosophizes about a future contingent, it is
as though he were speaking about a contingent that is not future. For the thing
he calls a future contingent is no more future than not future. It is by its
nature indifferent to futurition or non-futurition, since it is merely possible;
and it is considered in precisely this way by the Novarian in this passage.

But if it is granted that contingents of this kind are future, however
contingently—for not everything future is future necessarily—then, in whatever
way they are future, provided that they are future, they possess determinate truth
and are consequently intelligible. Unless I am mistaken, however, Penottus does
not acknowledge that something can really be future and yet be future only
contingently. For whatever is really future is by that very fact knowable as
future, and is known infallibly by an infinite intellect. Penottus appears by no
means to admit this. Therefore, to establish an infallible divine intuition of
future things, he has recourse to their actual presence as the sole foundation of
the certainty of divine cognition in this respect. We shall have to inquire into
this point at greater length.

Until now we have been accustomed to object against the defenders of middle
knowledge that they gratuitously suppose contingent things to be future while
giving no cause by which they pass from the condition of possible things into
the condition of future things. We will readily grant that the divine intellect,
insofar as it is infinite, thereby comprehends everything intelligible, whether
in the category of the possible or in the category of the future—provided that
the things are possible, and provided that they are future. But what is merely
possible and not future, although it can be understood as possible, nevertheless
