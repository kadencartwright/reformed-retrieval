QUESTION VIII.

THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN THE OLD AND NEW COVENANTS.

*How do the Old and New Covenants differ from each other? Essentially, with
respect to the substance of doctrine, or accidentally, with respect to the
mode of dispensation? A distinction is necessary.*

I. Just as the meaning of Old and New Testament is manifold, so the proposed
question must be answered in different ways. The varying opinions of the
orthodox on both sides can also be reconciled here.

II. Sometimes the Old and New Testaments are considered with respect to
*writing*. By this name are designated the prophetic and apostolic writings
delivered to the church by men of God either before or after Christ was
exhibited (2 Cor. 3:14). In this sense the New Testament is said to be *veiled
in the Old and the Old Testament revealed in the New*. But the question now
does not concern them under that σχέσει, *relation*, but with respect to the
*doctrine* delivered.

[Marginal heading: How the Old Covenant is taken broadly.]

III. In this sense the Old Covenant is chiefly taken in two ways, either
broadly or strictly. *Broadly*, it denotes in general the whole dispensation
under which the fathers lived from the beginning of the world

[Printed p. 207 | Internet Archive PDF 235 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

until Christ. It contained the doctrine of grace delivered to the ancients,
which promised salvation and life to the people—openly, indeed, under the
condition of perfect obedience to be rendered to the moral law and the threat
of death against transgressors, together with the intolerable burden of
ceremonies and the yoke of the most constricted Mosaic polity, but covertly
under the condition of repentance and faith in the coming Messiah. In this
respect the Old Testament chiefly embraces three things: 1. The old doctrine,
which was partly legal and partly evangelical. 2. The old, servile,
laborious, and shadowy form of ecclesiastical worship and ministry. 3. The old
form of external polity, bound to one people and place.

[Marginal heading: Strictly.]

IV. *Strictly*, however, it designates the covenant of works, or the moral law
given through Moses, with the ἀβαστάκτῳ, *unbearable*, burden of the legal
ceremonies added, considered precisely and separately from the promise of
grace. The former meaning is proper and intrinsic if the Lawgiver’s aim and
intention are considered, because in that first administration he joined
these three things together. In giving the old covenant, or legal
dispensation, his purpose was not to abolish the promise but to lead to
Christ. The latter meaning is accessory and accidental, arising from
ignorance of the true end and the invention of a false one.

The true end was Christ for righteousness to everyone who believes (Rom.
10:4). But the self-righteous Jews did not attain this end because it was
proposed under a veil (2 Cor. 3:14), that is, under a covering of types and
figures, since the promise of grace on account of Christ was clothed in legal
rites. Hence they contrived a false end, maintaining that the law was given
so that through observing it they might be justified before God and saved
(Rom. 10:3, 4, 5). The apostle argues everywhere against this error, under
that supposition taking the law strictly and opposing it to the promise.

[Marginal heading: The New Covenant is taken either broadly or strictly.]

V. The *New Covenant* is also taken in two ways. *Broadly*, it is put for the
covenant of grace in general, made with sinners, which had a place under the
Old Testament both before Christ was exhibited and under the New after he was
now manifested. *Strictly*, it is the covenant of grace promulgated after the
manifestation of Christ in the flesh, which must endure to the end of the age.

VI. Those of our theologians who, like *Rollock, Piscator, Trelcatius*, and
others, make two covenants diverse in substance take the old covenant
strictly, not only separating the promise of grace from it but also opposing
that promise to it. Paul seems more than once to take it in this sense, as in
2 Cor. 3 and Gal. 4, so that the old covenant is the covenant of works but the
new is the evangelical covenant of faith. Those, however, who maintain only
one covenant, such as *Calvin, Martyr, and Ursinus*, take the word *covenant*
more broadly, so that it also embraces the promise of grace, though more
obscurely. And because that promise was dispensed differently before and
after Christ, they distinguish it into two, old and new—not by a division of
a genus into species, as do the former theologians, but by a division of a
subject according to its accidents, which the others do not deny. Thus the
disagreement lies only in the different use of terms and not in the thing
itself, as Calvin observes (Institutes 2.7.2).

[Marginal heading: The Lutheran opinion concerning the difference between the
Old and New Covenants.]

VII. The Lutherans also disagree with us in assigning the difference between
the Old and New Testaments. They raised the question concerning this topic on
the occasion of the sacramentarian controversy, when they insisted not only
that those two covenants are distinguished in substance but also that the
promise of grace is entirely excluded from the Old Covenant. They accuse our
theologians of erring in declaring the nature of the Old Covenant. In his
*System*, in the locus on the Old and New Testaments, 2.4, *Brochmann* says,
“With one voice the Lutheran theologians teach that, properly and in the
language of Scripture, the name Old Testament signifies that legal covenant
which God made with the Israelites through the ministry of Moses.” Second,
he adds that this also is in question between us: “Whether that

[Printed p. 208 | Internet Archive PDF 236 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

gratuitous covenant made with the believing fathers at the time of the first
fall is the same in substance as the New Testament?” He says that this is
asserted by us but denied by them.

VIII. We readily grant them that the Old Testament and Mosaic covenant,
*strictly* taken and considered precisely with respect to the legal σχέσιν,
*relation*, differs in its whole substance from the New. Hence John opposes
law to grace and Moses to Christ (John 1:17), and Paul opposes letter to
Spirit and the ministry of condemnation to that of righteousness (2 Cor.
3:6, 7). Yet we insist that when taken *broadly* and joined with the other,
evangelical σχέσει, *relation*—that is, clothed in the pedagogical
circumstances of the Mosaic administration—it did not differ in substance,
but only in the mode of dispensation.

IX. They gratuitously suppose that the Old Mosaic Covenant is so restricted
to the moral law and its ceremonies and polity that the promise of grace is
contained under it neither openly nor covertly. The contrary is evident from
various considerations. 1. From the *Author of the covenant himself*. He was
the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, that is, God as joined with the people
through the Abrahamic covenant and, indeed, acting from the gratuitous
adoption and love of the people (Deut. 4:6, 7, 8; 7:6, 7, 8), which belongs
not to the legal but to the evangelical covenant.

2. From its *threefold matter*: first, the *kind of doctrine*, which was legal
in the moral precepts in such a way that it was also evangelical in the
promises concerning Christ and his grace; then, the *form of worship*, which
was wholly shadowy and typical and referred to Christ; and then, the *mode of
polity*, which was subordinated to both doctrine and worship insofar as it had
to distinguish the people from whom Christ was to arise and among whom he was
to preach and suffer.

3. From its *twofold form*. The *external* form was a legal obligation, both
of the people to keep the law and of God to give life if the law was kept.
The *internal* form lay in the gratuitous promise from which God was to be God
to the people and the people in turn were to be a people to him (Lev. 26:11,
12; 2 Cor. 6:16).

4. From its *effects*, which are three: 1. The preparation of the sinner to
seek Christ through the moral law (Rom. 10:4; Gal. 3:24). 2. The showing of
Christ through the ceremonial law (Heb. 10:12; Col. 2:16, 17). 3. The
offering of the same Christ through the promise of grace joined to each.

X. They cannot deny this if they wish to be consistent with themselves. For,
as Brochmann confesses in the same place, they acknowledge that before Christ
was exhibited in the flesh, the doctrine concerning Christ and gratuitous
reconciliation through Christ already flourished in the church of God and
that the fathers of the Old Testament were saved in no other way than by the
grace of Christ alone. Nor do they deny that each covenant, under the Old and
the New, treated of Christ as θεανθρώπῳ, *God-man*. But they maintain that it
treated of him in a different mode. Once this is granted, who does not see
that the difference lies only in the mode of dispensation, not in the
substance of the doctrine itself?

XI. The author adds in vain in the same place, “The difference between the
ancient covenant of grace and the New Testament is as great as that between a
promise and the performance of what was promised, between signification and
exhibition, between the figure and the body itself, and between an image and
the thing designated by the image. The Old exhibited Christ only in the
promise and in various types, while the New exhibits the body itself and the
thing promised to the fathers and foreshadowed by the types.” For although we
grant that this applies with respect to the fulfillment of the promise, which
regarded the exhibition of Christ in the flesh, the actual expiation of sin,
and the acquisition of salvation, which had to occur only under the New
Testament, we deny that it can be said with respect to the application of
salvation, participation in Christ’s benefits, and the fruit of his death and
satisfaction, which were not denied to the fathers. In this sense they are
rightly said to have possessed the thing signified and promised, the body
itself, and the living image of the

[Printed p. 209 | Internet Archive PDF 237 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

legal shadows—not by the power of the law, but of the promise. Brochmann freely
acknowledges this himself when he says, “The fathers did not remain fixed in
these signs, but lifted their heart to the thing foreshadowed by the signs, as
did Abraham, who saw the day of Christ (John 8:56), and Moses, who preferred
the reproach of Christ to the riches of Egypt (Heb. 11:26).”

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XII. It is one thing for the Old Covenant strictly taken to differ
essentially from the New, which the apostle intends in Gal. 4:24 in the
allegory of Sarah and Hagar—the former begetting to liberty and the latter to
servitude—where he argues against the false apostles who confused law and
gospel. The antithesis of “letter and Spirit, ministry of condemnation and of
righteousness” in 2 Cor. 3:6, 7 also concerns this. But it is another thing
for the covenant broadly considered with respect to the administration to be
opposed in substance, which we deny.

XIII. What is to be made old does not immediately differ essentially from
what remains if the making old concerns only the mode and circumstances of
dispensation, not the thing itself. Even the Old Testament broadly taken had
to be made old with respect to its evangelical σχέσιν, *relation*, not with
respect to what was essential to it, since *Christ is the same “yesterday,
today, and forever”* (Heb. 13:8), but with respect to the circumstances and
mode of dispensation, which were more obscure and burdensome on account of
the church’s infant age. To this mode also belongs the carnal worship which
obtained in the Old Testament and had to be abrogated.

XIV. What was sanctioned only by the blood of animals differs essentially
from what was sanctioned by Christ’s blood. But although the Old Testament is
said to have been sanctioned by the blood of animals (Exod. 24:3, 4, and so
forth; Heb. 9:18, 19), this must be understood only with respect to the
letter. With respect to the mystical sense it was also sanctioned by the
blood of Christ, since Christ’s true blood was foreshadowed by that typical
blood (Matt. 26:28), and the ancients could be saved only by its efficacy and
merit.

XV. The opposition between the Old and New Testaments is not in all respects
the same as that between law and gospel. For the *opposition of law and
gospel*, insofar as they are properly and strictly taken for the covenants of
works and grace and are considered in their absolute being, is *contrary*.
They are opposed as the letter that kills and the Spirit that gives life, as
Hagar begetting to servitude and Sarah begetting to liberty, although the law
taken more broadly and in its relative being is subordinated to the gospel.
But the opposition of the Old and New Testaments broadly considered is
*relative*, insofar as the Old contained shadows of future things (Heb. 10:1),
but the New τὴν εἰκόνα, *the image*.

XVI. Those things do not agree in substance whose sacraments in one case are
both significative and exhibitive and in the other only significative. But
the sacraments in the Old Testament were not merely significative; in their
own mode they also exhibited the thing signified. In this sense
*circumcision* is called the *seal of the righteousness of faith* to Abraham
(Rom. 4:13), and the fathers are said to have drunk Christ (1 Cor. 10:4). It
is no objection that Christ had not yet been incarnate and therefore could
not have been exhibited. For although he did not yet exist *physically*
through the incarnation, he nevertheless existed *morally* in the promise
through faith’s reception, which is τῶν ἐλπιζομένων ὑπόστασις, *the substance
of things hoped for*.

XVII. Moses and Christ can be compared with each other in three ways. 1.
*Relatively*, as type and prototype, because Moses prefigured Christ in
various ways and therefore foretold that a Prophet like himself would arise
(Deut. 18:15). 2. *Comparatively*, as the lesser is compared with the greater
and a servant with the Lord (Heb. 3:4, 5, 6). 3. *Disparately and
oppositely*, insofar as the law was given through Moses but grace and truth
through Jesus Christ (John 1:17). *Grace* is opposed to the curse of the moral
law, and *Truth* to the shadows and figures of the ceremonial law; or it is
called *true Grace* insofar as it is not veiled by typical and carnal figures.

[Marginal heading: The Reformed opinion concerning the difference between the
Old and New Covenants. They differ, 1. With respect to time;]

XVIII. The orthodox maintain that the difference between the Old and New
Testaments broadly considered is only accidental, not essential, concerning
the circumstances, mode, and degree of the thing, not the thing itself, which
was the same in each. This, already proved above, is shown further, 1. With
respect to *time*. The Old preceded Christ, but the New follows him. The
former regarded Christ as one to be exhibited and to come; the latter regards
Christ as now exhibited and manifested. Hence Christ pronounces the apostles’
eyes blessed because they see what the prophets and kings wished to see but
did not see (Luke 10:23, 24).

[Marginal heading: 2. With respect to clarity;]

XIX. 2. With respect to *clarity and obscurity*. In the New the mysteries are
proposed far more clearly, the veils and shadows of ceremonies and types
having been removed. Hence in 2 Cor. 3:18 the apostle says that we, with
ἀνακεκαλυμμένῳ προσώπῳ, *unveiled face*—in antithesis to Moses, who covered
his face with a veil in v. 7 as a symbol of legal obscurity—“κατοπτρίζεσθαι,
behold as in a mirror, the glory of the Lord” in the gospel. The Old Testament
was wholly symbolic and figurative, possessing a shadow of future things, not
their perfect image (Heb. 10:1). But the New is plain and open, in which grace
“ἐπεφάνη, has been clearly manifested,” to us (Titus 2:11). Thus the time of
the Old Testament is compared to night because the Sun of righteousness had
not yet risen, but the time of the New to day, in which the Sunrise from on
high has arisen.

To this belongs the fact that although the same heavenly inheritance and
promise of eternal life occur in each, they are nevertheless proposed
differently in the Old and New Testaments. In the New Testament they are
proposed clearly, openly, and nakedly without any coverings. But in the Old
they were wrapped in earthly types and pledges, among which, after the
covenant was made with Abraham, the inheritance of the land of Canaan was
preeminent. Reason itself dictates that it was typical, since the possession
of no earthly and finite good, however excellent, can satisfy and bless the
soul, but only enjoyment of the eternal and infinite God himself. Paul also
expressly testifies to this in Heb. 4.

[Marginal heading: 3. With respect to facility;]

XX. 3. With respect to *facility*. The administration and λατρεία, *worship*,
was far more laborious in the Old than in the New Testament, whose sacraments
are few in number, august in meaning, and very easy to observe. To this belong
these words of Augustine (*Against Faustus* 19.13): “The first sacraments,
which were observed from the law, foretold the Christ who was to come. When
Christ fulfilled them by his coming, they were taken away, and taken away
because they were fulfilled,” and so forth. “Other sacraments were instituted,
greater in power, better in usefulness, easier in performance, and fewer in
number, as the righteousness of faith was revealed and the children of God
were called into liberty, the yoke of servitude which suited a hard and
carnally minded people having been removed.”

For the signs added to the Word reflected the character of the Word. The Word
belonged not so much to the gospel as to ἐπαγγελίας, *promise*, not announcing
Christ as exhibited but foretelling that he was to be exhibited. Thus the
sacraments were types of future things, not memorials of past things as ours
are (1 Cor. 11:24), promissory notes rather than mere receipts, a remembrance
of sin rather than a remembrance of the sacrifice already performed for sins.

It is no valid objection that the blood of victims foreshadowed Christ’s death
far more clearly and easily than the symbols of bread and wine in the
Eucharist, and that the analogy between flesh and flesh is greater than that
between flesh and bread. For this signification must not be sought simply
from *physical analogy*, or with respect to the *physical being* of the
things, but chiefly with respect to their *moral being* and from *divine
institution*, which is far clearer in the New Testament than in the Old.
Previously the death of victims and the blood of the lamb were required to
prefigure Christ’s death, which was still future. But after it occurred in the
New Testament, it was not necessary—indeed, it ought no longer—to be
foreshadowed through

[Printed p. 211 | Internet Archive PDF 239 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the blood of victims as future. It was sufficient that it be commemorated in
its communion and application, which is better represented through the
symbols of bread and wine.

[Marginal heading: 4. With respect to pleasantness;]

XXI. 4. With respect to *pleasantness*. In the Old Testament the law and its
perfect obedience were urged more frequently, though the evangelical promises
were not simply excluded. The end was that by this rigorous demand for
obedience, the people, still infants, might be kept as if under guardianship
and compelled to seek Christ. But in the New the evangelical promises are
more frequent, though the law is not excluded as it drives believers to
render the new obedience by the grace of regeneration.

Nor should the hard burden of the cross, which Christ imposes on his own in
the New Testament, be opposed here. Besides the fact that it was not lacking
to the godly of the Old Testament, since this is the lot of believers in this
world, Christ’s disciples should regard it not so much as a burden as an honor
(Gal. 6:14), and Christ’s yoke is light and pleasant (Matt. 11:30). To this
belongs the gift of the Holy Spirit. Although he was not denied to the fathers
under the Old Testament, he then exercised his powers more sparingly and
moderately in keeping with that time and mode of revelation. But now, as the
revelation of grace is greater and more ample, he had to be communicated far
more richly and abundantly under the New, according to the oracle of Joel 2:28,
whose fulfillment at the first Pentecost Paul presses in Acts 2:17, 18.

[Marginal heading: 5. With respect to perfection;]

XXII. 5. With respect to *perfection*. Although the Old Testament possessed
essential perfection with respect to the substance of the covenant of grace,
it did not possess accidental perfection with respect to degree. That whole
administration was typical and shadowy, differing from the New no less than
σκία τῇ εἰκόνι, *a shadow differs from the image*: a faint foreshadowing and
rough outline from a living and exact image. Hence the apostle attributes
ἀσθένειαν, *weakness*, to it (Heb. 7:8, 9, 10) when he treats the imperfection
of the Levitical priesthood. Once it was abrogated, another had to be raised
up according to the order of Melchizedek, since perfection did not lie in the
former. The apostle proves its ἀνωφελὲς καὶ ἀσθενὲς, *uselessness and
weakness*, both from the high priest’s infirmity and mortality and the
defilement of sin from which he was not immune, and from the imperfection of
the sacrifices and washings. These did not reach consciences and souls, being
effective only for legal and external holiness; nor could the sacrifices in
themselves expiate sins before God (Ps. 40:7, 8).

Therefore, all these things, together with the tabernacle and worldly
sanctuary, had to be abrogated so that the way to the heavenly sanctuary might
lie open. This also applies with respect to the worship that prevailed under
the Old Testament, which was wholly shadowy and carnal if compared with the
worship preached by Christ and instituted by the apostles (John 4:23). The
latter is no longer attached to one place as before, but must be performed in
*Spirit* without carnal types (Heb. 9:10) and in *Truth* without Mosaic
shadows (Col. 2:17; Heb. 10:1).

[Marginal heading: 6. With respect to liberty;]

XXIII. 6. With respect to *liberty*. Under the Old Testament the Spirit of
servitude to fear prevailed (Rom. 8:15). For although the fathers were
children of God and on that account lords and heirs, they nevertheless did
not differ from *servants* with respect to the external dispensation. As
infants and minors they were kept under the law’s hard and rigid pedagogy as
under tutors and curators, by whom they were driven to duty by fear of
punishment, threats, and blows rather than by promises and love of virtue
(Gal. 4:1, 2). Hence, although the *right of liberty* belonged to them by
virtue of the covenant of grace and the evangelical promises, they possessed
only a slight *sense* and use of it. The law’s threats and ἀβαστάκτῳ,
*unbearable*, yoke bound them to servitude and struck terror into them, so
that they were overwhelmed by fear at God’s appearances and had this common
saying on their lips:

[Printed p. 212 | Internet Archive PDF 240 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*We will die, because we have seen God.* But in the New Testament is the time
of *liberty*, because the covenant of grace which begets to liberty is fully
revealed (Gal. 4:26). In it liberty for captives is proclaimed through the
evangelical Jubilee (Isa. 61:1, 2), and we are given no longer the Spirit of
servitude to fear but the Spirit of *adoption*, and thus of liberty to
confidence, through whom we cry, “Abba, Father” (Rom. 8:15). “Where the Spirit
of the Lord is, there is liberty” (2 Cor. 3:17): not only the liberty of
*internal* communion, which was not lacking to the fathers, but also of
*external* dispensation; not only the liberty of *filiation*, which belongs
also to infants, but also of *emancipation*, which belongs only to adults; not
only with respect to its *right and use* and *inchoate and imperfect sense*,
but *total and perfect* with respect to its *full sense and use*.
Through it, as believers are put into possession of the goods, so they have a
full knowledge and sense of them for their consolation and faith’s
παῤῥησίαν, *boldness*.

[Marginal heading: 7. With respect to breadth;]

XXIV. 7. With respect to *breadth*. The Old Testament was restricted to one
nation; salvation then belonged only to the Jews (John 4:22), and God had not
dealt thus with every nation (Ps. 147:19, 20). But the New extends
indiscriminately to all, so that in every nation the one who fears God and
practices righteousness is accepted (Acts 10:34, 35). It is confined by no
boundaries, nor is God’s worship any longer bound to a particular place, but
in every place, provided it is sincere and pure, it is equally acceptable to
him. Previously the nations were said to be *far off* and *strangers to the
covenant* (Eph. 2:12). But now, after the dividing wall has been dissolved,
the two—namely, Jews and gentiles—are united into one body, so that they are
no longer two but one (v. 14). Thus in Christ there is no longer “Jew or
Greek, Scythian or barbarian, male or female, but Christ is all in all” (Col.
3:11).

[Marginal heading: 8. With respect to duration.]

XXV. 8. With respect to *duration*. The Old was to be made old and had to
endure only until the time of διορθώσεως, *reformation* (Heb. 9:10; Jer.
31:31), as is plainly demonstrated by the overthrow of the Jewish republic,
the confusion of the tribes, and the irreparable destruction through so many
ages of the temple in which that worship had to be performed. But the New
Testament is immutable and will endure perpetually, ἕως συντελείας τοῦ αἰῶνος,
*until the consummation of the age*. Since perfection belongs to its
administration, no other revelation is again to be expected, nor any other
priesthood and sacrifice besides that of Christ, who was made a Priest and
sanctioned by an oath by the power of an *indissoluble life* (Heb. 7:16).
Hence it is called the eternal gospel (Rev. 14).
