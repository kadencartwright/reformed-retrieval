CHAPTER II. On the visibility of the Church and its immunity from error.

THESIS I.

Bellarmine sets out the second question, one plainly like the first and wholly
connected with it. Without any distinction or explanation he attributes
this opinion to us: *The true Church is invisible and known to God alone.* He
undertakes to establish its contrary, likewise without any declaration of his
meaning. Thus there is nothing here but a confusion of darkness. We, however,
confess that the Church militant, although invisible with respect to its
essential form and as a whole taken together, is nevertheless visible with
respect to its accidental and outward form, in its parts, both separately and
jointly in ecclesiastical assemblies instituted by Christ. Bellarmine likewise,
although he sharply contends that the Church

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n258 | printed p. 41]

is visible, nevertheless finally concedes in chapter 15 that it is invisible
with respect to its essential form. *We see*, he says, *that body of people
which is the Church; but that this body is itself the true Church of Christ we
do not see, but believe. The thing itself, or that non-complex subject of which
the account and definition of the Church are predicated, is indeed visible;
but the complex proposition formed by predicate and subject* (this body is the
true Church) *is held by faith alone.* Thus no controversy remains about the
very nature of the true Church, whether it is visible or not. A question can,
however, be established here whether the whole Church militant is in some way
visible in that dependence which it is said to have as a whole at once upon one
visible head and one definite order of government; but Bellarmine does not
touch that controversy. And yet it is on account of that relation to the Roman
Pontiff, and in order to safeguard his judgment in advance, that the
Pontificians contend for the Church's absolute visibility. *Since plainly all
dogmas depend upon the testimony of the Church* (that is, of the Pontiff as the
head of the Church), *unless we are entirely certain which is the true Church,
all things will be altogether uncertain*, says Bellarmine in chapter 10.
Consequently, in the same respect, they also teach that inward faith is not
required in a member of the Church: *because otherwise*, says Bellarmine in the
same place, *there will no longer be any question between us and the heretics
about the visibility of the Church; and from chapter 9 it would follow that a
wicked Pontiff is not the head of the Church, and that other bishops, if they
are wicked, are not the heads of their churches. For a head is not a humor or a
hair, but a member, and indeed the chief member.* Such is the concatenation of
political imaginings in Roman theology.

2. The third question also rests upon pure slander, for we do not say that *the
visible Church sometimes errs so far in faith and morals that it utterly
fails*, which is the opinion Bellarmine fastens upon us; but we say: I. This or
that visible Church, even the Roman, can utterly fail. II. The whole Church
militant is sometimes so obscured that it may comparatively be called
invisible, because at one time there is no conspicuous particular Church, open
to the world and publicly celebrated, such as there are many at another time.
III. The Church is sometimes visible not only with many defects in doctrine,
discipline, sacraments, and manner of administration, but also with various
impure admixtures, so that the Church is visible while such a church as we can
safely follow in all things is nevertheless invisible. IV. We teach, however,
that this common defection hardly ever occurs except when Antichrist reigns.
But Bellarmine himself, together with other Pontificians, teaches that a state
of the Church of this kind will exist under Antichrist's reign. Thus Bellarmine
says in *On the Roman Pontiff*, book 3, chapter 7: *In the time of Antichrist,
because of the severity of persecution, the public and*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n259 | printed p. 42]

*daily office of the Church and the sacrifice must cease, as Daniel chapter 12
plainly teaches. By common consent, Antichrist will prohibit all divine
worship.* Thus no question remains about the defection of the visible Church
militant, but only about Antichrist and his reign, matters discussed elsewhere.

3. The fourth question is whether the Church can err. On this we make a
distinction and maintain that the Church, if by this name the whole body of the
faithful is understood, cannot err in those things necessary for salvation,
but can err in other things. If, however, by this name is understood the whole
body of such bishops as constitute the representative Church among the
Pontificians, we say without qualification that they can err. Bellarmine
maintains the contrary: *The Church absolutely cannot err, either in matters
absolutely necessary or in other things that it proposes to us to be believed
or done, whether by the Church is understood the whole body of the faithful or
the whole body of bishops.* Here he rather absurdly teaches that things to be
believed and done are proposed by the whole body of the faithful as
distinguished from the whole body of bishops, as if each and every believer
were a judge determining what is to be believed and what is to be done.

Bellarmine's Arguments.

4. Bellarmine. *The Church of God is the pillar and foundation of the truth, 1
Timothy 3. Here the Apostle intends to signify that, with respect to us, the
truth of the faith rests upon the authority of the Church, and that whatever
the Church approves is true and whatever it disapproves is false.*

Protestants. I. By the Church in this passage is properly understood neither
the whole body of all the faithful nor that of the bishops, but such a
particular Church as the Ephesian Church was, in which Timothy was then
dwelling. II. Experience itself teaches those who today look upon the Ephesian
Church that such a Church can err and fail. 3. *It is not called a pillar
because the Church establishes and supports the truth*, says Contarini on this
passage, *but because it is established and supported by the truth.* IV. Yet it
is so established and supported by the truth that at the same time it also
*strongly bears the truth of the Gospel*, as Dionysius the Carthusian says,
possessing a knowledge and profession of the truth that is *not wavering*, as
Thomas notes, *but firm.* Yet it does not follow from this that no error can be
found in it, for the truth can support that pillar and be borne by that pillar,
which at the same time bears some error. V. Timothy is not sent by these words
to the Ephesian Church in order to learn the truth from it as from a certain
rule. Rather, that care is entrusted to him so that he may provide for that
Church with respect to sound doctrine and administration ordered according to
Christ's will, so that the truth may have a firm seat there as upon an
immovable

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n260 | printed p. 43]

pillar. VI. The truth that must necessarily remain there if it is to be the
house of God is indicated by the Apostle to be *the great mystery of
godliness*, not every truth. VII. This truth is hung upon the Church as upon a
pillar so long as the profession of necessary truth is preserved there.

Bellarmine. *In this way booksellers' shops and chests guard the truth; and if
guardianship were the subject, Paul would have compared the Church with a
chest rather than with a pillar.*

Protestants. Those things indeed preserve books of truth as instruments, but
they do not preserve the truth as its proper subjects, nor do they preserve
the profession of truth as its witnesses. These functions belong to no other
things or people besides the Church alone, Philippians 2:15–16. And by reason
of its firmly preserved profession, the Church is compared with a pillar
rather than with a chest or a shop.

5. Bellarmine. *The Church is governed by Christ as by her bridegroom, and by
the Spirit as by her soul. Therefore, if the Church errs in doctrines of faith
or morals, error would be attributed to Christ and the Holy Spirit. Christ is
bound to remove from the Church every error from which a great evil arises.*

Protestants. I. The Church is governed by Christ so that it does not err unto
death, not so that it does not err at all; and Christ's government ought not to
be extended beyond its purpose. II. Christ's government is not perfectly
efficacious in the Church while she remains here. The Church, therefore, is a
bride who is prepared in this world so that in the world to come she may be
presented to Christ, 2 Corinthians 11:2. III. The imperfection of the effect is
not to be attributed to the perfect cause but to the incapacity of the subject.
IV. Christ is not bound to avert from the Church an error from which a great
evil arises by its nature, because he can either avert that evil or turn it
into an occasion of greater good. V. If this petty argument had any force, it
would follow from it that no believer can err, for all believers are governed
by Christ and by the Holy Spirit.

6. Bellarmine. *If he will not hear the Church, let him be to you as a heathen,
Matthew 18.*

Protestants. I. Bellarmine previously understood the Church in this passage to
mean a general council, book 2, chapter 15; later, in chapter 19, a bishop, an
assembly with a bishop, or finally the Pope. Here he now wishes it to mean the
whole body either of the faithful or of the bishops. Yet it is certain that
none of these is properly understood, but rather that Church of which the
offending brother is a member and to which, by Christ's institution, he can
and ought to be referred. II. To *hear* is not to believe the Church in all
things, but to heed a rebuke or admonition concerning sin drawn from the word
of God.

7. Bellarmine. *The Church is holy with respect to its profession; therefore,
the profession of the Church contains nothing but what is holy.*

Protestants. I. By this reasoning it would follow that this or that individual
believer who has a holy profession cannot err. II. The profession of individual
believers and of the Church contains nothing but what is holy according to its
primary intention, but through error and infirmity it can have something unholy
mixed with it.

8. Bellarmine. *Then the greatest part of the dogmas of the faith could be
called into doubt.*

Protestants. This is truly said of those whose faith depends upon the Church's
authority, but not of those who through the Spirit of grace are founded and
rooted in their faith by and on account of the authority of God.

An Error of the Church.

9. In place of many demonstrations, we shall give one instance of a manifest
error of the Church. *The opinion of Augustine and Innocent I, that the
Eucharist is necessary even for infants, prevailed in the Church for about six
hundred years.* These are the words of the Jesuit Maldonado on John 6, which
can be elucidated from Augustine, *On the Merit and Forgiveness of Sins*, book
1, chapter 24. *They hold it as an institution of Christ's Church that no human
being can attain eternal life without participation in the Lord's table.* He
expressly affirms this of infants with the agreement of Pope Innocent, *Against
Julian*, book 1, chapter 2, and book 3, chapter 11; *Against Two Letters of the
Pelagians*, book 2, chapter 4. But now the contrary opinion prevails in the
Church, and the Council of Trent pronounced an anathema upon the former
opinion, and therefore upon Pope Innocent and Augustine along with the whole
Church of their age, session 21, canon 4: *If anyone says that communion in the
Eucharist is necessary for children before they have reached the years of
discretion, let him be anathema.* Or certainly either the Pope together with
the Church, or the Council together with the Pope, must have gravely erred.
