# TOME IV, BOOK I

## ON THE GRACE OF THE FIRST MAN

### Chapter I. The state of the question

#### Thesis I

The inquisitive minds of certain Roman Catholics have devised various
questions about the state and condition of the first man before the fall.
Master Alexander of Hales and Bonaventure, along with many others, contend that
Adam was created in a state of pure nature, without grace. Others maintain
that he was created with the gratuitous gift of original righteousness, but
without sanctifying grace, which he nevertheless received before the fall.
Thomas, however, together with his disciples, appears to defend the view that
man was created in sanctifying grace. They also dispute very keenly about the
difference between original righteousness and sanctifying grace—whether it is
a real distinction or one of reason only. See Bañez on 1.2, question 95. Nor
are there lacking among them those who remove every distinction between pure
nature and nature adorned with original righteousness. “Certain Doctors,”
says Medina on 1.2, question 48, article 2, “understand by a man constituted in
a state of pure nature a man constituted in an integral nature, with a gift
whose power heals nature, so that he could remain and persevere, if he wished,
in the good and in the exercise of the virtues. This view undoubtedly appears
to be Augustine's.” Bellarmine passes over all the other questions and takes
up this last opinion for attack—really against other Roman Catholics, as
chapter 2 shows. “For we did not receive what we teach from Domingo de Soto
alone, nor did St. Thomas, together with the other more approved authors, write
the contrary, as certain foolishly learned men judge.” Instead, by hunting for
words he attributes this position to our theologians, who meanwhile remain
within the bounds of Scripture and modesty and have always religiously
abstained from questions of this sort as far as possible. He himself states
the reason in chapter 1: “because our theologians define the struggle of the
flesh against the spirit as truly and properly sin, and consequently hold that
the righteousness which removes this rebellion belongs to the natural
constitution of integral man.” Here, then, is the genuine state of the
controversy insofar as it concerns us: whether that rectitude which excludes
the struggle of the flesh against the spirit is natural to integral man. We
affirm it; Bellarmine denies it.

[Printed p. 224 | Google Books PA224 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

It will nevertheless be worthwhile to attend to the explanation Bellarmine
offers.

2. Bellarmine. *Man naturally consists of flesh and spirit. From their
different or contrary inclinations toward bodily and spiritual good,
respectively, a certain conflict exists in one and the same man, together with
great difficulty in acting well.*

Protestant reply. I. Integral man naturally consists, not of flesh which is
hostile to the spirit, but of flesh which is subjected and subordinated to the
spirit in a certain holy harmony. II. By the order of nature, the inclination
of a spirit joined to a body is not only toward spiritual good but also toward
the bodily good suited to nature; and the body's inclination is toward service
to the spirit, even in seeking spiritual good. Therefore, the inclinations of
spirit and body in integral man are not contrary. III. The conflict of the
flesh against the spirit has no place in a man entirely without enmity against
the law of God. IV. It is not difficult for integral nature to act in a manner
consistent with its nature—that is, to act well.

3. Bellarmine. *As a remedy for this disease or debility of nature, God added
original righteousness to man. By it, as by a kind of golden bridle, the lower
part would be kept readily subject to the higher, and the higher part to God.*

Protestant reply. I. Righteousness was added to man as a perfection of a nature
that was to be perfected. II. Disease and debility cannot be attributed to
integral nature without contradiction, for disease is a defect. III. Integral
man needed a bridle no more than he needed spurs.

4. Bellarmine. *Original righteousness can be called natural: 1. insofar as it
was possessed from birth; 2. insofar as it perfected and adorned nature; 3.
insofar as it aided nature in natural works—but not as a part of nature or as
something flowing from nature's principles.*

Protestant reply. Nor do we say that original righteousness is an essential
part of physical nature, or that it flows physically from nature's principles.
We say that it is a moral condition owed to that nature which was created to
act righteously.

5. Bellarmine. *That rectitude was so supernatural a gift that the state of
man after Adam's fall differs no more from his state in pure nature than a
person stripped of his clothes differs from a naked person.*

Protestant reply. According to Bellarmine, then, through sin man was stripped
only of external clothing, not of inward life and virtue.

### Chapter II

#### Bellarmine's arguments

1. Bellarmine. *Genesis 3: “You are dust,” that is, by your nature you are
dust: mortal, frail, and subject to pain and labor.*

Protestant reply. The passage concerns bodily nature alone with respect to its
material principle, not the whole of nature together with its moral
perfection. Therefore, in our question these words do not carry even as much
weight as a fine speck of dust on a great balance.

2. Bellarmine. *In Psalm 8 the prophet marvels that man was made almost equal
to the angels.*

Protestant reply. And rightly so. For in verse 4 he observed that the heavens,
moon, stars, and so forth display such great glory and majesty of God that no
reason appears why he should take any account of man, whether in constituting
and preserving his nature or in exalting him in that same nature.

3. Bellarmine. *The other gifts bestowed on the first man at the beginning he
calls honor and glory; but honor and glory undoubtedly signify something
extrinsic and superadded.*

Protestant reply. I. It is stated ambiguously and sophistically that “he calls
the other gifts honor and glory.” If “other” means other than natural gifts,
nothing is established; if it means other than the essence of soul and body,
then we ask for proof. II. Bellarmine plays very rashly and dangerously with
these words, which Hebrews 2:9 explains of Christ the Lord. For he appears to
deny that Christ himself possessed righteousness, whether natural or even
communicated to him before his exaltation. III. If honor and glory are taken
for outward reputation, then they cannot designate inherent righteousness. If
they are taken for the ground, foundation, or cause of such reputation, then
they can include natural righteousness.

4. Bellarmine. *In Ecclesiasticus 17 man is said to be clothed with virtue;
but clothing is something extrinsic and superadded.*

Protestant reply. One part of a nature can, by a very apt metaphor, be called
the clothing of another. Thus bones are said to be clothed with flesh, and the
body is called man's house and tent in 2 Corinthians 5. Yet a tent is more
extrinsic than clothing.

5. Bellarmine. *It is natural for an animal body furnished with sensation and
appetite to desire sensible good, and natural for a rational spirit to desire
spiritual good. Therefore, if one nature is made up of a rational spirit and
a natural body, it will be natural for it to have different and mutually
conflicting inclinations.*

Protestant reply. It is natural for man to have different inclina-*

[Printed p. 226 | Google Books PA226 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*tions, but not conflicting ones. For since the union of nature from a
rational spirit and a natural body was made in a perfect manner, it entails
the subordination of the one inclination to the other, which excludes all
conflict.

6. Bellarmine. *If the corruption now present in human nature did not follow
solely from the removal of a supernatural gift, and would not have existed in
a man created in pure nature, I ask for some cause of it.*

Protestant reply. The most proper cause of corruption is man corrupting his
own nature, both by defecting from God and by deserving God's desertion.
Natural desire is from nature; ordered desire is from nature observing its own
order; but disordered desire—that is, the corruption of human nature—is from
nature falling away from its order.

7. Bellarmine. *The sins committed by men after the fall of the first man,
however many and very grave, do not corrupt any natural principle or gift; nor
is an inclination to every vice acquired or increased, nor an inclination to
every virtue diminished. But Adam's sin was of the same kind as the other sins
committed by men.*

Protestant reply. I. All the premises are false. Through sins the conscience
is often seared as with a hot iron, although conscience is a natural gift, 1
Timothy 4:2. And after men “have become past feeling, they give themselves over
to the competitive practice of every impurity,” Ephesians 4:19. II. The first
man's sin was not only a sin of the person but also of nature. It therefore
harmed nature much more, and in a different manner, than any personal sins
possibly could. Indeed, nothing further can be taken away from human nature if
civil societies themselves are to continue.

8. Bellarmine. *If human nature, as it now is once guilt has been removed, is
evil and necessarily falls into sins, and if for this reason it could not be
created by God without original righteousness, it certainly follows that a
dead man cannot be recalled to life by God unless furnished with original
righteousness. But from Holy Scripture we know of many who were raised from
the dead by divine power, including some whom it is wholly credible had been
purged from original sin, such as Lazarus and the children whom Elijah and
Elisha recalled from the dead. Yet even in that new life they undoubtedly
lacked original righteousness.*

Protestant reply. I. This objection does not conclude the question. It proves
neither that original righteousness was supernatural nor that human nature
could have been created without that righteousness. It proves only that this
was not the reason it could not be so created—namely, that it would then be
evil, and so forth. But from this concession it does not follow that there was
no other reason why it ought not to have been so created. II. The creation of
a man who had never experienced sin is plainly not of the same kind as the
restora-*

[Printed p. 227 | Google Books PA227 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*tion of a man oppressed by death through sin and not yet completely freed
from it. III. It is not established that anyone who was purged from original
sin was ever recalled from death to life so that he might live here without
original righteousness. On the contrary, in *On Purgatory*, book 2, chapter
18, Bellarmine teaches that the very people whom he says here it is “credible”
were purged from original sin are “believed” not to have been purged. “The dead
whom Elijah, Elisha, the Lord, Peter, and Paul raised are believed to have been
in purgatory or limbo, and only to have exchanged exile or prison. But a
prison”—as the same Bellarmine has it in chapter 7—“even a senatorial and
honorable one, is a punishment; and punishment is appropriate only to a soul
that is not yet fully purged. They were also confirmed in grace, because
otherwise an injustice would have been done to them. If any holy men returned
to the body, they were not blessed, but God, foreseeing their resurrection,
deferred their beatification and meanwhile detained them in some excellent
place.”

9. Bellarmine. *The fifth argument properly opposes those who contend that all
the gifts granted to our first parents at creation were natural.*

Protestant reply. Therefore, it does not oppose us, for we rashly affirm
nothing about matters not revealed. Nevertheless, let us hear what sort of
argument it is.

Bellarmine. *Our first parents were raised to such dignity that they were sons
of God and certain gods—that is, sharers in the divine nature. But it is most
absurd that a created thing should naturally be made God or the Son of God.*

Protestant reply. I. Before the fall our first parents were not raised to the
grace of justification and adoption, which depended upon a condition not yet
fulfilled. Grant that they were. II. Yet it is not absurd if they are called
sons of God naturally—not born from God's substance, but created by God's will
in his image in their natural constitution. But Bellarmine most absurdly calls
them gods.

### Chapter III

#### Our arguments

1. Protestant. If original righteousness had been supernatural, it would
follow that the desire of the flesh would have been natural, and consequently
good and from God. But Scripture denies this, 1 John 2; Romans 7; and so forth.

Bellarmine. *The desire of the flesh would undoubtedly have been natural in a
man created in pure nature—not as some good of nature, but as a defect and, as
it were, a disease of nature following from the condition of matter. It is
therefore said most correctly not to be from God, to deserve hatred rather
than love, and to be contrary to nature. 1. Because it would have existed in
man apart from the inten-*

[Printed p. 228 | Google Books PA228 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*tion of the author of nature if man had been created in pure nature. 2.
Because God had removed it, and it has a place in us through man's own fault.*

Protestant reply. I. In chapter 5 Bellarmine calls concupiscence “the
inclination of the flesh toward bodily and sensible good, toward which it is
borne through sensation and appetite.” But a thing's inclination toward its
natural object is a good of nature, not a defect or disease, and does not
depend on matter. It comes from the cause which gave each faculty its own
inclinations. II. Man was not made a living animal, with all the inclinations
suited to animal nature, apart from God's intention. III. The final point—that
God had removed it—is irrelevant, for even the things the Lord has taken away
can have been from God, according to the words of Job: “The Lord gave, and the
Lord has taken away.”

2. Protestant. As soon as children begin to grow, a very great abundance of
vain desire appears, and of their own accord they are borne headlong into vice
with enormous force. A man born in pure nature would not have been so.

Bellarmine. *The assumption needed to be proved. For the clearest reasoning
teaches that a man in pure nature would have been precisely such that this
headlong inclination toward vice was in him.*

Protestant reply. I. Accordingly, with respect to the inclination toward
impure vices, Bellarmine establishes no difference between man's pure and
impure natural condition. II. Since the Master of the Sentences, on book 2,
distinction 42; Alexander of Hales, part 2, question 96, member 1; Bonaventure,
on book 2, distinction 21; and many other Roman Catholic doctors teach that
man was created in pure nature without grace, it follows that they teach that
nature is no more corrupted from birth now than it was at creation. Bellarmine
also concedes that, as regards inclination toward vice, man could have been
created as he is now born.

3. Protestant. If God placed in nature itself a rebellious and unjust impulse,
an evil, before any demerit of man, it can scarcely be otherwise than that God
is the author of sin.

Bellarmine. 1. *The state in which Adam was actually created is improperly
confused with the state in which he could have been created.*

Protestant reply. We do not confuse them; we join them. I. The Scholastics just
cited affirm that Adam actually was created in this way. Bonaventure also adds
that this is the more common opinion. II. According to the other opinion, it
follows that God could have been the author of sin—which, in a question of
right, means precisely that he was.

Bellarmine. 2. *God would not have made that rebellion; it would have followed
from the condition of matter.*

[Printed p. 229 | Google Books PA229 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. I. Nature's positive inclinations toward natural objects are
made by the author of nature; they are not conditions of matter. II. Even if
they were conditions of matter, they would nevertheless be made by the one
who made matter itself.

Bellarmine. 3. *That rebellion is not sin, nor does it necessarily lead to
sin.*

Protestant reply. Rebellion opposed to the obedience that is owed cannot fail
to be sin.

4. Protestant. Eternal blessedness is man's natural end; therefore, man ought
to have had natural means for attaining that end. Consequently, the
righteousness necessary for attaining blessedness was natural to man.

Bellarmine. *Blessedness is man's natural end with respect to appetite, not
with respect to attainment.*

Protestant reply. This is novel: that man alone naturally desires something as
his end which he cannot attain by any natural means.

Bellarmine. *The souls of men separated from their bodies have a natural order
and inclination toward their bodies, yet they will not be able to be joined to
their bodies again without supernatural help.*

Protestant reply. This inclination of the separated soul toward reunion with
the body arises from the fact that the conjunction itself is natural and the
separation contrary to nature. It therefore has no similarity to the
inclination of nature as nature was created and ordered to its end. In its
ordinary course, nature does not lack necessities.

# BOOK II

## ON SIN

### Chapter I. On venial sin

#### Thesis I

Bellarmine. *The whole question is whether there are sins venial by the nature
and character of sin—that is, sins which are not opposed to love for God and
one's neighbor. We teach by common consent that certain sins, by their own
nature, do not make a man unworthy of God's friendship and guilty of eternal
death, but make him guilty only of temporal punishment and fatherly
chastisement.*

Protestant reply. Medina most clearly describes the Roman Catholics' agreement
on the doctrine of venial sin in 1.2, question 88, article 1: “There is no one
opinion of the Doctors about the principle by which venial sin is distinguished
from mortal; their opinions vary without end.” Nevertheless, we ad-*

[Printed p. 230 | Google Books PA230 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*mit this statement of the controversy, and with unanimous consent deny what
Bellarmine affirms is taught by common consent.

#### Bellarmine's arguments

2. Bellarmine. *Matthew 5:22: “Whoever is angry with his brother shall be
liable to judgment; whoever says ‘Raca’ shall be liable to the council; whoever
says ‘Fool’ shall be liable to the fire of hell.”*

Protestant reply. By what machinery can venial sin be wrung from these words,
in which the smallest sins are pronounced worthy of grievous penalties?

Bellarmine. 1. *One is said to be liable to judgment when he has done something
for which he can be summoned to judgment, but it is not established whether he
deserves any penalty or none.*

Protestant reply. This first postulate is exceedingly shameless and mad. For,
I. In the preceding sentence—“whoever kills shall be liable to judgment”—no one
will say that a bare summons to court is signified, while the sentence to be
passed and punishment to be inflicted remain uncertain. II. For Christ to
pronounce someone guilty of sin when it is not established whether he deserves
punishment is a manifest contradiction. III. It cannot be said without
blasphemy that in the very judgment of God it is uncertain whether someone
deserves punishment.

Bellarmine. 2. *One is said to be liable to the council when his guilt is
certain, but its extent and the punishment it deserves are uncertain; and so
the judges convene in council to determine the measure of punishment.*

Protestant reply. This dream is altogether like the first. For, I. If it were
not established whether the one rashly angry deserved punishment, a council
would be needed to define the matter no less than in the case of the one who
said “Raca.” II. In the judgment to which the angry man is liable, the measure
of punishment must also necessarily be defined if any punishment is to be
inflicted. III. God determined the measures of punishments from eternity, and
with him there can be no hesitation about how far a sin is to be punished.

Bellarmine. 3. *One is said to be liable to the fire of hell or eternal death
when his guilt, the measure of his guilt, and consequently the gravity of his
punishment are certain.*

Protestant reply. I. By this reasoning every single sin ought to be mortal by
its own nature, because the guilt, measure of guilt, and gravity of punishment
owed for every sin is certain before God. II. Elsewhere Bellarmine teaches
that venial sins by their nature deserve purgatory, whose punishments he also
says differ from the fire of hell only in duration. III. Roman Catholics
further teach that “unless venial sin is forgiven by God's kindness, it will
last forever and for that reason be punished forever, because” *

[Printed p. 231 | Google Books PA231 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*“as long as God does not forgive, a man remains in guilt, and while that guilt
remains, its due punishment is exacted.” See Azorius, book 4, chapter 10.
Therefore, either what is called venial sin deserves eternal punishment in
itself, or God can punish beyond what is deserved while acting justly.

Bellarmine. *Matthew 23 and Luke 6 compare some sins to things by their nature
very slight, but others to very weighty things: straining out a gnat but
swallowing a camel; you see the speck in your brother's eye but do not see the
beam in your own.*

Protestant reply. An elephant does not catch flies. Therefore, so great a camel
as Bellarmine was ought not to have hunted for these gnats and specks. I.
Proverbial expressions must not be stretched by the teeth beyond their
purpose. II. What is a gnat or speck by comparison can be a mountain
absolutely, just as some say that the whole earth, compared with the circuit of
the entire heaven, is like a point. III. Among good works there is the same
difference of degrees between the greatest and the smallest as there is among
sins, yet the Roman Catholics say that the smallest works deserve eternal
life. What a beam in their eyes, that these same men do not see that the
smallest sins merit eternal punishment!

4. Bellarmine. *Luke 12:59: “You will not leave there until you have paid even
the very last mite.” By “the very last mite” he means minute and very slight
sins.*

Protestant reply. I. Even if this were so, the consequence would be no more
valid than the preceding one. For we do not owe sins to be paid to God, but
penalties to be suffered for sins. Therefore, by the mite is meant the last
part of the penalty. II. This minute portion of penalty is found in eternal
punishment; consequently, minuteness is not foreign to a sin that deserves
eternal punishment.

5. Bellarmine. *In 1 Corinthians 3 venial sins are compared with very light
things: wood, hay, and stubble.*

Protestant reply. I. Throughout Scripture impious and flagitious people are
also compared to wood to be burned, hay to be cut down, and stubble to be
driven by the wind and consumed by flame. Are all the impious, then, light and
venial sinners? II. Wood is not an extremely light thing. III. This passage
does not concern sins but doctrines, as Bellarmine himself testifies in *On
Purgatory*, book 1, chapter 5.

6. Bellarmine. *James 1:14 calls a certain movement of concupiscence sin, yet
distinguishes it from completed sin which brings forth death.*

Protestant reply. I. In Adam's first sin there was also a certain inward
movement before he ate the forbidden fruit—Bellarmine teaches in book 2,
chapter 4 that it was pride—which was the com-*

[Printed p. 232 | Google Books PA232 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*pletion of the sin and brought forth death. Yet the same Bellarmine denies in
chapter 8 that this pride was a venial sin. II. James does not distinguish the
sin of concupiscence from completed sin which brings forth death as one species
from another, but as a part from the whole. Since he teaches that death is due
to the whole sin, he shows that each of its parts is mortal.

7. Bellarmine. *Scripture speaks much more mildly about certain sins than it
usually does about those which by everyone's consent are mortal sins. Matthew
12 says that they will give an account of every idle word on the day of
judgment. In Ephesians 5, after the apostle had set forth six kinds of sins—
fornication, uncleanness, covetousness, shamefulness, foolish talk, and
scurrility—he affirms only of the first three, not the rest, that those who do
such things have no inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and God.*

Protestant reply. I. Mild words do not prove venial sin, as appears from
Matthew 22:13: “Friend, how did you come in here without a wedding garment?”
II. The mild words used in Matthew 12 are used elsewhere, in 1 Peter 4:5, about
blasphemies. Are blasphemies, then, also venial sins among the Papists? III.
The apostle's silence in Ephesians 5, which clearly had other causes, testifies
nothing in favor of venial sin.

8. Bellarmine. *If venial sins were mortal by their own nature and were not
imputed to believers only because of the faith they possess, they would
certainly be imputed fully to unbelievers and be mortal. But that is manifestly
false and absurd. Therefore.*

Protestant reply. The assumption is denied by the Roman Catholics themselves.
Thus Azorius, book 4, chapter 10: “I think it must be said, according to the
common opinion of the theologians, that the remission of venial sin is a free
and supernatural benefit of God. Venial sin is forgiven to no one outside the
grace of God.”

Bellarmine. *The assumption is proved: within the same kind, a believer offends
God more gravely than an unbeliever.*

Protestant reply. The conclusion ought to be: therefore, it is manifestly
false and absurd that those sins called venial are imputed to unbelievers.
What connection does this conclusion have? It is not the slightness of the
offense that causes these sins not to be imputed to believers, nor the gravity
of the offense that causes them to be imputed to unbelievers. It is the grace
of our Lord Jesus Christ toward believers which makes their sins, but not the
unbelievers' sins, venial—not by their own nature, but by the mercy of God who
pardons them and does not impute them to the death they deserve.

9. Bellarmine. *A sin is called venial when it is worthy of pardon, or when
there is something in it on account of which it ought to be punished more
mildly. I therefore ask what it is that makes a sin worthy of pardon and keeps
God from imputing it to the guilt of death.*

[Printed p. 233 | Google Books PA233 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. I. There is something in certain mortal sins on account of
which they are punished more mildly than other, more atrocious mortal sins.
II. There is nothing in us which by itself causes a sin not to be imputed to
the guilt of death; God's mercy does this. Thus, in this question there is
neither a foundation nor a conclusion for any argument.

10. Bellarmine. *Venial sin by its own nature does not establish in man an
aversion from God, since it is not opposed to charity, as is evident. But
eternal punishment is not due to sin except by reason of aversion from the
eternal good, which is God.*

Protestant reply. In this begging of the question scarcely any statement is
true, even by the judgment of the Roman Catholics themselves. For, I.
“Aversion and deviation from the rule belong to the nature of sin, and from
the fact that a human act is contrary to the law, aversion from God follows.”
These are Medina's words in 1.2, question 72, article 1. II. Every deviation
from the rule of charity is in some way opposed to charity. III. Eternal
punishment is due to sin according to every aspect of the guilt that remains
forever.

11. Bellarmine. *Not every offense by its own nature dissolves friendship
between men; therefore, neither does every offense dissolve friendship between
God and man.*

Protestant reply. I. The consequence is denied, because man is not subject to
man in this way and does not owe as much to man as he does to God. II. By the
same reasoning Bellarmine could conclude that God is bound to forgive men's
sins because men ought to love their enemies and, as regards vengeance,
forgive injuries done to them.

Bellarmine. *What belongs to a thing by its nature belongs to it always and
everywhere.*

Protestant reply. Provided, of course, that the same nature remains. But the
nature both of friendship with God and of offending him is one thing, and the
nature of human friendship and offense another.

12. Bellarmine. *“Sin” is not said univocally of mortal and venial sin, but
according to a certain analogy. Therefore, venial sin is not perfectly sin as
mortal sin is, but imperfectly so; hence the punishment of eternal death is
not due to it.*

Protestant reply. I. Medina replies in 1.2, question 88, article 1: “It is
certain and established that in Holy Scripture venial sin is simply, and
without qualification, called sin. We freely concede that venial sin is simply
sin.” II. The Jesuit Azorius further replies in book 4, chapter 8: “Certainly
it cannot be denied that Scripture calls venial sin, absolutely and simply and
without any addition, sin. Venial faults are therefore truly and properly
sins. Yet the nature of sin is greater in lethal guilt; and sometimes things
are said to be analogous which, although they agree simply in some one
respect, nevertheless participate unequally in that common character.” The
same is the opinion of Durandus, on book 2, distinction 42, question 6. III.
Imperfection *

[Printed p. 234 | Google Books PA234 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

of degree in sin does not remove the nature of sin or the desert of eternal
punishment.

Bellarmine. 1. *Every venial sin either is not perfectly voluntary or is not
perfectly contrary to the law; therefore, it is not perfectly and simply sin.*

Protestant reply. As regards the first, Azorius replies in book 1, chapter 14:
“It is sufficient for sin if the act is voluntary, whether indirectly, by
itself, or through something else.” On the second point, the same author
testifies more fully in book 4, chapter 8: “It must be said, as Durandus,
Cajetan, Vega, and Major taught, that venial sin is indeed contrary to God's
law, because God's law, since it is just and right reason, prohibits both grave
and light sins.” Therefore, when Bellarmine denies that sins of this sort are
perfectly—that is, as he himself interprets it, simply—voluntary and contrary
to law, he is abandoned and refuted by his own associates.

Bellarmine. 2. *A sin of surprise is not perfectly voluntary.*

Protestant reply. Not in the highest degree, certainly, but it proceeds from
true and proper consent.

Bellarmine. 3. *A venial sin arising from smallness of matter is indeed
perfectly voluntary but not perfectly contrary to the law. For the law does
not prohibit the theft of one small coin specifically, but prohibits theft
generally.*

Protestant reply. What is prohibited generally is also prohibited
specifically, or according to every species. Otherwise the theft of a talent
would not be prohibited, because the Decalogue makes no specific mention of a
talent.

Bellarmine. 4. *A sin venial by its kind can be perfectly voluntary and
perfectly contrary to the law if there is some special law concerning it; but
that law will not perfectly and strictly be law.*

Protestant reply. Every law of God is perfectly and strictly God's will
revealed to us as the rule of our actions, binding us to observe it. By
mutilating distinctions with segments of this sort, every truth can be
evaded.

Bellarmine. 5. *If venial sin were perfectly and properly contrary to the law,
then as soon as a just man committed any one venial sin he would truly and
perfectly be a transgressor of the law and guilty of all the heavenly
commands. But this is absurd.*

Protestant reply. It is not absurd that when a just man acts unjustly, he
begins, according to the law's strictness, truly and simply to be a transgressor
of the law and a violator of all its authority.

Bellarmine. *Scripture calls people simply holy and just, although they are not
free from venial sins.*

Protestant reply. They were not just by law, but by God's grace and mercy.

Bellarmine. 6. *Venial sins do not exclude charity.*

[Printed p. 235 | Google Books PA235 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. They exclude perfect charity, which is legal righteousness.

#### Our arguments

Protestant. Scripture speaks generally of every sin: “Cursed is everyone who
does not continue in all the things written in the book of the law,”
Deuteronomy 27. “The wages of sin is death,” Romans 6; and so forth.

Bellarmine. 1. *These and similar passages do not concern sins universally,
but only mortal sins.*

Protestant reply. They concern all sins opposed to legal righteousness: “Do
this, and you will live.” All sins universally are of this kind. For in God's
law there is no middle way between the law's blessing and curse, or between
moral works of life and death.

Bellarmine. 2. *If those passages of Scripture included venial sins, it would
follow that just men actually fall from the life of grace merely on account of
venial sins.*

Protestant reply. This indeed follows concerning legal righteousness, but not
concerning the righteousness and grace of the gospel, which consists in the
remission of sins.

Bellarmine. 3. *That curse in Deuteronomy 27 refers to violators of the
commands handed down in that very passage.*

Protestant reply. In Galatians 3:10 the apostle Paul expressly interprets
those words of Moses “of all things written in the book of the law, to do
them.” He extends that curse as widely as the blessing of the law mentioned in
verse 12: “Whoever does them will live by them.” That cannot be restricted to
the duties opposed to the crimes proclaimed in Deuteronomy 27.

Bellarmine. 4. *In Romans 6 Paul discusses the sin which conflicts with grace
and cannot coexist with righteousness.*

Protestant reply. I. He discusses every sin from whose guilt believers are
freed through Christ. II. He discusses sin that is not perfectly voluntary,
chapter 7, verses 20 and 24. III. Finally, he discusses all the sins of which
the repentant are ashamed, chapter 6, verse 21.

14. Protestant. Whoever does not love God with all his heart, and so forth,
violates the law and is guilty of death. Whoever sins lightly does not love
God with all his heart, and so forth. Therefore.

Bellarmine. 1. *We are indeed commanded to love God so that nothing is
preferred or made equal to the love of God, but we deny that venial sins
conflict with this love.*

Protestant reply. I. Whoever sins, by turning inordinately to something else,
at least partially and interpretatively prefers it to God, insofar as he turns
away in that matter from God's law and will. II. What con-*

[Printed p. 236 | Google Books PA236 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*flicts with the fervor of charity conflicts in part with charity, for fervor
is a degree in charity. Yet the Roman Catholics themselves concede that
charity's fervor is diminished by venial sins. Filincius, treatise 22, number
378; Bonaventure, on book 2, distinction 42, where he also teaches that sins of
this kind “distance and retard one from God.”

Bellarmine. 2. *To love God so that God is always actually thought about and
loved, and so that nothing contrary to love of God or neighbor can creep in,
is not properly commanded to us in this life, but only indicated.*

Protestant reply. I. What God indicates must be done, he properly commands,
according to Micah 6:8: “He has shown you, O man, what is good; and what does
Jehovah require of you but to practice justice,” and so forth? If this
perfection of love is indicated anywhere in all Scripture, it certainly is in
this command, “with all your heart,” and so forth, for it cannot be expressed
in more forceful words. But whatever this command indicates must be done is
also properly commanded. II. Bellarmine describes this perfection of love in
a confused way. His description does not touch the perfection of the habit or
inward disposition, nor the intention of acts, nor their mediate relation to
God. These exclude every sin even though something other than God is thought
about. But what excellent interpreters the Roman Catholics are of this chief
command of charity may appear from these two examples. Bañez, on 2.2, question
44, article 5, teaches “that any act of charity, however weak, suffices to
fulfill all the commandments, and that no determinate intensity is required
for anyone in this life to fulfill the command to love God.” The Jesuit
Filincius, treatise 22, numbers 286, 287, and 290, discusses when the command
to love God obliges, and teaches: “The most probable opinion of all is that
between the first moral beginning of perfect reasoning and the point of death,
however long the intervening period of life may be, the command of charity
does not oblige of itself except at least every fifth year. The reason is that,
since no determinate time is fixed, it is left to the judgment of the wise; and
the wise theologians Soto, Ledesma, and Suarez have so judged.”

Bellarmine. 3. *Scripture testifies concerning various people who were not free
from every sin that they loved God with all their heart.*

Protestant reply. With respect to sincerity of quality, not perfection of
quantity.

15. Protestant. According to the Roman Catholics' opinion, the smallest good
works deserve eternal life; therefore, the smallest sins deserve eternal
death.

Bellarmine. *It does not follow. To give the greatest reward for the smallest
work would be immense generosity and mercy, but to give the greatest
punishment for the smallest sin would be injustice and cruelty.*

Protestant reply. I. Here Bellarmine is plainly looking for an escape. The
question *

[Printed p. 237 | Google Books PA237 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

concerns the merit of a good and evil work, but he responds only about reward.
II. Here he attributes to immense mercy a reward which all Roman Catholics
teach is given to merit from justice and *ex condigno*. III. He calls injustice
and cruelty what other Jesuits acknowledge can justly be done. “It would not
be contrary to justice,” Filincius says in treatise 22, number 277, “if God
willed all charity to be corrupted through venial sins.”

16. Protestant. Every fault can be punished by God with the punishment of
eternal death; therefore, every fault is mortal by its own nature and venial
only through God's mercy.

Bellarmine. *God cannot justly punish every sin, even a venial one, with the
most grievous punishment of all, which is eternal death.*

Protestant reply. The question is not about the degree of punishment in
eternal death, but simply about eternal death. We have just shown from the
Jesuit Filincius that it can justly be inflicted for a sin called venial. For
when he affirms that God can justly will the corruption of charity to follow
from venial sin, he affirms at the same time that God can justly punish such a
sin with eternal death, which necessarily follows from the corruption of
charity. The Jesuit Azorius, book 3, chapter 9, speaks more openly and fully.
He approves in this sense the opinion of Gerson, part 3, treatise *On the
Spiritual Life*, lecture 1, corollary 1, teaching that “every venial sin is
worthy of eternal punishment”: “namely, that venial sin, except insofar as it
is committed by a just man furnished with God's grace and charity, would be
punished perpetually.” It can therefore justly be punished by God with eternal
punishment. The reason is that unless it were pardoned from grace, the sin
itself would remain forever.

17. Protestant. The evil of fault, even the lightest fault, is greater than
any evil of punishment. For one ought to undergo any punishment, however
grievous, rather than commit any sin, even the lightest. Therefore, every fault
can justly be punished by God with any punishment, including eternal death.

Bellarmine. *By reason of justice, not just any absolutely greatest evil is due
to a fault, but an evil of punishment proportionate to the evil of the fault.*

Protestant reply. The argument still retains its force. For the question is
whether eternal punishment is beyond the proportion of a fault which is a
greater evil than any punishment. The question is also whether it is unjust
and cruel to inflict a lesser evil for a greater one that has been committed.

18. Protestant. What is called venial sin can remain forever unforgiven;
therefore, it can be punished forever.

Bellarmine. 1. *It is possible accidentally that it should never be destroyed
through all eternity, and thus accidentally be punished forever, but not of
itself.*

Protestant reply. I. Here Bellarmine affirms that God can accidentally do what
just a little earlier he denied could justly be done. II. *

[Printed p. 238 | Google Books PA238 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

No sin is ever destroyed of itself, but through God's grace. What God does
from grace, he can refrain from doing.

Bellarmine. 2. *Since God has promised forgiveness if we forgive, Matthew 6,
then—even if not absolutely, certainly by reason of the promise and covenant—
he will be unable not to forgive if we also forgive.*

Protestant reply. I. The question concerns absolutely the nature and desert of
sin in itself according to justice, not the promise according to grace. II. By
this sophism Bellarmine can conclude that even mortal sins are venial by their
own nature, because God has also promised their remission to the repentant.

Bellarmine. 3. *If God willed not to pardon venial sin, he could require only
its temporal punishment, not an everlasting one.*

Protestant reply. The Roman Catholics have therefore finally found a way by
which the guilt of sin can be removed without any pardoning grace of God. One
may rightly doubt whether they enjoy any greater madness than this.
