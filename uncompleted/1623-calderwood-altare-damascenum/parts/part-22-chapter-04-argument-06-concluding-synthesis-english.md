Since, therefore, both powers belong to a presbyter—the power of order and the
power of jurisdiction—the bishop and presbyter are not of different orders
and ranks of ministry. Spalatensis reduces to the power of order those acts
which, although external and visible, nevertheless seek to produce an
internal effect that reaches into the soul. He locates the power of
jurisdiction in those external ecclesiastical acts in which the church also
principally and directly intends an external effect. *True and proper
essential ecclesiastical power is wholly spiritual and produces only a
spiritual and internal effect; this I properly call the power of order or
ministerial power. Accessory to it, however, is power concerning the external
affairs of the church: the rites of divine worship, the correction and
exclusion of those who err, the proper administration of temporalities, the
election and appointment of ministers, and similar things. These directly and
in themselves concern the church’s peace, dignity, purification, and due
order, though all are directed to the spiritual end that God may be rightly
worshiped, etc.* He says that both powers, whether of order or jurisdiction,
flow directly and immediately from order itself; and that when holy order is
given, all sacred and ecclesiastical power proper to the church is given
together with it, whether you call that power order or jurisdiction. Thus it
is inept to separate

any power of jurisdiction you please to understand, provided it is properly
ecclesiastical—that is, spiritual or essentially directed to spiritual
things. He departs from the accepted explanation of the Schoolmen in
distinguishing the power of order and the power of jurisdiction. But does not
accessory power also belong to the man to whom true and proper essential
ecclesiastical power belongs? Is it not absurd to think that presbyters are
fit for spiritual and sacred acts but utterly unfit for the merely external
acts of government? *If you except external governing rule and the ordination
of ministers, presbyters can by virtue of their presbyteral order do whatever
bishops can do, even in the blessing and consecration of things and persons;
and this certainly by divine law and, as I have said, by virtue of their
order. Nevertheless, by ecclesiastical law some things are reserved to
bishops,* says Spalatensis. We have shown that ordination too is reserved by
ecclesiastical law, not divine law. And since he says that the power of
jurisdiction flows from the power of order, why do you deny external
governing rule to those to whom he has granted the principal thing? For the
accessory ought to follow the principal. He likewise says *that the keys were
given equally to all the apostles, and not to Peter alone; and that from the
apostles they passed equally to all bishops and presbyters.* Did they then
pass in one manner and another—one way to bishops and another to presbyters—or
to presbyters in some way different from the way they were delivered to the
apostles? Yet Spalatensis is not so unjust toward common government. For he
acknowledges that it is not fitting for bishops to govern their churches
without their presbyters, especially in weightier affairs; and that, since by
divine law bishops and presbyters are colleagues and associates in the
essential ministries, they ought even now to apply themselves to
ecclesiastical affairs by common counsel and labor. What? Even now? Even after
such a great jumble of canons and constitutions has been amassed? Let the
other hierarchists, then, be gone with their pretext of canons. *Who does not
know,* says Tilenus, *that Augustine numbered Aerius, who attempted to abolish
this distinction, among the heretics?* He should add Epiphanius, whom
Augustine and Philaster followed. But Aerius was no heretic, even if either
Augustine or Epiphanius judged him so. The arguments of Aerius, whom Theodorus
Bibliander calls a most learned man, were perhaps more numerous than the ones
Epiphanius selected for himself to refute. Epiphanius’s responses against
Aerius are certainly airy; we examined each of them above in its proper
place. Epiphanius was the very first to list Aerius among the heretics,
condemned on his own private authority and by no synod. Epiphanius’s character
can be discerned from his disputes with Chrysostom, his usurpation of the
ordination of deacons in another diocese, and his altercation with the
empress Eudoxia for and against the Origenist monks. Let the reader consult
Socrates and Sozomen. Nor should much weight be given to the judgment of those
who also entered Aerius in the roll of heretics because he maintained that
the dead should not be invoked, that offerings or prayers should not be made
for the dead, and that fixed

fast days should not be established. And if, as is alleged, he made a
secession because of these and the many other corruptions creeping into the
church, because of the growing arrogance of bishops who usurped the rights of
presbyters and of the church and aspired to dominion in most places, it is
probable that he did so. It is not likely that he favored the Arians, for who
was a more bitter enemy to Aerius than his own Arian bishop Eustathius?
Epiphanius alone charges Aerius with Arianism, followed by those who later
took the same claim from Epiphanius. Theodoret, Socrates, Evagrius, and
Sozomen record nothing of the sort. *Jerome certainly agreed with Aerius, so
all the less should we care that Aerius is so often cast at us by unjust men.
If Aerius was a heretic in this matter, he had Jerome as his companion in
heresy—and not him alone, but other ancient fathers as well, Greek and Latin
alike, as Medina admits. Aerius held that a presbyter does not differ from a
bishop by divine right and authority; Jerome maintains this very thing and
defends it with the same testimonies of Scripture that Aerius used. Everyone
can see how ineptly and childishly Epiphanius answered those testimonies.*
Saravia acknowledges that Jerome’s opinion was in agreement with Aerius.
*That Epiphanius numbered this casting down of bishops among the heresies was
the judgment of a private individual, and not a bad one, for Augustine too
subscribed to it; nevertheless, it was not a public definition of the church.
Indeed, Jerome read the most recent book On Heresies by Epiphanius, who was
still alive, yet on this equality of bishops and presbyters which Epiphanius
condemned he dissented from him, and believed that Epiphanius had wrongly
placed this equality among the heresies,* says Spalatensis. *Michael Medina,*
says Bellarmine, *in book 1, chapter 5, On the Origin and Continence of Sacred
Men, affirms that St. Jerome thought entirely the same as the Aerians, and
that not only Jerome belonged to that heresy but also Ambrose, Augustine,
Sedulius, Primasius, Chrysostom, Theodoret, Oecumenius, and Theophylact. And
so,* says Medina, *these men, otherwise most holy and most deeply versed in
the sacred Scriptures, held an opinion which the church nevertheless
condemned first in Aerius, then in the Waldensians, and finally in John
Wycliffe. And below: Therefore in Jerome and those Greek fathers this opinion
was formerly either passed over in silence or tolerated in them because of
the honor and reverence due them; but in heretics, because they also departed
from the church in many other matters, it was always condemned as heretical.*
Petrus Suavis relates in his History of the Council of Trent that, when the
celebrated passage of Jerome and the authority of Augustine acknowledging
that the episcopate was an ecclesiastical constitution were adduced, Michael
Medina replied *that it was no wonder if Jerome, Augustine, or any other of
the fathers had fallen into that heresy, since the matter was not entirely
clear. The boldness of his statement that Jerome and Augustine had held
heretical views greatly disturbed his hearers. Yet that doctor, rendered all
the more tenacious of his opinion by this very response,*

*defended it to the best of his ability.* I could cite several more who
contend that Jerome and other fathers agreed with Aerius. Richard Bancroft,
who was eventually elevated to the archbishopric of Canterbury, preached a
sermon in London at the cross called Paul’s Cross on February 9, 1588, while
the estates of the realm were assembled. He said that Aerius had been
condemned for heresy by the consensus of the universal church because he had
asserted that by divine law there was no difference between a bishop and a
presbyter, and that in Aerius the Puritans had been condemned by the church.
The most distinguished John Rainolds was asked by letter by the most
illustrious Francis Knollys, one of the queen’s counselors, to give his
opinion on Bancroft’s assertion. In reply Rainolds adduces the Jesuit
Bellarmine acknowledging the weakness of Epiphanius’s response to Aerius’s
argument. He proves his position from Jerome and from other writers who were
contemporaries of Epiphanius or flourished after him. Augustine, he argues,
considered the assertion that a presbyter ought to be distinguished from a
bishop by no difference to be heretical only because he found it reported by
Epiphanius, while Augustine himself did not know how far the name “heresy”
should be extended, as he testifies in the preface of On Heresies to
Quodvultdeus. Yet Augustine himself, Rainolds says, in fact declares at
Epistle 19 that by divine law there is no difference between a bishop and a
presbyter. He also cites Jewel against Harding, who asserts the same thing as
Bancroft and adduces Chrysostom, Augustine, Jerome, and Ambrose. From Michael
Medina he adds Theodoret, Primasius, Sedulius, and Theophylact. He himself
adds Oecumenius on 1 Timothy 3; Anselm, archbishop of Canterbury, on the
Epistle to Titus; another Anselm in Collection of Canons, book 7, chapters 87
and 127; Pope Gregory and Gratian, distinctions 39 and 95; and the gloss on
those distinctions. *To these can be added,* he says, *the fact that those who
have labored during these past five hundred years to reform the church taught
that all pastors, whether called bishops or presbyters, possess equal
authority and power from the word of God. First the Waldensians,* Aeneas
Sylvius, History of Bohemia, chapter 35, and Pighius, Ecclesiastical Hierarchy,
book 2, chapter 15; *then Marsilius of Padua,* Defender of Peace, part 2,
chapter 15; *Wycliffe and his disciples,* Thomas Waldensis, Doctrine of the
Faith, tome 1, book 2, chapter 60, and tome 2, chapter 7; *afterward Hus and
the Hussites,* Aeneas Sylvius in the cited place; *Luther,* Against the
Falsely-Named Order, epistle, and Against the Roman Papacy; *Calvin,* on
Philippians 1 and Titus 1; *Brentius,* Apology of the Wittenberg Confession,
chapter 21; *Bullinger,* Decade 5, sermon 3; *Musculus,* Commonplaces, title On

Lambert, and others about whom Foxe writes in Acts and Monuments. Dr. Fulke,
against Bristow’s motive 40 and against the Rhemists, title 1.5, *agree on
this, as do, among foreign nations, all those I have read who treat this
argument and, without doubt, several others whom I have not yet read.* Later
he appends the common opinion of the Reformed churches in Switzerland,
Savoy, France, Germany, Belgium, Hungary, and Poland. I think that Aerius
asserted these three things. First, that by divine law there is no difference
between a bishop and a presbyter, for which reason he drew an argument from
Scripture, Philippians 1. Second, that by ecclesiastical law a presbyter ought
to be distinguished from a bishop by no difference—that is, that the church
cannot establish any essential difference between a bishop and a presbyter.
Third, that no essential difference had in fact yet been introduced in his
own age. As to the first, Augustine himself held the same when he wrote:
*Although according to the titles of honor which usage has now established
in the church the episcopate is greater than the presbyterate, nevertheless
in many things Augustine is less than Jerome.* He says that the episcopate is
a greater honor than the presbyterate and that by the church’s usage its name
is a title of greater honor than “presbyterate.” He does not say that by the
church’s custom he is greater than Jerome merely by the title “bishop.” For
this would have done little to win Jerome’s goodwill. He therefore says that,
although as a bishop he has attained a greater honor by the usage now
prevailing in the church than Jerome has as a presbyter, nevertheless in many
things Augustine is less than and inferior to Jerome. Augustine listed the
second among the heresies because he understood it as Epiphanius had reported
it, not because he himself determined that it was a heresy. For he admits
that he does not know how far the definition of heresy should extend. He
enumerates heresies which he found noted by other writers, yet he does not
apply the definition of heresy to each of them. And certainly, if by divine
law there is no difference between a bishop and a presbyter, it is not heresy
to assert that there ought to be no essential difference between them. For
the church cannot lawfully alter what is of divine law—indeed, not even the
apostles themselves could do so, as we said above. As to the third, Aerius
maintained that as well, as is clear from the arguments he draws from the
present state and practice of the church. *The bishop lays on hands, and so
does the presbyter. The bishop sits on a throne, and so does the presbyter,*
says Aerius, and Epiphanius could not deny these things. Without doubt, if
the writings of Aerius survived, we would have received a fuller enumeration
of the things which in his age were common to bishops and presbyters even by
ecclesiastical law, and of the arguments he brought against bishops who
aspired to a greater power than belonged to them by divine or ecclesiastical
law. Among those bishops was his own bishop, Eustathius of Sebaste, founder
of monasticism in Armenia and a bitter enemy of Aerius, who no less than
Epiphanius had been made a bishop from a monk.

Concerning the Waldensians and Albigensians and their descendants, the Poor
of Lyon, Rainerius of Pisa, who wrote around the year 1250, speaks as
follows: *Of all the sects which are or have been, none is more pernicious
than that of the Poor of Lyon, for three reasons. First, because it is more
long-lived: some say that it has endured from the time of Sylvester, while
others say from the time of the apostles. Second, because it is more
widespread, for there is scarcely any land in which this sect does not
creep. Third, because all the other sects inspire horror by the enormity of
their blasphemies against God, while this sect of the Leonists possesses a
great appearance of piety: they live justly before men, believe all things
rightly about God and all the articles contained in the Creed, and blaspheme
and hate only the Roman church.* Jacob de Riberia says in his collections on
Toulouse that they were outstanding in learning and erudition and that the
priests were not equal to the Waldensians in ability. Rainerius saw and heard
an uneducated peasant who could recite Job word for word, *and several others
who knew the New Testament perfectly.* These Waldensians and their offspring
taught that a presbyter and bishop were one and the same, as we heard above
from Thomas Waldensis; and that the ranks of ministers were the image of the
beast described in Revelation, as Ussher reports. Yet Rainerius says that this
sect—so speaks their adversary—spread widely through almost every region and
had endured from the time of the apostles, as some maintain. Michael of
Cesena, minister general of the Franciscans, who flourished 230 years ago and
was condemned by Pope John XXII and excommunicated by a bull directed against
him personally, asserted that *all priests, of whatever rank they may be, are
of equal authority, power, and jurisdiction by Christ’s institution,* as
Antoninus of Florence relates in part four of his Summa. Peter of Corbara
maintained the same. John of Wesel said that all bishops and presbyters were
equal. See Goldast in The Monarchy of the Empire. We therefore see that even
in the Cimmerian darkness and under a fully grown Antichrist this light shone
upon no small number. Why should I mention the testimonies of more recent
writers, when almost innumerable ones are readily available to anyone? *It is
agreed among everyone,* says Cassander, *that in the age of the apostles there
was no distinction between bishops and presbyters.* And we have taught that
after the apostles’ time a bishop was by ecclesiastical law only an
archpresbyter, while everything was conducted by the common counsel,
assistance, and consent of presbyters and bishop, although some men aspired to
exclusive power. We have also taught that, after the church’s senate was
excluded from common government, traces of common government nevertheless
remained in the chapter, and traces of the power of the keys remained among
parish priests as lesser prelates even in the corrupt church. And among the
champions of purer doctrine and reformation, the full and lawful power of
presbyters was always acknowledged, even in the most corrupt centuries.
