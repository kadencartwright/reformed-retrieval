TOPIC THREE

ON THE ONE AND TRIUNE GOD

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 153 | IA leaf 207 | PDF p. 207]

TOPIC THREE—THE ONE AND TRIUNE GOD

QUESTION I

Can God's Existence Be Demonstrated Invincibly against Atheists? Affirmative.

I. Although “the divine is ineffable and incomprehensible” (ἄρρητον καὶ
ἀκατάληπτον … τὸ Θεῖον), as Damascene rightly notes in book 1, chapter 4, and
although it is dangerous to speak true things about God because of his supreme
excellence (ὑπεροχὴν), as Cyprian says, nevertheless God has deigned to reveal
himself to us both in nature and in Scripture. Moreover, one who approaches
God must believe that God exists and that he rewards those who seek him,
Hebrews 11:6. The treatment of God therefore holds the first place in theology
and embraces within its compass the sum of all the saving wisdom that we must
learn. Thus, searching out what the Lord has revealed in fear and trembling,
we must not rashly pry into the secrets which he wished to reserve for himself,
lest “we be found unlawfully curious in the former, but damnably ungrateful in
the latter,” as Prosper says in book 1, chapter 7 of *The Calling of the
Gentiles*.

II. This treatment can be completed in three parts. First, that we may know
*that he is*, against atheists, with respect to his existence. Second, *what he
is*, against the Gentiles, with respect to his nature and attributes. Third,
*who he is*, against the Jews and heretics, with respect to the persons. The
first two treat God as considered essentially (οὐσιωδῶς); the last treats him
as considered personally (ὑποστατικῶς) and relatively with respect to the
persons.

III. Although *God exists* is the undoubted principle of religion, which ought
to be assumed rather than proved—so that one who ventures to deny it should be
restrained by punishments rather than refuted by arguments, as the Philosopher
says—nevertheless the execrable madness of the atheists, of whom this most
corrupt age is all too prolific, makes this question necessary today. They do
not blush impiously to oppose this most evident truth.

IV. The question is not whether a true and saving knowledge of God has a place
everywhere among human beings. For no one denies that the true God was and
still is unknown to most nations, which are therefore called “without God”
(ἄθεοι) by the apostle, Ephesians 2:12. Rather, the question is whether the
knowledge of deity is so implanted in human beings by nature that no one can
be wholly ignorant of it, or whether God's existence can be demonstrated by
invincible arguments, not only from Scripture itself but also from nature
itself. The profane and atheists do not shrink from denying this; we affirm it.

[Marginal heading: That God exists is proved.]

V. The demonstration of deity rests upon four principal foundations: 1. the
voice of all nature; 2. the contemplation of the human being himself; 3. the
testimony of conscience; and 4. the consensus of peoples. For God, the
wonderful maker of the world, impressed the trace of his majesty so deeply
upon each of its parts that what is commonly said about Minerva's shield must
be said here with far greater right. Phidias had inserted his own likeness
into that shield so skillfully that it could not be removed without destroying
the whole work. In the same way, God cannot be torn away from nature without
the whole of it perishing and being thrown into confusion.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 154 | IA leaf 208 | PDF p. 208]

[Marginal heading: 1. From the subordination of causes.]

VI. Nature demonstrates God when it cries out that it not only exists, but
exists from another and cannot exist without another. For if this must be
certain and indubitable—that *nothing comes to be from itself and nothing is
the cause of itself*, since it would thus be prior and posterior to itself—it
must also be certain that there is some first and unproduced being from which
all other things come, while it comes from none. For if every being were
produced, it would have to be produced either by itself or by another. It could
not be produced by itself because, as has just been said, nothing can be its
own cause. It could not always be produced by another, because then either
there would be an infinite regress among producing causes or a circle would be
formed, both of which involve a contradiction. As to the circle, it is clear
that it cannot occur, since among things made there is a last thing which made
no other. Add that such a circle is impossible because, if it were posited, it
would follow that the same thing made itself and was at least mediately its
own cause, than which nothing is more absurd. Nor can an infinite regress have
a place among causes, because among causes there must necessarily be some
order according to prior and posterior. But an infinite regress rejects every
order, for then there would be no first cause, but all would be intermediate
causes having some cause before themselves. Indeed, there would be no cause
which did not have infinitely many causes superior to itself before it, which
is incoherent (ἀνύστατον). For if there were infinitely many causes before
each and every cause, there would already be infinitely many causes before the
whole multitude and collection of causes, and thus that whole collection
would not exist. Again, if by ascending from effects to a cause one could
never arrive at the first cause, then by descending from causes to effects one
could never arrive at the final effect, because what is infinite can no more
be traversed by ascending than by descending. We must therefore necessarily
stop at some cause which is so first that it acknowledges no superior. Thus
that progression of causes ought not to be *to infinity* in the sense of an
endless regress, but *to the infinite*, in which it will terminate.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the newness of the world.]

VII. 2. The newness of the world together with the beginning of motion and
time necessarily proves that God exists. For if the world began, it must have
received its origin from someone, since it could not exist from itself; and it
could not exist from another except God. Many things prove that the world
began and is not eternal. Time cannot exist from eternity, for if it were
eternal, the alternation of night and day would have existed from eternity,
which is incoherent (ἀνύστατον). For it is contradictory that day and night
should have existed from eternity: either they would have existed
simultaneously, which involves a contradiction, or successively, which
abolishes eternity. Next, if time were eternal, there could have been no first
day. For if there is a first day, then time had a beginning; but if there is no
first day, every day was preceded by a day—that is, there was a day before
there was a day. Third, if time were eternal, then there would have been
infinitely many years, infinitely many months, days, and hours. But the number
of months, years, and days would either be equal or unequal. If equal, it would
follow that the part equals the whole and the whole is not greater than its
part. If unequal, there would be more and less in the infinite. Fourth, either
no day existed from eternity, every day existed from eternity, or only some
day did. If the first, time is not from eternity. Experience teaches that the
second is false. The third cannot be said, because if some day existed from
eternity, its duration would have to be infinite and without a beginning,
which involves a contradiction, since its duration is bounded by twenty-four
hours. Fifth, every motion occurs with succession, which includes prior and
posterior and is contrary to eternity. Sixth, if the sun moved itself and
illuminated the globe from eternity, it did so either with respect to our
hemisphere, with respect to the opposite hemisphere among the antipodes, or
with respect to both at once. The first cannot be said because what occurs
successively cannot be called eternal; neither can the last, because it is
impossible for the sun to illuminate both

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 155 | IA leaf 209 | PDF p. 209]

hemispheres at once. It would both have been in several places at the same
time, and it would have been day everywhere and night nowhere.

VIII. If human beings had existed from eternity, there would be infinitely
many generations of human beings succeeding one another, and the number of
human beings who have lived up to the present would be infinite. But there
cannot be an infinite regress in generations, as we have already said, nor can
the number of human beings who have lived up to the present be infinite,
since it increases every day, while nothing can be added to the infinite. Next,
either some human being existed from eternity or none did. If none did, human
beings are not from eternity and were not produced by God. If someone did, he
could not have been begotten by another, since what is produced by another is
posterior to that other; and what is eternal acknowledges nothing prior to
itself.

IX. The atheists vainly bring forward the consideration of eternity *a parte
post* to confirm the possibility of an infinite regress. They argue that just
as, when descending in eternity *a parte post*, there is a first even though
there is no last and one never arrives at the last, so when ascending in
eternity *a parte ante* there can be a last even though there is no first. But
things wholly dissimilar are compared here: what existed and actually passed
away, and what will never come to be or pass away. For the duration of past
time and the succession of human beings who have lived up to the present
existed and no longer exists. But the duration of the age to come and of the
human beings who will live in it is such that it can never be said to have
passed away. Next, there can indeed be a beginning of a thing without an end,
as in the duration of angels and souls, but there cannot be an end without a
beginning, because an end necessarily presupposes some beginning from which
the thing took its origin. Therefore the consequence is invalid: In eternity
*a parte post* there can be a duration which had a beginning and will have no
end; therefore in eternity *a parte ante* there can be a duration which has an
end and yet never had a beginning.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the adornment of the world.]

X. 3. The wonderful adornment and order of the world proves the same thing.
For if order requires wisdom and intelligence, the most perfect order
necessarily presupposes the most perfect and infinite wisdom, which we call
God. The person who does not see the most beautiful order existing everywhere
is blind, and the person who does not confess it is thoroughly wicked. It is
seen in so fitting an arrangement of the parts, so constant a harmony among
such discordant things, so beautiful a concord and agreement among the most
diverse creatures, so swift and yet uniform a motion of the heavenly bodies,
and so unchangeable a stability and constancy of the order once established.
Thus not only do the heavens declare the glory of God, but every blade of
grass and little flower in a meadow, every little stone on the shore, and every
little shell in the sea proclaims not only his power and goodness but also his
manifold wisdom (πολυποίκιλον), which is so evident to everyone that God may
be found even by feeling after him. Hence Augustine says in *The City of God*,
book 11, chapter 4: “Apart from the prophetic voices, the world itself, by its
most orderly changeability and motion and the most beautiful appearance of
all visible things, silently proclaims in a certain way both that it was made
and that it could have been made only by God, who is ineffably and invisibly
great and ineffably and invisibly beautiful.”

XI. Perhaps you will say that these things were arranged this way by chance
and a fortuitous collision of atoms. But I do not know whether so impious and
absurd a fabrication deserves refutation, since these things indicate not
chance (τύχην) but the greatest artistry (τέχνην). For things that occur by
chance are uncertain and disordered, having nothing constant or like
themselves. But nothing can be imagined more ordered and composed than this
structure of the universe. Therefore, to say that this most ornate and
beautiful world was made by a fortuitous collision of atoms is the same as if
someone thought that, *if innumerable forms of the twenty-one letters were
thrown together in one place, from them could be produced*

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 156 | IA leaf 210 | PDF p. 210]

*the Annals of Ennius when they were shaken out upon the ground, so that they
could then be read*, as Cicero says in *On the Nature of the Gods*, book 2. In
the same place he also splendidly says from Aristotle: “Suppose there were
people who had always lived underground in good and splendid dwellings but
had never come out above the earth, and had received by report that there was
a certain deity and power of the gods. Suppose that afterward, at some time,
the mouths of the earth were opened and they were able to escape and come out
into these places which we inhabit. When they suddenly saw the earth and seas
and sky, beheld the sun and recognized both its most beautiful magnitude and
its efficacy, and then perceived the whole heaven distinguished by stars,
together with the risings and settings of them all and their fixed and
unchangeable courses throughout all eternity—when they saw these things, they
would surely judge both that the gods exist and that these great works are the
works of the gods.”

[Marginal heading: 4. From the tendency of all things toward an end.]

XII. 4. The tendency of all things toward an end confirms this same point.
For since all natural beings act for the sake of some end which they always
attain certainly and infallibly, they must be governed by the counsel of some
ruler. Since nature does nothing in vain, if it acts for the sake of an end,
either it must itself know and seek that end, or, if it neither knows nor seeks
it, it must be directed toward it by another. But many natural things are
incapable of counsel because they are either inanimate or irrational (ἄλογοι),
and therefore they need another's counsel by which they may be directed. That
other counsel can be attributed to none but the author and ruler of nature.
Nor should it be said that nature itself exists in individual things and that
by its counsel the individual things are directed to their ends. For that
nature will be either the natural property of each thing and the particular
nature of each—but how can it be capable of counsel if the things themselves
are brute?—or some common nature collected from the natures of individual
things. But a common nature does not exist outside the individual natures. Or
it will be some intelligent and subsistent substance by whose counsel all
things are directed. But this is to make God out of nature and, while denying
God, to acknowledge God under the name of nature. To this pertains the saying
of the Philosopher that the work of nature is the work of an intelligence
which does not err.

[Marginal heading: 5. From the human being himself.]

XIII. 5. But a human being does not need witnesses summoned from afar or need
to go outside himself, since within his own bosom he has a domestic teacher of
this truth. If he turns his sight away from all other things and reflects upon
himself, he will recognize no less wisdom in the microcosm than in the
macrocosm and will marvel at divinity visible in the body and sparkling in the
mind. For whence comes the body, constructed with such wonderful and plainly
astonishing artistry? Whence come so many unlike members, formed together by
mutual interweaving and so suitably arranged for the proper duties of each,
except from the immense Spirit? Whence comes the mind, a particle of divine
breath, equipped with so many faculties and adorned with so many gifts, except
from the supreme intelligence? This image certainly represents its prototype,
and there is no one who, if he is willing to pay attention, will not only hear
and see God present in himself but in a certain way even touch and feel him.

[Marginal heading: 6. From conscience.]

XIV. 6. This is especially taught by the power and goad of conscience, the
inseparable companion of a crime either contemplated or committed. Its
sensation cannot be blunted, its accusation evaded, its testimony corrupted,
its summons abandoned, or its tribunal avoided. For how is it that conscience
is distressed when a crime has been committed even in secret and with all
witnesses removed, where no danger from human beings threatens—even in those
who are placed at the highest pinnacle—unless it is affected by a deeply
rooted sense of deity? This was seen in Nero, Caligula, and not a few others.
Hence Juvenal says in *Satire* 13:

Why do you think that they have escaped,
whom a mind conscious of a dreadful deed holds thunderstruck,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 157 | IA leaf 211 | PDF p. 211]

and strikes with a silent blow,
while the hidden torturer shakes the lash within the soul, and so forth?

Ovid, *Fasti*:

As each person's mind is conscious of his own deeds, so within his breast
he conceives hope and fear according to what he has done.

Whence come those terrors of conscience which tremble at more atrocious
crimes, unless from the sense of some avenger and judge whom, although it does
not see him, it nevertheless perceives everywhere? For those terrors cannot
arise from some fear of civil laws or of a temporal punishment or disgrace.
First, these are feared only because of manifest crimes, the only ones civil
laws and judges acting according to them can punish. Second, such terrors also
seize those who acknowledge no superior on earth and who therefore can be
judged by no one. Again, how is it that when an unforeseen danger presses in
or a sudden terror arises, those who seemed to have erased all divinity from
their mind dread an angry deity and implore his help with sudden prayers and
sighs? And what makes those tremble who are profane in secret and in hiding,
or who deny the existence of God only within their own mind? Perhaps you will
say that the fear is empty. But if it is empty, where does it come from? Why is
it so persistent and unconquerable even when there is no cause for fear? Whom
or what is feared by the person who alone is conscious of his own thoughts
and is persuaded that no one is their observer, witness, or judge? Does he
fear himself? But he is his own best friend. Does he fear others? But they do
not know what he thinks or plans in his mind. Then, if they wish to be safe,
let them approve with the mouth what they deny in the heart. Why are they not
secure then? Therefore, willing or unwilling, they must believe that some God
exists, whom right reason itself teaches them to fear and commands them to
acknowledge everywhere as the Lord and judge of all.

XV. Nor is it an obstacle that Paul in Ephesians 4:19 says that human beings
sometimes arrive at such a degree of wickedness that they become “past
feeling” (ἀπηλγηκότες)—that is, devoid of every pain conscience otherwise
tends to implant—and even that they have a conscience “seared with a hot iron”
(κεκαυτηριασμένην), and thus deprived of every feeling and remorse, as the
apostle says in 1 Timothy 4:2. For this indeed indicates the effort and zeal of
such profane people and the outwardly visible effect of that effort, when
those wicked people externally display confidence and a disposition hardened
toward crimes, as though they were oppressed by no sense of their crimes or
bite of conscience. But it does not show what they are like inwardly in their
souls. This is not visible, and they can conceal it in public. Although they
appear outwardly to be without feeling and without pain, and will even openly
affirm that they are without it, yet if one could see within them, their soul
would appear restless and agitated by the harshest goads. Nevertheless, I do
not wish to deny that through the habit of sinning they can bring such a
callus over conscience that sometimes and for a time they seem to have lost
every sense of sin and neither feel nor care about the bite of conscience,
especially when everything flows according to their desire, their strength
remains, their health is firm, and public favor is present. But it cannot be
said that they have wholly lost every sense. Conscience is asleep, not dead;
it is intoxicated by the flesh, not extinguished. Otherwise, how could Paul
say that human beings *who do not have the law are a law to themselves and
show the work of the law written in their hearts, while their conscience bears
witness together with it and their thoughts from time to time and at intervals
accuse and excuse one another in things done badly or well*?

[Marginal heading: 7. From the consensus of peoples.]

XVI. 7. To these is added the constant and perpetual sense and consensus of
all peoples. Although they have held different opinions about the nature and
number of the deity and the manner and mode of

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 158 | IA leaf 212 | PDF p. 212]

worshiping it, and for the most part have erred most shamefully, nevertheless
amid such great variety there was this consensus: that there is some God who
must be worshiped religiously. Cicero says in *On the Laws*, book 1: “There is
no nation so savage and untamed that, even if it does not know what kind of
God it ought to have, it nevertheless knows that it ought to have one.” Indeed,
this notion has driven such deep roots in human minds that people persuade
themselves of anything more readily than that there is no God, and prefer to
have a false god rather than none. Hence it happened that they lowered
themselves to stones, pieces of wood, and all the basest things rather than be
without every deity. This would never have happened in a human being who is
proud by nature unless the conviction of divinity were most powerful. But if
at times there are certain human monsters who with gigantic audacity declared
war upon nature itself in order to deny God—as the Psalm testifies that the
fool says in his heart that there is no God, Psalm 14—apart from the fact that
this must be understood of practical rather than theoretical atheists, as will
be proved afterward, they must not be opposed to the common and general
consensus of all. Nor should the frenzied effort of a few who strive to
suppress this knowledge and even die in that obstinacy prejudice the universal
judgment of the rest, which, spread everywhere throughout all regions of the
world, has endured most constantly for so many ages. Monsters and prodigies
which at times seem contrary to nature can no more overthrow the ordinary
laws established by God, nor does the insanity of a few deranged people
overthrow the definition of the human being as a rational animal.

XVII. This constant and universal consensus of all peoples concerning this
primordial truth cannot arise from a simple desire, which in many would tend
more toward the removal of the deity they dread on account of their crimes
than toward his presence. Nor does it rest only upon the authority of the more
powerful or the tradition of ancestors, which could never have been so
effective as to produce this universal consensus in the minds of all. It must
therefore take its origin from the evidence of the thing itself, which is so
great that it can escape no one who possesses a mind, and from the deeply
rooted sense of deity which God impressed upon each person. Thus no one could
flee to the pretext of ignorance; rather, since every person understands that
God exists and is his maker, they might be condemned by their own testimony
because they did not worship him. To this pertain these words of Iamblichus in
*On the Mysteries of the Egyptians*, chapter 1: “Before every use of reason a
knowledge of the gods is naturally implanted, indeed a certain touch of
divinity better than knowledge.” This is “what may be known of God” (τὸ
γνωστὸν τοῦ Θεοῦ), which God willed to reveal among the Gentiles, Romans 1:19.
Damascene explains this in book 1, chapter 1, saying, “The knowledge that God
exists is naturally implanted by him in everyone” (πᾶσι ἡ γνῶσις τοῦ εἶναι
Θεὸν ὑπ’ αὐτοῦ φυσικῶς ἐγκατέσπαρται).

XVIII. It matters little whether we explain this sense as an implanted natural
knowledge of God, a common notion, a conception of the mind, or, with more
recent authors, an idea of God as the most perfect being impressed upon our
minds. The matter seems to come back to the same thing: a sense of divinity is
implanted in each person from birth which does not allow itself to be hidden
and spontaneously exerts itself in all adults possessing a sound mind. We
observe only that it is less proper to say that an idea of God is impressed
upon us if by it is meant some intelligible form and image of God in our minds
which clearly and distinctly represents God's quiddity and essence to us.
Both God's infinite majesty rejects this and our finite and frail intelligence
does not grasp it. For how could an image of an infinite being exist in a
finite mind, either at all adequate or clear and distinct? Nor, if someone can
know with certainty from this common notion and the instinct of conscience
*that God exists*, does it immediately follow that he can grasp with a clear
and distinct conception, such as the idea of which we speak is supposed to be,
*who and what he is*. Moreover,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 159 | IA leaf 213 | PDF p. 213]

it is certain that a clear and distinct idea of God, if any is given in us, is
obtained not from nature, which has been darkened and blinded by sin, but only
from the supernatural revelation of the Word, in which God openly revealed
himself to us. Even this knowledge, in comparison with glory, is only through
a mirror and in an enigma—that is, still imperfect and very obscure.

XIX. Not a few other arguments can be added to confirm the same truth: from
predictions of future and merely contingent events, which could not have been
foretold with certainty long before the event except by an omniscient mind;
from the heroic impulses of great men, which could not have arisen without
divine inspiration; from the changes and overthrows of empires, which the
thing itself cries out happen by the finger of God; from public judgments and
the punishments of crimes inflicted by avenging Nemesis; and from miracles
which surpass the power of all nature. For since nothing can act beyond its
own powers, and not only Scripture testifies but Jews and Gentiles also
acknowledge that many miracles have frequently occurred in the world, there
must be some most perfect being, greater and more excellent than all nature,
to whose power they may be ascribed. This being can be none other than God.

XX. These and similar arguments, drawn from the contemplation of the divine
works and brought forth from the innermost recesses of nature, by which these
blasphemous fighters against God (θεομάχοι) can be pierced, are confirmed more
clearly by the irrefragable testimony of the Word. Since it bears before
itself the illustrious marks of its divinity, as seen previously, and
everywhere proclaims God as its author, it engraves this conviction upon the
minds of believers with an indelible character.

XXI. These arguments *ad hominem* can also be added. They can move the atheist
himself to believe in a deity, if not for the sake of vindicating God himself
and religion, at least for his own sake and for the sake of his own advantage.
For if there is no God, no commonwealth and no society in the world will be
safe, because without virtue and religion nothing can be safe; but if there is
no God, there is neither virtue nor religion. What will the world be except
mere brigandage, in which whatever anyone pleases will be permitted, there
will be nothing right or wrong, no right to command, no necessity to obey, the
best person will be the craftiest, and the master the most powerful? No bridle
will restrain the license of rulers and the rebellion of subjects; everyone
will serve his own inclination with impunity. Again, if there is no God, no
mortal will be safe or secure for even a moment from violence, fraud, perjury,
murder, and bloodshed. Everything will have to be feared each hour. For when
the barrier of deity is removed, what place will be given to fidelity and
innocence? What will not lie open to license or violence? As to human edicts,
apart from the fact that they cannot change the disposition for the better but
instead render it crafty and intent upon every art of deception, what place
will be given to human edicts if, once the sense of deity has been removed,
conscience casts off every consideration of right and wrong?

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XXII. Although God is not comprehensively open to the senses as he is in
himself, he can nevertheless be apprehensively perceived insofar as he shines
forth in his works, is seen in signs, is heard in the Word, and is manifested
in the structure of the whole universe. 2. It is falsely supposed that nothing
is in the intellect which was not previously in the senses. For universals are
in the intellect and never were in the senses. The soul is known, as is an
angel, although they are neither sensed nor seen except from their effects.
Why, then, should God not be most certainly known by the mind from his works
and *a posteriori*, though we cannot perceive him with our eyes or the other
bodily senses?

XXIII. Apparent confusion and disorder (ἀταξίαν) in the world with respect to
us is one thing, which we concede; true and real disorder on God's part is
another,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 160 | IA leaf 214 | PDF p. 214]

which we deny. For the things which seem disordered to us are most orderly
before God.

XXIV. Although various things in the world seem useless, and indeed many seem
harmful and dangerous, fitted for their own destruction and the extreme misery
of the human race—such as dreadful fire-belching mountains which, by their
continual eruption of flames and ashes, bury fields, villages, cities, and
whole regions together with human beings and other living creatures beneath a
most unjust fate; whirlpools in the sea; shipwrecking rocks; poisonous plants;
harmful animals; and other things of this kind—it does not follow that the
world was not created by some supremely good and wise being and is not
directed by the same being. For apart from the fact that the Creator's glory
shines forth most clearly from them all, nothing seems so useless and harmful
to us that it is not serviceable in various ways both to human beings and to
other creatures. Nor, if we cannot grasp the various uses of these things,
does it immediately follow that there are none.

XXV. The prosperity of the wicked and adversity of the godly display the
supremely wise dispensation of him who turns all these things to his own glory
and the salvation of the godly. They ought not, however, to weaken belief in
the deity. Indeed, they confirm the truth of the final judgment to be executed
after this life, when each person will receive things corresponding to his
faith and deeds.

XXVI. The infinite good does not immediately remove all evils if it is a most
free and not a necessary agent, because it judges it to pertain more to its
wisdom and omnipotence to permit evil so as to elicit good from it than not to
allow evil to exist.

XXVII. When God is said to exist *from himself*, this should be understood
negatively—because he is from no one, for he is self-life (αὐτοζωὴ) and
self-existent (αὐτοὼν)—rather than positively, as though he were his own
cause. That involves a contradiction, because he would be prior and posterior
to himself.

XXVIII. It is one thing to use religion and the sense of deity to compel
peoples to obedience and keep them within the bounds of duty; it is another to
impose such a conviction of deity, even a false one, upon peoples. I admit
that legislators did the former and that crafty people invented many things
in religion by which they might instill reverence and strike terror into the
common people so as to make their dispositions more compliant. But they would
never have accomplished this unless human minds had already been imbued with
that constant conviction concerning God, from which an inclination toward
