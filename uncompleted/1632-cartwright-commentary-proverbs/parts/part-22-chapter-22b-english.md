Verse 4. The reward of humility together with reverence for Jehovah is riches, honour, and life.

In עקב (*praemium*, reward) there is a metaphor from the heel, the extreme part of the body, to signify the recompense which is given at the end, or heel, of a work. Thus it is evident that the Papists are deluded when they infer merit from the words *recompense* and *reward*.

The purpose is to encourage people toward sincere and wholly unfeigned submission of mind.

It has been aptly said, Παραπεπήγασι ταῖς ἀρεταῖς αἱ κακίαι, καὶ εἰσὶ πῶς ἀγχίθυροι (*The vices have been fastened alongside the virtues and are, as it were, next-door neighbours*). For there is a certain humility in appearance which does not deserve the name of true humility. Thus some people, from a certain dejection of mind, flee from the duties which fall upon them by reason of their calling. See the example of Saul, 1 Sam. 10.22. Others make a display of great submission of mind while inwardly they swell with pride in their mind. See the example of Absalom, 2 Sam. 15.5. Very many among us are found in this class: they profess themselves servants to people over whom they would exercise dominion if they could and over whom they think themselves worthy to obtain rule. These are the people whose mind is erect and lofty when their heads are lowered and their knees bent. Even in divine worship there is a certain humility of idolaters which is hateful to God; the Prophet speaks of it at Isa. 2.9. There is likewise another humility which arises only from a sense of divine wrath and fear of punishment, such as Ahab's, 1 Kings 21.27.

The Prophet therefore distinguishes true humility of mind from those masked forms by this distinguishing mark, the fear of God, so that true humility is finally that by which a person, from fear of God and a serious perception of his own worthlessness and iniquity, thinks submissively about himself. For one who is furnished with true knowledge of God, affected by the greatness of the divine majesty, and afraid for himself of offending him must be cast down by the consciousness of his own baseness, which cannot fail to arraign him as wholly unfit for and averse to obedience to God.

He exhorts people to this submission of mind from its accompanying reward, and that a manifold one. First, in general God has promised the conveniences of this life to the godly and, by that very fact, to those of submissive mind, 1 Tim. 4; Deut. 28. More particularly, God adorns those who are of submissive mind with the blessing of riches. For just as valleys are made fruitful by waters running down from the mountains, so the humble and submissive in mind are enriched by the blessing of God, which alone can make people rich. An example of this appears in Abraham, who, after with singular

[1632 witness | PDF p. 490 | printed columns 964-965]

submission of mind he had yielded to Lot and had given him the choice to select whichever part of the land he pleased, immediately received the promise of a broad domain and a most ample inheritance, Gen. 13.14, while Lot suffered the loss of everything as the recompense for his arrogance. To this pertains Christ's saying, [*Blessed are the meek, because they will possess the earth by inheritance.*] Likewise Ps. 22.27.

That honour is further given to those who are humble and submissive in mind is declared by the Proverb so often repeated: *He who humbles himself will be exalted*; just as, conversely, he who exalts himself is cast down. Likewise that saying at Prov. 18.12: *Before destruction a man's mind is exalted, and submission of mind precedes honour.* See 1 Pet. 5.6.

Finally, life is promised to the person who is of a submissive spirit. For those who cultivate peace—such as those who are of a humble spirit, just as the proud, according to Solomon, are contentious—enjoy a long and pleasant life, Ps. 34; 1 Pet. 3.10; Ps. 22.27.

It is known that each of these three things is sought separately with great effort, the highest exertion, and the greatest vigilance. But the Holy Spirit indicates to us the shortest summary for obtaining all these things. In human judgment, one who is of a humble and peaceable spirit appears likely to be deprived above all of all these benefits, since because of his meekness and his mind utterly alien to vengeance he appears likely to be pillaged and trampled by everyone. But the Prophet meets this difficulty when he teaches that by humility a road is prepared for enjoying these things. Conversely, in human judgment the one way to acquire riches, honour, and so forth appears to be for someone to make a display of himself and exalt himself, and so forth.

Verse 5. Stings and snares are in the way of the perverse man; he who watches over his soul is far from them.

In צנים (*aculei*, stings) there is a metaphor from the endeavour of fishermen and their skilful effort in transfixing fish with a hook. There is also another metaphor from hunters and fowlers ensnaring wild beasts and birds in the word פחים (*laquei*, snares).

The purpose is to exhort people to the pursuit of godliness and uprightness by deterring them from the enticements of sinning.

In the former proposition, where it is said that stings and snares are in the way of the perverse man, it is asked, first, who is perverse? The answer is that every wicked and wholly unregenerate person is perverse, since supreme perversity is not present in all the wicked. For the more people of this kind are pressed toward godliness, the more averse they are to it; like quicklime, the more water of salutary admonitions is poured upon them, the more they are ignited toward sins of every kind. And just as a river is carried downward with so much greater violence the more obstacles are placed to retard its course, so the more they are pressed by God's commandments, the farther they withdraw from duty, Rom. 7.9, 10, 11. Therefore, the argument of the Papists who endeavour to undermine the truth of the Gospel from the fact that more enormous sins are committed in the time of the Gospel than were perpetrated under the papacy is frivolous, as though the Gospel patronized sins because the ungodly leap over all the barriers of honour and shame. If absolutely none who cultivated godliness and righteousness were found in the Evangelical Church, they would perhaps with good reason—certainly with a greater pretext—accuse the Gospel itself. But since not a few truly godly people, by God's grace, are found among us, let those who turn the crimes of certain people who break through the Gospel's restraints into a fault of the Gospel itself see how equitable judges of affairs they are.

In the way, or course of life, of this perverse man are snares and stings. What else are they

[1632 witness | PDF p. 491 | printed columns 966-967]

than the evils of this life, and above all of the life to come, by which the wicked man who encounters them is pierced through and held entangled? See Job 18.8, 9, 10, and so forth. But see there, and at 22.10, how numerous they are. For he is said to walk in nets and traps and to be surrounded by snares. There they are also said to be hidden, so that he is entangled in them before he perceives it.

Here it is asked who those fishermen and fowlers are who construct these snares. They are many: Satan and the wicked, his satellites. For God, as a most just judge, permits the perverse man to be taken by the snares of Satan and the ungodly, by which he may rush to destruction. Yet the perverse man himself supplies to all these people the bronze and iron from which the hook is made and the thread from which the snares are fashioned. Indeed, the most excellent good things—such as the preaching of the word, the administration of the sacraments, and finally Christ himself—are like snares and hooks to the perverse, by which they bring destruction upon themselves. See Isa. 8.14, 15. Consequently, they stumble not only against errors, schisms, and heresies, but also against the truth and every most holy doctrine, not only from zeal for the crimes which they love and embrace but also from hatred of virtue and of its followers; and they stumble not only against adverse circumstances but also against prosperous ones which flow according to their mind's desire. Hence their misery reaches the point that, whichever way they turn themselves, everything is full of snares and hooks.

From this it is evident how true is that statement at 2 Pet. 2.12, where the wicked are said to be like wild beasts made and destined for capture and destruction. Indeed, after the manner of country people they place their necks in snares of their own accord. More than this, they become even more brutish than the brutes themselves. For wild beasts strive with the greatest effort to extricate themselves, and if perhaps they are freed, they afterward become more cautious. The perverse, however, delight in their snares and, by repeating their crimes, fall back into the same evils which they escaped.

In the latter hemistich we are taught how we may escape these snares and hooks, namely by watching, and so forth. If someone understands that snares are being constructed for him or that traps of any kind are being set, he will walk cautiously and will carefully watch his steps lest he fall into them unawares. In the same manner Solomon teaches that provision must be made with the utmost care lest we perish entangled in those traps of sins and offences. For if we take such solicitous care against the snares to which the body is exposed, how great must be the vigilance applied so that we preserve safe and sound the soul itself, by the preservation of which there will be no danger in the members of the body! Prov. 4.23.

Let us observe further that this teaching is delivered to the godly and those who fear God. Although their way is not strewn with snares and hooks, they can nevertheless fall into snares of this kind just as people can fall into traps set for wild beasts.

Next it must be noted that he says the person who watches over his soul is far from them. For it is not sufficient if we do not perpetrate the things which are evil unless we also withdraw as far as possible from them by avoiding their occasions and enticements. Nor should we be like elephants which, although they refuse to enter water, nevertheless desire to remain near it.

This Proverb can also be taken actively, so that its meaning is that the perverse man constructs snares for others, especially for the godly. Here he intimates that the perverse man has no more regard for the godly than fowlers have for birds or hunters for wild beasts.
