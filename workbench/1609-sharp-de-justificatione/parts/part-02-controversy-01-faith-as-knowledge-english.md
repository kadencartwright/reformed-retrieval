### First controversy, concerning the instrumental cause

Bellarmine, in book 1 *On Justification*, disputes against us concerning faith,
the instrumental cause of justification. In order that the whole dispute may
be better understood, however, first the several senses of the word “faith”
must be examined from Scripture; second, the causes and definition of faith
must be explained.

The name “faith” is used variously in Scripture.

1. For the profession of the doctrine of faith, as in Romans 1:8.

2. For a particular instance in relation to a deed, if one regards the deed
itself rather than those who perform it, as in Matthew 9:2.

3. For the simple knowledge by which the things contained in Holy Scripture
are believed to be true. This is historical faith, which, unless it proceeds
further, is called dead and diabolical faith, as in James 2:9.

4. For the gift of miracles, 1 Corinthians 13:2. This is called miraculous
faith and is a persuasion concerning a future miraculous effect, conceived
from a particular revelation or promise, or from a prompting of the Holy
Spirit. Its object is the power of God, 1 Corinthians 12:9; Matthew 16:20. Its
own doubt is opposed to it and reproved in Numbers 20:12; Matthew 14:32 and
17:20.

5. For knowledge of the truth with a sense and taste of God’s grace, but one
that is fleeting. Such was the faith of Simon Magus, concerning which see Acts
8:13, and that of apostates and hypocrites, concerning whose faith see Luke
8:13; Hebrews 6:4 and 4:6. This is called temporary, feigned, and hypocritical
faith.

6. For faithfulness and constancy in keeping promises. It is attributed even
to God in Romans 3:3.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 22 | image 15574994 | printed p. 20]

7. For the doctrine of faith which we believe, 1 Timothy 3:9 and 4:1;
Galatians 1:23.

8. It signifies that by which we believe and by which, as an instrument, we
are justified, as in Romans 3 and elsewhere. Therefore the Master of the
*Sentences* rightly says, book 3, distinction 23, that faith is sometimes that
by which we believe and sometimes that which is believed. It is taken in this
sense in the present dispute.

Justifying faith is twofold with respect to its subject: the faith of infants
and that of adults. The faith of infants is kindled, without external means,
by the Holy Spirit in the souls of those infants who are elect and are in the
covenant of grace. According to their measure the Spirit illuminates and
regenerates their mind and will. Their faith, indeed, cannot possess the
knowledge or application of the promises of grace found in adults.
Nevertheless, infants have their own motions stirred up by the Holy Spirit,
since, according to the apostle in Hebrews 11:6, without faith it is impossible
to please God; and according to Christ in John 3:3, unless someone is born
again from above, he cannot see the kingdom of God. See also 1 Corinthians
15:50 and Revelation 21:27. It is impious, however, to exclude these infants
from salvation, since they are in the covenant, Acts 2:39. The example of John
the Baptist leaping in his mother’s womb, Luke 1:41, teaches that infants have
had these motions.

The faith of adults—which is to be treated in this dispute—must first be
explained from its causes.

The principal efficient cause is God the Father, who in the Son, through the
Spirit, begets and nourishes faith, John 6:29; Romans 12:3; Philippians 1:29;
Ephesians 2:8; Galatians 5:22; Hebrews 12:2; Luke 24:32, 45.

The instrumental efficient cause ordinarily is the ministry of the word and
sacraments, 1 Corinthians 3:5; Romans 1:16.

The matter, or subject in which faith resides, is the soul. For faith has its
seat both in the intellect, which is called *nous*,

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 23 | image 15574995 | printed p. 21]

Luke 24:45; Ephesians 4:23, and in the will or heart, Acts 16:14; Romans 10:10.

There is no matter from which faith is made. It is produced solely by the
motion and efficacy of the Holy Spirit working through the word, so that the
mind is enlightened to understand and the will is moved to assent and rest
content.

The subject to whom it is given is not all humanity, but only the elect, Acts
13:48; John 8:47; Titus 1:1.

Its object is not the decrees of the Church, but only the word of God, and
especially the promises of grace, or Christ promised and exhibited, Romans
1:17 and 4:26; 1 Corinthians 15:14.

Its form is not love, although faith works through love, Galatians 5:6, any
more than the body is the form of the soul by which the soul works through the
body. Rather, its form is the trust by which Christ, when known together with
his benefits, is apprehended and applied. In order, knowledge precedes this
application: by it we understand the word of God, and especially the word
concerning Christ, 1 Corinthians 2:2; 2 Corinthians 4:14. Hence faith is called
*sophia*, *gnōsis*, and *synesis pneumatikē*—wisdom, knowledge, and spiritual
understanding—in Colossians 1:9 and 1 Corinthians 8:7. Yet this knowledge does
not by itself constitute faith, since it is common to the godly and ungodly.
Along with it, a particular application is required, by which we certainly
determine that the things promised in the word pertain to us and that we in
particular have God propitious to us, and therefore we rest content and rejoice
in God in Christ and through Christ, Hebrews 11:6; James 1:6; Romans 8:38.
Hence *epignōsis*, *plērophoria*, and *pepoithēsis*—full knowledge, full
assurance, and confidence—are attributed to faith in Colossians 1:9 and 2:2;
Hebrews 10:22; 2 Peter 1:3; Romans 4:21; 2 Corinthians 3:4.

The application of Christ together with his benefits is followed by
*koinōnia*, fellowship, with Christ. For through faith we are joined to Christ
as members to the head, are grafted into him, and remain united to him as
branches to a vine, and from him draw all good

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 24 | image 15574996 | printed p. 22]

things. In short, Christ becomes ours and we become Christ’s; through Christ
we cultivate fellowship with the Father and the Holy Spirit; and we are
temples of the Holy Spirit, John 15:1; Ephesians 5:30; John 1:12, 18 and 6:35;
1 Corinthians 6:19.

The proximate end is our justification. The remaining ends, however, are
common with justification itself.

### Definition of faith

It follows that faith is a firm and certain knowledge of God’s benevolence
toward us, which, founded upon the truth of the gracious promise in Christ, is
both revealed to our minds and sealed upon our hearts through the Holy Spirit.
With this definition agrees the apostle’s in Hebrews 11:1, which is drawn from
the form and objects of faith: faith is that by which the things hoped for
exist and which demonstrates the things that are not seen. The form of faith
is explained by these two words when it is called *hypostasis* and *elenchos*.

Here *hypostasis* signifies not “person,” as in Hebrews 1:3, but trust itself,
or the support upon which a godly mind rests and leans, since through faith we
have certain and secure possession of the things promised to us by God. Or it
signifies that faith firmly subsists, stands, stands against, and yields no
place to an advancing enemy. By this the power and nature of faith are
expressed exceedingly well, because by faith we resist the devil, the flesh,
and sin, and far surpass all our reason, Ephesians 6:17; 1 John 5:4.

Faith is also called *elenchos*, that is, demonstration, because faith is
evidence of things not appearing, sight of things not seen, clarity of things
obscure, presence of things absent, and demonstration of things hidden. For we
cannot by nature or sense perceive the mysteries of God, nor can we understand
them with the mind; or, as Augustine says in Homily 39 on John and *On the
Merits and Remission of Sins*, chapter 31, it is a connection.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 25 | image 15574997 | printed p. 23]

Finally, it is called *elenchos* because from the divine promises, as from the
most certain principles of demonstrations, a conclusion of certainty is drawn,
by which faith most firmly embraces the promises, applies them, and rests
peacefully content in them.

Two objects of faith are posited: things hoped for and things that are not
seen. The things not seen extend more widely than the things hoped for.

### Six things to be observed in the doctrine of faith

1. Although faith is said to be one in all believers, Ephesians 4, it differs
in each both numerically and in degree. It differs numerically because each
person lives by his own faith, Habakkuk 2:4; Romans 1:17. It differs in degree
because God distributes to each his own measure of faith. Hence it is greater
in some and smaller in others, whether you regard knowledge or trust; firmer in
some and weaker in others; indeed, in one person it is at one time smaller and
at another greater, Luke 17:15; Matthew 8:20; Romans 14:1.

2. However great faith may be in this life, it is imperfect, 1 Corinthians
13:12; Ephesians 4:13.

3. Firm faith apprehends no more, if you regard the object, than weak and less
perfect faith, because weak faith apprehends the whole Christ, though not so
firmly. The firmer and more perfect faith is, the closer the union with Christ
is, and conversely. Therefore, with respect to their object, the faith of all
is called *isotimos* in 2 Peter 1:1—that is, of the same dignity and value in
Christ with respect to the object, but not with respect to degree and
perfection.

4. Faith is sometimes obscured and, as it were, lulled to sleep, as

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 26 | image 15574998 | printed p. 24]

in temptations and more grievous falls, but it is never utterly taken away and
extinguished, because the gifts of God are *ametamelēta*, irrevocable, Romans
11:29; Matthew 24:24.

5. Faith is known in two ways: by others or by ourselves. It is known by others
through the judgment of charity, and only from its fruits. This judgment can
indeed be mistaken. It is known by us to be in us through the judgment of
truth, and in the following ways.

First, by the inward testimony of the Holy Spirit, as in Romans 8:16.

Second, from a true sense of true faith in us. For one who believes knows that
he believes: when a true and unfeigned desire to receive Christ and his
benefits is kindled in the heart; when Christ is received through the means by
which he is communicated; and, finally, when the soul rests content in Christ
received as its own highest good.

Third, from faith’s struggle with doubt on account of various internal and
external temptations—for example, heresies spread by false teachers,
disagreements of teachers among themselves, the faithlessness and small number
of believers, the deformity and oppression of the Church, the defection of
many, the delay of things promised, judgments of God’s indignation, or,
finally, scandals and stumbling blocks. By these and similar things it is
tested whether faith is true.

Fourth, from patience under the cross, joined with invocation and expectation
of deliverance, as in 1 Peter 1:7; James 1:12.

Fifth, from a true and unfeigned purpose and desire in the soul to obey God.

6. Faith is an indivisible act and from itself begets hope, which is the
certain expectation of the things promised by God which faith has believed.
For faith and hope agree with each other in that they regard the same benefits:

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 27 | image 15574999 | printed p. 25]

both are occupied with things promised by God in Christ and salutary to us, as
in 1 Thessalonians 1:8; Titus 2:13. Hence faith is put for hope and conversely
in Romans 8:24; 1 Corinthians 15:19; Hebrews 3:6; 6:11; 10:3; 1 Peter 1:13;
1 John 3:3.

They differ, however, in the following ways.

First, faith is occupied with present things and therefore apprehends and
embraces them. For this reason, it regards the grace and reconciliation offered
in Christ, and even eternal life, as present, whereas hope awaits a future
good.

Second, faith begets hope. For when faith believes the promises, it believes
in such a way that it simultaneously begets the hope by which we patiently
wait until God fulfills the promises at the proper time. Hope, however,
sustains,
supports, and confirms the faith from which it arises. It does this as
follows: (1) it restrains faith, lest by hastening too much faith plunge
headlong when God delays the fulfillment of the promises; (2) it confirms
faith, lest it waver or doubt concerning the promises, and holds faith back
when it is wavering and doubting; (3) it leads faith to its ultimate goal, and
nourishes and restores it so that it does not fail.

### State of the first controversy

The question between us and the Roman Catholics is what that faith required
for justification is, or what constitutes it.

Bellarmine says in book 1 of *On Justification*, chapter 4, that historical
faith, miraculous faith, and the faith which he calls the faith of promises
but which we call properly justifying faith are the same. He says that this
faith is not properly knowledge or trust, but assent. “And this faith,” he
says, “is called simply catholic faith, or dogmatic faith.” The same Bellarmine
says in *On the Marks of the Church*, chapter 11, that one is not justified by
a special faith by which

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 28 | image 15575000 | printed p. 26]

one believes oneself righteous before God on account of Christ; that this
position can be compared with any paradox whatsoever; and that it is not only
above or beyond reason, but contrary to all reason.

The Roman Catholics and we agree in the following points: (1) verbally, that
faith believes the whole word of God and extends as widely as the word itself,
Bellarmine, book 1 of *On Justification*, chapter 4; (2) that each person may
rightly apply the promises to himself by faith, in the same work, chapter 11;
(3) that faith is a cause of justification, in the same work, chapter 10.

We differ from the Roman Catholics, however, in the following points. First,
we assert that the whole word of God is the object of faith, but especially the
promises, and that each person ought to apply all the promises particularly to
himself. The Roman Catholics, however, teach that the special application of
the promises of grace pertains not so much to faith as to presumption. Second,
the Roman Catholics affirm that the seat of faith is only in the intellect,
whereas we affirm that it is both in the intellect and in the will. Third, the
Roman Catholics affirm that we believe only through assent, whereas we affirm
that we believe through knowledge, Bellarmine, book 1 of *On Justification*,
chapter 4.

### The first question, therefore, is this: Is assent alone required for faith, or is knowledge required?

This question is explained as follows by the Roman Catholics themselves. The
Anglo-Rheims Jesuits say in their annotations on Luke 12: “If an unlearned
Catholic stands before a secular tribunal to be examined concerning a matter
of faith, and if he answers that he is a Catholic and that the Church in which
he makes his profession can give an account of the faith, he has sufficiently
performed the duty

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 29 | image 15575001 | printed p. 27]

of a Catholic man.” The Scholastics, however, called justifying faith implicit
faith, because faith is not of its nature knowledge of the things to be
believed, but assent, whether knowledge is present or not. Bellarmine, in book
1 of *On Justification*, chapters 5 and 7, says that faith is better defined by
ignorance than by knowledge, and that justifying faith is not properly
knowledge, but assent to an unknown thing.

We, however, concede that faith may be called implicit in two respects. First,
with respect to knowledge, because the godly do not simultaneously and all at
once know distinctly and explicitly everything necessary for salvation. Even
the apostles themselves at first did not know many things distinctly, such as
the mystery of Christ’s death and resurrection, the state of Christ’s kingdom,
and the calling of the Gentiles. Thus, because in this life we understand only
in part and by degrees, we concede that faith may in this respect be called
implicit.

Second, with respect to the apprehension and sense of particular grace and
mercy in Christ. It sometimes happens that those who truly are faithful and
elect cannot distinctly and certainly affirm that they believe the remission
of sins, but can affirm that they desire to be joined to Christ by faith. This
happens either in the first conversion to God—which is indeed called faith in
John 4:39, 53—or at the time of some most grievous temptation, which lasts only
for a time. For faith has its degrees both in knowledge and in the application
of the promises.

The question between us and the Roman Catholics, however, does not concern
implicit faith as taken in these ways, but faith as it is commonly found in
the justified. We therefore affirm that faith is not assent to an unknown
thing, but that faith requires

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 30 | image 15575002 | printed p. 28]

knowledge of the word, and especially of the promises, relying upon the
following arguments.

1. If individual believers are bound to be ready to give an account of the
faith that is in them to everyone who asks, then faith requires their knowledge
of the things believed and for which an account must be given. But the former
is true from 1 Peter 3:15, where by hope the apostle understands faith’s
inseparable companion and begetter—or, because of their common object, he
understands faith by hope.

2. If faith requires not only a sense in the heart, but also confession with
the mouth arising from preceding knowledge and acknowledgment, then faith is
not assent to an unknown thing, but knowledge is in faith. But the former is
true from Romans 10:10. Therefore no one can confess with the mouth what he
does not know.

3. Isaiah 53:11 says, “By knowledge of himself my servant will justify many.”
If we who are justified are justified by cognition and knowledge of Christ,
then we are justified not by assent to an unknown thing, but by the cognition
that is in faith. The former is true in the passage cited. Therefore the
conclusion follows.

4. If the faith by which we are justified were not knowledge, then the faith
of the justified would be inferior to the faith of demons and to historical
faith. But the latter is absurd; therefore the former is also. Proof of the
major: that faith which does not have knowledge is inferior to faith which
does. But diabolical and historical faith possess knowledge, as in James 2:19,
nor can historical faith be so called without historical knowledge. Therefore
the conclusion follows.

5. If eternal life, which is the end and effect of justification, is not given
except to those who know the true God and Jesus Christ whom he sent—in which
the whole Christian doctrine is contained as in a compendium—then, since we
are justified by faith, we must know these things before we are sent into
possession of eternal life.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 31 | image 15575003 | printed p. 29]

Accordingly, faith is knowledge and not assent to an unknown thing. But the
former is true from John 17:3. Therefore the conclusion follows.

6. If the relation between faith and the word is so great that the word known
is the basis of faith, the fountain and mirror in which we behold God, and if
faith would be taken away were the word taken away, then faith necessarily is
knowledge of the word. But the former is true from Isaiah 55:3; John 20:31;
Psalm 95:7; Romans 10:17. Therefore the conclusion follows.

7. If the word of Christ ought to dwell in us in all wisdom, and if each of us
ought to be fit to teach and admonish others, then assent to an unknown thing
is not enough for faith; rather, knowledge of the word is required, so that
from it we may be wise enough to teach. But the former is true from Colossians
1:26 and 2:2. Therefore the conclusion follows.

8. If the apostle condemns Christians who are always learning and never arrive
at knowledge of the truth, then it is not enough for believers without
knowledge to have assent to an unknown thing. But the former is true from
2 Timothy 3:7. Therefore the conclusion follows.

9. In Hebrews 11:1, the apostle defines faith as *hypostasis*. According to
Bellarmine’s interpretation in book 1 of *On Justification*, chapter 5, things
hoped for have no foundation or subsistence except in the intellect. Faith is
also *elenchos*, that is—according to his interpretation in the same
place—demonstration or conviction, which pertains to the intellect. Hence we
argue as follows.

First, what subsists in the intellect is knowledge, for every act of the
intellect is understanding; and whatever is in the intellect is there through
and on account of some act of the intellect, and exists in no other way than
insofar as it is understood. But faith, according to Bellarmine, is in the
intellect. Therefore faith is knowledge.

Second, every demonstration is made from a preexisting knowledge of
principles. Faith is demonstration. Therefore faith arises from a preexisting
knowledge of the principles of faith, namely, the word. Finally, every
conviction occurs through an argument

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 32 | image 15575004 | printed p. 30]

which contains knowledge of the principles by which we are convinced to
assent. But faith is conviction, according to Bellarmine in the place cited.
Therefore the conclusion follows.

10. If the act of faith is described in Scripture by the verb “to understand,”
and if by faith we understand the things we apprehend by faith, then faith is
knowledge. But the former is true from Hebrews 11:3. Therefore the conclusion
follows.

11. If faith is better defined by ignorance than by knowledge, as Bellarmine
maintains, then faith is rightly called ignorance. But this is absurd, because
if faith is ignorance, then, since we are justified by faith, we are justified
by ignorance. This too is absurd, because then we would be justified by sin,
since ignorance of what we ought to know is sin. Therefore the conclusion
follows.

### Objections

The first is from Hebrews 11:1: faith is the *elenchos* of things not
appearing. Bellarmine argues from this as follows: the office of faith is not
to cause obscure things to become clear, but to cause them to be believed even
though they do not appear. Therefore faith is not knowledge.

Response: the antecedent must be distinguished. It is true that the primary
office of faith is not to render obscure things clear. Rather, this is the
office of the word and of the ecclesiastical ministry, through which the Holy
Spirit works and renders clear the things that are to be believed. Yet
secondarily, and properly with respect to us and in us, faith renders obscure
things clear. For as the word explains things otherwise obscure to us, and as
the Holy Spirit inwardly enlightens our minds and grants faith, we begin to
understand things which previously, as natural people, we were unable to
understand, as in 1 Corinthians 2:14. Second response:

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 33 | image 15575005 | printed p. 31]

The conclusion does not follow. The office of faith is to believe things that
do not appear; therefore faith is not knowledge. This does not follow, because
by faith we understand those very things that do not appear to sense—namely,
our sense and that of the natural person. Therefore the apostle calls faith
*elenchos*, as above. For there are two modes of knowing, one natural and the
other supernatural. By the first, natural things are known; by the second,
supernatural things are known. We know spiritual things in a spiritual manner
and by spiritual instruments no less truly than we naturally know natural
things.

Objection in reply: faith does the same thing as demonstration, namely, causes
us to assent, but not in the same manner. Demonstration causes us to assent by
convincing the intellect when it discloses the causes and properties of
things. Faith, however, causes the intellect to assent not through knowledge
of the cause, but by believing the authority of the one speaking. Therefore
faith is not knowledge, but in faith the thing remains as obscure as before.

Response 1: we believe the authority of God speaking because he speaks, but in
such a way that this authority does not exclude knowledge of the things to be
believed. For he proposes nothing to us for belief unless in his word he has
disclosed its causes, insofar as and to the extent that they can be conceived
by the human intellect. It is indeed true that we know the things believed in
different ways. The causes of things that have causes are explained in the
word, and we know those things through their causes. Things that have no
causes, however, such as the deity itself, are known through their effects,
which likewise are described in the word. Nor does the following inference
hold: We believe God speaking; therefore the causes of the things to be
believed are not proposed to be known and are not understood by faith.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 34 | image 15575006 | printed p. 32]

Response 2: the cognition of natural things by natural light through natural
causes and properties does indeed differ from the cognition of spiritual
things, which are known neither by sense nor by natural reason, but
supernaturally. With respect to this difference, the latter are called *mē
blepomena*, things not seen. The following therefore does not follow: We do not
know in the same way through faith as through demonstration; therefore we do
not know through faith. It is the fallacy of arguing from a qualified sense to
an unqualified one.

Objection 2, from Isaiah 7:9: “Unless you believe, you will not understand.”
Faith is not understanding but the way and step toward understanding, and it
precedes knowledge. Therefore faith is not knowledge.

Response 1: the words are not found in the prophet as Bellarmine cites them.
Rather, the words are these: “If you do not believe, will you not be
confirmed?” In the Hebrew text one word is used in different conjugations. In
the first place it is in the *hiphil* conjugation, and in that conjugation it
signifies “to believe.” In the second place it is in the *niphal* conjugation
and signifies “to be confirmed,” “to be stable.” Symmachus therefore rightly
translates it *bebaiōthēte*. Thus no mention of knowledge is made in this
passage. The sense of the passage is that Achar is reproved there because he
did not believe the divine promise and did not confirm himself and the people
against their enemies by resting upon the divine promise.

Second, even if the false interpretation is granted, it does not follow that
faith is not knowledge. For although knowledge increases in different acts of
believing through particular promises when those promises are embraced by
faith, it does not follow that there is no knowledge before particular
promises are believed.

Objection 3, from 1 Corinthians 13:2 and 12:8: the apostle distinguishes faith
from knowledge. Therefore faith is not knowledge.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 35 | image 15575007 | printed p. 33]

Response: the inference is denied. For although faith is knowledge, not all
knowledge is faith, because more than knowledge is required for true faith;
otherwise demons would possess true justifying faith. Second, in that passage
he speaks not of justifying faith, but of miraculous faith.

Objection 4, from 2 Corinthians 10:5: if knowledge of the truth were in faith,
it would not be necessary to take the intellect captive. But according to the
apostle, the intellects of all must be taken captive. Therefore knowledge is
not in faith.

Response: the proposition is denied. First, because the apostle speaks in that
passage of taking captive an intellect in which there is no knowledge of
Christ. Second, even if they are believers of whom he speaks, the inference
does not follow: the intellects of believers must be taken captive to the
obedience of Christ; therefore knowledge is not in faith. For even the
intellects and wills of believers themselves, as long as life is lived here,
must be taken captive more and more, because they understand only in part and
the flesh lusts against the Spirit. Therefore they must daily renounce
themselves, natural knowledge, and all their affections more and more, so as to
give place to Christ.

Objection 5: in the one who believes there are two things, apprehension and
judgment or assent. Apprehension, however, is not faith, but something
preceding faith; and the ungodly can apprehend the mysteries of faith.
Apprehension is twofold: (1) confused, which is not properly knowledge; (2)
distinct and clear, which is not necessarily required for faith. For a pagan
philosopher can apprehend the mystery of the Trinity, its essence and the
relations of the persons, and other mysteries of faith of that kind, yet not
believe. An unlearned Catholic, however, believes if he apprehends those three
terms, even confusedly.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 36 | image 15575008 | printed p. 34]

Judgment or assent, however, is twofold: (1) that which follows reason and the
evidence of the thing, and is called knowledge; (2) that which follows the
authority of the one proposing, and this is faith, but is not knowledge.
Therefore faith is not knowledge.

Response 1: neither apprehension nor judgment nor both together is justifying
faith, although both are required for faith. Second, the following does not
follow: apprehension precedes faith and is common to the ungodly; therefore it
does not pertain to faith. For justifying faith itself has knowledge in common
with historical faith, yet historical faith is not justifying faith.

Third, just as apprehension of things is distinct or confused, so too is
judgment, and consequently knowledge. But the following does not follow:
knowledge is crude or confused on account of a crude and confused
apprehension; therefore it is not knowledge. This is the fallacy of arguing
from a qualified sense to an unqualified one.

Fourth, the first part of the comparison concerning the philosopher is true,
because the ungodly can know the mysteries of faith and not be believers. From
this it follows that knowledge is not enough to constitute justifying faith,
and consequently Bellarmine’s claim that justifying and historical faith are
the same is false. The second part of the comparison is false. For those who
possess only a confused grasp of the very words by which the mysteries of
faith are explained do not have faith, much less those who apprehend only
confusedly those three terms—Trinity, essence, and person.

Fifth, the assent by which Bellarmine defines faith is in reality the same as
judgment, but knowledge is necessary in every judgment. Accordingly, if faith
is judgment, it is knowledge.

Sixth, the assent in faith is on account of the authority of God speaking;
therefore we do not know the things to which we assent.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 37 | image 15575009 | printed p. 35]

The inference does not follow. For we assent to and believe God speaking, but
in such a way that we apprehend with the mind the things he says and a judgment
