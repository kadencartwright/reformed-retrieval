SECTION NINE NOTION VII

PERKINS

Nothing evil can be avoided unless God prevents it; and we avoid evil insofar as God prevents it to a greater or lesser degree.

ARMINIUS

It is likewise certain that nothing evil can be avoided unless God prevents it. But the manner of preventing is disputed: whether it arises from an omnipotent action of God acting upon the human will according to the manner of nature, whence the necessity of the prevention arises; or from such an action as acts upon the will according to the manner of the will, insofar as it is free, whence the infallibility of the prevention arises.

RESPONSE 1

Is it indeed certain? So he says; but does nothing occur here that could be corrected, so that the statement might become more accurate? Clearly it seemed so to Arminius on p. 113, where Perkins had said (as he also speaks here), “Just as nothing good can exist or be done unless God does it, so nothing evil can be avoided unless God prevents it.” Arminius says this about those words: “Those words too could be corrected, if someone wished to examine them more accurately. For they ought to have been stated thus: ‘Just as nothing good exists or occurs unless God brings it about, so nothing evil is avoided unless God prevents it.’”

Regarding the manner of preventing, we reply that it is conceded on both sides, if we are speaking of the intrinsic prevention of evil, by which it comes about that the will does not will evil, that this is done by God through efficacious grace. But we disagree in the definition of efficacious grace.

1. We interpret efficacious grace as a motion of God passing into the human will—not a moral but a physical motion, that is, one immediately and truly effecting that we do whatever good we do—that is, determining the will itself to act, but to act freely. This, we say,

[Praefatio; printed p. 29; BSB canvas 37]

can be shown by demonstration once this principle is posited: that God concurs efficiently in producing every act of the human will. Arminius indeed concedes this principle and adds that God concurs not only efficiently but also principally. Consequently, he acts upon the will itself according to its nature and according to the manner suited to its nature, since it is naturally borne toward the end by a necessary inclination, but toward the means to the end by free choice. Yet why this action of God should be called omnipotent I do not sufficiently understand. For it cannot do all things, but only that at which it is directed. But if it is called omnipotent because it cannot fail of its end, we concede that it is omnipotent; or if because it cannot be resisted, we concede this too, since it proceeds from the divine will to prevent, and the Holy Spirit teaches that the divine will cannot be resisted. Nevertheless, this is said very improperly of the action of God acting upon the creature’s will to elicit its own act. For that is properly called irresistible which no one can resist even if he wished, and this has no place at all in the act of willing. From a divine operation of this kind there arises a necessity of the effect, but not one—as Arminius understands it—that is repugnant to the freedom of the human will; rather, it agrees perfectly with the will’s own freedom, as we shall also demonstrate in its proper place.

2. But although Arminius avoided treating this topic (namely, the nature of efficacious grace), he nevertheless indicates throughout his writings that efficacious grace is nothing other than persuasion. For he distinguishes in this way the impediment placed by God upon the creature so that it does not will: “It is imposed in two ways, according to the two ways in which God can act upon the will. For he acts upon the will either by the manner of nature or according to the manner of the will and its freedom. The action by which he acts upon the will according to the manner of nature can be called a physical impulse; but the action by which he acts upon it according to the manner of the will and its freedom may appropriately be called persuasion, whence the will is prevented not necessarily but certainly,” p. 153. Let the reader see in passing what follows from this opinion. God, he says, certainly and infallibly prevents evil through persuasion. But whence this certainty, whence this infallibility? From God’s foreknowledge, namely, “knowing what arguments, in that state of affairs and at that time, will move the mind of the man in the direction in which God desires it inclined, whether according to his mercy or according to his justice,” p. 153. But whence, then, is this impediment called efficacious? From the same foreknowledge of God, ibid., p. 153. 1. But then, I say, this grace or impediment to sin is not efficacious from its own nature, but from God’s foreknowledge. And since, according to Arminius, things are known by God because they exist, so too they are foreknown because they will exist. It follows that this impediment to sin, or this gracious persuasion, is called efficacious because the effect for which that persuasion is instituted will in fact occur. But this is for efficacious grace to be efficacious from the outcome—a position that not only we detest, but that the Arminians themselves have sometimes been ashamed to assert. Thus Corvinus, p. 155: “For Arminius an ineffectual calling is truly such only from the outcome and is so called—and indeed from an outcome not intended in itself by God who calls, but arising incidentally from the wickedness and hardness of the human heart as its own proper cause. But, on the other hand, that a calling of grace is called efficacious only from the outcome, I say is nowhere written by Arminius.” In this he acts prudently indeed, when he denies that it was written by Arminius, for they have not yet thought it advisable to profess this. But let the fair-minded reader consider whether it manifestly follows from the doctrine just proposed—that the efficacy of grace depends upon God’s foreknowledge and God’s foreknowledge upon the futurity of things—that grace is called efficacious solely from the outcome.

2. Again, since evil is prevented through efficacious grace for doing what is good, it will follow from this that God works good in us in no other way than by persuading. Hence it will follow that God works neither faith nor repentance in us in any other way than through persuasion. 1. But this, I say, does not prevent faith and repentance from being from us and from our free choice just as much as any natural, moral, or political work to which we are customarily persuaded. 2. Indeed, it also follows from this that faith is no more to be called God’s work in our hearts, or repentance God’s work, than some moral or civil act is to be called the gift of the person who had urged us to perform it by his counsels and persuasions. 3. It also follows from this that God is not the principal cause of a good work. For the one who persuades another to act is not to be called the principal cause of the action, but the one who acts is.

3. Certainty, you say, arises from God’s foreknowledge, and foreknowledge in turn from future things. But how did it come about that this act was going to occur from eternity, when by its nature it was only possible? How did it come about that it passed from the possible into the future? For according to Arminius, God foreknows the future act because it was going to occur; it is not going to occur because God foreknows it. Behold the mystery here. Namely, one member of a pair of contradictory propositions had determinate truth from eternity. Today’s Jesuits are accustomed to maintain this; but whence did it have that truth? Not from God’s decree, for then, as they say, Stoic fate would be introduced; but not from divine foreknowledge either. For because something was going to occur and consequently was knowable, it was therefore foreknown by God. It is necessary, then, that it have determinate truth from its own nature. Behold here a truly Stoic fate, or perhaps one more than Stoic, which binds God himself. For with God’s decree excluded, according to this theology the world was going to exist from eternity, and consequently God could not refrain from creating the world. See here, reader, with what effrontery they reproach us with Stoic fate, since we do indeed teach a fate, but a Christian fate depending solely upon God and his decree. Meanwhile, as they avoid this, they plunge into a truly Stoic fate, or perhaps a fate more Stoic than Stoic fate, which did not proceed from God but was founded in things outside God: namely, they are not at all ashamed to affirm that one member of each contradiction had determinate truth, and that this truth came not from God but nevertheless existed from eternity and therefore from its own nature.

3. Finally, what is this statement: “It acts upon the will insofar as it is free, whence the infallibility of the prevention arises”? Cannot angels, cannot human beings, act by their persuasions and suggestions—indeed, do they not often act—upon a person’s will insofar as it is free, to prevent him from doing something? But does infallibility immediately arise from this? It certainly does not. Therefore infallibility arises not from action upon the will insofar as it is free, but from the divine manner of acting upon the will insofar as it is free. Yet according to what Arminius teaches on p. 153, it arises not even from the divine manner of acting, but from God’s foreknowledge. Nor is it surprising if someone is little consistent with himself whenever, from pride of mind, he has disdained the track of truth as too worn and common, and has desired to open through the byways of errors a new road for himself and others.
