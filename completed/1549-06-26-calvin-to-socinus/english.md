# John Calvin to Laelius Socinus

Letter 1212, assigned by the editors to 26 June 1549

This is a new English translation from the normalized Latin transcription of *Calvini Opera* XIII, 307–311. It is an AI-assisted draft checked against the printed page images, not an independently reviewed critical translation.

[CO XIII, p. 307]

Your letter reached me a few days after my return. Since then a catarrh has been very troublesome to me, and it has not yet ceased causing discomfort.

To answer your questions briefly: since a Christian man should marry a wife on no other terms than that she show herself his helper and companion in every duty of a godly life, whenever there is even the slightest departure from this aim, I do not doubt that the marriage is faulty. What else does a man bring into his home but profanation when he marries a wife still entangled in the impious superstitions of the papacy? For if a wife is her husband’s body, the man who joins himself to a woman who daily prostitutes herself to perverted forms of worship certainly cannot be excused: he is himself in some measure polluted in half of his being. I pass over the fact that, if anyone marries such a wife, he will be compelled to consecrate the marriage under magical auspices. Nevertheless, I would not venture to place a girl who otherwise thinks rightly, but is held in the pretence of idolatry by fear for her flesh, on precisely the same footing as an enemy of religion. The more anyone is

[CO XIII, p. 308]

estranged from Christ, the more we too ought to recoil from that person. But the woman you describe to me certainly stands a long way from his professed enemies.

Whoever presents his children for papal baptism, defiled as it is by so many depraved superstitions, is not free from fault. Indeed, unless I am mistaken, I list this among the hardships by which godly people ought to be spurred to emigrate. But Melanchthon and Martyr do not forbid it, provided that the man, whoever he may be, detests the things that conflict with Christ’s institution. There would be nothing absurd in that, except that whoever dared to do it would have to be prepared for certain death. Yet I approve the answers of those whom you name in such a way that I do not subscribe to every particular. I judge that a playful imitation of baptism should be regarded as the mere mockery that it is. Papist baptism, however, though it teems with a thousand mockeries, retains its efficacy insofar as it is administered for this end: that, by Christ’s command, the children of believers may receive the sign of adoption. For I see that circumcision, though corrupted by many superstitions, was nevertheless regarded as a symbol of grace. Your objection that baptism ought to be celebrated in an assembly of the godly does not prevent a baptism administered in a gathering of idolaters from being valid. For when we teach what ought to be done so that baptism may be pure and free from every defilement, we do not abolish God’s institution even if idolaters corrupt it. Amid those corruptions of papal baptism, something belonging to God remains. Moreover, we withhold the name of church from the papists in such a way that we nevertheless do not deny that some remnants of the church remain there. Certainly, when Josiah and Hezekiah gathered from all Israel those who had defected from the Lord, they did not summon them to a second circumcision. And although the temple itself had been polluted by various sacrileges, circumcision did not cease to be valid. Therefore, however much I say that those who present their children for baptism there sin, I do not immediately judge a baptism to be null when some mark of the divine institution appears in it, even though otherwise stained by many blemishes.

I do not venture to condemn without qualification those who do not refrain from an outward profession of idolatry, even if they conceal their faith from fear of danger. For when we see that godly people who hid during persecutions were approved by God, it is not right for us to demand more. Yet I am not one to indulge their inertia. On the contrary, I will urge them to be watchful for every opportunity to profess their faith, and not to listen in silence to the ungodly openly blaspheming God in such a way that they seem to assent. I do not, however, venture to impose a rule prescribing how far they must proceed. But those who, while the rites are being performed, wander about churches for pleasure deserve no excuse, whatever pretexts they put forward. The very delight they take in such a spectacle shows how little they are

[CO XIII, p. 309]

affected by insults offered to God. They also recklessly give many people occasion for stumbling, as though they acted from contempt for religion rather than for superstition. I therefore rightly condemn this license: since it differs little from a pretence of idolatry, it savors of something—I do not know what—more profane than Christian.

The last question, about the resurrection of the flesh, which you so greatly desire me to resolve for you, presents no great difficulty to me, but it requires more time and effort. I would have to collect and carefully explain a great many passages of Scripture that pertain to the matter if I undertook to examine the subject thoroughly. Yet it is not so much to avoid the labor as because this question seems more curious than useful that I prefer, by my very brevity, to testify that I approve nothing more than its sober treatment. The delusion of the Manichaeans, who deny that the flesh will rise because it is unclean, must be refuted in passing. It is no wonder that the resurrection of this flesh seems incredible to you. But when this consideration moves you to decide that it is enough to believe that we will one day be clothed with new bodies, that is foreign to the teaching of Scripture.

In the first place, the very word *resurrection*, especially when it is ascribed to the flesh, conveys something else. For this is the promise: “He who raised Christ from death will also give life to your mortal bodies because of the Spirit of Christ dwelling in you” (Romans 8:11). Likewise elsewhere: “Christ will refashion our abject body so that it may be conformed to his glorious body” (Philippians 3:21). Immortality is certainly promised by name to these bodies, which are now subject to decay. Otherwise these words would be left without force: “This corruptible thing will put on incorruption.”[^1] Tertullian says that he could not speak more expressly unless he were holding his own skin in his hands.

There is also an argument from opposites: if the death of this flesh comes from Adam, then the resurrection of the same flesh comes from Christ, because he restores us from corruption. The passage of Ezekiel that contains a type of the resurrection should be cited (chapter 37). God kills and makes alive—what does he make alive if not what was dead? From the fact that the Athenians laugh at the resurrection preached by Paul, we infer what kind of preaching it was. For the godly, that laughter confirms a sound understanding of it. “Fear him who can destroy soul and body in hell.” What body? Precisely the one that tyrants kill.

The resurrection of the flesh is also rightly proved from the use of the sacraments. Baptism sanctifies not only the soul but the flesh as well. Participation in the Supper, inviting us to confidence in heavenly life, is directed even to the bodily senses. The passage in Romans 8 will serve, especially where it speaks of the redemption of the body. “Bearing about Christ’s dying, so that the life of Christ also may be manifested in our body” (2 Corinthians 4:10).

[CO XIII, p. 310]

Tertullian says: “He carried the pledge of our resurrection into heaven.” Therefore let those who deny that we will rise deny that he is in heaven. Unless God raises us in the body, he does not raise the dead. Tertullian calls Enoch and Elijah, preserved in body and soul, candidates for future immortality.

Next, believers are not only called temples of God; their bodies are distinguished by this honor (1 Corinthians 6:19). They are likewise called members of Christ. But it is far too absurd that temples of the Spirit and members of Christ should pass into decay without hope of restoration. Indeed, in the same place Paul makes sufficiently clear that these are the bodies for which God has destined heavenly and eternal glory. Furthermore, people are summoned before Christ’s judgment seat so that each may receive according to the things done through the body (2 Corinthians 5:10). But it is wholly unfitting that the reward for deeds should be repaid to us in newly acquired bodies when the bodies we now bear were the instruments used to perform them. Is Paul’s body, in which God’s name was glorified (Philippians 1:20), to be deprived of honor, while a body assembled from somewhere else is crowned with the glory of martyrdom?

Add to this that Christ, in whom we have not only a living image of our future resurrection but also its pledge, took up again the body he had laid aside. We must be conformed to this pattern. Otherwise he would not properly be called, as he is called, “the firstfruits of those who rise” (1 Corinthians 15:20). And certainly those whom that day finds still alive will rise in the same bodies by a sudden transformation. If you object that our case is different, I answer you with Paul’s words: “We will not all sleep, but we will all be changed” (1 Corinthians 15:51). If transformation applies to everyone, this very flesh will certainly be renewed. And what could you desire clearer than these words? “This corruptible thing must put on incorruption” (verse 53). Nor is there any contradiction in Paul’s teaching in the same passage that the flesh will be different, for he refers this to its quality. He means nothing other than what he later conveys in other words: “so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life.”

For this reason the bodies of the saints are said to have risen from the tombs (Matthew 27:52). Just as the Lord brought them out of their graves, he will undoubtedly bring us out too. Thus, when Isaiah portrays the restoration of the people under the image of resurrection (26:19), he says: “Rise, inhabitants of the dust.” Christ speaks no differently: “The dead who are in the tombs will hear the voice of the Son of God” (John 5:28). That statement would not stand unless they rose after receiving back their corpses. Isaiah also expresses this there when he says: “Your dead will live; my corpse will rise.” This is also the source of the term *sleep*: it would have no meaning unless bodies were to awaken as if from sleep. For it is false to say that bodies laid in the earth sleep if, once they have been consumed, new ones must be created in their place. Indeed, if the bodies with which we are now clothed do not rise, the rite of burial has been superstitious from the beginning of the world. Yet it must be beyond dispute that this rite

[CO XIII, p. 311]

was delivered by divine authority to be a symbol of the future resurrection.

And surely, if we were to hope for the creation of a new body rather than the restoration of the one we now inhabit, Paul would pray in vain that spirit and soul and body be blameless until the day of the Lord (1 Thessalonians 5:23). The other exhortation tends to the same point: that we cleanse ourselves from every defilement of flesh and spirit (2 Corinthians 7:1). For if this flesh is polluted by the filth of sin, it must bear the punishment of its own filth. I rest so fully in these testimonies that I leave no opening for divergent speculations to shake my faith.

I hope these things will satisfy you, unless they find your mind too preoccupied—something far removed from both your piety and your modesty. Yet, in view of our friendship, I thought I should gently admonish you, because when I heard you recently I was somewhat afraid that this opinion had taken deeper root in you than would allow you to cast it away easily. Nevertheless, trusting in the agility of your mind, I considered it enough to point out with my finger matters that I would have pursued further with a person less acute or experienced. Farewell, distinguished man and brother most dear to me in Christ the Lord. Greet your host, Master Pellican, and the others carefully in my name. May the Lord Jesus always be with you and govern you by his Spirit.

## Translator’s note

[^1]: Calvin’s terse Latin is *Frigidae sunt voces istae*, literally, “Those words are cold.” In context he appears to mean that the quoted promise would be drained of force if it referred to a replacement body rather than “this” corruptible body.
