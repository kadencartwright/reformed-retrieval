introduced a law for dividing the lands in Gaul that Marius

had acquired by his arms. He added to the law that every senator must ratify
it by his oath, or else be expelled from the Senate and fined twenty talents.
Marius spoke in the Senate against this oath and by his example dissuaded
everyone from taking it—but see the crafty dissembler! When Saturninus pressed
the oath, Marius took it without scruple, and after him everyone except
Metellus, the one man for whom Marius had set this snare. When Metellus
refused, Saturninus drove him not only from the Senate but from all Italy by
denying him fire and water. But behold swift vengeance from heaven. While
Saturninus was seeking the consulship with Marius’s favor, Memmius was a
candidate alongside him. When Saturninus perceived that the people favored
Memmius over him, he sent assassins to have Memmius killed in the Forum
itself. The people rioted at this atrocious crime, and Saturninus occupied the
Capitol. But when his water was cut off and he was nearly dead of thirst, he
surrendered to Marius. Marius placed him in custody, as if reserving him for
trial, but the people suspected Marius’s deceit and immediately stoned
Saturninus to death. Once he was removed, the younger Metellus, urged on by
the people, pleaded for his father’s return. While everyone consented to let
him return, Furius alone, a tribune acting at Marius’s instigation, rejected
the young man’s plea. The people tore this obstinately perverse man to pieces
in the assembly. The whole city received the returning Metellus with great
joy. Marius alone could not bear the sight of the man and departed for Asia,
ostensibly to fulfill vows to the Mother of the Gods, but in reality to incite
Mithridates against the Romans, so that Marius himself might be assigned to
finish that war. Yet Marius was thoroughly disappointed in this hope.

The peoples of Italy, by joining forces with the Romans, had subjected all
Rome’s neighbors far and wide. They therefore thought it fair—since in every
war their forces had always been greater than the Romans’—that they should
share in the citizenship and in the empire and wealth acquired by their blood
and arms. The Romans kept them hoping for this for a long time, but when they
finally learned for certain that they were being mocked, they united their
plans and took up arms. This Social or Italian War was extremely harsh and
bloody for four years, for the two sides fought with equal arms, strength, and
skill. Many savage battles were fought in various places, generally with
better fortune for the allies. In the end, however, the valor of Marius,
Sulla, and Quintus Pompeius, father of Pompey the Great, weakened the allies’
power. Above all, the following Roman device broke the allies: the Romans
granted citizenship to the Volsci, the Etruscans, and others nearer the city;
then, with their strength, they utterly destroyed the other allies, though
they themselves had been exhausted and nearly broken. Sulla’s valor shone so
brightly in this war that the consulship, Asia, and the Asiatic war were
decreed to him. While he was hastening toward Asia with five legions and
dispersing the remnants of the Social War at Nola, word came that the tribune
Sulpicius had introduced a law revoking Sulla’s command and entrusting the
legions and the Mithridatic War to Marius, now seventy years old.

When Pompeius, son of the other consul and Sulla’s son-in-law, spoke against
it, the tribune had him killed. On receiving this news Sulla returned to the
city. The Marian faction met him, and after a battle Sulla took the city in
victory. He proscribed Marius, Sulpicius, and the other instigators of the
faction. Sulpicius was captured in flight and stabbed to death, and his
severed head was placed before the rostra. Marius hid for a long time,
unrecognized and submerged up to his eyes in the marsh of Minturnae. At last
he was dragged out, a rope was thrown around his neck, and he was cast into
the public prison. The executioner of Minturnae, a German captured in the
Cimbrian War, was sent into the prison with a sword to kill the captive. But
when he recognized Marius shouting with a great wail, he became terrified,
threw down the sword, and fled the prison. The people of Minturnae pitied the
misfortune of so great a man and sent him away in a small boat with provisions
for his journey. Landing in Africa, he hid for many days in a poor hut near
Carthage while the storm raged; he would soon emerge from there to bring great
calamity upon his homeland.

> Marginalia, printed p. 292: *Marius narrowly escapes death*.

Mithridates, king of Pontus, was a man of the greatest intelligence and
courage. Although he had long contemplated arms against the Romans, he wished
to be driven to them by the Romans’ open injustice. Through his contrivances
he had arranged for Ariobarzanes to be expelled from Cappadocia and Nicomedes
from Bithynia. But when Sulla as praetor had restored them to their kingdoms,
each was instigated by the Roman proconsuls to provoke Mithridates without any
cause. Mithridates endured many injuries and brought a complaint about them to
the Roman magistrates in Asia, but they were seeking a cause for war and
ignored his complaint. While he attempted to avenge the injuries Nicomedes
had done him, the proconsuls declared war on him. Mithridates pleaded that
they should refer the whole matter to the Senate; they spurned his plea and
led into the field more than forty thousand legionaries and fifty thousand
allied troops. Mithridates entered battle with a fully equipped army of two
hundred thousand infantry and fifty cavalry. Victorious, he inflicted a very
great slaughter upon the Romans and occupied Cappadocia, Bithynia, and many
surrounding provinces. Indeed, throughout Asia he sent secret instructions to
the satraps and governors of the cities that on a single day every Roman

or person of Italian stock found within their districts should be killed with
his wife and children, his goods plundered, and his corpse cast out unburied.
This immense calamity fell upon the Romans, for more than one hundred and
fifty thousand Roman citizens were stabbed to death on one day, most of them
in temples.

This news hastened Sulla toward Asia, though not before he had recovered
Greece, nearly all of which—and the Athenians in particular—had defected to
Mithridates. Sulla was long detained in the siege of Athens, but at last he
took the city by force after hunger had subdued it. He inflicted a great
slaughter upon all he encountered, sparing no age or sex. At the same time
Marius was no gentler toward Sulla’s friends at Rome. Sulla had scarcely left
Italy when Cinna obtained the consulship, having first sworn to Sulla that he
would not desert his party. But a gift of three hundred talents from the
Marians caused him, as soon as he became consul, to despise the sanctity of
his oath and pass over to Marius’s side. Cinna summoned a great number of
Italians under arms into the city and called slaves to the cap of liberty so
that he might establish new laws by force. The other consul, Octavius, drove
him from the city with a greater force and proscribed him. The proscribed
Cinna won over with gifts the army that was besieging Nola, together with a
great number of Italians and slaves whom he gave a sure hope of obtaining
citizenship.

Marius had also returned from Africa and joined Cinna. Octavius in turn
summoned the proconsul Pompeius with his army. These opposing armies fought
for some time at the walls of the city, until Pompeius and many of his men
were struck by lightning and died, and the city was pressed by a shortage of
food. The Senate then admitted Cinna and Marius into the city on whatever
terms they wished. Once admitted, they proscribed Sulla, plundered his goods,
destroyed his house, and sought to kill his wife and children. They most
cruelly stabbed to death the consul Octavius, Metellus, Catulus, Antonius, a
great number of the noblest senators and equestrians, and everyone of Sulla’s
party whom they could find. They set the heads before the rostra and cast out
the naked bodies unburied. Marius and Cinna declared themselves consuls.
Marius died at the beginning of his seventh consulship, while daily drunkenness
drove away his fear of Sulla.

Cinna appointed Valerius Flaccus consul in place of Marius and sent him to
Asia against Mithridates with two legions. Flaccus was wholly inexperienced
in military affairs and hated by the soldiers, but his legate Fimbria was
quite popular with them. When Flaccus slighted Fimbria, the latter raised a
mutiny, killed the consul in Asia, and took command of the army on his own
authority. He routed Mithridates several times and plundered the cities of
Asia. He destroyed Ilium with sword and flame because, fearing him, it had
sent to Sulla, and he demolished its walls and its buildings, both sacred and
profane.

> Marginalia, printed p. 293: *Fimbria destroys Ilium*.

Most of the senators and all Sulla’s surviving friends fled to him in Greece,
urging him to hasten to recover his ruined homeland. Nevertheless, he spent
three full years in the Mithridatic War, determined not to return to Rome
until he had first defeated the foreign enemy. Archelaus met him at Chaeronea
with one hundred and twenty thousand men. Sulla did not equal even a third of
their number, yet when battle was joined he killed more than one hundred
thousand of the enemy. When Archelaus appeared again at Orchomenus with a new
army of one hundred thousand, Sulla’s soldiers were paralyzed by fear and
refused to fight. But Sulla dismounted from his horse, snatched the eagle from
the standard-bearer’s hands, and advanced upon the enemy with his bodyguard.
The legions, stirred by shame, followed him and routed the enemy a second time
with an immense slaughter.

After this double victory Sulla crossed into Asia and granted peace to
Mithridates when he sought it, on condition that he surrender the entire
fleet together with the costs of the war and confine himself to his ancestral
kingdom of Pontus.

Having finished this war, Sulla turned against Fimbria. When he approached
Fimbria’s camp, many soldiers deserted. Fimbria killed himself with his own
sword, and after their commander’s death the army immediately joined Sulla’s
men. Sulla at once extorted from the cities of Asia five years’ tribute—more
than twenty thousand talents—to their great distress.

> Marginalia, printed p. 293: *Sulla subdues Mithridates and Fimbria*.

Only then, prepared for civil war, did Sulla hasten to Rome as quickly as
possible with a brave army wholly obedient to him in everything, and landed
at Brundisium with a fleet of sixteen hundred ships. The consul Cinna had
resolved to meet him in Greece and for that purpose had sent several legions
ahead into Dalmatia. But while he was restraining mutinous soldiers too
imperiously, his own men stabbed him to death. Sulla’s friends Metellus Pius,
Cethegus, and Pompeius met him in Italy with some troops. He advanced through
Calabria and Apulia in a highly ordered column, but soon the flame of a most
cruel war blazed throughout Italy.

The valiant consuls Papyrus Carbo and Gaius Marius, the adopted son of the
elder Marius and a young man of twenty-six, had two hundred thousand
legionaries in various armies. Fighting continued for several years in many
bloody battles. Most Italians adhered to the consuls, but in the end Sulla’s
side prevailed. The younger Marius killed himself. On coming to Rome Sulla
proscribed two thousand senators and equestrians. With monstrous cruelty,
many of the noblest men were stabbed by anyone who met them throughout Italy,
their heads fixed before the rostra, and their bodies dragged with hooks into
the Tiber

and cast away without burial. Many cities were emptied by forced migrations.
This storm of Sulla and Marius consumed no fewer than five hundred thousand
Italians.

Pompeius pursued Carbo into Africa and defeated him there. He cruelly killed
many captured leading men, slaughtered Carbo—a three-time consul—after casting
him in chains at his feet, and sent his head to Sulla.

At last, weary of slaughter and power, Sulla abdicated the dictatorship and
retired as a private citizen to the district of Cumae, with the security of
one who had never injured any mortal. There he passed the rest of his life in
the greatest luxury. He had, indeed, more than one hundred and twenty thousand
men in Italy among whom he had distributed the proscribed men’s lands; at
Rome he had ten thousand slaves called Cornelii, to whom he had given freedom
and citizenship; and he had many others who had been the instruments of his
greatest crimes. All of them remained dependent upon his nod even when he was
a private citizen. From this arose the man’s supreme security amid the
greatest tyranny, and such confidence that after laying down the dictatorship
he offered to render an account of all his actions to anyone. One young man
was indeed found who followed Sulla as he returned home without lictors,
pouring out a flood of insults through the streets. Sulla silently endured
them all. At last, turning to his friends on the threshold of his house, he
said, “This young man will ensure that no one else lays down such an office
after my example.” Unbroken good fortune attended this most depraved tyrant
to the end of his life in his seventieth year; at his flatterers’ advice he
therefore assumed the surname *Faustus* and *Felix*. Only as he died was the
seal of divine wrath impressed upon him: he expired in great pain from
phthiriasis, which overcame every remedy, and from vomiting blood. Nevertheless,
the most splendid funeral Italy had ever seen was held for him in the Campus
Martius, chiefly through the efforts of Pompeius.
