QUESTION IV.

*Was only the second Person of the Most Holy Trinity incarnate, and why?*

I. In the marvelous mystery of the incarnation, which the apostle rightly
calls “great beyond controversy” (1 Tim. 3:16), four matters above all are
called into question: 1. The *Person assuming*; 2. The *nature assumed*; 3.
The *manner of the assumption* through *hypostatic union*; 4. Its *effects*.
Here we discuss the first.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The question is not whether the whole Trinity acted in the mystery of the
incarnation. For since external works are

[Printed p. 269 | Internet Archive PDF 297 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

indivisible according to the theologians’ accepted axiom, this work of the
incarnation, which is external, cannot fail to be common in some respect to
the whole Trinity. Hence the Father is said to have sent the Son (John 3:16),
and the Holy Spirit to have conceived the Son in the womb of the blessed
Virgin. Indeed, three Persons were necessarily required for this work. One
would uphold the Majesty of God, the Author of all things and supreme Judge,
who was the injured party; a second would act as Mediator between God and
human beings and make satisfaction for them; and a third would perfect the
work of salvation in us. The appointment of salvation is said to pertain to
the first, its acquisition to the second, and its application and
consummation to the third. Therefore the question is not whether the
incarnation pertains in any way to the Most Holy Trinity, but only whether the
whole Trinity became incarnate terminatively and appropriatively. For
although with respect to efficient causation it is *originally and
principiatively* the work of the whole Trinity, it is not *subjectively and
appropriatively* the work of the whole Trinity with respect to its
*terminus*. In this sense it belongs to the Son alone.

III. The question is not whether God had to become incarnate, which was proved
before, nor whether all three Persons could have become incarnate, because the
unity of the Mediator necessarily required unity of Person. Rather, which
Person of the Trinity received this into himself? Was it the first or the
third, or only the second? We affirm the last.

IV. The reasons are: 1. Scripture attributes this to the Son alone, not to the
Father or the Holy Spirit. Only the Λόγος, *Word*, is said to have become flesh
(John 1:14). The Son of God, who was in the form of God, assumed the form of a
servant (Phil. 2:6); and in Heb. 2:14–15 it is said of the Son that he
participated in flesh and blood, and so forth, and was made like us in all
things except sin.

V. 2. The *Father* could not become incarnate, for since he was first in
order, he could not be sent by anyone or act as Mediator toward the Son and
the Holy Spirit. Nor was it fitting that he who was the Father in divine
things should become a son in human things by being born of the Virgin. The
*Holy Spirit* could not become incarnate, for he had to be sent into the
church by the Mediator (John 16:7), but could not be sent by himself. And so
there would have been two sons: the second Person by eternal generation, and
the third by temporal incarnation.

VI. 3. The Son alone could rightly undertake this office: 1. So that he who
stands between the Father and the Holy Spirit would be Mediator between God
and human beings; 2. It was fitting for him who was the Son by nature to make
us adoptive sons by grace, so that we would be coheirs of him who is heir of
all things; 3. It belonged to the one who had created to ἀνακτίζειν, *create
anew*, so that the Word through whom all things were made in the first
creation would reform us to his own image in the second. The image of God,
obscured by sin and all but destroyed, could not be better restored in us than
through him who was the image of the invisible God; 4. No one was better suited to
effect our reconciliation than he who was the Father’s ἀγαπητός, *beloved
Son*, in whom he εὐδόκησε, *was well pleased* (Matt. 3:17).

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VII. Although the divine nature is rightly said to have become incarnate in
the Son, it does not therefore follow that the whole Trinity became
incarnate. For the divine nature is called incarnate not *immediately*, as
considered absolutely and in itself, but *mediately* in the Person of the Son,
or under the qualification by which it is determined and characterized in
the Person of the Son. Thus the incarnation is not a natural but a personal
work, terminating in the Person, not the nature. The whole divine nature is
therefore rightly called incarnate—not because it is incarnate in all the
Persons, but because nothing is lacking to the Person of the Son of God with
respect to the perfection of the divine nature. This singular incarnation of
one Person without the incarnation of the others is not prevented by the
σχέσις, *relation*, the Persons bear among themselves. Since it denotes
distinction and opposition, such as begetting and being begotten, it does not
present an impediment but

[Printed p. 270 | Internet Archive PDF 298 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

rather removes it. Nor is it prevented by the relation between Person and
essence. Since that relation does not abolish the distinction of the Persons
or prevent one Person from being truly distinguished from another, much less
does it prevent one from becoming incarnate without another.

VIII. If the incarnation is attributed to God in some places, as in 1 Tim.
3:16 and Col. 2:9, this must be understood of the divine nature not absolutely
and in itself, but in relation to the character of the Son by which he is
distinguished from the Father and the Holy Spirit. This is what the fathers
mean when they affirm that the divine nature became incarnate: namely, ἐν μιᾷ
ὑποστάσει, *in one hypostasis*, and ἐν ὑποστάσει λόγου, *in the hypostasis of
the Word*. Thus Damascenus explains this phrase in book 3, chapter 6, as does
Lombard in book 3, distinction 2.

IX. Although the essence cannot be divided into parts because of its supreme
simplicity, nevertheless it exists differently in the Father—namely,
ἀγεννήτως, *unbegottenly*—differently in the Son, in whom it is γεννητῶς,
*begottenly*, and differently in the Holy Spirit. Therefore human nature could
be assumed according to the one mode of subsistence that constitutes one
Person of the Trinity without being assumed according to the other modes that
constitute the other Persons, since these modes differ not only conceptually
but also in reality.

X. The intuitive vision of the blessed and the hypostatic union in Christ are
different in character. The former terminates first and in itself in the
divine essence as it is common to the three Persons, because it must attain
the thing as it is in itself. That is, God must be seen and known by the
blessed as he is, and thus as both one and three at the same time. But human
nature did not have to be hypostatically united to the whole Trinity. It was
enough for it to be joined to the divine nature, not insofar as that nature
extends broadly and also belongs to the Father and the Holy Spirit, but as it
is characterized and determined in the Person of the Son.

XI. If our theologians have not adopted the Lutherans’ phrase asserting that
the divine nature became incarnate, they do not do so as though they
absolutely and simply denied that the divine nature became incarnate and said
that only the τρόπον ὑπάρξεως, *mode of subsistence*, became incarnate apart
from the divine nature, as they are unjustly accused. They only censure a less
precise manner of speaking, which cannot agree with either Scripture or the
truth of the matter unless it is skillfully explained.
