Fifth Controversy: The Administration of Public Sacred Rites

The Council of Trent, session 22, chapter 8, denies that it is expedient for
the Mass to be celebrated everywhere in the vernacular language; there “Mass”
signifies the whole liturgy. The Jesuit Stapleton says in his English book
against Jewel, article 3, page 75: “Devotion is not helped but hindered by a
language that is known and understood.” Bellarmine contends in book 2, chapter
15 of *On the Word of God* that public sacred rites throughout the whole world
were adminis—

[1620 Tome I | col. 49 | BSB frame 00039]

—tered in ancient times in the Latin language and that they ought still to be
administered in it.

We, however, assert that in all the ancient churches the readings, prayers,
and other public sacred rites were conducted in a language the people
understood, and that they still ought to be so conducted.

The state of the controversy, therefore, is whether prayers and the other
public sacred rites ought to be conducted in the vernacular language of each
nation, rather than only in Latin. They deny this; we affirm it, and we
establish our position with these arguments.

1. Argument. What God has commanded ought to be done in the church. But God
has commanded that prayers and the other public sacred rites be conducted in
the vernacular language known to the people (1 Cor. 14:37 and verses 9, 14, 16,
19).

2. Argument. Whatever things are done in the church ought to be done for its
edification (1 Cor. 14:26). But the fact that a language is unknown edifies no
one (ibid., vv. 6, 9). Therefore.

3. Argument. If a minister prays in an unknown language, he will be a
foreigner to the people and the people to him (1 Cor. 14:11). But this ought
not to happen. Therefore, he ought not to offer prayers in an unknown language
before the people.

4. Argument. All things ought to be done in the church decently and in a
fitting order (1 Cor. 14:40). But to conduct prayers and public sacred rites in
an unknown language is contrary to this rule. For when the minister prays
publicly in an unknown language, the people do not understand what he says,
and therefore can neither pray nor say “amen.” Indeed, only the one who
understands—namely, the minister—prays. Thus prayers that ought to be public
become private, which is contrary to the church’s decency and order.
Therefore.

5. Argument. Just as the prophets, Christ, the apostles, and their true
successors administered sacred rites, so they ought still to be adminis—

[1620 Tome I | col. 50 | BSB frame 00039]

—tered. But they administered sacred rites in the vernacular language known
to the people. Therefore.

6. Argument. Some of the papists themselves concede that prayers which are
understood are more useful and better, such as Lyra on 1 Corinthians 14 and
Cardinal Cajetan in his commentary on the same chapter. But what is more
useful and better is to be retained in the church.

7. Argument. The papists themselves concede that the Egyptians, Muscovites,
and Ethiopians conduct public sacred rites in the vernacular language, and
that the Moravians have been permitted to use their own language in sacred
rites. Add that, according to Eck in his *Commonplaces*, the same was permitted
to the South Indians, a practice which their clergy in that place observe even
to this day. Therefore, if they do rightly when administering public sacred
rites in the vernacular language, let the other churches possess the same
right.

Testimonies

Basil the Great says in epistle 63: “When day is now dawning, all together, as
though with one mouth and one heart, offer the psalm of confession to the Lord,
and each professes repentance in his own words. These customs agree in all the
churches of God.”

Augustine says in book 12, chapter 8 of *On Genesis*: “No one is edified by
hearing what he does not understand.” The same author says on Psalm 99:
“Blessed is the people that understands jubilation. Let us run toward this
blessedness; let us understand jubilation and not pour it forth without
understanding.”

Chrysostom says in homily 35 on 1 Corinthians that one who speaks in an
unknown language is not only useless and a foreigner to others, but even to
himself, if he does not understand.

Similarly, Ambrose says on 1 Corinthians 1: “If you assemble to edify the
church, those things ought to be said which the hearers understand.”

Cassiodorus says on Psalm 46: “We ought not only to sing psalms, but to do so
with understand—

[1620 Tome I | col. 51 | BSB frame 00040]

—ing. For no one does wisely what he does not understand.”

Jacques Lefèvre says in his commentary on 1 Corinthians 14: “When the majority
of people now pray, I do not know whether they pray with the spirit; yet they
do not pray with the mind, for they pray in a language they do not understand.”

Objections

Objection 1. The figures and ceremonies of the Old Testament were useful to
the people. But the people did not understand them. Therefore, prayers that
are not understood can nevertheless be useful to us as well.

Response. 1. There is more in the conclusion than in the premises. 2. The
minor premise is false. For although they did not understand them as clearly
as we now do after the advent of the truth, they nevertheless understood them
according to their measure, for the Levites taught the people and explained
all the ceremonies. 3. The Jews had express commands to practice these
ceremonies. But to conduct prayers and the other public sacred rites in an
unknown language is contrary to God’s command, as was proved above.

Objection 2.

In Leviticus 16:17 the people are commanded to remain outside and wait for the
priest while he enters the sanctuary and alone offers prayers for himself and
for the people. An example of this command is found in Luke 1:10 concerning
Zechariah. Thus the people did not understand the priest; indeed, they did not
even hear him. Therefore, it is not necessary that the people understand the
prayers offered to God by the priest.

Response. 1.

There is more in the conclusion than in the premises, for only this follows:
in the Old Testament it was not always necessary for the people to understand
the priest’s prayers. But an argument from the worship of the Old Testament to
the worship of the New is not valid, because, like the other things, that too
was typological. In it the priest bore the type of Christ, who has now entered
heaven and intercedes for

[1620 Tome I | col. 52 | BSB frame 00040]

us. 2. A command concerning this existed in the Old Testament, but let them
produce a command concerning the conduct of New Testament sacred rites in
Latin, if they can. For in religion nothing is to be attempted without God’s
command. Finally, that priest did not then use a foreign language, much less
did he use an unknown language when he spoke to the people.

Objection 3.

If the people ought to pray only those things which they understand, then
they ought never to recite the Psalms or other chapters from the prophets,
evangelists, and apostles, because they do not understand everything contained
in them. But the latter is absurd. Therefore, so is the former.

Response.

The proposition is denied, because the rationale of prayers is one thing and
that of reading Scripture another. Scripture is to be read even if it is not
understood, and it is to be read so that it may be understood all the more:
first the words, then the sense. For the fact that we understand so little
results from neglect of reading and meditation. But since prayer is a
conversation with God and ought to arise from our own understanding, we must
know with certainty what we say while praying.

Objection 4.

Religion and piety have diminished since some began to use the vernacular
language in the church. Therefore, sacred rites ought to be conducted only in
the Latin language.

Response. 1. The antecedent is denied, because true and living piety now
exists among many in place of the senseless superstition that existed
formerly, although the church has never been, and never will be, without many
profane people. 2. The inference is denied. For the gospel is to many an aroma
of death leading to death, and Christ himself is called a stone of stumbling.
Indeed, when Christ himself preached, the Pharisees became more obstinate; yet
it does not therefore follow: the gospel ought not to have been preached.
Likewise, it does not follow that because the apparent piety of some has
diminished on account of public sacred rites conducted in the ver—

[1620 Tome I | col. 53 | BSB frame 00041]

—nacular language, therefore those rites are not to be administered in the
vernacular language, unless the adversaries show that the vernacular language
is the true cause of this impiety.

Objection 5.

In the time of the apostles, the churches of Asia Minor used the Greek
language in public sacred rites. But the whole people did not understand the
Greek language. In Acts 14:11 the people speak in Lycaonian after the lame man
has been healed, and Acts 2 recalls the various languages that then existed in
Asia and which the apostles used. Therefore.

Response.

The argument is drawn from particulars; if the proposition is universal, it
is false. 2. The assumption is denied, because the Lycaonian language was only
a dialect of the Greek language. The apostles preached to the people in Greek,
and the people undoubtedly understood them. Nor does “they spoke in Lycaonian;
therefore they did not speak in Greek” follow any more than “they spoke in
Doric; therefore they did not speak in Greek.” It is evident that the people
of Asia Minor at that time understood the Greek language, because otherwise
the apostles would not have preached to them in Greek, nor would Paul have
written in Greek to the Galatians, Ephesians, and Colossians. Finally, those
languages of Asia Minor mentioned in Acts 2 were not altogether different
languages, but only various dialects.

Objection 6.

Whatever contributes to the unity of the church is to be preserved. But to
administer sacred rites in one language in all the churches contributes to
the unity of the church. Therefore.

Response. 1.

The proposition is true only of holy and pious unity.

2. The assumption is false, because the unity of the church depends not on
unity of language, but on unity of doctrine.
