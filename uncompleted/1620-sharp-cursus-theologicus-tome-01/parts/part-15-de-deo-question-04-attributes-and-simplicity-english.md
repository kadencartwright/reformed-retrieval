QUESTION IV

What God Is Like: Where the Divine Attributes Are Treated

In Scripture various attributes or names are attributed to God, by which not
so much God’s nature as it is in itself as what can be known by us concerning
God’s nature is explained.

The names of God are of two kinds: (1) those by which the subject, namely God,
is signified; and (2) those which are predicated of the subject.

Those which signify the subject are of two kinds: (1) those which indicate
the essence, such as יהוה; and (2) those which indicate the persons, such as
אלהים. Of the things predicated of

[1620 Tome I | col. 181 | BSB frame 00105]

the subject, some are predicated of God and creatures, such as those by which
realities that are accidents in creatures signify substance in God—for
example, *good*, *wise*, and *just*. They are predicated of creatures κατ᾽
ἀναλογίαν, “according to analogy.” Others are predicated of God alone, and
these are spoken either absolutely, without relation to creatures, such as
those which signify the essence, the persons, and essential attributes; or
with relation to creatures, such as creation, redemption, sanctification, and
so forth.

Again, of the attributes which belong to God alone, some belong to him from
eternity, such as the attributes of the essence and of the persons, while
others belong to him only in time, such as those which are said of God with
relation to creatures.

Again, some attributes are common to the whole Trinity, while others are
proper to the persons. The latter are the properties of the individual
persons, such as to beget, to be begotten, and to proceed. The attributes
common to the whole Trinity are of four kinds: (1) those by which the essence
is signified, such as יהוה; (2) those by which the persons are indicated in
general, such as the name אלהים in the Old Testament and the name ὑποστάσεως,
“of subsistence,” in the New Testament; (3) those by which God’s essential
properties are explained, such as *omnipotent* and *just*; and (4) those which
are predicated of God with relation to creatures. For just as the works *ad
extra* are common to the three persons, so are the names by which they are
indicated.

Again, God’s attributes are ascribed to God either negatively or
affirmatively. The negative ones remove from God every imperfection which is
in creatures, as when it is said that he is infinite, immeasurable, immortal,
and so forth. The affirmative ones are those which signify some perfection in
creatures and are predicated of God, in order to signify that God is the
author of all the perfections which are in creatures, as Damascene teaches in
book 1, chapter 13, *On the Orthodox Faith*.

[1620 Tome I | col. 182 | BSB frame 00105]

Of those things which are said affirmatively, some are attributed to God
properly and others improperly. The following are attributed properly: that
essential attribute יהוה; the personal attributes; those which are said of
God with relation to creatures; and the essential properties. Of the latter,
some are in the abstract, such as life, light, wisdom, justice, and so forth,
by which God is signified to be of the simplest and most perfect essence and
the cause of all perfection. Others are in the concrete, such as *just*,
*living*, and so forth, by which God is signified to be a truly subsisting
reality.

Whatever in its proper signification signifies realities of a sort which
belong only to creatures and not to God is spoken improperly or figuratively.
Such are attributes signifying realities, words, and actions which are
ascribed to God by ἀνθρωποπάθειαν, “anthropopathism,” and signify spiritual
realities: for example, eyes signify omniscience, and the right hand signifies
power, and so forth.

The use of God’s attributes, therefore, is as follows: (1) by some it is
signified what God is not, and the negative attributes belong here; (2) by
some God’s essence, which as it is in itself is incomprehensible, is indicated
according to our capacity; (3) by some a plurality of persons distinct from
one another is shown; (4) by some God’s essential properties are signified;
(5) by some the relation which God has to creatures is signified; and (6) by
the names of attributes in the abstract God is signified to be of the simplest
and most perfect essence.

Rules Concerning God’s Attributes

1. No attributes can express God as he is in himself, who or what sort of
being he is, or how greatly he differs from creatures, just as he cannot be
comprehended as he is in himself by a cre—

[1620 Tome I | col. 183 | BSB frame 00106]

—ated mind. For we comprehend only finite things, in a finite manner, under
the aspect of the finite. God’s attributes are therefore taken from created
things. Nevertheless, they suffice for knowing God insofar as it is useful
and necessary for our salvation that we know God.

2. Although God’s attributes are drawn from created things, nevertheless, if
the reality itself which is signified by these attributes is considered, some
are said properly of God, such as life, goodness, and so forth, because the
things signified here are in God prior to and in a more perfect manner than
they are in creatures. But if you consider the manner of signifying, they are
predicated of God improperly, because they express God not as he is in
himself, but as he can be perceived by us. Therefore, when we attribute
something to God, in order to distinguish the manner in which the attributes
are in God from the manner in which they are in men, something must be added,
as when we say that he is supremely just, supremely merciful, and so forth.

3. God’s many and various attributes do not conflict with his unity, but all
signify God’s one most simple essence. For the attributes are not diverse
powers in God as they are in creatures, but are in reality one with God’s
essence and with one another; indeed, they are God’s essence itself. They
therefore differ not in reality but in reason, and they are attributed to God
on account of our capacity, since under one name or act of the intellect we
cannot understand what must be perceived concerning God, and so that we may
perceive God to have in himself the power of all virtues and perfections.

4. Although the same reality is signified by all the attributes, these names
are nevertheless not synonyms, because synonyms signify not only the same
reality but also according to the same concept and manner. The names of the
attributes, however, comprehend the same reality under diverse concepts and
manners, because

[1620 Tome I | col. 184 | BSB frame 00106]

they are diverse conceptions in our intellect concerning the same reality,
as we perceive one thing concerning God by the name *justice* and another by
the name *mercy*, and so forth. Accordingly, the use of diverse names is of
the greatest importance among us, and there is no ταυτολογία, “tautology,” in
them.

5. Attributes predicated of creatures which signify perfection not
absolutely but with the mode proper to creatures are said of God only
μεταφορικῶς, “metaphorically,” and by similitude. The same is true of
properties and actions arising from the proper principles of species, such as
to laugh, to walk, and so forth.

6. Attributes which signify perfections of which creatures cannot be capable
can be predicated of God most properly, but in no way of creatures; such are
infinity, simplicity, and so forth.

7. Names signifying the divine essence—even in the concrete—cannot be
predicated properly of a creature, but only on account of the similitude which
they have with the divine essence, such as *God*, and so forth.

8. God’s attributes are predicated of God more properly in the abstract than
in the concrete, as *goodness* is said more properly than *good*.

9. Names which signify vice or imperfection cannot be attributed to God.
Therefore, he is never called impious, a sinner, and so forth.

10. God’s attributes are not accidents in God, but essentially God himself.

11. Attributes which indicate a relation between God and creatures, such as
the name *Creator*, are predicated of God in time in such a way that meanwhile
nothing new is added to God and no change occurs in God, but in creatures. In
creation, for example, God was not changed, but creatures which did not exist
began to exist. Thomas therefore rightly says that God does not have a
relation to creatures, but creatures to God,

[1620 Tome I | col. 185 | BSB frame 00109]

because creatures have a real relation to God as Creator, while God has only
a relation of reason insofar, namely, as creatures are referred to him.

Having reserved the personal attributes and those relative to creatures for
their proper place, we will treat the remaining ones here. They are divided
into ἀκοινώνητα, “incommunicable,” and κοινωνητά, “communicable.”

The ἀκοινώνητα are those of which nothing similar inheres in created things,
but which are so proper to God that they belong to no one besides God. They
are therefore infallible marks of Deity and denote the divine essence to us *a
priori*, and do so in two ways: (1) affirmatively, as simplicity; and (2)
negatively, as infinity. Thus there are two ἀκοινώνητα attributes: simplicity
and infinity.

On the Simplicity of God

Simplicity is an attribute of God by which the divine essence is signified to
be free from accidents, composition, division, and multiplication, and
therefore to be incorporeal or spiritual, invisible, and impassible, Exodus
33:20, John 4:24, 1 Timothy 1:17 and 6:16, and James 1:17.

Something is called simple in two ways: (1) properly, namely, that which is
not composed of diverse things; and (2) comparatively. Among the latter, some
substances are called simple and others composite. Thus angels and souls are
called simple in comparison with the elements, while the elements are called
simple in comparison with things composed from them.

God alone is properly simple, without any composition, which is proved as
follows.

Argument 1. That which is absolutely and perfectly being by its essence, and
so exists from itself and through itself, is properly simple. But God’s nature
is properly such. Therefore, he calls himself *I AM*, without any predicate,
Exodus 3:6.[^1]

2. That which is most perfect is most simple, because the more perfect
something

[1620 Tome I | col. 186 | BSB frame 00109]

is, the more simple it is. But God is the most perfect being, as was proved
above. Therefore.

3. If God is composite in any way, he must have been composed by something.
For nothing composes itself or is its own efficient cause; otherwise it would
be prior to itself. But God is eternal, and nothing is prior to him, Isaiah
43:10. Therefore.

4. Every composite is by nature posterior to the parts of which it is
composed. But God is in every respect the first being, as above. Therefore.

5. Every composition is either from integral parts, as a quantified body; or
from matter and form, as man; or from subject and accident, as any created
substantial things; or from act and potency; or from genus and difference; or
from supposit and nature; or from existence and essence. But God is composed
in none of these ways. Therefore. The assumption is proved. Not in the first
way, because God is not a body but a most spiritual essence. Not in the
second, because composition occurs only in things that are merely corporeal,
or partly corporeal and partly incorporeal, while God is pure Spirit. Not in
the third, because no accident befalls God, since he is wholly ὁ ὢν, “the One
who is,” that is, existing from himself and through himself, whereas accidents
do not exist through themselves. Not in the fourth, because God is altogether
immutable; accordingly, the philosophers called God pure act, free from all
passive potency. Not in the fifth, because God is the first and transcendent
being; accordingly, he is under no genus and cannot have a specific
difference. Not in the sixth, because in this way Christ alone is composed as
θεάνθρωπος, “God-man,” not properly by a composition of parts, but of number,
as Damascene speaks. But God is his own life, and his essence and nature are
the same, because he is his own most pure form and his own life. Therefore.

Not in the seventh, because the existence of a thing is that which

[1620 Tome I | col. 187 | BSB frame 00110]

each thing actually is, while essence is that through which each thing is
what it is. These differ in creatures, as being a man and humanity, being
living and life; but this does not apply to God, because in God existence and
essence are the same, as are the living one and life, John 14:6, 1 John 4:8
and 1:5. For God is being through himself and is most perfect, and does not
exist by participation as creatures do.

6. If there is composition in God, then it is either in the essence, in the
persons, or in the operations. But none of these can be asserted. Therefore.
The assumption is proved. Not in the first, as was proved above. Not in the
second, because the essence is whole and the same in all the persons, and
remains the same in each; hence the individual persons are said to be in the
individuals. Not in the third, because essence and operation are the same in
God. Therefore.

Objections

1. Christ as God is from himself, but as Son he is from the Father. Therefore,
with respect to the persons there is not supreme simplicity in God.

Response. I deny the consequence. For although Christ as Son is from the
Father, there is not one essence in the Son and another in the Father, but it
is in another mode. A diverse mode, however, does not make the essence
different or composite, but only makes the Son distinct from the Father. But
distinction is not composition.

Instance. But a person properly consists of essence and a mode of subsisting.
Therefore, there is a kind of composition.

Response. I deny the consequence. For if the persons were composed of these,
they could be resolved into them. Furthermore, the mode of subsisting and the
essence differ not in reality but only in reason, for to be the Father is
nothing other than to be the essence which communicated itself through
generation.

Instance. Where there are many real things, there is real composition. But in
God there are

[1620 Tome I | col. 188 | BSB frame 00110]

many real things, namely, the essence and real relations. Therefore.

Response. I deny the proposition, because a relation does not compose but
only distinguishes; for paternity adds nothing to the Father’s essence.

Instance. If relations are real entities, then they add something real to the
terms. But they are real entities. Therefore.

Response. Relations are called real entities not from themselves, but by
reason of their foundation. Therefore, they add nothing real to the
foundation but only distinguish the terms.

2. In Scripture human members are attributed to God. It therefore follows
that God is composite.

Response. This is a fallacy from the figure of speech, because human members
are attributed to God not properly but metaphorically, so that we may
understand what must be known spiritually concerning God.

The use of the doctrine concerning God’s simplicity is as follows.

1. That we may know there is one God, free from every imperfection and full of
every perfection—indeed, that he is perfection itself.

2. That God is true and sincere in his promises, sayings, and deeds, and
therefore no one is deceived by him. Hence the certainty of our salvation
arises from his promises.

3. That God pursues hypocrites with hatred and loves the simple who are like
himself. Hypocrisy must therefore be fled, and simplicity or sincerity must
be pursued.

4. We learn what sort of people we will be after this life, namely, like God
without any admixture of sin.
