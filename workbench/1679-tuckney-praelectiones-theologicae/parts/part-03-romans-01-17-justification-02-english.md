From the matters which have perhaps been treated at excessive length, then,
it is clear, I hope, that in the sacred volume—especially where it treats the
justification of a sinner and that righteousness in which we wish to stand
before God’s tribunal and according to and on account of which we wish
sentence to be passed upon us—the word *justification* does not denote the
infusion or increase of inherent righteousness. In a forensic sense it
signifies judging and pronouncing a man righteous and freeing him from guilt
and punishment. Our theologians ordinarily prove this rightly and soundly:

1. From Paul’s disputation, especially in the Epistle to the Romans, which
treats this matter thoroughly and is wholly accommodated to a judicial
process. There and elsewhere the whole act of justification is described by
forensic terms, such as judge, judgment, defendant, party at law (διάδικος),
advocate, witness, and others like them.

2. From the opposition established between justification and accusation or
condemnation. So it is in the human forum in Deuteronomy 25:1 and 1 Kings
8:32, and in the divine forum in Romans 8:33.

3. From synonyms and equivalent expressions. Such are reconciliation in
Romans 5:10 and 2 Corinthians 5:19; free acceptance in Christ in Ephesians
1:6; imputing righteousness in Romans 4:6; not imputing sin in verse 8;
covering sins in verse 7—not with good works, which is a gloss, nor by the
infusion of righteousness, which is Cornelius a Lapide’s interpretation, but
by the remission of sins, as is plainly clear also from the first member of
the verse; not being condemned in John 3:18; not coming into condemnation in
John 5:24; and many other expressions of this kind. None of these speaks of
inherent or implanted righteousness, but they intimate the forensic sense
which we assert.

Nevertheless, it must by no means be concealed that some of our writers
affirm that the word *sanctification* is put for justification or a
declaration of holiness, in accordance with the Levitical phrase in which a
priest is said to sanctify a leper—that is, to pronounce him holy or clean.
They wish the word *sanctification* to be taken in this sense also in Acts
26:18: “that they may receive remission of sins and an inheritance among
those who are sanctified by faith”; in Ephesians 5:26, “Christ gave himself
for the Church that he might sanctify it, having cleansed it by the washing
of water through the word”; and in Hebrews 10:10, “We have been sanctified
through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all.” Although I
might rightly dispute with them about their interpretation of these passages,
I do not wish to do so at present. This is enough.

Just as they assert that *sanctification* is put for *justification*, so,
conversely, others of our writers—because of their supreme and exceedingly
close connection—think that *justification* sometimes appears to include
sanctification itself as a consequence (*Synopsis of Purer Theology*,
disputation 33, sections 3 and 36). But perhaps that only appears to be so and
is not so, at least not in the passages they adduce.

The first of their passages is Romans 8:30: “Those whom he called, he also
justified.” They think that because sanctification ought not to be omitted
from that golden chain, it must be understood under the word
*justification*. But perhaps it is rather understood either under the word
*calling*, which is the beginning of sanctification, or under
*glorification*, which is the result (ἀποτέλεσμα) of sanctification and
differs from it only in degree.

The second passage brought forward for this purpose is Titus 3:7. After first
mentioning the richly poured-out renewal of the Holy Spirit—which speaks of
sanctification—the apostle immediately adds ἵνα δικαιωθέντες, “that, having
been justified, we

[Marginalia: Gerhard, *Commonplaces* 3, p. 446; Beza on Titus 3:7; so Forbes,
chapter 10, section 32.]

[1679 printed p. 41 | IA leaf 49]

might be made heirs according to the hope of eternal life,” as though this
δικαίωσις, or justification, meant the same thing as that ἀνακαίνωσις, or
renewal. But nothing here, in my opinion, compels the word δικαιωθέντες,
“having been justified,” to be drawn from its native meaning to that
figurative one. The sense of the passage flows perfectly well if
δικαιωθέντες is understood of justification properly so called. Nor does the
apostle confuse two things in this enumeration of the causes of salvation.
He distinctly sets forth two principal actions of God: regeneration in
verses 5 and 6, and justification in verse 7, as Piscator rightly shows in
his analysis of the passage. There is therefore nothing to compel that sense;
indeed, there are several considerations which suggest the contrary:

1. The word δικαιωθῆναι, “to be justified,” does not ordinarily have that
meaning in Paul.

2. In particular, δικαιωθῆναι τῇ χάριτι, “to be justified by grace,”
whatever Roman Catholics and Grotius have thought, denotes for our
writers—even for Beza himself and the Leiden theologians, who wish to expound
this passage in that manner—not an infusion of inherent grace, but the free
remission of sins in Paul.

3. Since κληρονόμοι γενώμεθα, “that we might become heirs,” follows ἵνα
δικαιωθέντες, “that, having been justified,” the word κληρονόμοι, “heirs,”
suggests that δικαιωθέντες should be restricted to justification properly so
called. For a right to the inheritance flows immediately from those two
relative benefits of Christ, namely justification and adoption.

3. From verses 4 through 8 the apostle enumerates, with an altogether divine
and inimitable brevity, all the causes of our salvation, in my judgment—as
Piscator shows at length on this passage—and in exactly the same order as in
Romans 8:30. First he places election, then calling or regeneration, afterward
justification, and finally glorification or eternal life. There is therefore
no reason why justification properly so called should be excluded here,
since it is primary among these causes of salvation, especially when it
occurs word for word (αὐτολεξεί) and by its proper name. They do not wish it
to be excluded altogether, but wish it here to include sanctification in its
meaning. So say the Leiden theologians, and so too Beza on this passage:
“I take the name *justification* broadly, so that it includes whatever we
obtain from Christ, both through imputation and through the efficacy of the
Spirit in sanctifying us. The verb *to justify* is likewise taken in this
way in Romans 8:30.” Thus far Beza. But we have already shown that it need not
be taken that way there; nor is there any greater necessity for that meaning
here.

Yet this may be added. Even if everything these learned men desire were
granted—namely, that in these passages the word *justification* includes
sanctification in its meaning as something connected with it—nothing thereby
prevents it from retaining its own forensic sense. A husband does not
renounce his identity because he imparts his name to his wife on account of
their closest connection; nor does a father because he imparts his name to
an adopted son. Thus this detracts nothing from the forensic sense and
contributes not the least thing to the Roman Catholic cause.

From these matters thus explained it is at last sufficiently clear, in my
judgment, that throughout the sacred volume, wherever it treats this
controversy, this forensic meaning of the word is true and genuine and in
every respect consonant with the mind of the Holy Spirit.

It is also clear, as we said finally, that all uses of the same word which
are perhaps bent in another direction in some passages can readily be, and
must altogether be, referred back to that meaning. It will be worthwhile to
review a few of them here.

[1679 printed p. 42 | IA leaf 50]

1. *To justify* is sometimes used for approving, bearing witness to,
acknowledging, and celebrating as just. In this sense wisdom is said to be
justified by her children in Matthew 11:19. But this comes from the usage of
the forum, where the one who is justified is approved and acknowledged as
just. For in every language it is customary for terms belonging to a species
to be transferred to the genus, as Chemnitz rightly observed—although to the
most learned Gataker it seems more likely that this meaning of the word
passed from a common use, which existed first, to the usage of the forum.

2. *To justify* is sometimes put synecdochically, as some would have it—or I
would prefer, metonymically—for *to free*. So in Romans 6:7, ὁ γὰρ ἀποθανὼν
δεδικαίωται ἀπὸ τῆς ἁμαρτίας, “for he who has died has been justified from
sin.” The Vulgate and Erasmus render it literally in this way; others render
it “acquitted.” But Grotius renders it “is free from sin,” as Beza before him
had “has been freed,” lest anyone take it as speaking of the remission of
sins or acquittal from sins. For the apostle is treating there not the
remission of sins but mortification. Therefore δεδικαίωται here is the same
as ἀπηλλάχθαι, ἠλευθερῶσθαι, and πεπαῦσθαι—“to have been released, freed,
and made to cease”—as Basil explains it. Grotius says that the verb
δικαιοῦσθαι is used very frequently in this sense in this epistle, in order
to establish his Roman Catholic meaning of the word and at the same time to
weaken and utterly enervate the sweetest doctrine of justification. Yet, with
his leave, I would say that neither here nor in his *Prolegomena* did he prove
or even attempt to prove that this word is taken in this sense very
frequently in the epistle; nor will anyone ever prove it. He has us freely
acknowledging that it is taken so in this passage—and this one alone. Yet, as
even Estius the Roman Catholic admits, “the phrase is taken from forensic
judgments, where one who was acquitted was said to be justified, that is,
freed either from a charge laid against him or from a master who wished to
claim him as his slave.” Thus Estius. This follows the Hebrew idiom, which
comprehends the consequence together with its antecedent. For the man who is
justified or acquitted is also freed from the punishment which he would
otherwise have undergone. So Beza on this passage.

3. *To justify* sometimes means to instruct unto righteousness. In this way
the Vulgate renders Daniel 12:3, where the teachers are called מַצְדִּיקִים,
“those who justify many,” namely efficiently (ἐργαστικῶς). This occurs not
only because God uses them as instruments for generating inherent
righteousness in those people, but also because he uses them to lead people
through the doctrine of the Gospel to Christ, so that by his righteousness
they may in the forensic sense also be regarded and constituted righteous.

4. Those who remain in the state of justification can also be said to be
justified. So, as we showed before, several writers expound Revelation 22:11,
ὁ δίκαιος δικαιωθήτω ἔτι, “Let him who is righteous be justified still,” as
a continual or repeated sense and application of justification properly so
called and its external demonstration. But the fact that it commands the
continuation of the thing does not deny the thing itself, nor draw it away
from the proper meaning of the name from which it takes its origin and to
which it must also be referred.

5. The comparative justification of which the prophet speaks in Ezekiel
16:51–52 must also be referred here: “Be ashamed and bear your disgrace,
בְּצַדֶּקְתֵּךְ אַחְיוֹתֵךְ, “in your justifying your sisters”—that
is, virtually, with reference meanwhile to the formal act of justifying; or
not absolutely but comparatively, because Samaria did not commit half your
sins, as is explained in the

[Marginalia: Piscator, *On Justification* 2.2, page unspecified; Matzdikim;
Betsaddakteek; Achiotheek.]

[1679 printed p. 43 | IA leaf 51]

preceding verse. This, as I note in passing, confirms the meaning of
justification which we defend and plainly destroys the Roman Catholic
position concerning inherent righteousness. For Samaria, which is said here
to be justified, was not just with respect to inherent righteousness. Since
she too had acted unjustly, she was by no means just in this sense. Rather,
although she acted badly, she acted less badly than Jerusalem.

6. Here we must also consider the common distinction between the
justification of a cause and that of a person. The justification of a person
is more universal, when a man is declared altogether righteous. The
justification of a cause is more particular, when a man who is guilty in
other respects is accused either of some crime of which he is innocent or
concerning some matter for which he deserves praise and reward instead. Thus
Job, David, Jeremiah, and other godly men, who never dared justify themselves
before God, nevertheless ordinarily defended their righteousness and
innocence against human slanders in some matter in which they had perhaps
acted well, or at least had done nothing wrong, and thus justified
themselves. Yet both of these—the justification of a person and of a
cause—are forensic. Indeed, the justification of a cause is also that of a
person to the extent that the cause under accusation is declared good and
just, so that the person whose cause it is, or who defends it, may be regarded
as just. Our Bradshaw truly teaches us this in his little book on
justification, which the renowned Gataker deservedly judged to be golden and
worth any price.

6. It must not be denied that *to justify* signifies not only justification
before God but also before men. L. de Dieu treats the latter at greater
length on Romans 8:4. With respect to this, de Dieu says that works justify;
Calvin himself says they are imputed to us. De Dieu draws Psalm 106:31,
Revelation 22:11, 1 Corinthians 6:11, and several other passages to this
heading. Consult him.

8. Nor should that other division of justification into active and passive
be omitted. Each is one and the same justification, just as one and the same
motion is an action in the agent and a passion in the patient. Thus the same
justification is called active with respect to God who justifies, but passive
with respect to the man who is justified. Maccovius defines the latter as
the reception through faith of the remission of sins, and the sense of the
active justification. I would prefer the former expression, that it is the
reception of the active justification. As for the latter expression, that it
is the sense of the active justification, I would not dare affirm it with
him, lest I assert that all who lack this sense have not yet been justified.
But he said this according to the opinion of several of our most learned and
godly theologians, who define it either from their own experience or, against
the Roman Catholic suspension of judgment (ἐποχὴν), from a sense or knowledge
of this kind. Hence the same author also appends this statement: “After God has
justified us, he creates faith in us.” This is true insofar as it concerns
this distinction between active and passive justification with regard to
faith. For God not only justifies us before this sense of faith and assurance
of his grace—which often follows later in time, and sometimes a considerably
long time after justification—but at least by nature he first offers and
applies Christ and his righteousness to us. To that extent he may be said to
justify us actively before, aided by his grace, we can receive through faith
the offered righteousness of Christ, in which at last the true result
(ἀποτέλεσμα) of justification consists. Yet these two are so joined that
they exist at the same time. There is no moment of time of which it can be
said, “At this moment God justifies us actively, but we are not passively
justified.” Least of all can they ever be

[Marginalia: See Chamier, vol. 3, book 21, chapter 5, section 28; Tilenus,
*Syntagma*, p. 216, section 17; *Commonplaces*, chapter 69, pp. 669, 791,
793.]

[1679 printed p. 44 | IA leaf 52]

so separated that those who are justified as far as God is concerned never
in themselves and in the outcome arrive at the true state of justification.
I would not be reluctant to grant that this act of God which precedes the act
of faith in our justification comes under the name of *justification* in
Holy Scripture, perhaps in the passage where God is said in Romans 4:5 to be
τὸν δικαιοῦντα τὸν ἀσεβῆ, “the one who justifies the ungodly.” For before
faith is produced in us, we are ungodly—although that passage can, and perhaps
must, be explained differently, as we shall see later. But that in the
operation (ἐνεργείᾳ) of that grace those should be said to be justified by
God (δικαιωθῶσιν) who neither now believe nor ever will believe is, in my
judgment, nowhere said or written in Holy Scripture.

Yet this seemed right to our most learned paraphrast and scholiast. On the
words in Romans 5:9, πολλῷ οὖν μᾶλλον δικαιωθέντες νῦν ἐν τῷ αἵματι αὐτοῦ
σωθησόμεθα δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἀπὸ τῆς ὀργῆς, “Much more then, having now been
justified by his blood, shall we be saved through him from wrath,” he asks
whether δικαιωθέντες, “having been justified,” is taken actively or
passively. That is, does it signify only the act of Christ meriting and
obtaining pardon and life by his death, and the act of God the Father
admitting men to pardon and granting room for repentance—which, according to
this interpreter, means only offering pardon and acceptance under the
condition required in the Gospel—or does it also signify the act of the
sinner accepting that pardon, by which act he becomes a participant in it?
His answer is that the word comprehends both senses in itself, so that we may
be said to be justified either, first, as far as God and Christ are concerned,
because the price has been paid by Christ and accepted by the Father, so that
if we are not actually justified it comes about through our own fault because
we did not fulfill the condition; or, second, as far as we are concerned,
when the benefits Christ merited have now been conferred upon us and we are
actually justified. He says that the word is taken in the second meaning in
the first verse of that fifth chapter: “Therefore, having been justified by
faith, we have peace with God.” But in the ninth verse of the same chapter,
“Therefore, having now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be
saved through him from wrath,” he says it bears the first sense of
justification. He attempts to prove this with three arguments.

1. From the scope of the chapter, which is entirely to illustrate the love
of God the Father and Christ toward us, as the immediately preceding eighth
verse states in particular: “But God commends his love toward us in that,
while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.” There the whole matter is
restricted to Christ’s death, namely, to what Christ accomplished on his part
for justification, without taking account of what is extrinsic to him—the
condition to be fulfilled by us. The latter is regarded in verse 1, “having
been justified by faith”; only the former is regarded in this verse, “having
been justified by Christ’s blood.”

2. He seeks further to prove the same point from the statement in verse 8,
“in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.” “He died for us”
is the same as if he had said, “We were justified by his death.” He wishes
this to be clear from comparing verses 8, 9, and 10 with each other. Moreover,
when he says that Christ died for us while we were still sinners, by
*sinners* he understands those who are such habitually and in the worst
sense, whom he also calls enemies in verse 10. Yet he says that while they
were such they were reconciled by Christ’s death. But those reconciled are
the same as those justified. Actual pardon or justification does not belong
to enemies as long as they remain such, and yet they are called reconciled
or justified. It must therefore be in the former sense. Thus that meaning of
the word *justification* occurs there.

3. This

[1679 printed p. 45 | IA leaf 53]

he finally confirms from the comparison made in verse 16 between
justification through Christ and condemnation through Adam. The latter
condemnation belongs to all, but actual and passive justification does not
belong to all. There is therefore some justification which extends as widely
as Adam’s condemnation. This is the justification by which Christ merited
pardon for all, God likewise proposes it to all, and in this sense justifies
them, although far more do not apprehend it by faith and therefore are never
actually, passively, or perfectly justified. This is the opinion of that most
learned man; these are the arguments by which he attempts to prove it.

I have the following brief replies to make.

As to the first argument:

1. I deny the argument. For the scope of the entire chapter is not to
illustrate Christ’s and God’s love toward us if that love is restricted, as
it is by the renowned man, to the merit of Christ’s death and its
proclamation to us, to the exclusion of what God further works in us so that
we may become participants in Christ’s merits and in the grace offered to us
by God—which he regards as something extrinsic. For in that chapter the
apostle not only commends the love of God and Christ toward us. Throughout
almost the whole chapter he also copiously illustrates its effects, which
indeed flow from that love but are distinct from it. What else do those words
at the beginning of the chapter mean: “Therefore, having been justified by
faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom
also we have access by faith into this grace in which we stand,” and the
remaining statements that follow? They speak not only of the merit of
Christ’s death and its proclamation and offer to us, but also of its actual
application to us and the effects which follow it.

2. I also deny the consequence of the argument, which is this: if the scope
of the chapter is to illustrate the love of God and Christ toward us, then
in the ninth verse it does not speak of actual justification, or of our
actual apprehension through faith of divine love and Christ’s merits, which
is extrinsic to that love. This, I say, does not follow.

First, although our actual and full justification is distinct from the love
of Christ dying for us and of the Father offering pardon and salvation
through him, since it is an effect of that love, it is not therefore so
extrinsic to it merely because it is an effect that, because verse 8 speaks
of God’s love and Christ’s death, verse 9 would not speak of that actual
justification. Indeed, the same love of God is regarded in that actual
justification as in Christ’s death and merit, and much more than in the mere
proclamation of this love. Faith, through which God justifies us, is no less
from grace than the merit of Christ on account of which he justifies us,
Ephesians 2:8. Nor is God’s love less in fulfilling this condition in us than
in proposing it to us. There is therefore no valid inference: “This chapter
speaks of God’s love and of Christ’s death and merit; therefore it nowhere,
and specifically not in the ninth verse, speaks of actual and perfect
justification.” This becomes even clearer from the following consideration.

Second, if the ninth verse does not speak of actual justification because
the scope of the chapter is to illustrate the love of God and Christ, then
by the same reasoning neither can the first verse. For why can the first
verse agree with the chapter’s scope—or depart from it—any more than the
ninth? Yet with the most learned man himself admitting that

[1679 printed p. 46 | IA leaf 54]

in the first verse the apostle speaks of actual and perfect justification—he
will not, I think, say this conflicts with the scope of the chapter, for it
would have been shameful to stumble on the very threshold—why then can he not
speak of the same thing in the ninth verse without departing from his scope,
since the very same word δικαιωθέντες is used in both places?
δικαιωθέντες οὖν ἐκ πίστεως εἰρήνην ἔχομεν, “Therefore, having been
justified by faith, we have peace,” in the first verse; δικαιωθέντες νῦν ἐν
τῷ αἵματι αὐτοῦ, “having now been justified by his blood,” in the ninth. The
same word, δικαιωθέντες, appears in each. The same justification also appears
in each, namely actual and perfect justification.

Nor can he answer that the first verse speaks of justification by faith,
which places the condition of justification on our side and therefore
speaks of perfect passive justification, while the ninth speaks of
justification by Christ’s blood, which indicates only what Christ
accomplished on his side by his death and therefore the merit of
justification, not its full effect. For both passages speak of the same
justification, and one already accomplished—δικαιωθέντες is in the aorist.
The first verse describes it from its instrumental cause, namely faith; the
ninth from its meritorious cause, namely Christ’s blood or death. Nor, I
think, will he deny that a man who has actually been justified can truly and
properly be said to be justified through Christ’s blood just as much as
through faith. The general scope of the chapter therefore proves nothing;
this was the most learned man’s first argument.

2. But perhaps the immediately preceding eighth verse will accomplish this;
that was the second argument. Yet what does that verse contain which will
prove the point?

1. This: Christ is there said to have died for us. But what of it? According
to our scholiast, this is the same as if it had been said that we were
justified by Christ’s death.

I answer that these two statements do not mean or signify precisely the same
thing: “Christ died for us,” and “We have been justified by Christ’s death.”
The former speaks of Christ’s death and its end with respect to us—namely,
our justification, but as future. The latter presents its effect as already
actually produced. According to the most learned man’s own opinion these two
are so distinct that they can even be entirely separated, so that the one
for whose justification Christ died may never be justified. I myself would
not say that. Yet I do say that, although they are never separated in that
way, they are nevertheless so distinguished in themselves that they are not
altogether identical.

But, he will say, at least here they are taken for the same thing, as is clear
from the circumstances of verses 8, 9, and 10 compared with each other. I
deny it. What are those circumstances? He gives two.

The first is that verse 8 says Christ died for us while we were sinners, so
that by *sinners* are understood those who are habitually such and sinners of
the worst kind. Actual justification does not belong to such people as long
as they are such, yet here in verse 9 they are called δικαιωθέντες,
“justified.” Since this cannot be said with reference to actual
justification, it remains that it is said only with reference to Christ’s
death suffered for them and its proclamation made to them in the Gospel. I
answer:

1. I grant that those who in this eighth verse are called ἁμαρτωλοὶ,
“sinners,” are called ἀσθενεῖς, “weak,” and ἀσεβεῖς, “ungodly,” in verse 6,
and ἐχθροί, “enemies,” in verse 10. By these names are meant not only the
more abandoned among the Gentiles, as the most learned man intimates here and

[1679 printed p. 47 | IA leaf 55]

elsewhere speaks more openly, but all the unregenerate without exception.

2. I also grant that as long as they are such, they have by no means actually
been justified—that is, with actual and perfect justification.

3. But I deny that the apostle says they were justified while such. He says
that the same persons, as to the underlying subjects, had been such sinners,
that Christ had died for them as such in verses 8 and 9, and that the same
persons had been justified. But they were not justified as long as they
remained such sinners; rather, when they had once believed in Christ. It was
not only that Christ had died for them and that God had proclaimed pardon and
life to them in the Gospel, but that they had now apprehended Christ by faith
and God had now actually returned into favor with them. This will plainly be
clear, I think, from the following points.

1. The apostle very plainly sets forth a twofold state of these men according
to two different times and even opposes the one to the other, as verses 8 and
9 read: “But God commends his love toward us in that, while we were still
sinners, Christ died for us.” So we have it in verse 8. The ninth then
follows in this way: “Therefore, having now been justified by his blood, much
more shall we be saved through him from wrath.” Here ἔτι ἁμαρτωλῶν ὄντων,
“while we were still sinners,” and δικαιωθέντες νῦν, “having now been
justified,” are manifestly opposed. They designate the same men, indeed, but
a widely dissimilar—indeed, an entirely contrary—state of those same men. It
is like the statement in the immediately following chapter 6, verse 21: τίνα
οὖν καρπὸν εἴχετε τότε ἐφ᾽ οἷς νῦν ἐπαισχύνεσθε, “What fruit did you have
then from the things of which you are now ashamed?” τότε and νῦν, “then” and
“now,” are no more clearly opposed there than ἔτι and νῦν, “still” and “now,”
are here. Another time requires other conduct. He also plainly sets forth
another state.

2. It must also be observed that just as the apostle opposes times and states
to each other, he argues from the lesser to the greater on that basis. “When
we were sinners, Christ died for us; πολλῷ οὖν μᾶλλον, much more therefore,
having been justified by his blood, shall we be saved through him from
wrath.” Yet the argument from the lesser to the greater would have been
ridiculous, and he would have said πολλῷ οὖν μᾶλλον, “much more therefore,”
most ineptly if μᾶλλον οὐδὲν, “nothing more,” but the same and entirely
identical thing appeared in verse 9, “having now been justified by Christ’s
blood,” as in verse 8, “while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.”
That would be the case if this justification contained nothing in itself
beyond Christ’s death and merit for us and its proclamation.

3. This appears even more clearly because in that ninth verse the apostle
speaks of the Romans and the others he has in view as believers according to
their present condition: δικαιωθέντες νῦν, “having now been justified.” At
that time they were not merely persons for whom Christ had previously died
and to whom God had proclaimed pardon and salvation through the ministry of
the Gospel and set it before them. They had so accepted the offered Christ
and his grace, as he says expressly in verse 11, δι᾽ οὗ νῦν τὴν καταλλαγὴν
ἐλάβομεν, “through whom we have now received reconciliation,” that they were
not merely to be justified because the price had been paid and offered. By
faith—according to the phrase in verse 19, δίκαιοι κατασταθήσονται, “they
shall be constituted righteous”—they had actually been justified, or
actually and fully constituted in the state of justification. The many other
statements which occur everywhere in the same chapter also proclaim this.
The first objection therefore proves nothing.

2. But perhaps the second will press more strongly. It is this: in verse 10
we read, ἐχθροὶ ὄντες κατηλλάγημεν τῷ Θεῷ, and so forth: “When we were
enemies,

[Marginalia: See Ames, *Marrow* 1.24.4.]

[1679 printed p. 48 | IA leaf 56]

we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son,” so that
justification is the same as reconciliation. Therefore, since men are said
in verse 10 to have been reconciled through Christ’s death while existing as
enemies, why by the same reasoning might those not yet regenerated or
actually justified nevertheless be said to have been justified by Christ’s
blood in the immediately preceding verse 8?

I acknowledge that this argument has more weight in itself, but truth does
not succumb to it. I therefore answer as follows.

There is no opportunity at present to inquire anxiously what identity
reconciliation has with justification or how it differs from it. There will
be a more suitable place to speak of these matters later. For the present
purpose, let what I have now said suffice.

1. Although our theologians go in different directions concerning their
formal nature, I think they all agree concerning their subject: to the extent
that anyone has been reconciled, they judge him also to have been justified.
Thus at least in general an argument can rightly be drawn from reconciliation
to justification, as the most learned man does here.

2. I also grant that, as regards the formal character of the subjects to
which they belong, very similar things can be said of both and actually are
said on the sacred page. In the passage under discussion, Romans 5:10,
enemies are said to be reconciled; so also in chapter 4, verse 5, God is said
to justify the ungodly, τὸν δικαιοῦντα τὸν ἀσεβῆ. For it belongs equally to
the formal character of the subject—the man to be justified—that he is in
himself guilty and ungodly, and to the man to be reconciled that he, as such,
is an enemy. Formally, I say, the one whom God justifies was guilty in
himself. Likewise, the one who is reconciled to God is formally an enemy in
himself.

3. I reply, however, that there is some difference here: reconciliation is
mutual, but justification is not. Both God is reconciled to us and we are
reconciled to God. But although we are justified, God is not also
justified—not, that is, in the way we are. God is indeed said to be justified,
as in Romans 3:4, “that you may be justified in your words,” insofar as in
justifying us, even though ungodly, he shows himself to be just, Romans 3:26.
But far be it from us ever to think him ungodly or guilty as we are, so that
when he justifies us he should in turn need to be justified as much as we do.
He can be said to be reconciled just as much as we are, because by our sin he
was an enemy to us just as much as we were to him. Although this enmity in us
toward him was most unjust, his toward us sinners was most just. But he never
was, nor could or ever can be, subject to sin and guilt as we were, so as to
need justification and pardon. Therefore, I say, although reconciliation is
mutual, justification is not. There is thus at least some difference between
them in this respect. I wished this to be noted because it serves our present
purpose.

4. I therefore say that in this mutual reconciliation between God and us,
God may be said to be reconciled to us in two respects, as we also to him.
For God may be said to be reconciled to us either initially or perfectly and
fully. He is reconciled initially in the merit of Christ’s death once
undergone and endured. Thus the apostle says in Colossians 1:20 that peace
was made through the blood of his cross and that through it Christ reconciled
us to God, having slain the enmities through it, Ephesians 2:16. For through
Christ’s death sins were expiated and hence the cause of the enmities was
removed. To that extent God is immediately reconciled to us, so that even
though we are still enemies in ourselves, he will never

[Marginalia: See Forbes, *On Justification* 19, p. 69.]

[1679 printed p. 49 | IA leaf 57]

destroy us eternally. But he is fully reconciled only when, the Gospel of
peace first having been proclaimed to us and a covenant of peace with him
having been entered by us, he returns into favor with us, Hosea 2:19.

In these two respects God is reconciled to us. We too are reconciled to God
in two ways, passively or actively. Our passive reconciliation with God flows
immediately from God’s own reconciliation with us, however that
reconciliation is taken. For by the very fact that God is reconciled to us,
it is necessary that by this passive reconciliation we also be reconciled to
God. But active reconciliation on our part occurs only when in our effectual
calling, having laid aside our former enmities, we accept the conditions of
peace offered in the Gospel, according to Isaiah 27:5: “Let him take hold of
my strength that he may make peace with me, and he shall make peace with me”;
and also the apostle’s statement in Colossians 1:21: “When you were formerly
alienated and enemies in your mind intent upon evil works, he has now
reconciled you.”

Since these things are so, I say that by this passive reconciliation, with
regard solely to the merit of Christ’s death, we can be said to be reconciled
to God while we are in our sins, on account of the love of benevolence with
which even then God embraces us for the sake of Christ’s merit. Scripture
appears to say this, and our theologians do not deny it. But by active
reconciliation, before effectual calling, while we remain alienated and
enemies, neither is God reconciled to us with respect to the love of
friendship, nor are we, or can we be said to be, reconciled by active
reconciliation. Thus in 2 Corinthians 5 the apostle says that they had
already been initially reconciled by God and, with respect to themselves,
passively reconciled in verses 18 and 19, yet in verse 20 he not only commands
but begs them to be reconciled further and actively. Perhaps Scripture in
this sense says that before effectual calling we have been reconciled to God
through Christ’s death.

But does it also say we have been justified? I do not believe so. In view of
Christ’s death, God will not condemn forever the elect already redeemed by
Christ but not yet effectually called, Romans 8:34. Yet Scripture nowhere, in
my judgment, says that they were already justified by God at that time. Since
justification also is, as we said before, either active or passive, as far as
active justification is concerned, when God is already, so to speak, in
motion and just about to apply Christ’s merits to us, he actively justifies
us. Thus he is said in Romans 4:5 to justify the ungodly. But before faith,
which is the instrument of passive justification, though we are passively
reconciled we are not said to be passively justified. Let this be the sum of
what has been said:

1. We are said to be passively justified most properly and effectually
(ἀποτελεσματικῶς) when we apprehend Christ by a living faith. Thus Scripture
says that God justifies believers in Romans 3:28, 30, and everywhere else.

2. We are also truly and properly justified, or God justifies us by active
justification, when he prepares to justify us by granting us pardon on
account of Christ’s merits and imputing Christ’s righteousness to us. Thus
Scripture says that God justifies the ungodly in Romans 4:5. For before faith
he finds us all to be such, and as such grants us pardon: guilty of sin and
subject to eternal wrath.

3. Scripture says in the passages previously cited that before this—either
from our birth or from Christ’s death—we were redeemed by it and perhaps
also, in the sense stated before, reconciled. But it does not appear that
Scripture elsewhere says that either the elect or the redeemed are also
justified.

4. Least of all—which is what the most learned man claims—are all those for
whom Christ is said to have died in any sense, or to whom salvation is
proposed and proclaimed in the Gospel, called justified or said to be
justified, although they neither believe nor ever will believe. Least of all,
I say, are all such people called justified, either in the passage under
discussion, Romans 5:9, or anywhere else in Scripture. This will become still
clearer.

3. From the answer to the third argument which he adduces to prove this,
taken from the comparison in Romans 5:16 between the justification we have
through Christ and the condemnation we have from Adam. It can be set out as
follows: Justification through Christ extends as widely as condemnation
through Adam. But all are condemned through Adam; therefore all are
justified through Christ, verse 18. Yet all are not actually justified
through Christ. There remains, then, some other meaning of *justification*
which would comprehend all, just as that condemnation through Adam does.
This is the very meaning he understands: all are justified in that Christ
paid the price for all, God accepted it, and he proposes it to all through
the preaching of the Gospel to be accepted. Therefore, I say, at least in
this passage this meaning of the word *justification* is given. I answer:

1. The Gospel has not always been proclaimed to all who were begotten from
Adam and condemned in him. The apostle indeed says in Romans 10:18 that all
heard, but he means the Israelites, as is clear from the immediately
following verse 19: “Did Israel not know?” He also says, “their sound went
out εἰς πᾶσαν τὴν γῆν, into all the earth, and their words εἰς τὰ πέρατα τῆς
οἰκουμένης, to the ends of the inhabited world.” But πᾶσα ἡ γῆ ἡ οἰκουμένη,
“all the earth, the inhabited world,” in that passage, according to the
apostle’s meaning, does not include the whole compass of the earth or regions
of the world that were then entirely unknown. Thus not even on this
account—that is, with respect to the proclamation of the Gospel—can all
without exception be said to be justified, as he would have it.

2. The price was not paid by Christ for every single person to whom the
Gospel became known in such a way that all who persevere in their unbelief
until death can therefore be said to be justified. We indeed grant that
Christ died for all in such a way that if they believed in him they would be
saved. But to say that they are therefore equally saved and justified would
in both cases be equally absurd. It would be as though a prince promised to
grant pardon to rebels and traitors if they laid down their arms and returned
to their duty, and you called them pardoned and justified although they
remained rebels—indeed, when they had already been led away to pay the
penalty for their obstinacy and had mounted the gallows.

3. Finally, the major premise of the syllogism must be denied altogether if
it is taken universally, as it must be: namely, that justification through
Christ extends as widely as condemnation through Adam. The apostle indeed
says in Romans 5:18 that “as through one offense guilt came upon all men unto
condemnation, so through one justification the benefit abounded to all men
unto justification of life.” But it is not absolute: as guilt came upon all,
so justification came upon all. Rather, it is restricted according to the
limitation set down in the immediately preceding verse 17: upon all, that is,
whoever receive the gift of grace by faith. For in that apostolic comparison
Adam and Christ are set forth as two heads of covenants, as it were, or as
two fathers of families. Adam is the head of all naturally begotten from him,
who without exception

[1679 printed p. 51 | IA leaf 59]

are all those condemned through his guilt. Christ also is the head of all
regenerated according to grace. They alone are believers, and they alone are
both justified and saved.

At last, then, sufficient answer has been given, in my judgment, to the most
learned man’s arguments. From this it is clear that neither in that passage
nor anywhere in Scripture are any said to be justified merely because the
price was paid by Christ and proclaimed through the Gospel, unless at last a
true application of it is made on God’s part through the Spirit and on our
part through a living and life-giving faith, which is the sole instrument of
justification.

Thus far we have treated the first question: What is it properly to be
justified, or in what sense is the word *justification* taken in Paul’s
disputation about it? Only this appears still to be desired for a fuller
explanation: since we have now seen what it is in itself, let us also see how
it is distinguished from the other benefits connected with it and conferred
upon us by Christ—for example, how it differs from redemption; from
reconciliation and adoption; and finally from regeneration and
sanctification.
