### Assertion VIII

No one who has not been lawfully called, lawfully sent, appointed, and
ordained may administer the sacraments. The reasons are these:

I. To those to whom God gave the power to proclaim the gospel he also gave the
power to administer the sacraments. But God granted the former to no one
except a person lawfully called, sent, and ordained. Therefore he did not
grant the latter to anyone else. We proved the minor premise above in the
preceding assertion. Christ’s words in Matthew 28:19 confirm the major:
“Therefore go, teach all nations, baptizing them,” and so forth. A person ought
not to separate what Christ joined together. But in this commission Christ
firmly joined these two things. Therefore the conclusion follows. “For,” says
Bucanus in the locus *On the Sacraments*, question 61, “the sacraments are
appendages of the ministry of God’s word and seals of the divine promises.
They cannot lawfully be affixed without the declaration of that word of God,
for the accessory does not subsist unless the principal is present. And it
belongs to the same person, as a chancellor relying on the prince’s authority,
to draw up the documents of a testament in good”

[Volume II; printed p. 119; ONB canvas 127]

“faith and to seal them with his seal.

“II. From the same passage we can prove the same thing in another way: if the
power to baptize, or to administer the sacraments, was granted by Christ to
the apostles alone and to the pastors who succeed them, then others may not
arrogate this authority or assume this power to themselves. But the former is
true. Therefore, and so forth. The consequence of the major premise needs no
proof, since Christ’s institution must stand, the order Christ instituted in
his own house must be constantly and inviolably observed, and Christ’s laws
bind perpetually. The minor premise is established by the text, for here the
commission is given to the apostles. But that ordinary ministers and pastors
are comprehended in the apostles is clear from Christ’s statement: ‘And
behold, I am with you all the days, even to the consummation of the age.’ The
apostles were not going to remain on earth until the consummation of the age.
Therefore others are also understood here—those who, after the apostles’
departure, would preach the gospel and undertake this office—and consequently
they are distinguished from those who are taught and baptized.

“III. The right to dispense God’s mysteries belongs not to all members of the
church but only to ministers duly appointed and ordained. Therefore the right
to administer the sacraments also belongs to them. The reason for the
consequence is that the sacraments must be numbered among these mysteries.
Many hold *sacramentum* and *mysterion* to be synonyms. ‘Generally,’ as the
very learned Essenius speaks in his *System of Dogmatic Theology*, title 18,
thesis 4, ‘they signify a sacred and hidden thing; specifically, note well, a
sacred, hidden, symbolic thing; and in the strictest sense, a sacred, hidden,
symbolic thing that signifies and seals spiritual grace.’ And according to
Chemnitz in the *Examination of the Council of Trent*, part 2, chapter ‘On the
Word Sacrament,’ the Greeks called baptism and the Lord’s Supper *mysteries*
because ‘under visible elements spiritual grace is offered and presented in a
hidden manner, which is not apprehended by the senses’”

[Volume II; printed p. 120; ONB canvas 128]

“or understood by reason, but is known by faith from the revelation of the
word alone.” The premise is clear from 1 Corinthians 4:1: “Let a person regard
us in this way, as ministers of Christ and stewards of God’s mysteries.” “To
distinguish the teachers of the churches from the other ministers”—that is,
from the faithful, says Pareus on the passage—“he calls them household
managers or stewards of God’s mysteries: that is, those who perform and ought
to perform in the church, which is the house of the living God, what household
managers perform and ought to perform in a family or house—not in dispensing
wealth, but in connection with God’s mysteries. Divine and hidden things
concerning God’s worship, or the external signs, rites, and ceremonies of
these things, are called mysteries.” And a little later: “He does not say
*mystery* in the singular, but God’s *mysteries* in the plural. It therefore
extends more widely and at the same time embraces the divine sacraments, which
the Greeks called mysteries because by an analogy of signs they represent to
us the hidden mystery of our redemption and seal it to believers as with a
divine seal. The sum of this stewardship is therefore rightly reduced to
these two things: the preaching of the doctrine of the gospel and the
dispensing of the sacraments.”

Calvin also includes the sacraments on the passage, saying: “Moreover, since
the sacraments are annexed to these mysteries as appendages, it follows that
those who preside over administering the word are also their lawful
stewards.” That these words are spoken not only about apostles but also about
other lawful ministers is attested by the commentators just mentioned. For
Calvin says that “Paul here defines the office of an ecclesiastical pastor,”
and Pareus wishes them to be understood not only of the apostles but also “of
the other rulers of the churches.” Moreover, this is clear because Paul
himself applies them also to Apollos, who was neither apostle nor evangelist
but an ordinary minister of the gospel, and to Sosthenes in chapter 1:1.
Indeed, in Titus 1:7 he calls all bishops God’s stewards—namely bishops
ordained and duly appointed, verse 5. Of such”

[Volume II; printed p. 121; ONB canvas 129]

“stewards we read in Luke 12:42: ‘The Lord said, “Who then is the faithful and
prudent steward whom the Lord will appoint over his household, to give them
their measure of food at the appointed time?”’ Therefore not everyone in the
household is a steward. This office belongs only to those whom the Lord will
appoint. This order must be observed until Christ’s advent, as verse 43
clearly indicates.

IV. Who dares, without intolerable usurpation and the crime of lèse-majesté,
to use a prince’s seal to seal and confirm something when the prince himself
has not commanded it? Is this not considered a heinous crime in every
commonwealth? But the sacraments are seals of the covenant of grace, Romans
4:11. Who, then, if not sent, commanded, ordained, or appointed by Christ,
dares to use these seals and apply or affix them?

V. Ephesians 4:11–13. Apostles, prophets, pastors, and teachers were given for
the perfecting of the saints, for the work of ministry, and for the edification
of Christ’s body. Among other things, the administration of the sacraments
belongs here, for by their administration the saints are joined together and
Christ’s body is built up. And, as was shown above, the administration of the
sacraments is a work of ministry.

VI. The office of pastor does not belong to everyone; therefore neither does
the administration of the sacraments. The premise was proved above. The
consequence is clear because the flock is fed by the administration of the
sacraments.

VII. This right of administering baptism was given in a special way to John.
Hence he was called John the Baptist by antonomasia, Matthew 3:1; 11:12, and
he was the first to dispense this sacrament, Matthew 3:6, 16; Mark 1:4; Luke
3:12, 21; John 1:28, 33; 3:22. The Socinians, especially Ostorodus, say in
vain that it cannot be demonstrated that John baptized by divine command,
since the sacred text plainly says that he was sent to bap-”

[Volume II; printed p. 122; ONB canvas 130]

“tize, John 1:33, and that his baptism was not from human beings but from
heaven, Matthew 21:25, and was God’s counsel, Luke 7:30—that is, instituted by
God’s supremely wise counsel. See also Luke 3:3–4. All our writers against the
Papists prove that John’s baptism differed from the baptism administered by
the apostles and other ministers only in some circumstance. Therefore, if at
that time no one except John was permitted to baptize, since no one else had
yet been authorized, neither will anyone now be permitted who has not been
called and ordained.

VIII. In the New Testament we see no private person, and no person not
solemnly called to the ministry, administering baptism. Christ’s disciples
baptized, but in Christ’s name and by his command, John 3:22; 4:2. So did the
apostles, Acts 2:38, 41; 10:47–48; 16:14, 15, 31, 33; 1 Corinthians 1:14, 16;
Ananias, Acts 9:10, 11, 17, 18; 22:17; and Philip, Acts 8:12, 26, 29, 38. How,
then, will it be proved that anyone may administer this sacrament, since this
cannot be demonstrated by any example, nor can any precept be produced or
alleged, nor any commission given to individuals?

IX. By the administration of baptism, the baptized are solemnly admitted into
the visible church. But it belongs to the ministers, to whom the keys of the
kingdom of heaven have been given, to examine and judge who should and should
not be received into the church. Therefore the power to administer this
sacrament belongs to them alone. We shall prove later that the keys were given
only to ecclesiastical ministers.

X. This is the error of the Anabaptists and Socinians, as it was formerly of
the Pepuzians, who were sometimes called Quintilians, and of Marcion, who
permitted women to baptize, even in public assemblies. See the *Magdeburg
Centuries*, century 2, page 93.

XI. If everyone were permitted to baptize, why not women also, as Marcion
said? If not publicly, at least privately, as the Pepuzians wished, since in
Christ Jesus there is neither”

[Volume II; printed p. 123; ONB canvas 131]

“male nor female, Galatians 3:28. But all our writers, when disputing against
the Papists, condemn and reject this practice. See Calvin, *Institutes*, book
4, chapter 15, sections 20–22. And Beza, in *Questions*, part 2, question 135,
says that ‘this administration of baptism by private persons is to be valued
no more highly than an action a private person might undertake at whim in the
king’s or commonwealth’s name, or than a seal fashioned by some private person
in imitation of the public seal and affixed to some document, or than a
farce.’

XII. This license had its origin not in Christ’s institution or the practice
of the apostolic church, but in a corrupt opinion about the necessity of
baptism. Thus Jerome against the Luciferians: ‘If necessity compels baptism,
we know that it also belongs to laypeople.’ And Augustine, book 2 *Against the
Letter of Parmenian*, chapter 13: ‘If a layperson, compelled by some necessity
of a dying person, has given what he learned must be given when he himself
received it, I do not know whether anyone would piously say that it must be
repeated. For if it is done when no necessity compels, it is the usurpation of
another’s office; but if necessity presses, it is either no offense or a
venial one.’ And in his letter to Fortunatus: ‘In a necessity, when bishops,
presbyters, or any ministers cannot be found and danger presses the person who
asks, lest he end this life without the sacrament, we are accustomed to hear
that even laypeople give the sacrament they received.’ And the Fourth Council
of Carthage, canon 99: ‘Though learned and holy, a woman must not presume to
teach men in the assembly or to baptize anyone unless compelled by necessity.’
Likewise Isidore, book 2 of *On Ecclesiastical Offices*, chapter 29: ‘It is
established that baptism is to be handled by priests alone, and that even
deacons are not permitted to carry out its mystery without a bishop or
presbyter, unless, when they are far away, the final necessity of illness
compels it, which is also often permitted to faithful laypeople.’ The
Lutherans rely on this single foundation, as may be seen in Gerhard, *On Holy
Baptism*, section 4. But our writers, with Beza in question 140, say: ‘The
beginning of our salvation is derived”

[Volume II; printed p. 124; ONB canvas 132]

“from the tablets of the covenant itself, not from the seal affixed to them.
Although anyone who scorns it is rightly deprived of their benefit, the
person who could not obtain it while preserving lawful order does not appear
to have despised it.’ And, as Calvin says excellently at the end of
*Institutes*, book 4, chapter 15, section 22: ‘This principle will soon settle
the controversy without difficulty: infants who happen to depart this present
life before it is possible for them to be immersed in water are not barred
from the kingdom of heaven. But we have already seen that no slight injury is
done to God’s covenant unless we rest in it, as though it were weak in itself,
since its effect depends neither on baptism nor on any additions. The
sacrament comes afterward as a seal, not to confer efficacy on God’s promise
as though it were invalid in itself, but only to confirm it to us. Hence it
follows that believers’ children are not baptized in order then for the first
time to become God’s children, as though they had previously been aliens from
the church. Rather, those who by the benefit of the promise already belonged
to Christ’s body are received into the church by a solemn sign. Accordingly,
if there is neither laziness, contempt, nor negligence in omitting the sign,
we are safe from every danger. It is therefore much holier to pay this
reverence to God’s ordinance, that we seek the sacraments from nowhere but
where the Lord deposited them. Where it is not possible to receive them from
the church, God’s grace is not so bound to them that we cannot obtain it by
faith from the Lord’s word.’

XIII. What has been said about the celebration of baptism must also be said
about the celebration of the Lord’s Supper. Those who say that in some case,
namely necessity, private persons may baptize do not hold that in any case
private persons may administer the Lord’s Supper, as may be seen in Gerhard.
It cannot be demonstrated by any precept or example that Christ granted this
power to every private person. Christ himself first instituted and adminis-

[Volume II; printed p. 125; ONB canvas 133]

tered it, Matthew 26:26–27; Luke 22:17–20, and commanded the apostles to
imitate him. We see this done in Acts 2:42; 20:7, 11. And Paul says in 1
Corinthians 10:16: “The cup of blessing that we bless, is it not the communion
of Christ’s blood? The bread that we break, is it not the communion of
Christ’s body?” Therefore Paul and similar ministers blessed the cup and broke
the bread.

Next, from that blessing required in the celebration of the Supper, it follows
that not everyone may administer it. Otherwise women would be permitted to
speak in public assemblies contrary to the apostle, because the Supper must
always be administered in public assemblies. Moreover, public and solemn
blessing or thanksgiving in ecclesiastical assemblies is a ministerial
action. Further, not all the church’s members are permitted to partake of the
Supper. Therefore much less will they be permitted to administer it. Finally,
because those who approach must be examined as to whether or not they are fit
to partake, and this investigation must be performed by the rulers, it also
follows that the Supper must be administered by ministers.

It is now easy to answer what Velthuysen objects on page 314 of his book.
First he says that this must be demonstrated from some New Testament text.
This has now been done: the sacraments must be administered not by just
anyone, even though he is chosen to do it for the sake of order, but by the
person who by God’s special ordinance has been separated, consecrated to God,
and ordained to this office. Velthuysen distinguishes between these and thus
makes it clear that by someone “chosen for the sake of order” he does not mean
a separated and ordained pastor, but the sort of person whom Socinians and
Anabaptists can recognize. Moreover he says that “baptism is valued less by
the apostle Paul than the administration of the word,” 1 Corinthians 1:17.
Response. What? Does Velthuysen think that the apostle speaks here absolutely,
and not comparatively? 2. And granted that Paul preferred the administration
of the word to the administration of bap-

[Volume II; printed p. 126; ONB canvas 134]

tism, will he conclude from this that Paul thought the duty of baptizing did
not belong to him in any way? If so, why did he baptize Crispus, Gaius, and the
household of Stephanas, verses 14 and 16, and Lydia, Acts 16:15, and the
Philippian jailer? “The other reason,” says Pareus on the passage, “why he
baptized few was not neglect of his office, but the nature of the ministry
enjoined on him by Christ—not baptizing, but preaching the gospel. But did
Christ not send all the apostles together to teach and baptize: ‘Teach all
nations and baptize them,’ and so forth? These ministries are indeed joined,
but the duty of preaching is prior, more important, more difficult, and more
necessary. Therefore he wished the apostles, appointed witnesses of his
doctrine, to be occupied chiefly in this, leaving the province of baptizing to
their junior colleagues—not because he did not also send them to baptize, but
because he did not send them primarily for this.”

Calvin says excellently on the passage: “He therefore replies that these were
not the chief parts of his function, for the duty of teaching had chiefly been
enjoined upon him, and he was to devote himself to it. For when Christ says to
the apostles, ‘Go, preach, and baptize,’ he joins baptism to doctrine merely
as an addition or appendage, so that doctrine may always retain the first
place. Yet two things must be observed here. The first is that the apostle does
not simply deny that he had a command to baptize—for this pertains to all the
apostles: ‘Go, baptize’; and he himself would have acted rashly in baptizing
even one person unless instructed by a command—but only indicates what was
chief in his calling. The second is that the sacrament’s dignity or fruit is
by no means diminished here, as some suppose, for the question here is not
about the power of baptism.” And a little later: “Indeed, if readers examine
all the circumstances more closely, they will see that a tacit irony lies
beneath the words, by which those who hunt a little glory from another’s labor
under the pretext of a ceremony are wittily rebuked. Paul’s labors in building
that church had been incredible. Those beloved teachers had come afterward
and by the sprinkling of water had drawn disciples into their sect. Paul
therefore, conceding the title of honor to them, testifies that he is content
with the burden.”

[Volume II; printed p. 127; ONB canvas 135]

See also Beza on the passage. Finally, although the apostle prefers the
administration of the word to the sprinkling of water, it does not follow from
this that anyone may administer baptism. By what means, I ask, will Velthuysen
prove this consequence?

He continues: “The administration of the Supper was nothing other than the
action of a private person, performed by no other person and in no other place
than the person by whom and the place in which preaching had been performed,
Acts 2 and 20; 1 Corinthians 10 and 11.” Response. He says boldly enough,
without any appearance of reason, that administering the Supper was the action
of private persons. Indeed, what he adds proves the contrary: that it was the
action of public ministers. For if the Holy Supper was administered by those
who preached the word, it was administered not by private persons but by
public ministers solemnly sent and ordained—namely, by the apostles. In Acts 2
the apostles preached the word, and verse 49 says that “they continued in the
apostles’ doctrine”—not the doctrine of private persons—“and fellowship, the
breaking of bread, and prayers.” And in chapter 20:7 Paul discoursed and
continued speaking until midnight. Likewise verse 11: “He broke bread and
conversed until dawn.” Paul, however, was not a private person.

As for the place, baptism ought not to have been administered in a place other
than that in which the word, of which it was an appendage, was administered.
Therefore Velthuysen has here cut his own throat with his own sword and
confirmed the truth he had determined to attack. For great is the power of
truth, and it will prevail at last.

He continues: “A clear demonstration of this entire case is that the Passover
was celebrated by each father of a family—and the celebration of the Holy
Supper succeeded it.” Response. Josephus, book 7 of *The Jewish War*, chapter
17, seems to suggest that in later ages the Passover sacrifices were
slaughtered, or certainly counted, by the priests.

[Volume II; printed p. 128; ONB canvas 136]

And from 2 Chronicles 35:10–11 and following it can be gathered that after the
land of Canaan had been occupied—or at least at that time—the lambs were not
slaughtered house by house as had been done in Egypt, as were not a few other
things because circumstances required it, but in the temple by the Levites.
For the sacred text reads: “Thus, when this worship had been prepared, the
priests stood in their station and the Levites in their divisions according
to the king’s command. They slaughtered the Passover, the priests sprinkled
the blood poured out by their hand, and the Levites stripped off the skins.
And they removed what was to be consumed as an offering to Jehovah, that they
might give it to the classes of the paternal families of each of their
countrymen, as is prescribed in the book of Moses.” See Gerhard, *On
Circumcision and the Passover Lamb*, section 34, and the end of Bucanus,
*Commonplaces*, “On the Sacraments,” question 38. The same can also be gathered
from 2 Chronicles 30:15–17 and following, where the Passover celebrated in
Hezekiah’s time is mentioned.

But whether this is true or not, with respect to this point there is no
inference from the Passover to the Holy Supper, no more than there is with
respect to very many other things. This, however, does follow: just as no one
except those whom God had commanded and designated was permitted to celebrate
the Passover, so now no one except those instituted by Christ is permitted to
celebrate the sacred Supper.

These are the objections he makes. Though few, they plainly enough indicate
that he conspires with the Arminians and Socinians. For Episcopius in the
public disputation of 14 July 1618 says without qualification that “this was
his position: that any private Christian is also permitted to administer the
Lord’s Supper.” And the Remonstrants in their *Apology*, folio 250: “What
prevents an infant in a case of necessity, when an assembly or pastor cannot
be had, from being dipped in water by some believer, or the Lord’s Supper from
being celebrated among believers?” Likewise Socinus, *Treatise on the Church*,
folio 15: “It is not necessary that some one particular man be chosen to do
this—for when the Lord Christ himself”

[Volume II; printed p. 129; ONB canvas 137]

“instituted it, he did not specifically entrust anyone with the duty of
administering it. Nor in this rite is there any giving or ministering of a
divine thing, and therefore no particular person is required who, as if
acting in Christ’s place, distributes the bread and wine to the others.” And
letter 2 to Radecke, page 126: “As for the Lord’s Supper, there is nothing to
make us suspect that it cannot be celebrated by anyone professing the name of
Christ.” What? Does Velthuysen’s position also rest on this foundation?

Andreas Radecke, in his notes to the *Refutation of Gordonius’s Knots*, chapter
5, folio 19 (as cited by Peltius in his *Harmony*, page 242): “Hence it appears
that believers by themselves, without any minister of the divine word, can
celebrate the Lord’s Supper if it happens that they are without one, and that
no distinctive administrator of it is required.” Likewise chapter 15, folio
49: “The practice nevertheless observed, that ministers distribute the
Supper, must be thought to occur not from any necessity but for the sake of
propriety and good order.” Listen, Velthuysen: this Socinian says exactly as
much as you do. Recognize your words and your position.

Likewise Theophilus Nicolaides, *Refutation of the Treatise on the Mission of
Ministers*, folios 106–107 (cited by the same author there): “It is not
necessary that some particular person be chosen to administer the Lord’s
Supper; I had taught by the Corinthians’ example that all believers can do
this—and that water baptism can ordinarily be conferred without a minister.”
Smalcius, *Disputation on the Ministry*, section 7: “It is entirely false that
ministers of the divine word are called God’s fellow workers with respect to
the sacraments dispensed by them. That this by no means pertains to the nature
of the ministry is clear from the fact that Paul, when expressly describing
the duties of bishops, makes no mention of this matter, and that the examples
in Acts 10 and 1 Corinthians 11 teach that they can be administered by another.
Although in a church already constituted it is better and more fitting for
ministers to administer them than for others.”

[Volume II; printed p. 130; ONB canvas 138]

See the same author in the *Disputation on Ecclesiastical Order*, page 385:
“One thing remains, which I shall dispatch very briefly. It had been said and
proved by our writers that the things they call sacraments can be administered
not only by a minister but also by others.” And page 386: “Let it therefore
remain firm that, just as someone can teach others even if called or sent by
no one, so the same person can also administer those sacred rites without a
mission.” See Hoornbeeck, *Refutation of Socinianism*, volume 3, page 205.

Likewise Volckelius, book 4, chapter 22: “The fifth thing contains the choosing
of some particular person—namely, the church’s pastor—to dispense the Lord’s
Supper to the others. This is certainly an indifferent matter, since it was
neither commanded to us by Christ nor can any reason be given why it must
absolutely be done. We do not, however, say this as though we denied that it
is most conveniently administered by the church’s minister, since among us it
is done in no other way: both because something similar was done in the first
institution of this rite, and because by this arrangement *to euschemon*, or
propriety, of which Paul wishes account to be taken in the church, can most
easily be observed. But in order that religion’s purity may be vindicated from
all superstitions, let this sacred business be administered through the
church’s minister—not from necessity, but for the sake of order.” Therefore
Velthuysen is wholly a Socinian in this question, for he bids every necessity
of precept to be gone.

Likewise Ostorodus, *Institutes*, page 440: “If the adversaries should now wish
to say that the present-day teachers of the churches not only preach but also
baptize and administer the Lord’s Supper, and infer from this that it is not
permitted without a special calling and mission, we reply that this cannot be
proved—namely, that no one is permitted to perform these things unless called
and sent to do so.” Let Velthuysen now show, if

he can, how to free himself from this Socinian error. Meanwhile, we proceed.
