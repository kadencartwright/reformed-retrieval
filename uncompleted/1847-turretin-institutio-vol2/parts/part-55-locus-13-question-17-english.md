QUESTION XVII.

*Did Christ rise again by his own power? We affirm against the Socinians.*

I. The first degree of Christ’s exaltation is placed in the resurrection,
about which the Socinians raise the present question. It does not concern the
truth of the resurrection. For although the Jews

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

[Printed p. 320 | Internet Archive PDF 348 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

call it into doubt and even venture to deny it, it was proved by so many
witnesses and testimonies and confirmed by so many signs and miracles that to
doubt it even now is inexcusable unbelief. This is especially so after the
irrefragable testimony of the apostles, who, in giving it, were neither
deceived nor willing or able to deceive, as was proved in Part I, Locus 2,
Question 4; and after the miracles which followed Christ’s resurrection and
demonstrate that he lives and reigns gloriously in heaven. This is to say
nothing now of God’s justice, Christ’s glory, and our salvation, which
necessarily required it according to the oracles issued long before about it.
Rather, the question concerns the manner of his resurrection: did he rise by
his own power or by another’s?

II. For as the Socinians hold that Christ was a *mere man* (ψιλὸν ἄνθρωπον),
so they contend that Christ did not raise himself from the dead by his own
power but was raised by God through another’s power. Hence in his *Disputation
on the Existence of the One Son of God,* Socinus “calls this doctrine
ridiculous, absurd, and impossible,” and in chapter 2 of the Racovian
Catechism, on Christ’s office, he calls it a signal error. But the orthodox
attribute the causality of Christ’s resurrection no less to Christ himself
than to the Father. And just as the power of the Father and the Son is one and
the same, they judge both that the Father raised Christ and that Christ raised
himself.

[Marginal heading: That Christ raised himself is proved, 1. From John 2:19.]

III. First, because Christ expressly attributes this to himself in John 2:19:
“Destroy this temple”—speaking of the temple of his body—“and in three days I
will raise it up.” And in chapter 10, verse 18, he testifies that he has power
to lay down his life and to take it up again. He could not have said this if he
had not possessed the power to rise from the dead. Nor, if the former passage
is figurative, does it have any less force, because it was clearly explained
by Christ.

[Marginal heading: 2. From Romans 1:4.]

IV. Second, because Scripture ranks Christ’s resurrection among the principal
arguments by which his divinity is proved. In Romans 1:4 Paul says that he was
“appointed” (ὁρισθέντα)—that is, declared and proved to be the powerful Son of
God, as if by a public decree, or declared powerfully according to the Spirit
of sanctification by the resurrection from the dead. Yet this could not be
said of a passive resurrection, with respect to which Christ would have been
merely passive, just as it cannot be said of Lazarus, Jairus’s daughter, and
others raised by God. Hence an active resurrection must be understood here,
by which he raised himself through the proper power of his divinity. That the
Spirit of sanctification here designates his divinity is clearly proved not
only by parallel passages in which Christ’s divine nature is customarily
denoted by the Spirit—1 Peter 3:18; 1 Timothy 3:16; Mark 2:8—but especially by
the antithesis made with the flesh. For as he is called the Son of David
“according to the flesh” (κατὰ σάρκα), that is, with respect to his human
nature, so he is said to have been declared the Son of God “according to the
Spirit” (κατὰ Πνεῦμα) by the resurrection, that is, according to the divine
nature. Otherwise the antithesis would be defective and would not show in what
respect the Son of God was not made from the seed of David.

[Marginal heading: 3. From John 5:21.]

V. Third, because Christ does the same works as the Father and likewise raises
whomever he wills, John 5:21. But if he can raise whomever he pleases, what
room can there be for doubt that he also raised himself? This is confirmed by
his calling himself the resurrection and the life in John 11:25: “I am,” he
says, “the resurrection and the life; whoever believes in me, though he is
dead, will live.” Here the abstract put for the concrete indicates that he is
the principal cause of the resurrection and the very fountain of life.
Consequently, in giving life to others, he himself is *life in himself*
(αὐτοζωή) and the author of his own resurrection.

[Marginal heading: 4. From Colossians 1:18.]

VI. Fourth, because he is the firstborn from the dead, Colossians 1:18. This
implies not only the prerogative of order but also the power and ability of
the one rising himself, since he possesses by nature what his other brothers
obtain only through the grace of adoption.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VII. Although Christ’s resurrection is frequently attributed to the Father
(Rom. 6:4; Acts 2:24; 13:30; Eph. 1:19–20), it does not follow that it cannot
be ascribed to the Son. For whatever the Father does, the Son likewise does,
John 5:19; and since the resurrection is an external work, it must be
indivisibly common to the entire Trinity. Yet there is a special reason why it
is attributed to the Father because of the suretyship which Christ had
undertaken, from which he had to be released by the Father as judge. Just as
the Father had handed Christ over to death for our sins, so he had to raise
him for our justification, in order to testify that full satisfaction had
been made to him through Christ. Elsewhere, when the confirmation of Christ’s
calling and the vindication of his divinity are at issue, the resurrection is
frequently attributed to Christ himself, as has been said.

VIII. It is absurd and ridiculous for a *mere man* (ψιλὸν ἄνθρωπον) who has
died to raise himself from the dead. But it is so far from absurd for the
*God-man* (θεάνθρωπον) to raise his humanity by the power of his divinity that
denial of it is blasphemous and impious.

IX. Christ could obtain his resurrection from the Father, Hebrews 5:7, through
prayers poured out by him as man, and nevertheless effect it as God by the
same power as the Father. For since Christ could raise Lazarus and others,
since the Father gave the Son to have life in himself, and since the Son, like
the Father, gives life to whomever he wills, who would wish to deny him the
power to call himself back to life?

X. Furthermore, although Christ raised himself, it should not be thought that
his body penetrated the stone placed upon the tomb, as the Roman Catholics
would wish in order to support their error. For the body that rises spiritual
is no more turned into a spirit than a body is turned into a soul. And divine
power displays itself not in overturning the natures of things but in
preserving them. Materiality and finitude do not belong to the infirmities of
animal life but to the essential properties of the body. Thus either the
creature could yield to the Creator at the resurrection without penetration
of dimensions, or Christ used the ministry of an angel to remove the stone
from the mouth of the tomb, Matthew 28:2, before he went out. He did so not
from need but from condescension, to testify that his power was attended by
retainers drawn from every order of creatures; or, because he was still
constituted in the condition of suretyship, he wished to await the Father’s
sentence, which was signified to him by the angel.
