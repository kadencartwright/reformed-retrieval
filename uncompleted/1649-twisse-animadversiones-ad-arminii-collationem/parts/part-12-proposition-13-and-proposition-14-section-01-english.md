PROPOSITION 13.

ARMINIUS. Now, however, as regards the preparation of grace and the preterition opposed to it, the
question is not whether God deems only some, considered in whatever manner, worthy of saving grace,
but does not deem others worthy of the same. For that is most manifest from many places in
Scripture. But the question is whether, by the act of predestination and of the preterition opposed
to it, God deals with human beings considered in their pure natural endowments. I have not been
able to persuade myself of this with certainty

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 58 | BSB canvas 70]

thus far, either from Thomas or from the writings of others who assent to Thomas. Reasons of the
following kind stand in the way.

JUNIUS. We stated beforehand that God’s election and non-election concern the human being
considered in common, and that is most true. We also stated that “in pure natural endowments” is
said ambiguously. Therefore our question is not whether election and non-election concern human
beings considered only in their pure natural endowments, etc.

ARMINIUS. That the human being considered in common is the object of the decree with which we are
dealing has not yet become clear to me from this response, etc.

Let this be the question. I maintain its negative, etc.

RESPONSE. We have more than once explained the rationale of Junius’s opinion about the human being
considered in common, deriving it, of course, from Piscator’s more distinct formulation of the same
opinion. As regards the consideration of the human being in pure natural endowments, a human being
cannot be considered by God except according to his natural endowments either actually or
potentially. Yet it by no means pleases me that the human being should be presented to God as
created when God predestines. We have already shown in their place, as occasion arose, how much
Arminius’s arguments directed against that opinion of Junius were worth, and we shall do the same
with those that follow. We likewise deny that God, by the act of predestination and preterition
(insofar as it denotes a decree), deals with human beings as sinners.

PROPOSITION 14.

SECTION ONE.

ARMINIUS. First, because Adam and all human beings in him were created in supernatural grace;
therefore no one could be considered in his pure natural endowments. The antecedent is proved.
First, because all were created in Adam in the image and likeness of God, and that is supernatural
grace, as was said. Second, the law which was given to Adam was given to all. This is evident from
the fact that all sinned in Adam and were made guilty of transgression. But that law could not be
performed without supernatural grace.

JUNIUS. That antecedent—namely, that Adam and all human beings in him were created in supernatural
grace—is ambiguous; further, it cannot be proved. We explained this before Proposition 10. The
consequent is denied and is ambiguous. Since both matters were treated before, I come to the
proofs.

We refuted the proof from the image of God in the same place and showed that it is not
supernatural in itself, although it had a relation and ordination toward supernatural grace, not
from its own nature or essence, but from the institution of grace. Therefore the proof is denied as
before.

The major premise of the second proof must not be accepted without a distinction. For one law
given to Adam was common and another particular. The common law—that is, the natural law joined to
nature—was given to all; the particular law was not. The particular law was that he should not eat
of the fruit of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. It is not credible that this law, which
was particular by institution, was given in common. It is not credible that, if all had remained in
integrity, they would have come into Eden to that tree so that their obedience might be tested.
Finally, Scripture does not say it.

ARMINIUS. By the arguments which I offer here I undertake to prove that Adam and all in him were
created in supernatural grace—that is, that in the first state they had not only nature but also
supernatural grace, whether by the act of creation or by an act of superinfusion. Hence I conclude
that no one could have been considered in his pure natural endowments by God as he predestined and
passed over or reprobated.

The earlier argument is taken from the nature of the image of God to which, or in which, the human
being was created. The other is taken from the law given to Adam and to all human beings in him,
which I say could not be performed without supernatural grace. We spoke before about the earlier
argument, in the response to Proposition 10; I refer back to those remarks.

We shall now consider the latter, and first its major premise, which posits that the law given to
Adam was given to all human beings. The added proof is that all human beings sinned in Adam and
were made guilty of transgression. You touch upon the major premise by the foregoing proof. I shall
see how you break it down and what strength these things have to establish it. You distinguish
between the laws given to Adam and make them twofold: one common and natural, the other particular,
etc., as follows.

RESPONSE. I do not doubt that Adam and all who were created in him were in supernatural grace,
that is, in grace of such a kind as flowed neither from the principles of the species nor from the
principles of the individual. Yet it could also be both called and regarded as natural: absolutely
with respect to the individual (since from the origin of his nature he received as implanted this
grace which you call supernatural, and things implanted from one’s origin may rightly be called
natural), but conditionally with respect to the species, since it would have been propagated to all
his posterity provided that Adam had persevered in his primeval integrity. Junius had already
demonstrated, however, that the proof of this antecedent which Arminius appends is invalid, since it
is sufficiently clear that, even when the grace which you posit as supernatural is excluded, Adam
was still created in the image of God. But let us see the consequence. If Junius had rested in his
first response to that consequence, in my judgment it would have had more substance and far less
quarrel and ill will. That is, he intended “pure natural endowments” so called to exclude not
supernatural endowments, but only the corruption of natural endowments. For the opinions about the
object of predestination that prevail (*epipolazousai*) among theologians are distinguished by this
notable difference: some set before predestination the human being not yet created; others, the
human being created indeed but not yet corrupted; and still others, the human being both created
and corrupted. But I seem to see what moved Junius to refrain from that interpretation and
justification of his opinion. It apparently seemed incongruous that a human being already clothed
in supernatural grace, and considered in that same manner, should be set before God’s counsel
about either granting or denying supernatural grace. It appeared more fitting that he should be
considered as indifferent to either alternative. In my judgment, however, that rationale in no way
prevented the human being from being considered in pure natural endowments even with the
supernatural excluded, although he had not been created without supernatural endowments. For it is
not necessary that all things be said to enter into consideration, but only those things of which
account must be taken in the operation under discussion. Since, therefore, God when predestining
had to take account of the human being, and consequently of all the things which belonged to
completing the nature of the human being created by God (an account, I say, not motivating but only
material), but not of the things which in no way pertained to constituting human nature, it seems
that the human being, although created in supernatural endowments, could rightly be said to have
been considered by God in no other way than in his pure natural endowments when God established
his predestination. On the contrary, the Arminians advance to that degree of madness, and with
great boldness and confidence, that they pronounce a human being not yet created to be considered
by God as created and corrupted—that is, by a consideration altogether contradictory to the
nature of the thing. By the foregoing interpretation of the disputed opinion, however, we
introduce a consideration that is merely abstractive, not contradictory. Mathematicians abstract
and do not speak falsely; but how all those who introduce a consideration of a thing contradictory
to the condition of the thing itself do not speak falsely I cannot comprehend with my
understanding.

Therefore the same response could also have sufficed to weaken Arminius’s second argument. Thus,
although that opinion of Junius by no means commends itself to me, even if it had stopped at the
consideration of a created human being—a point at which the face of that opinion presents itself as
far more attractive and

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 59 | BSB canvas 71]

more apt to win credence with respect to the decree to permit sin (for we are very inclined to this
opinion, that a person is considered by God first as created before God decrees that he be permitted
to fall into sin)—yet, I say, although that opinion by no means pleases me, notwithstanding however
great its plausibility, I have nevertheless shown, so far as in me lay, how weak the arguments
produced by Arminius to overthrow it are. Let us next consider Junius.

2. He says that the antecedent is ambiguous. We have already explained in what sense that grace
could have been called supernatural, and likewise in what sense natural. He adds that it cannot be
proved—not as though he denied that grace of this kind had been granted to Adam, but because he
believed it had not been produced through the act of creation. On this point Arminius professed
that he was unwilling to pull the contentious rope; he contended only that Adam received this
grace together with nature, whether by the act of creation or by superinfusion. But Junius says
that in Proposition 10 he refuted the proof sought from the image of God to which Adam was created;
and he truly did refute it.

In approaching the second proof he distinguishes between the two laws given to Adam, one common
and the other particular. This punctilious precision (*akribeia*) of Junius, sought from so far
away, does not please me at all. We read of no other law given to Adam than that which forbade
eating from the fruit of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. Nor would I have believed, had
I not found by experience so great a theologian ever contenting himself with such an argument, that
the particular law was not given to all because, of course, it is not probable that all, if they
had remained in integrity, would have come into Eden to that tree so that their obedience might be
tested. For if this was going to happen, the law would have been given to all outside Adam, not
merely in Adam. But we say that the same law which was given to Adam was also given to all, not
outside Adam, but in Adam. Otherwise, all would neither have sinned in Adam nor died in Adam, as
Arminius rightly says on this point. But either he feared to contend with Junius in any arena other
than one in which he well knew his own strength and readiness, or he was prepared to oblige Junius
in anything, provided that by an agreeable conference and, as it were, the demand of reciprocal
courtesy he might gain something to advance his own cause. Now at last we have come to Arminius.

3. We showed in the first place that the consequence which Arminius constructs here is defective.
For although Adam was created in supernatural grace, no account of it had to be taken by God as he
contemplated predestination. No motivating rationale could be supplied from it, but only a material
rationale. Yet for the material rationale of the thing considered only those things are required
which concur in constituting its nature.

Junius had already shown in Proposition 10 that the first argument is invalid. For it cannot be
denied that the image of God still remains in a corrupted human being wholly destitute of
supernatural grace.

But when Junius’s distinction is reached, Arminius takes it badly (as it seems) that the palm of
subtlety is going to be snatched from him; and so he desires to surpass Junius’s precision in
distinguishing. Arminius wishes to distinguish the particular law given to Adam. “That law about
the forbidden tree,” he says, “was partly of a particular institution and partly common”; and he
expounds the mysteries of his distinction. “For it is symbolic,” he says, “and therefore consists
of two parts: the symbol and the thing it signifies.” And who taught you, Arminius, that this law
was symbolic? And which of the prophets or apostles ever disclosed the mystery of this prohibition
anywhere? Perhaps you will say that, in a matter plain and readily credible, there was no need for
those divers, since the matter itself speaks. Let us therefore see Arminius’s splendid disclosure
of this mystery. “The symbol,” he says, “was abstinence from the fruit of the forbidden tree; the
thing signified was abstinence from disobedience and evil, and a test of obedience.” From here he
proceeds to apply the distinction: “Insofar as abstinence from disobedience and departure from evil
were commanded by that law, to that extent that law is common,” etc. But see, I ask you, the
various absurdities (*alogias*) which occur here. Abstinence from the fruit of the forbidden tree
signified abstinence from disobedience and evil; therefore, I say, the fruit of the forbidden tree
signified disobedience and evil, for if you subtract equal things from equals, the things remaining
will be equal. Yet the fruit of the forbidden tree was as suitable to signify good as evil. For
since it was only good in itself, it will be an equally suitable symbol of both obedience and
disobedience according to the manner in which the human being conducts himself concerning the
fruit, either by eating it or by restraining himself from it. But neither was the fruit of the tree
forbidden. It was permitted to look at it, permitted to meditate upon it, permitted to discuss it
with the serpent, and to profess that God’s command—which permitted the other things and prohibited
this one thing—was most equitable on this very account; only eating was not permitted. And what
ought to have been proposed first, you say, was a test of obedience. But what? Was the symbol what
you posit as abstinence from the fruit of the forbidden tree, or was it the thing signified?
Whichever way you turn, you will nowhere find a suitable response. Abstinence itself was obedience.
But is it suitable that obedience should signify abstinence from disobedience? Ridiculous! For
what else is abstaining from disobedience than obeying or exercising obedience? Thus Arminius
becomes delirious in many ways while he desires to appear subtle. But let us come to the
application: “Insofar as there is abstinence from disobedience, to that extent that law is common.”
Therefore, I say, that law is also common insofar as by it abstinence from eating the forbidden
fruit was commanded, for this very abstinence is abstinence from disobedience. Without doubt, that
eating, whenever done, would have been disobedience. But what member, I ask, is opposed to this?
“Insofar as it commands observance of the symbol,” of course. Excellent—as though observance of the
symbol were not itself obedience, and consequently abstinence from disobedience. But let the
distinction proceed. What do you think of this observance of the symbol? To allow Arminius to abound
in his subtleties, it cannot be explained quite suitably without another superadded distinction.
For observance of the symbol can itself be considered in two ways: either insofar as it is of a
symbol simply, or insofar as the law prescribes the observance of this symbol. With exemplary
precision—lest you think this subtle theologian does not put the canons (*kanonas*) of distinction
under strain—let us await the outcome. “Insofar as a law prescribes the observance of some symbol
simply to attest a human being’s obedience toward God, in that respect it is common.” But I ask:
when you say that this law prescribes the observance of some symbol, do you mean it definitely or
indefinitely? If you mean it indefinitely, it is manifestly false. For the law commands the
observance of a certain and definite symbol, not an indefinite one: “You shall not eat of the fruit
of the tree in the midst.” If definitely, then it must not be distinguished from the observance of
the definite symbol. For although God had willed to test the obedience of all human beings by some
symbol—something which you devise from your own brain and by no means draw from an oracle of
God—nevertheless, it by no means follows from this that this very thing was prescribed by that law.
Arminius does not even attempt to confirm it, though this alone had to be proved. And what does it
matter that God willed to test them all? For in that case observance would have been prescribed to
them outside Adam, not in Adam. But we say that the same law was prescribed to all in Adam. Yet he
proves what he invents without necessity, namely, that God willed to test the obedience of all
human beings in the same way if they had been born in integrity. But the rationale is invalid. For
what if the rationale had been the same? Was God thereby bound to proceed by the same path to test
their obedience? So too the rationale of the angels was the same as that of human beings; tell me,
please, by what symbol did he test their obedience? But it was not

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 60 | BSB canvas 72]

the same rationale; for if Adam had persevered, thus far we have by no means doubted that the grace
of confirmation would have been propagated to all his posterity. But he adds further that it would
have been most fitting for the test of obedience to be taken in an indifferent matter. Yet what was
that indifferent matter in which the test of angelic obedience was taken? How rare is anyone whose
obedience God tests in an indifferent matter? It is also false that a law enjoining an
indifferent matter is symbolic and ceremonial. The laws of princes deal for the most part with
indifferent matters; so too do the commands of masters imposed upon servants and the orders of
fathers given to sons. Are they therefore symbolic and ceremonial, especially according to the
symbolic meaning fabricated by Arminius, as though the fulfillment of these commands were not
obedience but symbols of obedience? But what do all these things, treated so laboriously, have to
do with the matter?

But let us come to the other member, namely, the observance of the definite symbol prescribed by
this law. What ought to be thought of it? Arminius has not yet emptied himself of his subtleties,
for this cannot be suitably settled without a new, superadded distinction. This observance, he
says, can in one respect be called common and in another perhaps particular. With the circumspection
that is his, it was fitting for Arminius to be cautious, as one dealing with so ambiguous and
slippery a matter—I almost said ludicrous. “It was common to Adam and Eve as the roots of the human
race, in whom the whole human race was contained.” And what, I ask, restrained you from professing
this at the outset, omitting those circumlocutions which serve no use? That is, that the law
prescribed to Adam was simultaneously prescribed to all in Adam, and for the reason mentioned by
you long ago; otherwise, how did all sin in Adam? He adds that it was also particular “insofar as it
was given to them as individual persons, whereas perhaps it would not have been given to others
already born and considered in themselves, not in their parents.” But although it had not been
given to them when already born, from its not being given there would have followed only that the
same law was not prescribed for them to perform outside Adam. This does not prevent the law
prescribed to Adam from having been common to all as something to be performed by them in Adam. But
even amid these wanderings he proceeds with caution: “‘Perhaps,’ I say, because your prudence knows
that there are those who think that, if the first human beings had persevered, all who were to be
born from them would have dwelt in paradise—and not without probable reason. For if that earthly
Paradise was a symbol of the heavenly kingdom, as seems from the fact that the third heaven, the
seat of the blessed, is called Paradise in the Scriptures, it surely seems likely that no human
being would have departed from that earthly Paradise unless through sin he had rendered himself
unworthy of the heavenly Paradise.” If all these things were so, how would that prevent the law
prescribed to Adam from having been common, to be performed, of course, by all in Adam? For in that
case the prescription of the law which you invent would have been made for Adam’s posterity to
perform, not in Adam but outside Adam. But let us weigh the invention. What if the human beings who
were to be born from Adam would have dwelt in Paradise? Would the test of obedience therefore have
been taken from them in the same way as from Adam? Is it not much more probable that Adam’s
obedience would have redounded to life for all his posterity, just as his disobedience redounded to
death for all? Nor was it so necessary that they would dwell in Paradise (for then either very few
would have been born, or Paradise would have been much more capacious than it is said to have been,
and that by all learned persons) as that the whole world would have been Paradise to them, according
to the saying, “One’s fatherland is wherever it is well.” And what fear would there have been in
departing from Paradise, or what harm or loss was to be feared from it, provided that primeval
integrity had remained inviolate in Adam and all his posterity? The land of Canaan was likewise a
type of heaven. Yet did Daniel and his companions, and the rest represented by the basket of good
figs in Jeremiah 24, leave it for no reason other than that through sin they had rendered
themselves unworthy of the heavenly Jerusalem? Or because the third heaven is called Paradise, is
Adam’s Paradise therefore a symbol of heaven? Then because the holy Prophet calls himself a worm,
one may infer from this that a worm was a symbol of that holy Prophet. Nevertheless, I neither
condemn nor reject the opinion which Arminius favors; rather, I think it worth the effort to expose
everywhere that Doctor’s drowsiness in argument. What follows is sound; he could more commendably
have rested in those points rather than detaining his reader with such forced distinctions which
serve no use.
