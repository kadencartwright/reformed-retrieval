The heroes did not yet think that enough had been sacrificed to glory for
them to seek their homeland by a direct route. Entering the mouth of the
Ister, they are carried against the current for several hundreds of miles and
soon appear in the Adriatic Sea—but Apollo must point out the route, for
Mercury’s wand will certainly not suffice to show this journey beforehand.
Those who pass themselves off as swimmers from Delos conjecture that the Argo
was lifted onto shoulders and made the journey on foot where the Danube comes
nearest to the upper sea: that is, at least two hundred miles of a most
difficult journey—let Apella the Jew believe it. Yet even then the knot is not
untied: they must be led from the Adriatic into the Tyrrhenian, indeed the
Libyan, Sea, and Tiphys’s skill could not have accomplished this. But what are
we doing? The Argo itself could easily have flown across the breadth of Italy,
since afterward it sprang into Olympus itself with little trouble.

However this may have been, as they passed Crete they were attacked with
stones by Talus of Minos, Talos, the bronze man who guarded the island. Medea
had little difficulty bringing about his end: by her spells she removed the
barrier from the single vein that he bore throughout his body; all his blood
flowed out, and Talus fell dead.

Shortly before they passed the Sicilian promontory, Orpheus’s lyre saved them
from certain destruction. The sweetness of the Sirens’ voices joined to their
pipes was such that it would easily have drawn even the most unwilling onto
the precipices of the rocks, had not Orpheus, recognizing the danger, taken up
his lyre and compelled the Sirens themselves, carried away in admiration of
him, to fall silent.

At last the Argonauts return to their beloved homeland and reach the shores
of Thessaly for which they had so greatly longed. But instead of festive
applause for their victory they receive the saddest news: Pelias had put the
whole family of the absent Jason to the sword so that he and his own might
reign more securely. It cannot be said with what surge of grief and what fury
of anger the Argonauts were seized at this report. But Medea tells them to
rest and restrain their threats: they were too few to take open vengeance on
Pelias at present. She advises them to hide quietly in the harbor and allow
her to attack the man by her arts, promising a revenge that would satisfy
every mind. Medea persuades them. She immediately changes herself into an old
woman, fashions a splendid image of Diana, sets it on a carriage, and drives
into the city. She cries that the goddess has arrived. The citizens,
especially Pelias’s household, welcome her, and all revere Medea as a divine
prophetess. She openly professes that through the divine power she had brought
she could accomplish everything, even what surpassed nature. Before all
others Pelias’s daughters believe her, for in their sight she had given this
demonstration of her art: she cut a ram into little pieces, put it into a
boiling cauldron, and employed ceremonies and drugs that finally transformed
the dissected ram into a lamb. After seeing this, the maidens no longer doubt
that the prophetess could restore youth to their aged father. Medea pretends
this is very easy for her art, if only they will obey her commands. The
maidens declare themselves ready to carry out everything. She therefore
orders the wretched old man to be stripped,

repeatedly washed with pure water, and anointed with various oils; finally he
is to be chopped into little pieces. The utterly wretched old man cries out
and calls on the faith of gods and men against the unspeakable crime of his
daughters. He cries to deaf ears. They proceed eagerly, in the sure hope of
seeing their father young again after a few hours; they kill him with their
own hands, cut his limbs apart, and cast them into the cauldron. Medea adds a
drug and tells them to be confident of the result: “Tend your boiling father,”
she says, “until I offer a sacrifice to the Moon on the roof of the palace.”
Medea had agreed on this signal with Jason: as soon as he saw a flame spring
up, he was to come at once with his men, for everything was ready. The
Argonauts therefore arrive more swiftly than the word, in the fields, the
city, and the palace, with drawn swords. Nothing could withstand their valor;
all is occupied, and with Pelias now thoroughly cooked no one has any further
fear. Thus we end the first fable. After this the fortunes of Jason, Medea,
and the Argo were indeed varied, but we have long been weary of foolish
stories.

The Theban expedition runs as follows. Laius, king of the Thebans, is warned
by an oracle that he would one day perish by his son’s hand. He therefore has
his pregnant wife carefully watched and orders the child to be handed over
for death as soon as it is born. The man entrusted with the task takes pity
on the little boy and does not kill him at once, but pierces his ankles with
a cord and hangs him by the feet from a woodland tree. A shepherd passing by
chance frees the hanging child, arranges for him to be reared, and from the
swelling in his wounded ankles gives him the name Oedipus.

When the boy had grown up, wholly possessing a royal nature, he set out for
Apollo’s shrine to inquire about his true parent. As he hurried along the
road, a horseman coming from behind drove his horse’s hoof into the heels of
the one before it. Provoked by the injury, Oedipus rose up and killed the
rider; as was afterward discovered, that man was Laius himself, Oedipus’s
father.

Meanwhile a Theban proclamation flew from mouth to mouth offering the kingdom
of the city and the late king’s royal widow as a reward to whoever solved the
Sphinx’s riddle. Oedipus came and explained that the animal which walked on
four feet in the morning, two at midday, and three at night was man. With the
question solved, the Sphinx hurled herself headlong, and the Thebans greeted
Oedipus as king and as Jocasta’s husband. By her he had two sons, Eteocles and
Polynices. But afterward he discovered that the woman he had married was his
mother and the man he had killed his father. In grief over the former
parricide and the incest that followed it, he tore out his eyes. He was only
with difficulty restrained from suicide, yielded the kingdom to his sons,
and they agreed that each would rule for a year. But when Eteocles’s first
year had ended, he refused to surrender the second turn of rule to his brother
Polynices.

Polynices therefore fled to Adrastus, who reigned at Argos. Arriving at the
palace late, he diverted to a rear cottage rather than enter at an unsuitable
hour. At that same moment Tydeus arrived to be restored by the same Adrastus’s
resources to his ancestral kingdom of Calydon. The cottage was not large
enough to lodge both, and they fought over the lodging. Adrastus came at the
sound of arms and noticed the sign of a lion on one combatant’s shield and a
bear on the other’s. He remembered an oracle which had once foretold that his
daughters must one day marry a lion and a bear. He immediately recognized his
sons-in-law, brought them into the palace, celebrated their betrothals, and
promised ample help to each, but gave Polynices the first place. He enrolled
as large an army as he could and called among his allies Amphiaraus, a
neighboring prince as well as a prophet. Foreknowing the coming destruction,
Amphiaraus refused the journey. Adrastus did not dismiss him so easily, but
worked diligently upon his wife Eriphyle and gave her a jeweled necklace.
Once she had accepted it, she pressed her husband day and night to enroll in
the Theban army. At last he yielded, followed Adrastus, but at departure
commanded his son to kill the mother who had valued the necklace’s price
above his life. His parricidal son carried out his father’s command, while in
vengeance for so great a crime Vesta armed her teeth: as Amphiaraus fought
bravely beneath the walls of Thebes, the earth opened, and the unhappy prophet
was swallowed up with his chariot and horses.

On the march the army met a woman carrying an infant in her arms. She was
Hypsipyle, whom the women of Lemnos had exiled for saving her father. She had
borne twins from her union with Jason, and after her exile was nursing the
son of Lycurgus, king of Nemea, whom she carried in her lap when the Argive
army encountered her. The leaders asked Hypsipyle whether she knew of a
spring or river nearby. “I will show you springs close at hand,” she said. To
do so more quickly, she set her charge on the ground, but upon returning found
him lifeless from a serpent’s bite.

After several bloody battles at Thebes, all unanimously entrusted the whole
matter to single combat between the brothers. Eteocles and Polynices therefore
rushed together with the utmost ferocity. Meanwhile their mother Jocasta,
hearing of the duel, hastened to separate the brothers, but arrived too late,
after they had transfixed one another with wounds. Though already full of
sorrows, maddened by this sight she seized a sword and joined herself as a
third companion to the dead.

Even this was not the catastrophe of the tragedy. Creon persuaded the Thebans
that a divine power would remain hostile to the city as long as any remnant of
the incestuous Oedipus remained in it. By public decree the wretched Oedipus
was therefore ordered into exile with his whole family. Here one might see
the blind old man, driven from his homeland, creeping toward Attica as his
daughter Antigone led him. Creon forbade

in addition that no one, unless willing to be buried alive himself, should
grant a tomb to any slain enemy. Nevertheless, after leaving her father at Athens, Antigone
placed her brothers on the pyre by night. The flames of the burning bodies
separated from one another, so that one might recognize antipathy even among
the dead. No sooner did day dawn than the unlucky brothers’ bodies were
missed. A search was made for the authors of the theft; Antigone was seized as
guilty and placed alive in a tomb, at which her lover Haemon killed himself,
unable to bear his grief.

Outside the walls Adrastus, already broken by disastrous battles, had long
asked Creon in vain for the burial of his men’s bodies. He came to Athens,
fell as a suppliant at Theseus’s knees, and was heard. Theseus enrolled an
army against Thebes, took the city by force, and inflicted a deserved death
on Creon.

A thousand poets tell of Troy with this effort. The whole crowd of gods came
when summoned to the wedding of Peleus and Thetis; Nemesis alone was
overlooked. Grieved that she alone had been despised, she came though
uninvited, bringing from the gardens of the Hesperides an apple radiant with
gold. On its skin she inscribed *“Let it be given to the fairest.”* With these
letters carved upon it, she threw it among the feasting gods. Juno immediately
seized it, Minerva claimed it for herself, and Venus declared it hers. The
other goddesses were silent; all desired Jupiter to pronounce judgment. But,
foreseeing the resentment that would arise, he declined the judgment and
advised them to accept Paris as judge.

This son of Priam was then a herdsman on Mount Ida. While Hecuba carried him,
she dreamed that she bore a burning torch. The diviners she consulted
pronounced that her coming offspring would one day give Ilium to the flames.
Priam therefore ordered the newborn killed, but his mother Hecuba secretly
gave him to the shepherds of Ida to rear. While still a youth he had such a
reputation for fairness that neighboring shepherds customarily abided by his
decision in their disputes. But behold the occasion for a greater commotion:
Juno, Minerva, and Venus came to him from Jupiter and wished Paris to decide
concerning their beauty. The goddesses were not ashamed openly to bribe their
judge: Juno promised the empire of Asia if he would favor her, Minerva the
highest wisdom, and Venus the enjoyment of beauty. The lustful youth preferred
the last gift and decided in favor of Venus. She departed triumphant to
heaven; the others raged.

Paris thereafter often visited nearby Troy, where his valor shone so greatly
in public contests that he easily revealed his royal lineage. Once he was
recognized, Hecuba received her son into the palace. Not remaining there long,
he toured Greece with a splendid company of companions, more for pleasure
than to recover Hesione. He came to Sparta and Menelaus received him as a
guest. Helen immediately fell desperately in love with Paris and Paris with
Helen—this was Venus’s promised gift. Menelaus departed for Crete on business;
meanwhile Helen prepared to follow Paris wherever he led, and the Trojans
hurried under full sail to Phrygia. Euripides says winds drove them to Egypt,
where the servants’ information revealed to the Egyptians Paris’s wrongdoing.
He was released unharmed, but Helen was detained to be restored to her husband
if ever he sought her. Tyndarus’s daughter therefore never saw Troy, and the
Greeks’ ten-year miseries arose over an ass’s shadow. Yet Homer’s contrary
account prevailed: that she reached Troy, married Deiphobus after Paris, and,
once the city had been captured, was taken home by Menelaus. However this may
have been, it is agreed that Menelaus no sooner learned his wife was gone than
he called all Greece to his aid.

Jupiter, the leader of adulterers, is said to have changed into a swan and
impregnated Leda, who was already pregnant by Tyndarus. In labor she produced
two eggs instead of children: from one were born Pollux and Helen to Jupiter;
from the other, Castor and Clytemnestra to Tyndarus. Helen, a marvel of beauty,
was carried off by Theseus while still very young, but was recovered by her
brothers and returned to her father’s home. There a thousand suitors sought
her; all the distinguished Greek princes courted her. Tyndarus told none of
the princes to despair but required one thing of all: they must form a league
among themselves and take a solemn oath that with their common arms they
would attack anyone who tried to violate the maiden. This done, the father
gave his daughter free choice of the husband who most pleased her. That man
was Menelaus, who alone gained Helen, but the other princes remained bound to
contribute their resources if violence should assail her. Thus when Paris
violated the agreement, the leading men of Greece came to Menelaus in
accordance with the pact: Agamemnon, Ajax, Diomedes, Nestor, Protesilaus,
Palamedes, and a vast number of other princes. All judged that what Paris had
done to Menelaus, the barbarians had done to the Greek name, and that the
common injury must be avenged by common arms.

They allowed no one to withdraw from this defense of their common homeland.
Ulysses, desiring to enjoy his new wife Penelope, tried to escape military
service under the pretense of insanity. Those appointed by the common council
to summon him to arms found him plowing the shore and sowing salt in the
furrows. Palamedes, suspecting the truth, placed Telemachus in the plow’s path.
Ulysses carefully kept the plowshare from harming his son, a sufficiently
clear sign of a sound mind. There was no further room for pretense; willing in
deed though unwilling in spirit, he was led and dragged to war. The fleet was
assembled at Aulis, and

Agamemnon was chosen as commander. The prophets warned them not to sail before
Achilles arrived, for fate decreed that Troy could not be taken without the
son of Peleus. His mother Thetis, foreknowing that he would not return from
the Trojan war, had hidden her son in female dress among Lycomedes’s daughters.
Ulysses detected him by this device. Disguised as a merchant, he brought into
the women’s quarters a basket of feminine trinkets, with several finely made
little swords mixed among them. The maidens gathered to buy; he noticed one
with an entirely masculine face who despised the women’s wares and handled
only the little swords. Certain that it was Achilles, he called him, seized
his hand, and used such force of eloquence that the hero immediately cast off
his veil and ran to arms.

The fleet was detained a long time at Aulis by contrary winds. Agamemnon had
shot Diana’s doe while hunting. The goddess grew angry and refused to be
appeased unless Agamemnon’s daughter Iphigenia was sacrificed to her. The
father understandably proved difficult, but at last, overcome by entreaties,
he sent Ulysses to Argos to bring the maiden as though she were to marry the
son of Peleus. She came very willingly with her mother, but instead of a
marriage bed the young woman was dragged to the altar to be slaughtered like
a heifer. As Calchas raised the sword to pierce the breast of the garlanded
victim, Diana substituted a doe at that very instant. She carried Iphigenia
invisibly through the air as far as the Taurians, where she kept her as a
priestess who sacrificed strangers.

Meanwhile the winds favored the Greeks, but Calchas still did not permit them
to sail. He taught that Hercules’s arrows were absent and that Troy could not
be attacked without them. What were they to do in their distress? Philoctetes
alone knew Hercules’s tomb and the quiver; the dying Hercules had bound him by
oath not to reveal it and rewarded his silence with the fateful arrows.
Ulysses nevertheless approached the man. Unable by any art to force an answer
from him, he finally prevailed upon him to indicate the tomb’s place by
striking it with his foot, and even to accompany them with the arrows to Troy.
But it happened that one of the arrows fell upon the foot by which Philoctetes
had sworn falsely; its slightest touch inflicted so foul a wound that the
others, unable to bear the stench, left the man behind on Lemnos.

At last they reached the shore of Sigeum, with a thousand ships and a hundred
thousand men. Hector met them with his forces as they disembarked and made a
wide slaughter of Greeks around him. In accordance with fate he killed
Protesilaus, the first to leap ashore. The first greeting appeared so dreadful
to the Greeks that the others were not eager for the encounter, especially
since they knew from fate that Troy could not be taken before the tenth year.
They therefore thought it enough to fortify the camp, surround everything
widely with fires, and provide provisions.

Ulysses was sent to Thrace for grain but brought nothing back to camp, and
Palamedes condemned his negligence. Palamedes himself returned to the
Thracians and brought back a supply of food. Ulysses therefore burned with
implacable envy and plotted Palamedes’s death. He forged a letter from Priam
to the man, accused him of treason, and proved the charge from gold which he
had arranged to be buried in Palamedes’s tent. Every defense, though obvious
to the eyes, was fruitless; Palamedes, a chief pillar of the commonwealth, was
buried under stones.

After this Achilles, angered for the reason you know, abstained from battle
until, moved by Patroclus’s death, he obtained new armor from Vulcan through
his mother. Clad in it, he went out raging, laid waste everything far and
wide, and killed Hector himself, though not, it seems, in the magnificent
manner Homer proclaims. Not many days passed before Achilles felt himself
burning with love for Priam’s daughter Polyxena. He sent men to Priam to ask
for his daughter in marriage. The old man pretended to welcome the alliance,
and they agreed upon wedding rites in Apollo’s temple beneath the walls of
Troy. There Paris, lying in ambush, shot Achilles through the ankles, and the
hero fell dead. When he was newborn Thetis had dipped his whole body in the
Styx except for the ankles by which she held him while immersing him;
therefore Achilles could be penetrated by no force in any part except the
ankle. The Trojans did not return his body until the gold paid for Hector was
restored.

When Troy’s fated year arrived, Ulysses laid out a brilliant route to the end
of the war. Crossing the walls by night with Diomedes, he carried into camp
the Palladium from Minerva’s temple and Laomedon’s ashes from the Scaean gate.
He ambushed and killed Rhesus as he came from Thrace with a numerous army, and
took away his horses lest they taste the waters of Xanthus. On the strength
of these merits he claimed and obtained Achilles’s armor. Ajax, passed over,
went mad and cut his own thread of life.

Nothing remained for taking the city except the presence of Pyrrhus from
Aeacus’s seed and Philoctetes with Hercules’s quiver. Ulysses produced both,
and, to crown the undertaking, induced and persuaded Aeneas and Antenor,
leaders of the Trojans, to betray their homeland: hence came the deceits of
Sinon and the horse.

The shade of Achilles did not allow the Greeks to return from Troy before the
right hand of Pyrrhus, which had sent father Priam to the underworld, sent his
daughter Polyxena there also. As they prepared to sail, a vast storm from
Pallas fell upon them. Ajax son of Oileus was struck by lightning because he
had violated Cassandra in her sanctuary. Diomedes found Venus angry because
of her wound and heard that his wife had been polluted by
such filth that he was ashamed to

return to his homeland and chose instead to die an exile in Apulia. Agamemnon
had scarcely set foot at home when he was cruelly killed by his wife
Clytemnestra and her lover Aegisthus. Pyrrhus, after becoming betrothed to
Menelaus’s daughter Hermione, was killed by Orestes at Apollo’s altar at
Delphi. After another Iliad of troubles and a second decade of misery, Ulysses
had scarcely begun to breathe again from his labors at home when he was shot
through with a poisoned arrow by the unlucky hand of Telegonus, his bastard
son by Circe.

Such are the things authors tell about the Greeks in the age of the first
monarchy. An infinity of other stories of the same kind remains, but we have
lingered too long over Greek fables; let us pass into Italy to observe the
beginnings of Rome.

The renown of the Trojan people, whether because of their own valor or the
poets’ favor, was once so great that most later peoples eagerly traced their
lineage back to them. Yet this nobility does not belong to most of those who
claim it without the splendid mark of falsehood. Only two peoples in Europe
demonstrate with certainty that their stock is Trojan: the Venetians,
offspring of Antenor, and the Romans, blood of Aeneas. Though these peoples
were and still are very famous, they did not raise the head of their fame
from the obscure darkness of common nations until the fourth monarchy was
growing old; yet even before the second monarchy was born, they rightfully
claim many pages in history.

Maro composed the *Aeneid* in an entirely poetic fashion, that is, with the
utmost freedom to invent whatever could honor the Julian house. Certainly, if
we take Livy’s history as true and Virgil’s stories as fabricated, what
follows is nevertheless plausible.

After Ilium had collapsed into ashes, Aeneas was driven by various fates on
land and sea and finally came to Latium accompanied by about fifteen ships.
His companions went ashore and pitched tents on the coast; want and hunger
compelled them to plunder the neighborhood. The inhabitants called Aborigines
immediately came with King Latinus to avenge the foreigners’ injury by the
sword, and formed a battle line against the Trojans. But before the signal for
battle sounded, Latinus accepted Aeneas’s request for a conference and they
agreed to peace. A league of friendship was formed and Lavinia was promised
to Aeneas in marriage. She had formerly been pledged to Turnus, a leading
prince among the neighboring peoples. He would not bear the insult of a broken
pledge and prepared to crush Latinus together with Aeneas’s people. Latinus
fell in the battle, but fortune gave Aeneas victory over his enemies and a
kingdom over his allies. Defeated, Turnus did not cast aside arms, but enlisted
Mezentius and the Etruscans as allies and again cast the die of battle. The
Latins—under this name the voices of the Trojans and Aborigines were now
absorbed—left the field victorious, but Aeneas died and was transformed into
Jupiter Indiges.

Ascanius, the son born either of Creusa or Lavinia, left Lavinium, which
Aeneas had founded for his mother, and laid the foundations of Alba as the
royal seat for himself and his descendants. Fifteen princes from the line of
Julus reigned there, with almost nothing worthy of notice, until Numitor.
Numitor had a brother Amulius who, if any man ever was, was goaded by ambition.
It was not enough for him to drive his brother from the throne; he also sent
to the underworld Numitor’s sole male offspring and shut his daughter Rhea in
the Vestals’ prison. The ordained penalty for Vestals who violated perpetual
virginity was what one might call a living death. Rhea immediately became
liable to it when she soon appeared pregnant; to lessen her crime she named
Mars as her violator. Yet the god could not exempt his mistress from
punishment. Without delay Amulius ordered the corrupted maiden to be buried
alive, and commanded that Romulus and Remus, the twins she had borne, be cast
into the currents of the Tiber. By the providence of the divine power, the
river was then flooding widely beyond its usual banks. The man charged with
drowning the children did not dare approach the deep channel and thought it
enough to abandon them on the nearest flooded ground. As the infants wailed
amid the waters, a predatory she-wolf approached and, as though ashamed of
human cruelty, lowered her teats to their mouths to suck without doing them
harm. The royal shepherd Faustulus came upon the sight and, taught humanity by
a wild beast, gathered the boys to his breast, led them home, and gave them to
his wife to rear.

The youths quickly revealed a royal nature and became leaders of all the
neighboring shepherds, punishing robbers everywhere around. At last Remus was
captured in a conflict with bandits and delivered to his grandfather Numitor
for capital punishment. Meanwhile Faustulus revealed to Romulus the secret
he had long kept, teaching him his own and his brother’s lineage, and arranged
for the grandsons’ fortune to be revealed to Numitor. More quickly than the
word, plots were laid against Amulius from every side. Romulus arrived from
the fields with a band of shepherds, while Remus hastened up with another
company gathered through Numitor. They burst into the palace, killed the
tyrant, and restored Numitor to his rightful throne.

The brothers, contemplating greater things, began constructing a new city and
chose as its site the Palatine hill, where they had spent their youth among
the flocks and neighboring shepherds. While they were engaged in this, a
question arose: which of the two should give his name to the city and

rule it once founded? Neither had any advantage over the other in valor or
age; equal in everything, they submitted the matter to augury. As they watched
for omens, six eagles first appeared to Remus. While he loudly proclaimed his
omen of power, twelve flew to Romulus, who therefore maintained that his
greater number outweighed Remus’s priority in time. Remus denied it; from
words they moved to blows, and Remus fell in the tumult. By this event the
rule of the new city remained to Romulus alone. He named it Rome after himself
and laid its first foundations in world-year 3,246, on the twenty-first of
Romulean April but the fourth of Julian October, at half past one in the
afternoon, according to some.

To make Rome flourish with many inhabitants, Romulus employed this device: he
opened an asylum in the middle of the city, where anyone who fled would be
sacrosanct and safe from every punishment, no matter how many crimes covered
him. At once a huge flood of condemned men, scoundrels, and slaves arrived;
Romulus gave citizenship to all, and Rome suddenly filled with such citizens.
The Fathers foresaw that a population composed almost entirely of men, amid a
great scarcity of women, would last no longer than one generation. They
therefore sent to their neighbors seeking marriages for the Romans. But all
indignantly rejected alliance and would have no dealings with an impure city
into which the dregs of nearly every outcast had flowed. For some time the
Romans anxiously wondered how to provide descendants amid so much contempt
from their neighbors, until this device occurred to them. They celebrated
festive shows for Neptune with the greatest possible solemnity and invited
all the surrounding cities to watch. Many whole families came, especially
from the Sabines. In the heat of the spectacle a signal was given to the
Roman youth to seize the maidens, and each carried off to his home the woman
he could most conveniently take as his bride.

This novel insult stirred many to hostile arms. Roman courage easily checked
the others, but the struggle with the Sabines was dangerous. They approached
Rome’s walls with a great army and found Tarpeia, the daughter of the
Capitol’s commander, drawing sacred water from a spring for the gods’ altars.
The Sabine king Tatius conspired with her to betray the citadel, promising the
traitress the golden bracelets they wore on their left arms. She accepted and
delivered the Capitol to them. They indeed kept their pact, but lest the
inventor of so foul a betrayal go unpunished, they threw upon her not only
their bracelets but the shields they also carried in their left hands, until,
buried beneath the weight of shields, she paid the well-deserved penalty for
her treachery.

They rushed from the citadel into the city, and an uncertain battle filled
the streets. Much blood flowed on both sides, with the day’s fortune inclining
to neither, until the abducted Sabine women intervened, hair unbound and
clothes torn like Bacchants. Fearlessly rushing among the swords, they cried
out to husbands and fathers together, begging them to turn their weapons upon
the women themselves. They would gladly accept death and wounds, if only
husbands would spare their fathers and fathers their husbands. Their prayers
were so effective that all first restrained their hands, then laid down arms
and formed a treaty: the Sabines would migrate to Rome and live with equal
rights alongside the original citizens, and Tatius would share the kingdom
with Romulus.

A partnership in kingship is never reliable. It was not long before the
Latins complained to Tatius that his household had unlawfully beaten their
envoys contrary to the law of nations. Tatius ignored the complaints, and so,
while attending sacred rites at Lavinium, he was killed by the Latins.

Romulus cared so little about this crime that he was touched by some suspicion
of a guilty conscience. After Tatius’s death Romulus again ruled alone, and
not only ruled but in many things exercised authority with the severity of a
tyrant. It thus happened that the leading Fathers, fearing for themselves,
tore him apart. Lest the hateful murder become public, a story was spread that
he had been surrounded by a dark thundercloud while addressing the Fathers
and never seen again because Jupiter had carried the hero to heaven.

Romulus had no offspring to succeed to the kingdom. Each of the Fathers
claimed it and none would yield to another, until both senate and people
agreed upon a foreigner: Numa Pompilius of Cures among the Sabines, renowned
for wisdom and virtue. As soon as Numa was invested with the royal insignia,
he followed a path opposite to Romulus’s. He devoted himself wholly to the
arts of peace, and not without success, for throughout the full forty years of
his rule the doors of Janus remained closed. This goal was easy for him: the
ferocity of Romulus had filled the neighbors with so many disasters and such
terror that there remained little suspicion of war. The greater danger was
among the citizens, whose minds continuous wars under Romulus had made utterly
savage. Numa saw that religion alone would suffice to soften them. He
therefore devoted himself to worshiping the gods and immersed himself and his
subjects in superstitions. He openly went each day to a grove, where he
pretended that the nymph Egeria met him and taught him individually which
rites each god loved in his sacrifices. As though taken down from this nymph’s

[Printed p. 73 | IA leaf 103 | access PDF 103 | OCR object 102]

mouth, Pompilius described almost everything that the Romans used in their
sacred rites down to Constantine’s day.

We read no more of Numa’s descendants than of Romulus’s. By its own authority
the people chose Tullus Hostilius as third king. He revived the valor of
Romulus, which Numa’s religions had nearly extinguished and suffocated; Tullus
thought of nothing but Mars.

He led his first expedition against the Albans. As both battle lines stood
ready, waiting only for the trumpet blast, Mettius Fufetius, dictator of the
Albans, stepped into the middle and requested a conference with Tullus. He
urged them to spare their citizens’ blood and entrust both peoples’ fortune
to the right hands of a few: in the Roman camp were the three Horatii brothers,
and among the Albans the three Curiatii; let them descend into the arena and
fight for their countries. If the Curiatii fell, Alba would serve Rome; if the
Horatii yielded, Rome would serve Alba. All approved the plan. The unarmed
battle lines sat on either side, and the brothers advanced to fight for the
perpetual fate of their fellow citizens. Missiles flew, swords clashed, and
two Horatii fell dead; the Romans’ courage sank to their feet. But behold the
turning wheel of playful fortune: the third surviving Horatius, feeling
himself unwounded, did not despair of defeating all three Curiatii, since he
saw wounds on them all. To avoid fighting all at once, he separated them by
pretending to flee. They pursued, but one ran faster than another. When he saw
that the desired interval separated the three, he suddenly turned, rushed upon
the nearest, panting from his run and his wound, and instantly killed him.
Then he flew at the second and easily put him to death; the third, already
spiritless from the doubled slaughter of his brothers, was little trouble.
The Romans raised their shouts to the stars; the dumbfounded Albans fell
silent and sadly submitted their necks to the yoke.

But hear the pitiable catastrophe of this tragedy. Horatius returned to Rome
in triumph among the legion’s foremost standard-bearers. His rejoicing sister
met him outside the gates and embraced the victor, but among the spoils she
noticed a garment she had embroidered with her own needle for one of the
Curiatii, to whom she had been betrothed. She suddenly filled everything with
cries and mourning. Horatius grew furious at his sister’s most untimely grief
and did not cease his anger until he thrust a sword into the wretched woman’s
breast. Manifestly guilty, he was brought to trial. The laws demanded the
sister-killer’s death, but the people denied that the country’s deliverer
should die. At last the two capital judges pronounced sentence of
condemnation, but he appealed to the people—in whom supreme authority then
lay—and was acquitted.

Meanwhile the Albans, indignant at having submitted to the Romans without
bloodshed, turned every stone to free their necks from the yoke. They secretly
approached the Veientines and promised to betray the Romans at the very moment
of battle. In this hope the Veientines advanced against the city; the Romans
met them, joined also by the Albans whom they had called to help. The signals
sounded. The Veientines barely withstood Roman strength when someone whispered
in the king’s ear that the Albans were deserting to the enemy. Terrified by
the report, the king nevertheless concealed his fear and shouted loudly to
his men, so that the enemy also heard, that the Albans were doing nothing but
what he had commanded: he had ordered them to encircle and attack the enemy’s
rear. The Veientines believed it and, perceiving victorious Romans in front
and Albans about to attack behind, fled and were completely routed. The Albans
returned to the Roman camp and pretended they had no plan of desertion. The
Romans did not accuse them until the unarmed men had been surrounded by armed
legions. Hostilius then ordered Dictator Mettius seized and tied between
opposing chariots to be torn apart; he commanded the Alban people to move to
Rome and Alba, apart from the temples of the gods, to be reduced to ashes and
smoke.

After Tullus the people ordered Ancus Martius, a grandson of Numa through his
daughter, to reign. About this time Demaratus, weary of civil disorders, moved
from Corinth to Tarquinii, a town in Etruria. His wealthy young son Lucumo
took as wife Tanaquil, a woman of the highest nobility and no less spirit.
Seeing that she was not held in sufficient dignity in her country, she
persuaded her husband to move to Rome, where virtue and wealth opened the
highest honors. As they entered Rome’s gates together in a carriage, an eagle
flew over them, took Lucumo’s cap from his head, carried it briefly in its
beak, and gently replaced it. Tanaquil immediately embraced her husband and,
from her Etruscan skill in augury, announced that Roman kingship was promised
to Lucumo. With this hope he rented a house and called himself Lucius
Tarquinius. His generosity to the people and services to the king were such
that, when dying, Ancus named no other guardian by testament for his still
minor children. Tarquinius did not wish to remain at this rank. He went to the
assembly, set before their eyes Ancus’s sons’ unfitness for kingship because
of their age, and explained his own virtues and resources. He did not stop
speaking until every vote named him the fifth king of Rome.

In the Sabine war he desired to enlarge the old centuries of cavalry. An
augur denied that this could be done with favorable auspices. The king,
angered, had one desire:

[Printed p. 74 | IA leaf 104 | access PDF 104 | OCR object 103]

to brand the vanity of divination. “Tell me from your art, my augur,” he said,
“whether what I am thinking can be done.” After taking the auspices, the augur
answered that it could. “But,” said the king, “I was thinking of cutting a
whetstone with a razor.” The eager augur attempted it and cut the stone with
the razor—to the enormous amazement of the spectators; do not doubt that a
demon supplied the power.

In Tarquinius’s household lived a neglected boy, born at least to a captive
slave mother, whose name was Servius Tullius. While he slept, a heavenly fire
shone around his head. The servants seized water to extinguish the flame, but
Tanaquil hurried up and forbade them. The fire burned harmlessly in the boy’s
hair until he awoke of his own accord. Tanaquil, highly skilled in auguries,
taught her husband that this boy’s fortune would one day be very great; so it
happened. Such virtue shone in the youth that Tarquinius chose him as his
son-in-law in hope of the future kingdom.

The sons of the former king Ancus were indignant at being neglected and
plotted the death of Tarquinius and Servius together. They sent two very
powerful shepherds into the palace for the crime. By agreement the men began
quarreling at the palace doors and came to blows. A disturbance arose, and
both summoned the king as judge. One began to state the case; the other, while
the king was wholly intent on the speaker, sank an axe deep into his head. A
cry arose that the king had perished. Tanaquil immediately arrived, ordered
the palace closed, and carried Tarquinius to a bedroom as though still
breathing. She told the people gathering at the palace to hope for the king’s
life and meanwhile obey Servius Tullius. He promptly came from the palace clad
in the royal robe and punished the assassins. Ancus’s sons went into exile
through fear, and Tarquinius’s death was not certainly known until Servius
sat firmly on the throne.

Tullius wished to ensure that Tarquinius’s descendants could never inflict
upon him the same destruction Ancus’s descendants had caused Tarquinius; but
the unhappy man drew upon himself by this means the fate he thought to avert.
His two daughters he gave in marriage to Lucius and Arruns Tarquinius. Arruns,
a man of mild character, lived content with private fortune, while Lucius
longed entirely for his father’s scepter. The Tullia given to Arruns was of a
most proud and bold nature, while the one given to Lucius was, if any woman
ever was, very modest. Similarity of character soon joined Arruns’s Tullia and
Lucius. To make room for new marriages, Tullia secretly removed both her
sister and her husband. Now a widow, she moved into the house of the widowed
Lucius and with constant spurs drove her husband, already fierce enough by
nature, against her father. At last Lucius drove him from the throne, cast him
out of life, and ordered his body to rot unburied in the streets. As Tullia
rode home in her carriage, everyone pointed out her father cast down in the
mud. This woman, truly the fourth and most criminal of the Furies, ordered the
carriage driven across her father’s body.

Lucius Tarquinius, Rome’s seventh and last king, filled everything with
proscriptions and murder under the name Superbus. He was the first to add an
armed guard for his protection. While he sat deep in thought in his palace, a
huge serpent fell from the wall. The others scattered in fear; the omen alone
terrified the king. Anxious to learn what it foretold for himself and his
family, he sent his two elder sons to Delphi. Junius, Tarquinius’s nephew by a
sister, went with them more as entertainment and a joke on the road than as a
companion. He was surnamed Brutus because, from fear of the tyrant, he
pretended a bestial madness, concealing a very sharp mind beneath the cloak of
insanity. As a gift to Apollo he gave a horn staff that was hollow and filled
with gold. Among other things the youths asked the god which of them would
rule after their father. Apollo answered: the one who first kissed his mother.
The young men hurried home, but Brutus, as though falling by accident,
secretly kissed the earth, the common mother of all.

After this Tarquinius moved arms against the Etruscans and besieged Ardea. In
the royal camp the young men, merry after drinking, fell to talking about
their wives. Each praised his own most highly, but Collatinus spoke more
magnificently than the others of Lucretia. It was decided that very night to
ride to Rome and examine with their own eyes what they praised in their wives.
They visited each house and also came to Collatia, several miles outside
Rome. There late at night Lucretia was found working wool with her maids. All
awarded her first place among women’s praises. Glad of his victory, Collatinus
cheerfully kept his companions at home that night, but at great cost to
himself, for one guest, Sextus Tarquinius, the third of the king’s sons, was
inflamed with lust for Lucretia. All returned to camp the next day, but not
long afterward Tarquinius, secretly and with only one companion, came to
Collatinus’s house. Lucretia, still suspecting no crime, received him
courteously. But behold, in the dead of night when the whole household slept,
Tarquinius stood over the sleeping Lucretia with drawn sword. He threatened
immediate death if she refused and threatened also to cast the body of a
slain slave upon her after she died, so that she would appear to have been
killed in ignoble adultery. What could she do? Powerful lust conquered
determined chastity in a weak body. Violated, Lucretia sent word to her father
at Rome and her husband in the camp, commanding them to come without delay.
They arrived immediately, together with Junius Brutus. Lucretia told the deed
as it

[Printed p. 75 | IA leaf 105 | access PDF 105 | OCR object 104]

had happened and with many tears implored them to take vengeance. What more
would you have? Before everyone’s eyes she plunged into her heart the knife
she had secretly hidden in her clothing for this purpose. Husband and father
turned to lamentation, but Junius, judging this a fitting time to reveal his
character, drew the bloody knife from the wound and said: “By this chaste
blood I swear to strive with all my strength to avenge this injury and bring
destruction upon the whole royal line.” His deeds were no less than his words.
Lucretia’s body was brought into the forum at Collatia. The citizens, seized
with indignation at the deed, took up arms and flew to Rome under Junius
Brutus’s leadership. The people were summoned to an assembly, where Brutus
spoke so effectively against the crimes of the Tarquins, old and new, that by
an irrevocable decree not only was the whole Tarquinian clan condemned to
perpetual exile, but royal authority itself was forever proscribed from Rome.
Brutus and Collatinus were created consuls for the following year. Thus the
kingdom, after standing for 245 years, passed into democracy. When news of
these events reached the king in camp, he immediately hastened to Rome, but
the gates were shut in his face. Brutus with equal speed but opposite success
ran to the camp, where the soldiers received him with open arms and promised
all possible aid against the Tarquins. Meanwhile father Tarquin and his elder
sons fled to Rome’s enemies; Sextus the adulterer went to the Sabines, where
his old enemies soon killed him.

Superbus’s reign properly belongs to the second monarchy, but it seemed
inconvenient to divide the history of the kings.

## CHAPTER VIII

### Questions and difficulties concerning the preceding epoch

### QUESTION I

#### On the total sum of the epoch

First it is asked how many years the first temple stood, or how many years
this fifth epoch now before us contains. Answer: according to some
chronologists, 424; according to others, 430; but according to Scaliger,
Calvisius, and Henry Philippi, whom we chiefly follow, 427.

### QUESTION II

#### How the sum of the years in this epoch is proved

Second it is asked how this calculation is clearly demonstrated from sacred
Scripture. Answer: either by a more summary count or by adding the numbers in
detail through the lives of the individual kings of Judah and Israel whose
years Scripture expressly records. We establish the former as follows.
Ezekiel 4:5: *“I have given you the years of their iniquity by the number of
days, three hundred and ninety days; a day, I say, for a year I have given
you.”* This was a type of the years in which the Lord’s patience endured the
idolatry of the Israelite people. They begin in Rehoboam’s first year, when
Jeroboam set up those calves at Dan and Bethel, and end at the temple’s
destruction. To these 390 years add the thirty-seven years in which Solomon
reigned after the temple was founded, and you will have the required number,
427 years.

We establish the latter calculation as follows. Solomon reigned forty years
and founded the temple in the course of his fourth regnal year. We assign the
temple thirty-seven years from Solomon’s reign, and all the rest to Judah’s
later kings: Rehoboam is assigned seventeen, 1 Kings 14:21; Abijah three,
15:2; Asa forty-one, verse 9; Jehoshaphat twenty-five, 22:42; Jehoram eight,
2 Kings 8:17; Ahaziah one, verse 26; Athaliah six, 11:4; Joash forty, 12:1;
Amaziah twenty-nine, 14:1; Uzziah forty-two, 15:2; Jotham sixteen, verse 13;
Ahaz sixteen, 16:2; Hezekiah twenty-nine, 18:2; Manasseh fifty-five, 21:1;
Amon two, verse 19; Josiah thirty-one, 22:1; Jehoahaz three months, 23:31;
Jehoiakim eleven years, verse 36; Jeconiah three months, 2 Chronicles 36:9;
and Zedekiah eleven years, 2 Kings 24:18. If you add these numbers, you will
find a total of 430 years and six months. As to the six months, they are
dropped in rounding the number; the three years beyond the stated sum are
also easily leveled by joining several incomplete years into fewer complete
ones, as we will presently see.

### QUESTION III

#### How Solomon was both a boy and a father

Third you may ask how Rehoboam began to reign at the age of forty-one, 1 Kings
14:21. Since Solomon is called a little child at the beginning of his reign,
1 Kings 3:7, Rehoboam must then have been two years old; but how could Solomon,
a little child, have become the father of a two-year-old so early? Answer: do
not suppose with the rabbis that Solomon fathered him in his tenth or twelfth
year, for

[Printed p. 76 | IA leaf 106 | access PDF 106 | OCR object 105]

Solomon appears to have been at least twenty before his father’s death. What
is said about his childhood at the beginning of his reign must be understood
not of age but of experience in ruling and ignorance of going out and coming
in, as the context itself explains.

### QUESTION IV

#### How Baasha began to reign in both Asa’s third and fifth year

Fourth you may ask how verse 33 of 1 Kings 15 is reconciled with verse 9 of
the same chapter. In the former Baasha is said to have begun to reign in Asa’s
third year, and in the latter Asa is said to have begun to reign in Jeroboam’s
twentieth. Jeroboam, however, reigned twenty-two years and his son Nadab two;
after them Baasha began to reign in what was, counting from Jeroboam’s
twentieth, the fifth year, and hence not Asa’s third but his fifth. Answer: as
days in Christ’s resurrection are taken synecdochically—parts for wholes and
incomplete for complete ones, for the Lord rested in the tomb only one
complete Sabbath day, having been buried on the evening before the Sabbath
and rising very early on the first day of the week after the Sabbath—so here
Jeroboam’s last year, taken incompletely for several months of his
twenty-second, coincides with Nadab’s first. Nadab’s second, likewise taken for
several months of that year, coincides with Baasha’s first. By this calculation
two are subtracted from the first twenty-five years of the kings of Israel,
so that Baasha’s first chronological, full, and complete year corresponds to
Asa’s third, though it may be called Asa’s fifth in the common calculation of
incomplete years.

### QUESTION V

#### Whether Asa’s mother was Absalom’s daughter

Fifth you may ask how Maacah, Absalom’s daughter, could have been Asa’s
mother, when more than sixty years appear to have elapsed between Absalom’s
death and Asa’s birth. Answer: this Maacah was not the daughter of David’s son
Absalom, but of Abishalom of Gibeah. Yet the analogy of the Hebrew language
allows Absalom and Abishalom as the same name, for *yod* is frequently added
to proper names merely for euphony. Even if it stood as the suffix of the
first person, it would add to the meaning nothing but the simple inherence of
the attribute in the subject, namely possession of peace from one’s father,
or application of peace to one’s father. Indeed, in 2 Chronicles 11:20,
Abishalom, Maacah’s father, is expressly called Absalom, but that Absalom was
not David’s son. Besides the fact that the length of time scarcely allows a
man who died long before Solomon’s birth to have been the grandfather of
Solomon’s grandson Asa, that Absalom had only one daughter, Tamar. According
to 2 Samuel 14:28, while her father lived and before Solomon was born she was
a grown woman in the bloom of age and beauty, and by the ordinary law of
nature could not have remained capable of childbearing until Asa’s birth.
Moreover, just as Absalom’s three sons, so also his daughter appear to have
died before their father without posterity, for in the absence of surviving
children Absalom erected a stone monument on the royal way to perpetuate his
memory, 2 Samuel 18:18. Add that this Maacah of Abishalom is called Micaiah,
daughter of Uriel of Gibeah, in 2 Chronicles 13:2, with a double pair of
names: she is called Micaiah and her father Uriel; but Uriel was from Gibeah,
a city of Benjamin, which has nothing to do with David or his son Absalom,
whose city was Bethlehem and tribe Judah.

If you continue to inquire how Maacah could be Asa’s mother when 2 Chronicles
13:22 expressly calls her the mother of Abijah, Asa’s father, answer that
ancestors in the direct line are often called fathers and mothers. Thus in 2
Chronicles 22:2 Athaliah is called Omri’s daughter, though she was Ahab’s
daughter and only Omri’s granddaughter; and at 1 Kings 15, cited above, where
Maacah is called Abijah’s mother, David is called his father, though David did
not beget him immediately but through his son Solomon and grandson Rehoboam.
Maacah was Rehoboam’s most beloved wife, and for her sake he placed Abijah
over the kingdom though he was among the younger sons. It is therefore less
surprising that she held the reins of government during her grandson Asa’s
minority, until her adult grandson expelled her for idolatry.

### QUESTION VI

#### Whether Baasha invaded Asa ten years after his own death

Sixth you may ask how 2 Chronicles 16:1 is reconciled with 1 Kings 16:8. The
former says that Baasha invaded Judah in Asa’s thirty-sixth regnal year; the
latter says that Baasha died and his son Elah began to reign in King Asa’s
twenty-sixth. It is plain that Baasha began to reign in Asa’s third year and
did not continue more than twenty-four years, and hence ceased both to live
and reign in Asa’s twenty-sixth. How then could he invade Judah ten years
after his death, in Asa’s thirty-sixth year, as is expressly stated both in
the place just cited and in the final verse of 2 Chronicles 15? Answer:
Cajetan’s response is absurd and Paludanus’s desperate: the former declares
all the texts of Chronicles, Latin, Greek, and Hebrew, corrupt and reads

[Printed p. 77 | IA leaf 107 | access PDF 107 | OCR object 106]

twenty-six for thirty-six against every witness; the latter, though vigorously
defending the integrity of the texts, leaves the knot insoluble. Nevertheless,
Junius’s explanation in his notes is sufficiently sound and approved by the
most learned Catholics: namely, Chronicles expressly speaks of the years of
Asa’s kingdom, not of King Asa. It assigns two beginnings to Asa’s kingdom,
one when that king himself began to reign, the other the beginning of the
state and monarchy of which he was king. That state and monarchy in turn had
a twofold beginning: an absolute one, when Saul began to reign and Israel’s
democracy first changed into a kingdom, and a relative one in relation to the
kingdom of Judah, when it began to be distinguished by the separation of the
ten tribes in Rehoboam’s first year. From the relative and distinctive
beginning of the kingdom of Judah and Asa until Asa’s sixteenth, when Baasha
made war on him, there were in fact thirty-five years. Between those endpoints
elapsed Rehoboam’s seventeen, Abijah’s three, and Asa’s sixteen years. Thus
Baasha’s first of twenty-four years corresponds to Asa’s third, and Baasha’s
twelfth, when he began to attack Israel, to Asa’s fifteenth, which from
Rehoboam’s first year, and thus from the kingdom of Judah’s distinction, was
the thirty-sixth. Baasha’s twenty-fourth, when he died, corresponds to Asa’s
twenty-sixth, which from Rehoboam’s beginning was the forty-sixth. That sacred
years must sometimes be calculated from a beginning other than a reigning
king’s accession appears from 2 Samuel 15:7, where Absalom’s rebellion is
dated to year forty. These years cannot be referred to David’s coronation,
for David had not yet worn the crown thirty years; they must therefore be
referred either to Saul’s first accession, David’s private anointing in his
father’s house, or some other point.

### QUESTION VII

#### How Omri reigned both eight and twelve years

Seventh you may ask how verse 23 of 1 Kings 16 agrees with verse 29 of the
same chapter. The former says that Omri began to reign in King Asa’s
thirty-first year and reigned twelve years; the latter says that Omri died and his
son Ahab came to Israel’s kingdom in King Asa of Judah’s thirty-eighth year,
by which calculation Omri held the reins of the kingdom for only eight years,
not twelve. Answer: verse 15 of the same chapter easily unties this knot. It
says that Zimri began his reign in Asa’s twenty-seventh year and reigned only
seven days, and hence that Omri began to reign in the same year. We must
therefore establish two beginnings of Omri’s reign: one in Asa’s
twenty-seventh, when Zimri died and Omri gained Israel’s throne and half the people;
the other in Asa’s thirty-first, when his rival Tibni died and he peacefully
possessed Israel’s entire kingdom without competitor. From the former point
through Asa’s thirty-eighth year, counting both endpoints, are twelve years;
from the latter only eight.

### QUESTION VIII

#### How Jehoram reigned both four and eight years, and began to reign in his
#### father’s seventeenth and twenty-second years and after his father’s death

Eighth you may ask how 1 Kings 22:42 is reconciled with 2 Kings 1:17, 3:1, and
8:16–17. In the first passage Jehoshaphat reigns twenty-five years beginning
in Ahab’s fourth; in the second, Jehoram son of Ahab begins to reign in the
second of Joram son of Jehoshaphat; in the third, Jehoram begins to reign in
Jehoshaphat’s eighteenth and Ahab’s eighteenth; and in the fourth, while
Jehoshaphat still lived and reigned at Jerusalem, his son Joram begins to reign
in Jehoram of Israel’s fifth. From these it follows that Jehoram of Israel’s
first coincides with Jehoshaphat’s eighteenth. This also appears from 1 Kings
22:51, where Ahab’s elder son Ahaziah begins to reign in Jehoshaphat’s
seventeenth, which was Ahab’s twenty-second. Make the last year incomplete,
and by a clear calculation assign Ahab twenty-two years and Jehoshaphat
twenty-five. Jehoshaphat, beginning in Ahab’s fourth, will end Ahab in his own
seventeenth; Ahaziah, beginning in Jehoshaphat’s seventeenth and reigning two
years after his father, will die the next year, leaving Israel’s throne to his
brother Jehoram in Jehoshaphat’s eighteenth. These things flow smoothly
enough, but what follows is more troubled. Jehoram of Israel’s first and
Jehoshaphat’s eighteenth is Joram of Judah’s second. Thus Joram of Judah began
to reign in his father Jehoshaphat’s seventeenth, Ahab’s last and Ahaziah’s
first, when Jehoshaphat left his kingdom to accompany Ahab to Ramoth-gilead,
where Ahab received his mortal and final wound. Moreover, comparison of these
passages shows that Joram son of Jehoshaphat did not begin to reign until
Jehoram

[Printed p. 78 | IA leaf 108 | access PDF 108 | OCR object 107]

of Israel’s fifth, and hence his father’s twenty-second. Finally these passages
show that Jehoshaphat lived beyond Jehoram of Israel’s fifth, so that he
reigned for three or four years alongside his son Joram, and died no earlier
than Jehoram of Israel’s eighth or ninth. Joram son of Jehoshaphat died in
Jehoram of Israel’s eleventh; in the twelfth and last, Jehu killed both
Jehoram son of Ahab and Ahaziah son of Joram, in the first year of Ahaziah’s
reign at Jerusalem.

How then could Joram son of Jehoshaphat have had three beginnings of his
reign—his father’s seventeenth, then his twenty-second, and finally after his
father’s death at the end of his twenty-fifth? How also is he repeatedly said
to have reigned eight years when he did not complete four after his father’s
death? Answer: our scholars, with the approval of the most learned Catholic
chronologists, solve all this by the single foundation of co-regencies. They
say that, as often among other nations, so not rarely among the Jews sons
reigned with fathers, either as deputies and regents during their fathers’
illness or absence outside the kingdom, or as true kings when, to establish
the kingdom in their family, fathers admitted sons while they still lived to
share throne and crown. Thus they establish three beginnings for Joram: the
first in his father’s seventeenth, when Jehoshaphat went outside his kingdom
on that dangerous campaign with Ahab against the Syrians. Yet Joram’s eight
years are not customarily numbered from this point. Though his father then
used his viceregal service in administering the kingdom, he does not yet
appear to have admitted him to the full right and settled partnership of the
scepter. That honor was granted Joram only in his father’s twenty-second year,
from which his eight years begin to run. His sole reign could begin only after
his father’s death and could not last more than four years. Thus the most
celebrated chronologists customarily unravel these very involved and
perplexing knots.

### QUESTION IX

#### When Elijah’s letter to Jehoram was written

Ninth you may ask how Elijah could write to Jehoram son of Jehoshaphat several
years after his own death, in the letter at 2 Chronicles 21:12 threatening
intestinal disease and a miserable death for idolatry, fratricide, and other
crimes. Elijah was translated to heaven several years before Jehoshaphat’s
death; for in 2 Kings 3, after Elijah’s translation, we see Jehoshaphat
campaigning with the kings of Israel and Edom against the Moabites and rescued
from imminent destruction by the present prophet Elisha. Did Elijah send
letters from heaven to mortals? Answer: as Isaiah in the prophetic spirit
foresaw and foretold what Cyrus would do several centuries after his death,
and Ahijah what Josiah would do, so Elijah, knowing by divine revelation what
Jehoram would perpetrate after four or five years, wrote this letter before
his translation, but left it to be delivered at the proper time after his
ascension to heaven. Likewise before his translation he gave Elisha various
commands concerning the neighboring kings of Israel and Damascus which Elisha
executed only several years later by anointing Jehu and Hazael, 1 Kings 19:15.

### QUESTION X

#### How Ahaziah began to reign in both his twenty-second and forty-second year

Tenth you may ask how 2 Chronicles 22:2 is reconciled with 2 Kings 8:25. The
former says Ahaziah was forty-two when he began to reign; the latter that he
reigned at the age of twenty-two. Answer: this is one of the things called
*alyta*, insoluble. Many people both now and formerly have offered many
solutions to this knot; let each choose whichever he wishes. I confess that
here we abandon those whom we most love to follow elsewhere. Scaliger’s
answer, with many others, that the Hebrew text is corrupt displeases us most
of all. What Diodati, Voetius, and Henry Philippi offer concerning twenty
years in which they wish Ahaziah to have reigned with his father Joram is so
perplexing and unusual, and appears so entirely destitute of solid foundation,
that we cannot assent. We prefer what the English Annotations and the learned
and pious William Nisbet have from Junius: the latter passage gives Ahaziah’s
age and the duration of his life, while the former gives not his life’s
duration but that of his mother’s family, which by God’s most manifest
judgment was extinguished in that very year. In memory of this remarkable
miracle, the calculation was attributed to Ahaziah, who in that year was
removed by Jehu’s sword with the rest of Ahab and Omri’s family. Just as one
passage designates the true year of his life in which he began to reign, so
the other marks the end of that most unfortunate family. That the
forty-second year was the true end of this most criminal family appears by adding

[Printed p. 79 | IA leaf 109 | access PDF 109 | OCR object 108]

Omri’s six years in which he ruled alone to Ahab’s twenty-two, Ahaziah’s two,
and Joram’s twelve. Joram, the last of that family, was killed in the year in
which Ahaziah son of Jehoshaphat both began and ceased to reign, and was buried
together with almost his whole lineage in the common ruin of that family.

Though we rest in this, Junius’s excessively violent twisting of the former
passage to fit his and our position nevertheless displeases us and others. He
renders it: *“His mother’s name was Athaliah daughter of Omri, who was passing
through the forty-second year when Ahaziah reigned.”* This transposes the end
of the sentence to its beginning and its beginning to its end. Though it
solves the knot very well, it appears supported by neither a suitable reason
nor a convenient example.

### QUESTION XI

#### Whether Joash descended from Solomon or Nathan

Eleventh it is asked concerning King Joash whether he was Amaziah’s natural
son, since 2 Kings 11:1 says that Athaliah destroyed the entire royal line.
Answer: beware the dangerous error of Drusius and others, who from Annius’s
most corrupt chronology want Solomon’s line to have ended in Amaziah and
Nathan’s to have begun in Joash. If this is granted, Christ’s genealogy would
be shrouded in the thickest darkness. Let this delusion depart with the many
other falsehoods of the utterly mendacious Annius, whom sacred Scripture
openly contradicts many times.

As for the passage before us, Athaliah is indeed said to have destroyed the
whole royal line, but with the explicit exception of one Joash in the next
verse, who is stated here and there six times to be Amaziah’s son, begotten by
Amaziah.

### QUESTION XII

#### When Joash began to reign

Twelfth it is asked how 2 Kings 13:1 is reconciled with verse 10 of that
chapter. The former says that Jehoahaz began to reign in Joash’s twenty-third
year and reigned seventeen years; the latter says that, when his father was
dead and buried, his son began to reign in Joash’s thirty-seventh. From the
twenty-third to the thirty-seventh are not seventeen years but only fifteen,
even if both endpoints are included. Answer: Abulensis’s answer, which declares
all the texts here corrupt and reads thirty-nine for thirty-seven, must be
entirely rejected. The common solution through co-regencies is sufficiently
convenient without this troublesome alteration of time. Chronologists
establish two beginnings of Joash of Israel’s reign: one in Joash of Judah’s
thirty-seventh, two or three years before his father’s death, when his father,
occupied by the Syrian war, appears to have called his son into partnership
of the scepter; the other after his father’s death in Joash’s thirty-ninth and
his father’s seventeenth. It does not obstruct this solution that verse 4 says
the father died and was buried before his son Joash of Israel entered the
kingdom in Joash of Judah’s thirty-seventh. Such anticipations are common in
Scripture, especially where all the deeds of one man are gathered together
without mixture with those of others. That not only Joash of Judah’s
thirty-seventh but also his thirty-ninth was a beginning of Joash of Israel’s reign
appears from the first verse of the next chapter. There Amaziah, his father
having died in his fortieth regnal year, begins to reign in Joash of Israel’s
second. This cannot hold unless Joash’s first coincides with Amaziah’s father’s
thirty-ninth. Moreover, since Joash of Israel is assigned sixteen years and
Amaziah twenty-nine, while Amaziah survived Joash by only fifteen years, 2
Kings 14:17, so that he lived with Joash for fourteen and Joash’s sixteenth
and last is Amaziah’s fourteenth or fifteenth, these things cannot otherwise
be preserved than by placing Joash of Israel’s first in Joash of Judah’s
thirty-ninth and next-to-last, two years before Amaziah’s first; whether the
years are fractional or whole matters little.

### QUESTION XIII

#### Whether Jeroboam II reigned with his father for eleven years

This difficulty is of greater commotion. How is 2 Kings 14:1 reconciled with
verse 23 and with 15:1? The first passage says Amaziah reigned at Jerusalem
twenty-nine years; the second that Jeroboam II began to reign in Samaria in
Amaziah’s fifteenth; the third that Uzziah or Azariah, Amaziah’s son, began to
reign immediately after his father’s death at age sixteen and in Jeroboam’s
twenty-seventh. Since Amaziah’s fifteenth coincides with Jeroboam’s first,
must not Amaziah’s twenty-ninth and last, and hence Uzziah’s first, coincide
with Jeroboam’s sixteenth rather than twenty-seventh? Or, if Uzziah did not
come to the throne before Jeroboam’s twenty-seventh, Judah’s throne must have
stood empty for eleven years, and after Amaziah’s death there was an
eleven-year interregnum at Jerusalem, for that many are counted from Amaziah’s death
to Jeroboam’s

[Printed p. 80 | IA leaf 110 | access PDF 110 | OCR object 109]

twenty-seventh. Answer: this passage is very difficult, and all chronologists labor
greatly to solve it. Most Protestants admit the stated interregnum; Catholics
reject it, and Henry Philippi, with arrogance and abuse of our men, offers a
solution from Torniellus through co-regencies. The solution through
co-regencies sufficiently pleases us here, though Torniellus could have learned
it from the most learned Junius. But Henry the Jesuit is far too captious
against Capellus, Calvisius, and other Protestants for solving this most
difficult problem through interregna; for he and all Catholics solve the next
difficulty through an interregnum. Why they apply co-regencies to the present
difficulty and interregna to the next, rather than interregna here and
co-regencies there, the reasons they offer are of no weight to me. One point,
untouched by any of them, moves me to prefer a co-regency here: it is hard to
admit any vacancy on Judah’s throne without explicit Scripture, especially
one adding at least eleven years to the epoch’s total. Interregna in Israel
or any other nation which do not alter the epochs’ totals do not disturb
chronological reasoning. Answer the question, then, that Jeroboam II had two
beginnings of his reign: one in his father Joash’s sixth and Amaziah’s fourth,
which Junius notes on the passage, when he was summoned by his father,
occupied against the Syrians, into partnership in government; the other at
his father’s death in Amaziah’s fifteenth, from which his forty-one years are
to be numbered. His first eleven years, in which he administered the kingdom
with his father, are not chronological, though the twenty-seventh compared
with Uzziah’s first, in which Uzziah began to reign, belongs to the order of
these non-chronological years. Thus that twenty-seventh from the earlier
beginning of Jeroboam’s reign is only the sixteenth from the later beginning.
Most chronologists, old and new, rest in this answer.

### QUESTION XIV

#### Whether there were interregna in Samaria

Fourteenth it is asked how 2 Kings 14:23 is reconciled with 15:1 and 8. The
first passage says Jeroboam II reigned forty-one years and began his kingdom
in Amaziah’s fifteenth. Amaziah reigned twenty-nine years, down to Jeroboam’s
sixteenth in which Jeroboam reigned alone, or his twenty-seventh from when
Jeroboam first sat on the throne with his father. In the second passage
Uzziah, beginning in Jeroboam’s twenty-seventh or sixteenth, continued to
reign fifty-two years. In the third, Jeroboam’s son Zechariah began his
kingdom in Samaria in Uzziah’s thirty-eighth. The question arises: how can
Zechariah’s first coincide with Uzziah’s thirty-eighth, since the forty-first
and last of his father Jeroboam, and hence Zechariah’s first, coincides with
Uzziah’s twenty-seventh or twenty-sixth? Add to Uzziah’s twenty-seventh the
fourteen of Amaziah which elapsed from his fifteenth, when Jeroboam began to
reign, and you will have the whole sum of Jeroboam’s reign, forty-one years.
Thus this calculation lacks eleven years. Answer: nearly all chronologists
acknowledge an interregnum at Samaria, longer according to some and shorter
according to others. Most grant eleven years between Jeroboam’s death and the
beginning of Zechariah’s reign. Jeroboam must have died in Uzziah’s
twenty-seventh, since it was his forty-first and final regnal year. But Capellus,
Calvisius, and others extend this interregnum to twenty-three or twenty-two
years. Since they solve the former difficulty through an eleven-year
interregnum in Judah’s kingdom, not an eleven-year co-regency in Israel, they
consistently make Jeroboam’s last coincide with Uzziah’s fourteenth rather
than twenty-seventh. From this to the thirty-eighth, excluding the endpoints,
they count twenty-three years as the Israelite interregnum from Jeroboam’s
death to his son Zechariah’s inauguration—certainly far too long a span.

It is regrettable that many interpreters, especially the recent English
annotators, pass over so important a question entirely untouched; and it is
shameful that Junius’s annotator asserts that this first of Zechariah and
Uzziah’s thirty-eighth was Jeroboam’s last. This is a manifest anachronism
which cannot be defended, though opponents have not yet noticed it; otherwise
they would have triumphed insultingly over so great an error.

### QUESTION XV

#### How the years of Judah’s kings are to be compared with those of Israel’s

Fifteenth it is asked how the years of the later kings of Israel are properly
compared with those of Judah’s kings. In 2 Kings 15:

[Printed p. 81 | IA leaf 111 | access PDF 111 | OCR object 110]

8, Zechariah son of Jeroboam II is said to have reigned six months at Samaria
in King Uzziah of Judah’s thirty-eighth year; in verse 13 of the same chapter,
Shallum reigned one month in Uzziah’s thirty-ninth; in verse 17, Menahem
reigned ten years at Samaria beginning in King Uzziah’s thirty-ninth; in verse
23, Pekahiah reigned two years beginning in Uzziah’s fiftieth; and finally in
verse 27, Pekah reigned twenty years beginning in Uzziah’s fifty-second. How
are these years to be arranged so that the comparison agrees? The whole
difficulty is easy to solve. Put Zechariah’s six months in Uzziah’s
thirty-eighth not as it begins but as it ends. Let Shallum’s month fall at either the
beginning or middle of Uzziah’s thirty-ninth. Let Menahem’s first, according
to several of its months, be counted with Uzziah’s ending thirty-ninth, and
its remaining months with his fortieth. Thus Menahem’s tenth can be compared
with Uzziah’s forty-ninth and Pekahiah’s first with his fiftieth. Pekahiah’s
last then coincides with Uzziah’s fifty-first as it ends and fifty-second as
it begins, just as Ussher of Armagh correctly establishes the calculations in
his tables.

### QUESTION XVI

#### Whether Jotham reigned four years after his death

Sixteenth it is asked how verse 30 of 2 Kings 15 is reconciled with verse 33
of the same chapter and the first verse of the next. The first gives Jotham
twenty years, the second only sixteen, and the third proves that Jotham’s life
ended not in his twentieth but his sixteenth regnal year, since it began in
Pekah’s second and ended in his seventeenth. Answer: omitting the solutions
offered by others, we rest in Junius’s answer—though it should not have been
placed in the text itself, but, as the French interpreter did, in the margin.
The twentieth year was not one in which he reigned, but one counted from when
Jotham began to reign; it was actually Ahaz’s fourth and the fourth since
Jotham’s death. For there the calculation is established from Uzziah’s death
and the beginning of Jotham’s reign, the only kings of Judah of whom the
author had spoken, before he comes to Ahaz’s history, which is appended all at
once after that passage. Henry Philippi took this answer from Torniellus and
Torniellus from Genebrard, but those envious men made no mention of Junius, to
whom they owed gratitude for the discovery.

### QUESTION XVII

#### How Hoshea began to reign in Ahaz’s fourth and twelfth years

Seventeenth it is asked how the second difficulty, no less serious, which
arises from the same passages is solved: in 2 Kings 15:30, Hoshea begins to
reign in the twentieth year counted from Jotham’s accession, that is, Ahaz’s
fourth; yet in 17:1 Hoshea begins to reign only in Ahaz’s twelfth. Answer:
chronologists agree in attributing two beginnings of Hoshea’s reign: one in
Ahaz’s fourth, from which he reigned seventeen years; the other in Ahaz’s
twelfth, from which he reigned only nine. But there is great disagreement over
the true reason for this double calculation. Most Catholics say Hoshea spent
eight years after killing Pekah establishing the kingdom, so the first year
of his settled reign was Ahaz’s twelfth, although the absolute first of his
reign was Ahaz’s fourth. Some Protestants want Ahaz’s twelfth to be Hoshea’s
first because only then, after casting off the Assyrian yoke, did Hoshea assert
himself as a free king. Others, by contrast, count his last nine years from
Ahaz’s twelfth because throughout those years the king served as Assyria’s
tributary. Whatever we say of these or other causes, it is enough for solving
the question to have clearly demonstrated from Scripture, with the agreement
of all chronologists, two beginnings of Hoshea’s government.

### QUESTION XVIII

#### How Ahaz reigned sixteen years and yet only fifteen

Eighteenth it is asked how 2 Kings 16:1–2 agrees with 17:1 and 18:1, 10. The
first passage says Ahaz reigned sixteen years, but comparison of the latter
three passages allows his reign only fifteen. Ahaz’s twelfth was Hoshea’s
first, and Hoshea’s third was Hezekiah’s first; hence Hezekiah’s fourth
coincides with Hoshea’s seventh and his sixth with Hoshea’s ninth and last. If
these things are true, Ahaz reigned at most fifteen, not sixteen, years.
Torniellus answers that Ahaz reigned fourteen complete but sixteen incomplete
years. He wants Ahaz’s first to have coincided with his father Jotham’s last,
and his own last with his son Hezekiah’s first, so that the earlier part of
his first fell in Pekah’s

[Printed p. 82 | IA leaf 112 | access PDF 112 | OCR object 111]

seventeenth and the later part in Pekah’s eighteenth, while the earlier part
of Ahaz’s last and Hezekiah’s first must have fallen in Hoshea’s third and the
later part in Hoshea’s fifth. By this calculation Jotham separately receives
sixteen years and Ahaz separately the same, but together they receive only
thirty-one.

Henry Philippi answers differently, giving Hezekiah two beginnings of his
reign: one in his father’s fifteenth, after which Ahaz lived and reigned with
his son one or two years; the other upon his father’s death in his sixteenth,
from which Hezekiah’s deeds and twenty-nine years are commonly counted. Since
the question here concerns only one year, we do not anxiously inquire whether
the former, the latter, or yet another answer is preferable.

### QUESTION XIX

#### Whether Ahaz fathered Hezekiah in his eleventh year

Much harder is the knot which follows. According to 2 Kings 16:1 Ahaz was
twenty when he began to reign and reigned sixteen years, so he lived only
thirty-six. But according to 2 Kings 18:1 Hezekiah was twenty-five when he
began to reign. Subtract from Ahaz’s thirty-six those twenty-five years which
Hezekiah had when his dying father handed him the scepter, and only eleven
remain by which the father exceeded his son. Therefore Ahaz must have fathered
Hezekiah in his tenth, or at most eleventh, year. Answer: nearly all
chronologists, Catholic and Protestant, grant that Ahaz fathered Hezekiah in
his eleventh year; but with Petavius and Testatus Abulensis I would much rather
move the year of Hezekiah’s begetting to his father’s thirteenth or
fourteenth, although this is done by a subtler and rather intricate reckoning
of fractional and whole years. I dislike Calvisius’s solution here, which
asserts that Hezekiah was not Ahaz’s natural son but either his brother or
stepson, and only a legal son and heir-successor in the kingdom. No foundation
for this solution appears in Scripture.

### QUESTION XX

#### How long the kingdom of the ten tribes endured

Twentieth it is asked how many years the kingdom of Israel stood. Answer: the
kingdom’s endpoints are fixed in explicit Scripture. It began in Rehoboam’s
first and ended in Hezekiah’s sixth. The amount of time between these points
is derived from the chronological years which Scripture assigns to the
individual kings of Judah and Israel. Since the reckoning of these years
differs among many, the whole sum is variously composed by different writers.
All extend that rule—not so much a lawful kingdom as a true rebellion against
God and men—to 250 years. Divine patience delayed vengeance that long, but at
last the dreadful severity of punishment amply balanced the delay. Some add
four, some six, some eight or ten to this undisputed total according to their
different starting points for counting. But what came into our Nisbet’s mind
when he extended his calculation here to 306 I cannot conjecture, unless—as
too often elsewhere—I suspect a very gross printer’s error in that otherwise
learned and useful little book.

That learned man does not badly solve the knot arising from the fact that the
years of Israel’s kings exceed those of the contemporary kings of Judah by
four. He distinguishes complete from incomplete years, assigning two
incomplete years each to Nadab and Ahaziah, which do not increase the sum of
complete years among Judah’s kings.

### QUESTION XXI

#### How long the captivity of the ten tribes endured

Twenty-first it is asked whether the captivity of the ten tribes should be
regarded as temporary or perpetual. Answer: this is a distinguished but quite
perplexing and obscure question, which we will now touch upon only briefly.
First, we assert that the circumstances of the captivity of the ten tribes
were very different from those of Judah and Benjamin—together with Levi, which
was the Lord’s chosen portion and not counted among the brothers, its place
being supplied by Joseph’s other son. Hosea 1:6: *“I will no longer continue
to have mercy upon the house of Israel so as in any way to pardon them; but I
will again have mercy upon the house of Judah.”* The Jews returned to
Jerusalem after seventy years of captivity, but the Israelites did not
similarly return from Assyria, 2 Chronicles 17:23; to this day they have not
returned.

Second, the restoration of the Israelites promised by various prophets was
less universal than that of the Jews. Jeremiah 3:14: when God, having laid
aside the future matter, begins to have mercy upon the Israelites, he brings
back one from a city and two from a family.

Third, that solemn and splendid, though not universal, restoration of the
Israelites must be understood not only of the frequent defection of many from
Israel to the Jews in Rehoboam’s time, 2 Chronicles 11:16, and Asa’s, 15:9,
where

[Printed p. 83 | IA leaf 113 | access PDF 113 | OCR object 112]

it is expressly mentioned, but especially of the return of many Israelites
together with the Jews by Cyrus’s decree, as may be seen at Jeremiah 50:4.
After divine vengeance had destroyed Babylon and the Chaldeans for laying
waste the temple at Jerusalem, many Israelites who had been dispersed among
the Jews before the captivity and carried away with them shared in Jewish
liberty and returned to Jerusalem. Nothing prevents many others, stirred by
this miracle of divine mercy, from also returning home from the former
Assyrian captivity. In the times of the Persian and Greek empires they
multiplied greatly in Canaan together with the Jews. Though they did not
cultivate the fields of their tribes as distinctly as before, because the
Samaritan rabble had occupied most of their possessions, nevertheless down to
Roman times and the nation’s final devastation, many from all twelve tribes,
scattered here and there throughout Canaan, came to Jerusalem for the Jewish
sacred rites.

Fourth, there was a very notable fulfillment of the prophetic promises when
in the time of Christ and the apostles many Israelites together with Jews
were converted to faith in the gospel. Paul expressly interprets Hosea in
Romans 9 of this return of the Israelites to faith; Christ affirms that he was
sent to the lost sheep of Israel’s house, not only Judah’s; and the apostle
the apostle Jude addresses his letter to all twelve tribes.

Fifth, if these things do not exhaust all the promises made by the prophets
to the Israelites, everything remaining will be fulfilled in the final
conversion of the Jews to Christ. The distinction among the tribes,
accurately observed before Christ’s advent, has been confused for many
centuries. No conversion of Israelites distinguished from Jews should
therefore be expected. One must especially beware foul Jewish fables. Recently
Manasseh ben Israel, a rabbi at Amsterdam, notably attempted to dress old
fables in new clothes; our Dury and other hotheaded little men in England
embraced them far too eagerly. Anabaptist dreams about the saints’ worldly
kingdom on earth have prevailed more than enough; there is no need to spur
those already running. May God hasten the Israelites’ return to faith. The
privileges of Christians, whether Jewish or Gentile, are spiritual and
eternal; the old ceremonial and bodily pedagogy was abolished on Christ’s
cross. Besides the error, it is no small injury and slight loss now to obscure
the church’s eternal, heavenly inheritance with mortal, earthly gains.

### QUESTION XXII

#### How the produce of one year sufficed for three

Question twenty-two concerns the twofold sense of 2 Kings 19:29: *“But to you,
Hezekiah, this shall be the sign: eat this year what you find, in the second
year what grows of itself, and in the third year sow and reap.”* First, did
the produce of one year suffice to feed the people throughout three years?
Second, must miracles be multiplied without necessity? Did the Assyrian war
prevent the people from farming for three whole years? Third, did Hezekiah’s
illness precede the Assyrian defeat, or follow it? Answer to the first: it was
plainly a miracle of divine omnipotence that one year’s fruit nourished the
people throughout three whole years. To the second: not the war but a
sabbatical year prevented agriculture so long, for this war was completed in
one year, Hezekiah’s fourteenth. He must have been raised from illness at the
beginning of his fifteenth, so that the added fifteen years would end in his
twenty-ninth and final year of life and reign. Thus farming was abandoned for
only one year because of war; in the second the land remained uncultivated
because of the sabbatism. Most Catholic chronologists do not wish to
acknowledge a sabbatical year here, but, setting aside this cause for the
cessation of farming, they offer no more suitable one. In the third year no
produce from the ground could be gathered before the harvest and vintage at
year’s end. Answer to the third: though along with the Catholics our Ussher in
his recent accurate chronicle puts Hezekiah’s illness before the Assyrian
defeat, Scripture must be followed. Both Kings and Isaiah narrate Hezekiah’s
illness after Sennacherib’s flight, and prolepses should not be admitted into
history without necessity.

What they offer concerning Isaiah’s promise to the recovering king that the
Jews would be freed from fear of Assyrian oppression—a danger that did not
seem to exist after Sennacherib’s flight—is an objection of little weight.
Why could the Assyrian monarch, after losing one army, not soon return with a
much stronger one, as the far lesser Syrian prince Ben-hadad had done shortly
before?

### QUESTION XXIII

#### On the sun’s regress in Hezekiah’s days

Question twenty-three: What should be determined concerning the sun’s regress
in Hezekiah’s days?

[Printed p. 84 | IA leaf 114 | access PDF 114 | OCR object 113]

Answer: many people dispute many things on this matter at length and
curiously, especially the Catholics and before all Abulensis Tostatus; we
think only a few things should be noted. First, those appear to err who place
this regress not in the sun itself at all, but only in the shadow of its rays,
and that upon a single sundial of Asa. Isaiah 38:8 expressly affirms that the
sun returned ten lines through the degrees by which it had descended.
Moreover, a shadow is moved only when the luminous or opaque body causing it
moves. Finally, the Babylonian prince Merodach sent men to Hezekiah to inquire
about this miracle. It could not have been observed at Babylon or anywhere
outside Jerusalem if the whole movement existed only in a shadow and a single
sundial of Ahaz, not in the sun itself.

Second, we reject Ussher’s opinion. Contrary to the mind of nearly all ancient
and recent writers, he denies that Ahaz constructed any sundial, or that at
that time hours were known anywhere on earth, since Pliny attributes their
invention—together with the pole, gnomon, and the whole art of sundials—to
Anaximenes and Anaximander long after that age. Certainly Herodotus, much
earlier, admits that the Greeks received sundials from the Babylonians. And
the Chaldean paraphrast, more trustworthy than Pliny, affirms that Ahaz
constructed a stone for marking the hours. Daniel also speaks many times of
the division of the day into twelve hours, not as a recent invention but an
already established custom.

As for Ussher’s point from the Septuagint’s words *bathmous* and
*anabathmous*, by which they render the Hebrew *ma‘aloth*, no terms could be
more suitable for signifying lines and degrees marked on a sundial to indicate
the sun’s movement and progress in its daily ascent and descent.

Third, we do not approve what the recent English notes draw from Abulensis and
other Catholics against Augustine, Calvin, Musculus, and many others: that the
ten degrees named by Isaiah denote not hours but half-hours or quarter-hours.
Apart from the fact that lines are not customarily placed on sundials except
to mark whole hours, God’s glory in miracles should not be diminished without
necessity, and no necessity here compels us to shorten the sun’s regress. The
one argument they bring from Abulensis is not difficult to solve. They readily
grant the possibility that if the sun, at the Hebrews’ tenth hour of the day,
that is our fourth hour after noon and two hours before sunset, had been drawn
back ten whole degrees and placed at the eastern horizon at these Jews’ first
hour, our sixth before noon; but they maintain it was impossible for the sun
two hours before sunset to advance ten degrees on the sundial. There is no
shadow after sunset and no lines on a sundial marking hours and degrees after
twelve. Yet Hezekiah was given the choice, at his own discretion, whether the
sun should advance or retreat ten degrees on Ahaz’s dial. Musculus answers
that the choice was indeed given to Hezekiah, but was not to be carried out
immediately, at the very moment Isaiah offered it. It would be executed at
the appropriate time the Lord chose, whether that day, the next, or the day
after. Since Hezekiah chose the regress, the sun began to move backward at the
fourth hour after noon, so as to complete a ten-hour regress that day. Had he
instead chosen the rapid advance of ten degrees, that rapid advance could
have begun the following morning without difficulty. Moreover, if the advance
had begun from the Hebrews’ tenth hour, where the regress began, it could
easily have been observed on the sundial. Although only two lines were visible
after the tenth, twelve others were understood after sunset for the nighttime
hours. When the sun accomplished eight of these in a moment and remained in
that night’s shadow for only four or two hours, so that it lay beneath the
horizon for only two or four hours from sunset to sunrise, or from two hours
before sunset to sunrise, the miraculous fulfillment of the promise that the
sun would hasten through ten degrees would have been visible to the eye.

Fourth, the opinion of many doctors that the sun’s regress from the tenth line
to the first was instantaneous, and thus added only ten hours to that day’s
ordinary span of twelve hours, does not appear reasonable. God says he wished
to draw the sun back ten degrees on the dial, so that it would return again
through these ten lines in no less than ten hours. What boldness, without any
shadow of support from sacred Scripture, would assign a moment to the regress
and ten hours to its advance after the regress?

They object from Joshua 10:14 that no day was ever longer than the one in
which, at Joshua’s prayer, the sun stood at its setting for the space of a
whole additional day. That doubled day could have had no more than
twenty-four hours, while Hezekiah’s day by our calculation was nearer triple and
comprised no fewer than thirty-two hours. The answer is easy: Joshua’s double
day is called longer than every preceding and following day, not absolutely,
but only among the days which had elapsed down to the time when the book of
Joshua was written. This does not touch Hezekiah’s day at all.

Fifth, we do not admit Ussher’s claim that the length of this day was
compensated by shortening the next night to only two hours. Would Scripture
have been silent about so great a miracle? A two-hour night would be no less
wonderful than a twenty-two-hour day, indeed more wonderful: no night was ever
like it in brevity, while another day was nearly equal to this one in length.

The reasoning that this most learned man offers does not compel. He argues as
follows: unless the following night by its brevity compensated for the length
of the preceding day, the movements of the heavenly spheres and stars would
be found to have deviated from every proper course, with an irregularity of at
least ten hours. Yet, he says, their movement from the beginning of the world
to this day is found so regular that all lunar and solar eclipses fall upon
the very day, hour, and moment which astronomical rules require. In particular,
three lunar eclipses mentioned by Ptolemy before Hezekiah’s time are still
found today at the same moments when Chaldeans living in that age recorded
them as occurring. We answer that we most willingly grant the highly regular
movements of the heavenly spheres, especially the stars, even amid the
greatest irregularities, from the world’s beginning to this day. We also grant
that, just as the moon is observed to have stood with the sun in Joshua’s
days, so in Hezekiah’s days, when the sun retreated, the moon, every star and
sphere, and the whole fabric of the upper world likewise retreated together.
For the first and proper principle of that daily movement is the prime mover,
or tenth sphere, which in twenty-four hours carries all lower spheres around
with it contrary to their own proper motions. We do not, however, believe
that today’s practitioners find the motions of the heavenly lights and
eclipses of the planets at precisely the same places in which the ancients
observed them; if that were assumed, chronologists’ calculations would be far
more accurate and harmonious. But grant that those three lunar eclipses of
Ptolemy observed before the sun’s regress so preserve their positions that we
must acknowledge that the irregularity of those ten hours was compensated at
some time. It does not follow that this happened by contracting the immediately
following night to two hours. The appearances are sufficiently preserved if
that delay was recovered at any nearby time, even if not in the night which
immediately followed; indeed, it was entirely impossible for that delay to be
recovered in that night. Suppose the whole night was omitted and, by a still
greater miracle, the triple day was made quadruple. Even then the omission of
one night of twelve, or at most fourteen, hours—the longest night in the
Syrian climate did not extend farther—could not exhaust a delay of twenty
hours. That delay was distributed among nearby days and nights by the wisdom
of the omnipotent first mover so as to escape observation by human eyes. It
is not ours to inquire too curiously into the manner.

Sixth, Hezekiah’s answer concerning the ease of the sun’s advance through ten
degrees must be understood not absolutely and in itself, but comparatively
and in relation to human observation. It was supremely difficult to propel
the sun’s sphere beyond its ordinary course for even a moment, much less ten
hours, and this acceleration could be accomplished by no hand but the divine.
Yet since the sun’s progress is daily, while a regress contrary to order has
never been seen by any mortal, a short delay in regress would appear to human
eyes more observable and wonderful than a much greater hastening forward. The
difficulty was also comparatively doubled: in an advance only ten hours would
have been consumed; in the regress, though only ten hours were likewise
consumed, before the retreating sun could return to the point from which it
had departed it necessarily had to perform twenty hours of motion.

Finally, what Torniellus, Sanctius, and other Catholics offer here about a
third miracle that delayed the sun—as though before Joshua and Hezekiah a
certain Joachim from the tribe of Judah halted the sun’s course, 1 Chronicles
4:22—is a most foul fable. All Catholics are indeed bound to believe it by
divine faith, for their authentic Bible reads the passage thus: *“He who made
the sun stand, and the men of Falsehood, Secure and Burning.”* But this is
contrary to the Hebrew truth and the testimony of all other interpreters.
Admittedly, in this passage of Chronicles or Paralipomenon the Chaldean
paraphrase is lacking; but the Septuagint and all other translators—Latin,
French, English, Italian, and German—correctly read proper names in place of
those common nouns: *“Jokim and the men of Cosebha, Joash and Saraph.”* Why the
Old Latin translated these proper names, perhaps its author knew—whoever he
was, for we do not admit Jerome. But however he translated the rest, he
certainly erred grossly in the first, rendering Jokim “the one who stayed the
sun”—most truly, a man of falsehood. We grant that the
verbal form *Jokim*
can be formed from the root *kum* and signify “one who raises” or “one who
establishes.” Yet such a verbal common noun is not repeated in Scripture, nor
is either the root or any derivative from it found anywhere in historical
passages with the meaning “to stay,”

but only with the meaning “to raise” or “to establish.” Yet grant that this
verbal form is a common noun and means “one who stays.” Did that man have
nothing else he could stay but the sun itself? Certainly he could have stayed
the motions of many other creatures far more easily than the course of that
great giant and most splendid bridegroom. That Latin translator, whoever he
was, was far too bold to suppose that the sun was to be checked by this
imaginary one who stayed it. Certainly in that text there appears no
shadow of the sun, no trace or vestige of this miracle. Nevertheless, all
Catholics are bound to embrace it heartily unless they wish to renounce the
Tridentine faith.
