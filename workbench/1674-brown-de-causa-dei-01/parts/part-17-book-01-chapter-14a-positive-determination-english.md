CHAPTER XIV.

Whether any Moral Law becomes a purely positive Law because a positive
determination is annexed to it.

Our adversaries commonly concede that the Moral Law requires some solemn time
to be dedicated and set apart for God and his solemn worship; and they wish
this to exhaust everything Moral contained in the fourth precept of the
Decalogue. Yet they do not wish the weekly Sabbath to be anything Moral,
because the determination of the time, and its restriction to one of seven
days and to the seventh, is something dependent upon the mere will of God. For
this reason they wish the command itself concerning the holy observance of the
Sabbath to be merely positive.

Later, however, we will prove not only that it belongs to Natural Right for
some solemn time to be devoted to divine worship—because perhaps not all our
adversaries will concede this—but also that it belongs to Natural and Moral
Right, as we explained these terms above, for the weekly Sabbath to be
observed, or for one day out of seven to be spent in solemn worship. We
nevertheless concede that, speaking absolutely, God was free to destine one
day out of eight or one out of six for this use, just as he was free to
complete the whole world within the space of seven or five days. Yet we must
acquiesce in the determination now made by divine wisdom and say that this
proportion of time, as one judged best by the all-wise God, is best and is to
be preferred to all others, whether smaller or greater. It also depends upon
his free good pleasure that precisely the seventh

[1674 logical printed p. 178 | IA leaf 196 | PDF p. 196]

day of the week was formerly designated, and that now under the Gospel the
first day of the week has been appointed. Notwithstanding this, we affirm that
the precept concerning the weekly Sabbath is moral, or that this positive
determination does not remove the morality of the precept.

The general question to be investigated here—for we have not yet come to
particulars—is therefore whether a Moral Law to which, for various reasons,
some positive determination dependent upon God’s free will has been annexed
must on that account be called not moral but purely positive.

We proved above in Chapter III that God’s good pleasure is the source or cause
of all the morality in our actions. For our actions can be called moral or
morally good solely because they conform to a law enacted by God.

It is also well known that none of our actions, considered in the physical
category, are morally good or evil. Yet all our human actions in the
individual instance, or insofar as they are clothed with circumstances, assume
a moral character and are either good or evil. For every idle word—much more
an idle action—is evil; Matthew 12:36–37. We will have to give an account of it
on the day of judgment, and a word is called idle when it is uttered without
a due end. From this the Thomists prove that no human act proceeding from
deliberative reason is indifferent in its exercise or individual instance.
See *Didoclavius’s Damascene Altar*, page 498, and so forth. But from where
does it come that an action, whether with respect to its species or its
individual instance, becomes morally good or evil, if not from the divine law
or a sign of the divine will which establishes this one as evil or good in its
species, but that one only in its individual instance, according as it is
surrounded and clothed with these or those circumstances? I do indeed concede
that, in the order of reason and prior to the act of the will commanding or
prohibiting, some actions in their species have a certain agreement or
disagreement with human nature and are fitted either to perfect it or not to
perfect it. I deny, however, that these actions can on that account be called
morally good or evil. They are and are called such only when a law exists with
which they agree or disagree.

It is commonly said that every human action of ours may be considered in two
ways: either in the physical or natural category, or in the moral category.
Insofar as it is considered in the physical category or on the natural plane,
it has its physical goodness or badness, but is not morally good or evil. Yet
insofar as our actions are considered in the moral category, they then assume
the character of moral goodness or badness, because in this way they are
considered insofar as they conform to God’s law and are good, or insofar as
they do not conform to it and are morally evil. In this respect we can quite
appropriately say that an action conforming to God’s law is moral and morally
good, whether in its species or only in its individual instance, even when it
is indifferent in its species, or by reason of the object from which we say
that an action takes its species. By “law” here we do not understand only a
precept proposed to the ears, but every sign of the divine will which
establishes a duty, whether it becomes known to a human being through innate
notions, through the external works and examples of God, or through an audible
voice.

Therefore, in my judgment, our adversary, the distinguished *Burmann*,
criticizes his Reverend colleague in vain for saying that a thing or action
which is indifferent in itself and apart from God’s command ceases to be
indifferent through a positive precept, and from the

[1674 logical printed p. 179 | IA leaf 197 | PDF p. 197]

precept receives a moral character so that it becomes morally good. These
statements can certainly be both read and heard without any astonishment. Dr.
*Burmann*, however, says on page 24 that he could not read them without
astonishment. Let us examine the reasons for this astonishment for a moment.

“Scripture speaks about these things in an entirely different way,” he says
in the *Apology*, page 24, “and teaches us that even after the command was
given it greatly depreciates ceremonies by comparison with those truly moral
precepts. Thus Ezekiel 20:11–12 distinguishes between precepts which, if a
person does them, he will live by them—that is, those which are the life, or
the righteousness, of the human being, for that righteousness is the true life
of his spirit flowing from love of God; hence in Acts 7:38 Stephen calls them
λόγια ζῶντα, ‘living oracles’—and precepts that are not good, in which a human
being does not live; verse 25.” Reply: No one denies that there is a great
difference between those precepts in whose observance consists the life and
righteousness of the human being, and those in which there is properly no
righteousness. Nor does anyone deny the difference between those which are
here called good and those which are called precepts that are not good. But
what will he conclude from this? It does not follow that actions indifferent
in themselves do not receive a moral character through a positive precept.
The text does not say that only those precepts “which, if a person does them,
he will live by them” must be called moral. Who denies that the laws formerly
enacted by God for the Israelite people were distinct from one another? The
text does not say that the actions which had been commanded, whatever their
subject matter, cannot be called morally good. But what does he understand by
those precepts that are not good?

“These precepts that are not good,” he says, “are nothing other than those
ordinances which God gave to the people after the παροξυσμόν, ‘provocation,’
by which he subjected them to servitude and through which he reproached them
for sin.” Reply: Later we will examine this and the proof he attaches to it
more fully. For now, let it suffice to say this: on the supposition that these
precepts that are not good are only those ordinances, what will follow from
it? Can it be inferred by any consequence that the actions commanded through
the ordinances were not morally good? What, were they morally evil? He will
not say this. Were they morally indifferent? Then no obedience at all appeared
in them. But I hope he will not say this. Why, therefore, can they not be
called morally good—and morally good because commanded—and no longer
indifferent? Are these statements such that they cannot be read without
astonishment?

He falls into the same error when on page 25 he cites Jeremiah 7:21 and Micah
6:6–8 and adds: “Let the defender observe there the emphasis on the word
‘Good,’ after those ceremonial acts have been rejected. He calls acts of
justice, kindness, and humility Good. Does he not therefore explicitly and
expressly call such acts morally good as regards the thing itself, while he
denies that the former acts are morally good?” Reply: 1. The adversary himself
is not ignorant that Scripture often speaks comparatively and, when it
prefers these things to those, does not condemn the latter absolutely. We
could confirm this with very many examples if there were need. 2. The
adversary also knows that God never demanded myriads of torrents of oil, nor
that a person give his firstborn for transgression or the fruit of his body
for sin. Therefore he will not number these among good things by reason of a
precept, but rather among evil and prohibited things. 3. The adversary also
knows that on the point of our justification before God, Scripture rejects
acts of justice, kindness, modesty, and all similar things, and admits only
faith in Christ, which alone can in this respect be called good. Will he
therefore conclude that those good acts too are not good? 4. The text does not
say here that the acts God had commanded were not morally good by the force of
the precept. Indeed, elsewhere we read that the Jews were reproved because

[1674 logical printed p. 180 | IA leaf 198 | PDF p. 198]

they neglected those acts or did not perform them properly. See Malachi
1:7–8, 13–14 and 3:8–10. 5. It is true that when the Jews set aside the
substance, end, and purpose of those ceremonies and clung to the bark—that is,
when they sought salvation in those external administrations, such as
sacrifices, washings, and so forth, while setting aside Christ, to whom they
all pointed—God rejected all those works, as is clear from Psalms 50:8, 14,
23 and 51:18–20; Isaiah 1:11–12 and 66:3; Hosea 6:6. But it in no way follows
from this that those external actions were not good in the moral category by
reason of the divine precept enjoining them for an Evangelical end until the
time of reformation; Galatians 4:1–4; Colossians 2:16–17; Hebrews 9:9–10 and
10:1.

He further cites 1 Corinthians 8:8 and Romans 14:17–18 on page 26 and adds:
“Why should we not judge similarly concerning observance of the Sabbath and
of days? How, therefore, are these things Morals, when the Kingdom of God does
not consist in them and they do not contain the image of God, so that in
themselves they are neither good nor evil?” Reply: How great the difference is
between observance of the Sabbath and of those days will be shown in its own
place. 2. Although food in itself does not commend us to God, the kingdom of
God does not consist in it, and it is indifferent, nevertheless to eat food in
such a way that we give others a cause for stumbling is not indifferent. By
reason of the prohibition it becomes morally evil, because this is not to act
according to love; Romans 14:10, 15. Thus the image of God also consists in
things of this kind when they are properly performed from love, faith, and so
forth, although in themselves they are neither morally good nor evil.

We concede that “the law had a shadow of good things to come, not the very
express form of the things.” Yet we say that those actions which were done
according to that law, although they were a type and figure of the realities,
were morally good, because they were not indifferent or evil but necessary by
reason of the precept for as long as it was in force.

It is true that the old law was a tutor leading to Christ, engendered bondage,
and so forth; Galatians 3 and 4. Yet it will never follow from this that the
actions done according to that law could not be called morally good. For he
himself will concede that even those actions which he says are moral in
themselves cannot, without faith in Christ, make a human being free or remove
the spirit of bondage unto fear. The things which belonged to the old law
were also καλὰ ἢ ὠφέλιμα τοῖς ἀνθρώποις, “good or beneficial to human beings,”
in their own category when they were performed properly and Evangelically
according to the intention of the law, just as the use of the sacraments and
of Christ’s other institutions is now. As for Philippians 4:8, which he bids
us examine, who does not see that certain things which, considered in their
species, are indifferent—and which he does not wish to be called morally
good—can also be referred to things that are venerable, pure, lovely, and of
good report?

In the sixth place he says: “The Jews are considered either as carnal or as
spiritual, according to Paul’s division in Romans 2 between the Jew who is
carnal and outward and the one who is inward and in the spirit. Ceremonies
were observed by the latter as signs, not as realities. The former, unable in
their wretched bondage to raise the eye of the soul to the light of eternal
truth, embraced the signs in place of the realities.” These statements are
true. But what then? “If,” he says, “these signs became moral by reason of the
precept, they ought to have been performed and embraced for their own sake.
Thus the outward Jew, and circumcision in the flesh and letter, would have
been the Jew—something appears to be missing here—and his praise would have
been not from human beings but from God, who cannot fail to delight in moral
observance.”

[1674 logical printed p. 181 | IA leaf 199 | PDF p. 199]

Reply: The consequence is invalid. They ought to have been performed and
embraced for their designated end, but not for their own sake; they were
enjoined to be done in this manner. Thus performed, they would have been
morally good by reason of the precept, and the praise of those who performed
them in this manner would have been from God. God would also have delighted in
this observance. See Genesis 8:21; Exodus 29:25; Leviticus 4:31 and 8:28;
Numbers 15:24–26; Ezekiel 20:40–41.

He proceeds on page 27 and argues as follows: “If a precept makes things and
actions truly moral, why did it not make them perpetual?” This question is
easily answered: because God did not see fit to enjoin such a thing either
earlier or for longer. But does he think it absurd for something which is
indifferent at one time afterward to become morally good when a precept is
added? He certainly does, for he speaks as follows: “It seems absurd for a
thing to have once been indifferent and nonmoral before the precept, afterward
to become moral when the precept was added, and then to become deadly when
the precept ceased, as with circumcision and similar things. How is God’s
judgment concerning the same thing so variable? It belongs to a wise person
always to pass the same judgment concerning the same thing, especially if the
thing depends upon his judgment so that it is good and just, or evil and
unjust. It is not so when he disposes at will of things indifferent in
themselves. Since that is what God did here, it is argued from this that those
things were neither good nor evil, but indifferent.” Reply: These statements
do not oppose us, but God himself, who enacts laws which will last only for a
time, but which human beings are nevertheless necessarily bound to obey for
as long as they remain in force. 2. Was eating from the forbidden fruit as
indifferent after the command not to eat had been given as it was before that
precept? If not, let him answer why God judged what had once been indifferent
to be necessary after the precept and therefore morally good. 3. He appears
here to imply that all the ceremonies are no more dead or deadly now than when
they were expressly enjoined. For he wishes saying this to be the same as
affirming that God’s judgment concerning the same thing is variable, which he
judges absurd. What then? Is it as necessary to be circumcised now as it once
was? Or does God judge it to be equally necessary now? If not, he will not be
Wise—far be the blasphemy!—for according to the adversary, it belongs to a wise
person always to pass the same judgment concerning the same thing. 4. If what
was once indifferent were always indifferent, the Apostle argued badly when
he said that, through the use of indifferent things in a particular case, the
laws of love are not observed, the one for whom Christ died is destroyed, and
the work of God is ruined; Romans 14:15, 20. He also spoke badly when he said,
“It is Good not to eat meat, drink wine, or do anything in which your brother
stumbles”; verse 21. For when he said that this was GOOD, did he not imply that
it was morally good and therefore not indifferent in that case? 5. It does
indeed follow from this that those things, considered absolutely or in
themselves and in their species, were not necessary or morally good. But it
does not follow that, considered here and now and after a law concerning them
had been enacted, they cannot be called morally good when done according to
the law, and evil when done contrary to it.

From these things it is clear that the statement which the adversary opposes
here was not the greatest of absurdities and a perversion and corruption of
the whole doctrine of morals, as he openly asserts on page 24. For when we say
that all those things are morally good which God commands to be done, when and
in the manner in which he commands them to be done, we say nothing absurd. We
do not corrupt the doctrine of Morals in any way, for we know how, this
notwithstanding, a moral law must be distinguished

[1674 logical printed p. 182 | IA leaf 200 | PDF p. 200 | ends before Periculosam tamen]

from a positive one. Nor is the adversary ignorant that the same word can be
taken in different ways, sometimes more strictly and sometimes more broadly.
