[1679 printed p. 98 | IA leaf 106]

When the apostle denies that we are justified by works of the law, does he
exclude only ceremonial works?

Here certain things must be stated beforehand and even freely conceded.

1. We have said that this dispute first arose and was initiated over these
ceremonial works and the observance of the ceremonial law, as is plainly
evident from Acts 15:1. Hence circumcision is mentioned so frequently in
Paul's argument, and all power to justify us is denied to it.

2. Jerome had rightly said earlier that the subject matter in the Epistle to
the Galatians is the same as in the one written to the Romans: namely, that we
are justified by absolutely no works of the law, whether ceremonial or moral.
Nevertheless, one may concede—as some of our writers as well as Roman
Catholics affirm—that the Epistle to the Galatians treats the ceremonial law
more specifically than does the Epistle to the Romans.

For this is what their troublers, the pseudo-apostles, imposed upon the
Galatians: the ceremonial law, namely, by compelling them to be circumcised
(chapter 6, verse 12).

The Galatians had been taken by this enchantment and led away from the truth
of the gospel. Turning again to “weak and beggarly elements,” they observed
days, months, seasons, and years (chapter 4, verses 9–10); that is, they had
again been subjected to the ceremonial law.

Hence, finally, the apostle so often mentions circumcision in that epistle,
and warns that, since it had now been abolished, it was useless and even
destructive.

[1679 printed p. 99 | IA leaf 107]

He was not content to say only once that “in Christ Jesus neither
circumcision nor uncircumcision avails anything” (chapter 5, verse 6), but
presses and inculcates the same point still further in the same words in
chapter 6, verse 15.

3. Third, we also freely concede that although moral works are excluded from
the rationale of our justification just as ceremonial works are, they are not
excluded equally, if the things themselves are considered.

In one respect the ceremonial law does indeed possess a stronger claim in
this matter: its ceremonies had been certain appendages—or rather types—of
the New Covenant made with us concerning justification and salvation in
Christ through faith. They shadowed forth the grace of the gospel and led the
Jews by the hand to the Christ who was to come (Galatians 3:24). This perhaps
explains why Philip Melanchthon, in his argument on the Epistle to the Romans,
not only truly said with Luther in his work on Galatians that “the apostle
excludes works of the law generally—not only ceremonial works, but absolutely
all, even works of the Decalogue,” but went further: “Paul recalls the
question, as the prophets often do, and shows what the use of the law is. He
does not speak only of ceremonies, but of the whole law and principally the
Decalogue.” Indeed, in this sense the apostle speaks principally about the
Decalogue and especially bars works of the moral law from our justification:
the Decalogue contains the covenant of works, by which we are certainly not
to be justified, whereas the ceremonial law shadows forth the covenant of
grace, by which alone our justification is accomplished.

[Marginalia: See Chamier, earlier volume, on Romans 3; later volume, on
Galatians 2; p. 306.]

Yet on other grounds the ceremonial law must be barred from justification
more than the moral law.

1. Consider the laws as they are in themselves. As Arminius has it,
justification is attributed to the moral law because the works it prescribes
are pleasing to God through themselves and in themselves, and are themselves
righteousness strictly and rigorously taken. Thus the one who did them would
by that very fact be righteous, without absolution or gratuitous imputation.
If it does not justify us—which indeed it certainly does not now—this occurs
not simply because of the law itself, namely the moral law, but because of the
weakness of sinful flesh: Τὸ γὰρ ἀδύνατον τοῦ νόμου ἐν ᾧ ἠσθένει διὰ τῆς
σαρκός, “what the law could not do, in that it was weak through the flesh”
(Romans 8:3). For this reason justification is denied to it only because the
law has not been fulfilled on account of the impotence of our flesh. It is
denied to the ceremonial law, however, because its works are not pleasing to
God through themselves and do not possess the following relation: that if
men adhered to them, as the Jews did, absolution would be obtained by them
from sins committed against the moral law, or that they themselves would be
graciously imputed for righteousness. Thus Scripture denies justification to
it, not because it has not been fulfilled, but simply because of the law
itself—the ceremonial law—not because of the weakness of the flesh.

[Marginalia: Public disputation.]

2. At least now the ceremonial law must be banished from justification more
decisively for this reason: under the gospel, after the abolition of the
Mosaic pedagogy and Jewish polity, it is not only dead but deadly. Luther, it
is true, says in his work on Galatians 2 that the Roman Catholics fabricate
this from their own head. But that should be attributed to excessive ardor
either of his mind or his pen. Or rather, as appears from what precedes and
follows there, he speaks partly of the ceremonial law insofar as it was
instituted by God and therefore good and holy, and partly of its use after
Christ was born, but not after the Jewish synagogue had been destroyed.
Otherwise we believe Paul rather than Luther, for Paul solemnly testifies
that Christ will profit those who are circumcised nothing, which is the
deadliest thing of all.

[Marginalia: Volume 5, pp. 245, 396.]

[1679 printed p. 100 | IA leaf 108]

The ceremonial law, I say, has now utterly vanished as regards obligation,
practice, and use. Indeed, it has no use except insofar as, by reading and
meditating upon it as a law now antiquated, we see fulfilled in Christ what
was shadowed forth in it. Thus we are led not by it, as the Jews once were,
but from it to Christ, as befits Christians.

The moral law is far otherwise. It still remains and will remain forever. It
indeed cannot δύναται ζωοποιῆσαι, “give life,” as the apostle speaks in
Galatians 3:21; it does not justify or merit life, as will appear later. But
it remains:

1. A rule of life, which the ceremonial law is now nowhere. It is not such a
rule to the Gentiles, upon whom God never imposed it, nor to the Jews, upon
whom it had been imposed only ἄχρι καιροῦ διορθώσεως, “until the time of
reformation,” and was then utterly to cease (Galatians 3 and 4).

2. A law which even now, by its commands, threats, and curses, drives sinners
under the gospel to embrace Christ and seek justification through him. In
that respect, although it can by no means justify us, it nevertheless serves
our justification in some manner. This can no longer rightly be said of the
ceremonial law. Among the Jews it once had a twofold use for justification,
corresponding to its twofold relation: first to the moral law and sins
committed against it, and then to Christ and faith in him. In the former
respect it was a bond sealing debts and sins; in the latter it contained the
shadow and type of Christ. In the latter respect it typically displayed
Christ; in the former it compelled men to flee to Christ. But under the
gospel it is now deprived of both offices. It can no longer be a type of the
Christ to come, since Christ himself has now come; and since it has ceased to
exist, it compels no one to seek Christ. A nonexistent thing has no efficacy.
The moral law, however, even now retains the same efficacy it formerly had,
if it ever properly had any, in advancing justification.

[Marginalia: Arminius, at the place cited earlier.]

It was never a type of Christ, much less can it be a type of him now born and
suffered. Yet just as it formerly under the law drove sinners by its threats
to Christ shadowed forth in those types and to justification obtained in him,
so also under the gospel, by making them guilty of sin and wrath, it stimulates
them even more sharply to embrace that same Jesus now plainly revealed.

But I do not wish to linger further over comparing these laws with one another
in this business of justification, since we must now demonstrate separately
against the Roman Catholics that both laws and the works of both are simply
excluded from the formal rationale of justification. First, then, not only
works of the ceremonial law are excluded, as some of them have thought.

I. Not only ceremonial works.

To establish this by several arguments, let us begin, if you please, from
what the apostle immediately adds in Romans 3:20 to the conclusion which we
are now to examine. He says, “Therefore by works of the law no flesh will be
justified in his sight,” and adds, “for through the law is the knowledge of
sin.” Knowledge of sin also comes through the gospel, just as knowledge of a
disease comes from its medicine; but the law does this μόνως—“for great is
the contrary”—principally, and instills knowledge of sin by wounding the
wound.

[1679 printed p. 101 | IA leaf 109]

From this explanation let us draw the first argument.

The apostle excludes from justification the works of that law through which
comes knowledge of sin. But knowledge of sin does not come through the
ceremonial law alone. Therefore its works alone are not excluded from
justification.

The ceremonial law indeed reveals sin and leads to knowledge of it, insofar
as its purifications indicate sin's foulness and its sacrifices the death due
to it. In this respect it is called the hostile bond in Colossians 2:14; those
annual sacrifices are also called ἀνάμνησις ἁμαρτιῶν, “a remembrance of sins,”
in Hebrews 10:3. Circumcision reproached them for their native uncleanness and
hinted at the cutting off which they deserved on its account, as our
paraphrast rightly notes on that passage. Yet the ceremonial law does this
only accidentally, insofar as it leads by the hand to Christ, the expiator of
sin.

[Marginalia: Hammond; Pareus.]

That law does not do this primarily, as Cameron thought. Certainly the
ceremonial law is not alone in doing it, for the natural law does so in Romans
2:15.

Indeed, it does not do it principally, for the moral law claims that office,
according to Romans 7:7: “I would not have known sin except through the law.”
What law does he mean? The words immediately following indicate it: “For I
would not have known that covetousness was sin unless the law had said, You
shall not covet.” This is the moral law, the last commandment of the
Decalogue.

[Marginalia: *On the Threefold Covenant*, thesis 68.]

Its violation is sin in the most proper sense and from the nature of the
thing itself. It binds not the Jews alone but absolutely all men, and makes
all its violators liable to eternal penalties. By everyone's judgment the
first two of these points, and by at least some people's judgment the third,
do not belong to the ceremonial law. Since this argument establishes that
knowledge of sin is principally engendered through the moral law, it follows
from the apostle's reasoning in that passage that not only works of the
ceremonial law are excluded from justification.

Someone may reply that although knowledge of sin does not arise from the
ceremonial law alone, it was sufficient for the apostle's purpose if such
knowledge arose from it, though not from it alone. His purpose was simply to
prove that no one is justified by it alone, since it reveals the sin for
which they are condemned.

I answer:

1. This argument of the apostle does indeed prove that no one is justified by
the ceremonial law alone. But I say that it does not prove this alone. It
equally, and even more strongly, proves that no one is justified through the
moral law and its works, since the middle term which he uses in that
argument—knowledge of sin—belongs equally, or rather more, as we have shown,
to the moral law than to the ceremonial.

2. I also answer that no one, at least no one against whom the apostle argues
there, ever thought that a man was to be justified by the ceremonial law
alone.

This supplies our second argument that the apostle does not exclude only
ceremonial works from justification. He argues here not only against Jews and
Judaizing pseudo-apostles, as we showed earlier; but they summoned not only
legal ceremonies and works of supererogation, but also moral works, to
justification. Thus the apostle says that they rested in the law

[1679 printed p. 102 | IA leaf 110]

in Romans 2:17. In what law? In the law which he had just treated in verse
15, whose work he says was written in the hearts of the Gentiles. This could
by no means be said of the ceremonial law. Indeed, the ceremonial law is
opposed to the law he treats there, or at least distinguished from it, in
verses 25–26: “Circumcision will indeed profit if you keep the law,” that is,
the moral law, “but if you are a transgressor of the law, your circumcision
has become uncircumcision.” Circumcision, which belonged to the ceremonial
law, is here plainly separated from the law in which he had earlier said the
Jews rested. Throughout that entire chapter, whenever law is mentioned—and it
is mentioned repeatedly—the name “law” means, or at least comprehends, the
moral law, though not that law alone.

This also includes what we read in Luke 18 about the Pharisee boasting for
his own justification, from which he nevertheless fell short in verse 14. He
boasted not only of the frequency of his fasts and payment of tithes, which
perhaps in some measure belonged to the ceremonial law by reason of his vow,
but that he was οὐχ ἅρπαξ, “not an extortioner,” not unjust, and not an
adulterer, which pertained to the moral law.

This may serve as the third argument. The apostle says in Romans 3:27 that
all boasting is excluded in the business of justification, and asserts that
this is not by the law of works but by the law of faith. It follows that all
works from which anyone was accustomed to seize an opportunity for boasting
are excluded. These were not only ceremonial works but principally moral
works. The privileges once granted to the Jews above the Gentiles were indeed
distinguished, as the apostle acknowledges in Romans 3 and 9, and ceremonial
things must be given some place among them. This is evident from the passages
which list among their prerogatives ἡ νομοθεσία, αἱ λατρεῖαι, and αἱ
διαθῆκαι—the giving of the law, the services, and the covenants—from whose
number ceremonial matters certainly cannot be eliminated. Indeed, we freely
acknowledge that the Jews, being always boastful and τελεταρχικοί by their
more frivolous disposition, vaunted themselves more insolently in the name
and pomp of their ceremonies. Roman Catholics do the same today, like
barbarous Indians who adorn themselves with feathers instead of clothes, or
like children playing with rattles. But those among them who were somewhat
wiser and had a more substantial understanding boasted in their moral works,
like the Pharisee just mentioned. Others, to have greater material for
boasting, did not truly pursue true and solid piety and charity, but
nevertheless pretended to do so. Since in the apostle's doctrine all boasting
is excluded from justification, his meaning excludes not only ceremonial
works but above all moral works, in which lies the chief material for
boasting.

Fourth, the apostle denies justification to works of that law which, once
violated, works wrath (Romans 4:15) and a curse (Galatians 3:10). This was not
only the ceremonial law, but principally the moral. For the latter passage
continues: “Cursed is everyone who does not continue in all things written in
the book of the law.” Ceremonial matters alone do not fill both pages of that
book; moral matters are left their own and indeed the chief place. As for
Deuteronomy, although we concede that God does not wish his positive and
ceremonial commands to be violated with impunity, we do not think that he is
provoked to wrath more if we transgress the Decalogue than if we fail in some
rites and ceremonies. Especially relevant here is that the law which the
apostle calls “the ministry of death and condemnation” in 2 Corinthians 3:7,
9 is said there to have been formed in stones

[Marginalia: Peter Martyr, in the preface to Romans.]

[1679 printed p. 103 | IA leaf 111]

—that is, inscribed on those tablets of stone. This plainly means the
Decalogue, not the ceremonial law, which Moses, not God, wrote in a book, not
on tablets of stone (Exodus 24:4).

Fifth, in Titus 3:5 the apostle denies that our justification and salvation
are ἐξ ἔργων τῶν ἐν δικαιοσύνῃ ὧν ἐποιήσαμεν, “from works in righteousness
which we have done,” that is, from works of righteousness or righteous works
which we have done. Now some ceremonial righteousness can be considered in
the ceremonial law itself. In this sense Christ said that it became him “to
fulfill all righteousness” (Matthew 3:15): the word “righteousness” means not
the virtue of righteousness itself but whatever duty is laid upon us by a
command, as a learned man noted on the passage. Although, I say, in this sense
there can be some ceremonial righteousness, nevertheless moral righteousness
is true and solid righteousness, according to the apostle's words, “He who
does righteousness is righteous” (1 John 3:7). Therefore in the apostle's
intention even righteous works of this kind, not only ceremonial works, are
excluded from justification. Whether this passage proves that only those
moral works which can be done by our powers of free choice without God's
grace are excluded—which Roman Catholics labor to carve out of the words ἃ
ἐποιήσαμεν ἡμεῖς, “which we have done”—we will have occasion to discuss when
we come to their second opinion. Meanwhile:

[Marginalia: Estius on the passage; Bellarmine, *On Justification*, book 1,
chapter 17, and Becanus, *Scholastic Theology*, part 2, tractate 4, chapter 2,
use this passage to prove the same point.]

Sixth, the single passage Ephesians 2:8–9 strikes the opinion we oppose with
a threefold battering ram: Τῇ γὰρ χάριτί ἐστε σεσῳσμένοι διὰ τῆς πίστεως,
οὐκ ἐξ ἔργων, ἵνα μή τις καυχήσηται, “For by grace you have been saved,
through faith, not from works, lest anyone should boast.”

1. Justification is attributed entirely to grace. The same apostle states
this still more strongly in Romans 3:24: Δικαιούμενοι δωρεὰν τῇ αὐτοῦ χάριτι,
“being justified freely by his grace.” He says not only χάριτι, “by grace,”
but also δωρεάν, “freely.” We are justified not only by grace, but freely by
grace—on two grounds, and thus altogether freely. Yet our justification would
be no more, indeed less, gratuitous if it were from moral works than if it
were from ceremonial works, for grace is opposed to merit and debt. A moral
work comes closer to the rationale of merit than a ceremonial work, since a
moral work is good in and of itself, whereas a ceremonial work has no natural
and intrinsic goodness in itself but possesses goodness only by positive
right and from the lawgiver's command and authority. Thus if justification
were granted to a moral work, it would be more from merit and less from grace
than if it were attributed to a ceremonial work.

2. Just as justification is attributed to grace alone in this passage, so it
is denied to works altogether by the words “not from works.” This is said not
restrictively, but indefinitely and generally. He does not say, “not from
certain works, such as ceremonial works,” but absolutely and universally,
“from none”—not even moral works or those flowing from grace and faith, as
remains to be shown later.

3. The apostle adds, “lest anyone should boast.” But we have already proved
that moral works furnish more material for boasting than ceremonial works.

Seventh, the point which Peter Martyr rightly noted also bears upon this. Once
Paul had shown that we are now free from the law by Christ's benefit, he
objects to himself: “Let us sin more freely, so that grace may abound more.”
If he had previously argued only about the ceremonial law and our liberation
from it, no occasion for making this objection would have arisen. Another
direct, ready, very easy, and most obvious answer—about which the apostle
never said or thought anything—would instead have been available.

[Marginalia: Argument 7.]

[1679 printed p. 104 | IA leaf 112]

No occasion, I say, would have been given to object anything of the kind if
he had proved only that faith in Christ liberated us from the ceremonial law.
No one unless utterly bereft of reason could argue from that in the following
way: “Let us therefore remain in sins”—that is, “Let us therefore sin in moral
matters because we are liberated from ceremonies.”

Even if someone were so unsound as to make such an inept objection, the answer
would have been easy and obvious. Any simpleton could immediately reply that
it was a fallacy: Paul had proved only that we are liberated from ceremonies,
while we remain bound by the moral law not to sin with impunity.

But the apostle, who knows that his objection has a very different meaning,
also supplies a different answer suited to his proposition: namely, that we
who have been justified from sin are not free to sin any longer, because,
having been justified through Christ, we have been grafted into that same
Christ, and are therefore dead to sins, since we were buried with him.

“Since the apostle had truly proved that we are freed from the moral law as
regards deriving justification from it, it was easy for impiety—not right
reason—to object: Since we are free from the law, let us sin freely, because
with impunity. The apostle refutes this objection not by distinguishing works
preceding faith from those following it, which would have been required under
the Roman Catholic hypothesis, but by declaring that sanctification follows
justification.” Thus Tilenus.

Eighth, the apostle treats the law which forbids moral vices: covetousness in
Romans 7:7, murder, deceit, cursing, and similar things in Romans 3:10–20.
These pertain to the moral law. Therefore the apostle excludes its works from
justification.

[Marginalia: Argument 8.]

Furthermore, there was no reason for the apostle to labor so hard to prove
that a man is not justified by works of the ceremonial law, since it would
have been not only ungodly but utterly absurd for anyone to expect
justification from ceremonial works while being supinely and broadly
negligent of moral ones. I would not deny that there were then some Jews mad
enough in their impiety—as there are too many among us today, especially
among Roman Catholics—to regard themselves as the most blessed of all,
provided they had duly performed ceremonies, though they were most criminal
violators of all the moral commands. The Savior himself charges the Pharisees
with this, because they tithed mint, dill, and cumin while neglecting τὰ
βαρύτερα τοῦ νόμου, “the weightier matters of the law” (Matthew 23:23).
Nevertheless, we must observe that these hypocritical Pharisees did not apply
paint with bare ceremonies alone, but advertised themselves by displaying
rather long prayers, alms, and other moral works of that kind. Otherwise it
would have been more than absurd for impious violators of the moral law ever
to expect justification from bare ceremonies alone, since:

[Marginalia: Argument 9.]

1. Ceremonies were merely appendages of the moral law's first table, just as
political laws were appendages of the second. What could be more childish or
less useful for justification than to pursue appendages, minutiae, and
circumstances of this kind while treating with neglect or even contempt the
moral works which concerned the sum and substance of the matter? This accords
with the apostle's words, “If you are a transgressor of the law, your
circumcision has become uncircumcision” (Romans 2:25).

[1679 printed p. 105 | IA leaf 113]

2. The one circumcised was a debtor to keep the entire law (Galatians 5:3).
Consequently no justification was to be expected from circumcision or other
ceremonies without that fulfillment of the law.

3. If, as our opponents admit, the apostle proved that no one is justified by
ceremonies or ceremonial works, which pertained to the first table of the
law, he seems thereby to have proved—as Peter Martyr rightly infers—that much
less can anyone obtain righteousness by works of the second table. Therefore
one cannot obtain it by moral works.

[Marginalia: In the preface to the Epistle to the Romans.]

Finally, the following proves ἐντεῦθεν, from the foregoing, that the apostle
excluded both moral and ceremonial works from the rationale of justification.
In Romans 3:20 he said Διότι ἐξ ἔργων νόμου, “Therefore by works of the law no
flesh will be justified in his sight,” and again in verse 28, λογιζόμεθα,
“we infer,” or “we conclude by syllogism that a man is justified by faith
apart from works of the law.” In both clauses this is manifestly not a new
topic, as Cornelius a Lapide wants in verse 20, but a conclusion and an
inference from the premises and preceding argument, as that same Cornelius
admits at least in verse 28. Therefore the right understanding of the
conclusion will be most clearly established by properly weighing the
premises. Otherwise the apostolic syllogism would contain four terms and his
whole argument would labor under equivocation. If the premises dealt not with
the ceremonial law alone but principally with the moral, then certainly the
conclusion, if not principally, must at least be understood equally of the
moral and ceremonial laws. What, then, were those premises? What was the
apostle's entire argument in chapters 1–3, from which he finally drew this
conclusion again? It was this: all men, Gentiles in chapter 1 and Jews in
chapters 2–3, are violators of the law. He argues as follows: If all men,
Gentiles as well as Jews, are transgressors of the law, no flesh—that is,
neither Jews nor Gentiles—will be justified in God's sight by works of that
law. For no justification is to be expected from the law whose violation
entails condemnation. But he had proved that Gentiles in chapter 1 and Jews
in chapters 2–3 were violators of the law. Therefore they cannot be justified
by works of that law.

[Marginalia: Argument 10; Romans 3:20.]

What, then, finally, was the law whose violation makes all Gentiles and Jews
guilty there?

It was not the ceremonial law—either not at all, or at least not principally:
not at all with respect to the Gentiles and not principally with respect to
the Jews.

It was not the ceremonial law at all with respect to the Gentiles. Paul there
treats a law common to Jews and Gentiles, by whose violation he says he had
proved both Jews and Greeks to be under sin (Romans 3:9) and the whole world
to become ὑπόδικον, liable to God's condemnation (verse 19). But this was the
natural moral law, to which Gentiles were subject just as much as Jews. Since
the ceremonial law was never given to Gentiles, they cannot be prosecuted as
guilty of its violation: “where there is no law, there is no transgression”
(Romans 4:15). Nor does it appear anywhere in the entire preceding argument,
or elsewhere, that the apostle blamed the Gentiles for not observing the
ceremonial law.

It was not principally—indeed, not at all in that entire preceding
argument—the ceremonial law with respect to the Jews. For in all three
chapters the apostle does not indict the Jews for violating the ceremonial
law: not for omitting sacrifices, of which God was satiated (Isaiah 1:12–14),
nor for neglecting or lightly esteeming

[1679 printed p. 106 | IA leaf 114]

circumcision, which they pressed excessively in Acts 15 and in which they
boasted more insolently. Thus neither Jews nor Gentiles are prosecuted in the
preceding argument as guilty of violating the ceremonial law.

What, then, must be thought about the moral law? It is the very law whose
transgressors the apostle proves both Jews and Gentiles to be. He proves this
of the Gentiles in chapter 1 from verse 18 to the end, where he weaves a very
long catalogue of sins committed by them against the moral law. They are
prosecuted as idolaters, “filled with all unrighteousness, wickedness,
fornication,” and similar things. He also involves the Jews in guilt for
violating the same law in chapters 2 and 3. He does so partly confusedly and
generally, calling them transgressors of the law which he distinguishes from
circumcision in chapter 2, verses 23, 25, and 27. This was the moral law. He
also does so specifically and by name, charging them with theft, adultery,
and sacrilege in chapter 2, verses 21–22, and in chapter 3, verses 9–10 and
following, with deceit, cursing, murder, and the other moral sins, citing
testimonies for this from sacred Scripture. After he had shown in verse 19
that all these things were said about the Jews, he immediately concludes in
verse 20: “Therefore by works of the law”—the moral law, namely, whose
transgressors he had previously proved both Gentiles and Jews to be—by works
of this moral law, I say, even I may safely conclude that “no flesh is
justified in God's sight.” Hence in this Pauline argument justification is
denied to works of this law, not merely to works of the ceremonial law. ὅπερ
ἔδει δεῖξαι—what had to be demonstrated.

Sins and iniquities are opposed to justification (Romans 4:6–7), but these
have a place not only in ceremonies. Peter Martyr on Romans 4:6, p. 101.

In Philippians 3:6–7 Paul rejects that righteousness of the law according to
which he had been blameless; but he had been blameless not only concerning
ceremonial matters but also moral ones, and thus all parts of the law. See
Zanchi on the passage, pp. 191–192.
