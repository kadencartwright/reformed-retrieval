CHAPTER XIII.

Verse 1. A wise son [listens to his father’s instruction, but a scoffer does not listen even to any rebuke.

In the word בן there is a synecdoche of species: sons stand for all those who are subject to another’s dominion and power; likewise sons stand for children of either sex. Similarly, in the word אב there is a synecdoche of species for all those who hold authority in the household, the civil sphere, or the Church. Yet by these words those whom the proper sense of the words denotes are to be understood especially.

In the former half-verse there is an ellipsis of the word ישמע, which must be supplied ἀπὸ τοῦ κοινοῦ from the latter half-verse, just as בן must be supplied ἀπὸ τοῦ κοινοῦ from the former half-verse.

Praise, which people bestow upon those who remain in their duty, is accustomed to apply strong spurs to obedience. Therefore the glory that God bestows upon human beings ought to kindle them as much as possible to obey those to whom obedience is due. Since, then, God regards as wise all who obey those set over them, especially their parents, it is a mark of great faithlessness for people to pursue the empty scrap of glory that comes from human beings and not embrace the true esteem that comes from God. Although there were many epithets with which God could adorn obedient children and others who are subject to another’s authority, he nevertheless chose this one badge of virtue, because it was the most fitting for the present subject. For since all people naturally trust in themselves and their own abilities, and nearly all adopt this opinion of themselves—that they seem able to govern themselves admirably—Solomon, in order to meet this vice and corruption of nature, defines the wisdom of those who are subject as consisting in submission and obedience. Therefore he does not say that the son who listens to his father is good, or kind, or well-born, or grateful, but that the son who does so is wise, and so forth.

By the word *instruction* he understands not only precepts and lessons, but also chastisements suited to impressing the precepts more deeply upon the mind, as is most clearly evident from the antithesis of the latter half-verse. The sense, therefore, is that a wise son accepts his parent’s chastisement, even when it is hard and harsh and of whatever kind it may finally be. For most people are found to bear equally well a gentle chastisement applied with a light arm, but they reject one that is rather more difficult and severe. They do indeed admit the chastisement that is given in milder and gentler words; but they refuse one that has some bitterness. They calmly accept the one given in words, provided blows are absent; but they reject and spurn the one that adds blows to the bitterness of words and threats.

Lessons, especially those that concern eternal life, ought to be so dear and welcome to us that it is fitting to embrace them with both arms when they proceed even from our equals—indeed, even from those standing in an inferior position. How much more is this so when we are instructed and commanded by those who hold authority, and most of all when these things are urged upon us by parents, who hold us most dear and whom it is fitting that we embrace more closely. And they not only listen to parents who correct them properly and according to the prescription of the word, 1 Peter 2:18, but do not snarl at parents who exceed due measure, Hebrews 12:10. Yet obedience is not commended to us in such a way that it is not bounded by certain limits and barriers. For we must always remember the Apostle’s statement: [obedience must be rendered in the Lord.]

[1632 witness | PDF p. 199 | printed columns 372–373]

From this it is clear that it is a father’s duty to admonish and correct his children, and consequently the duty of all who have command of affairs to chastise those subject to them, both with words and with blows, if the matter so requires, so that they may keep them in their duty or restore to good conduct those who depart from it. Neither through carelessness nor through excessive indulgence must they allow those persons to loosen, tighten, slacken, or draw up the reins of their lives at their own pleasure. Nor should they suppose that they have done enough if they have imbued them with the precepts and lessons of piety and justice. Who would doubt that David and Eli did this abundantly? Yet because they added either no chastisement or one that was not just and due, they gave them cause for ruin and destruction both in this age and in the age to come. A good many parents, especially masters, think that they have discharged their duty admirably if they provide their children or servants with food and clothing. If they add lessons as well, then they applaud themselves wonderfully for having fulfilled their office. But from this passage and very many others it is evident that something more is required.

In the latter half-verse he sets the scoffing son opposite the wise son, and briefly defines who that scoffer is. It is one who does not listen even to his own parent’s chastisement delivered by a word, or bear it with equanimity, whereas the wise person refuses no chastisement inflicted upon him, whether by words or blows. The scoffer will indeed hear lessons delivered universally and in general terms, Isaiah 1:5, 6. Moreover, if there is something in which he is praised, he will seize upon it readily enough. But when statements made generally are applied to him by way of blame, he immediately grows hot and rejects everything through impatience. See Acts 7, how they bore Stephen, as he delivered a long speech, calmly and quietly for a considerable time, as far as verse 51. See Acts 22, down to verses 21 and 22. What is more, they not only refuse to receive such things with an even temper, but attack those who chastise them with words. See Proverbs 9 and Matthew 7. See Exodus 2:13, 14. Yet a person who once or twice rejects reproof or rebuke, or rises up against the one correcting him, must not therefore be considered a scoffer and immediately abandoned. For Asa, who is adorned with the testimony of a godly king, once struck against this stone, 2 Chronicles 16:7, 8, 9. Rather, the scoffer is the one who does this persistently and obstinately, and not without mocking those who correct him. See Matthew 18.

Verse 2. By the fruit of his [own] mouth each good person enjoys fruit, but the soul of the faithless enjoys [the fruit] of violence.

In יאכל (*has eaten*) there is a metaphor transferred from eating to enjoyment, because a person enjoys what he eats and possesses it. In the word נפש, *soul*, there is a synecdoche of the part for the human person. And the verb יאכל is to be repeated ἀπὸ τοῦ κοινοῦ from the former half-verse. In בוגדים, *of transgressors*, there seems to be a synecdoche of genus, for it is restricted to that transgression which is committed with the mouth and tongue.

When the Apostle was about to make an anatomy of human depravity in the members of the body, he dwelt more upon this one instrument of speech than upon all the other parts of the body. For he was not content to expose that depravity from the single instrument of speech unless he examined all its parts: [their throat is an open grave; they misuse their tongues for deceit; the poison of an asp is beneath their lips; their mouth is full of cursing and bitterness.] Therefore it ought not seem strange if the Holy Spirit labors so greatly in reforming and polishing that member, partly by applying gentle promises to be fulfilled toward those who govern their tongues rightly, and partly by thundering threats if anyone, with the reins loosened, does not restrain the tongue’s insolence.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 200 | printed columns 374–375]

And first he marks the reward of the lawful use of the tongue by a contrast with a tree, which produces fruit not for itself but for others, whereas the person who uses tongue and speech well and lawfully will himself receive the fruit he bears. Next, in the case of the fruits of trees it more often happens that one person plants and tends the garden while another carries off the fruit; but the person who governs the tongue well receives fruit that no one can snatch away from him. Finally, trees render fruit to farmers once a year, whereas the proper use of the tongue is continually fruitful in the good things in which the person who uses it rightly rejoices. The fruits produced by pure and rightly ordered speech are of various kinds—unlike trees, nearly all of which produce fruit of only one kind—but one, and the principal one, is that which Christ expressed: *by your words you will be justified*, Matthew 12. Nor is what the Apostle commends in Romans 6:22 a slight matter: ἔχετε τὸν καρπὸν ὑμῶν εἰς ἁγιασμόν, τὸ δὲ τέλος ζωὴν αἰώνιον, *you have your fruit unto sanctification, and the end, eternal life*. Since the recompense for pure and uncorrupted speech is so generous, and since nothing among human beings is accounted easier than to sow words and ply the tongue, our perversity of nature must be remarkable, since we do not obtain things of such value with such slight labor.

The soul of the faithless—or, the faithless will eat violence—means that they will be violently oppressed; it is in the same sense as the latter half-verse of verse 6 in chapter 10 of Proverbs. It is as though he should say: just as one who uses the tongue rightly will feed upon good things welcome and desirable to the one who feeds, so one who misuses it for insolence will be overwhelmed by evils that will be very unwelcome and bitter to him, and which he would never admit unless compelled. See Matthew 12: *by your words you will be condemned*. Besides this, very many are found who, foolishly blurting out things which he said less, have breathed out those same things against their own lives. See the example of Adonijah seeking Abishag in marriage, and of the Amalekite who reported that Saul had been killed by him.

Verse 3. He who guards his mouth preserves himself, but for him who opens his lips wide, destruction [comes].

In the verb פשק (*stretching wide*) there is a metaphor—nè—from the ugly stretching of the lips to signify a shameful excess of words, not unlike the one found at 4:24, where he illustrates perversity of words by distortion.

This proverb has a close kinship with the proverb found at 10:19, except that the order is reversed, because what is commended there in the latter half-verse is placed here in the former. It also has no small affinity with the immediately preceding proverb. It seems to differ only in this: while in the preceding proverb he praises pure and uncorrupted speech and condemns its contrary, in this passage he commends sparing use of the tongue and declares excessive use to be deadly.

The verb נצר (*guarding*) has the sense of restraint; see 7:11. He seems quite suitably to compare the mouth to the gate of a city, because of all the parts of the body it imitates gates: now it is opened, now closed, now unlocked, and soon barred, just as happens with a city’s gates. By that very fact he compares the rest of the person to a city. Therefore, just as in a city besieged by enemies it is of the greatest importance that the gates be closed or opened at the proper time, and that people take anxious care lest anyone of doubtful loyalty enter or depart, so also in diligent guardianship of the mouth, in timely speech or silence, is established the sum and, as it were, the chief point of a person’s safety. James 5:1, 2, 3 wonderfully enlarges and confirms this part of the verse. Therefore pains must be taken so that, just as watchmen for guarding a gate

[1632 witness | PDF p. 201 | printed columns 376–377]

are appointed, so we should strive with God in prayer, together with David, that he appoint his Holy Spirit to guard the mouth, for he alone is fit to govern it.

For no one, however vigilant or well advised—whether through some natural dexterity or through instruction in learning and letters—knows how to govern his mouth in such a way that his soul does not suffer eternal loss from the depravity and excess of speech, unless God through the Holy Spirit undertakes the care of governing it. What is more, very many who were judged wise according to the flesh have made themselves liable even to a human tribunal because they let the tongue rush forward unawares. More seriously, there has been no lack of godly and faithful people who, indulging their passions too greatly and neglecting the watches of the Holy Spirit, cast themselves into grave danger to their lives through wicked words. See the example of David in the oath by which he bound himself to the slaughter of Nabal and his whole household, 1 Samuel 25. See also the example of Jacob, who rashly vouched on behalf of his entire family that Laban’s idols had not been taken by theft, and by that very act made Rachel liable to death, Genesis 31:31.

In the latter half-verse he teaches the grave danger that awaits those whose mouth is opened too wide. For just as a city whose gates are not guarded is easily captured by an enemy, so also, and so forth. It must be observed that, in the word פשק, by which he denotes the stretching and opening wide of the lips—a metaphor transferred from bodily things to the soul—Solomon signifies the ugliness and deformity of corrupt, perverse, and especially excessive speech. For since everyone shrinks from one who draws apart and opens his lips wide, as from an uncivil and unmannerly person, they will all the more justly do this toward one who exceeds due limit and measure through loquacity. The expression is similar to the one we noted above at chapter 4.24.4.12, where he compares perversity of speech to a twisted and deformed mouth. Thus in this word lies an argument from the accompanying disgrace that follows immoderate speech. For just as the opening wide of the lips is hateful and shameful to civilized people, so profuse speech is regarded as unwelcome and hateful by God and the blessed angels, and finally by well-advised human beings.

The second argument by which he restrains an excessively loose mouth as with a curb is the harm that follows from it, wholly contrary to the benefit received by one who restrains himself in his speech. For just as safety both in this life and in the future life arises from the latter, so from profusion of speech we are not merely hurt and wounded, but utterly torn apart and crushed. For since chapter 10:19 says that defection or transgression will not be lacking in a multitude of words, it ought not seem strange if sin goes ahead as the body and crushing or fracture follows as its members. It seems scarcely credible to people that words, which are uttered and brought forth with such slight labor, possess so great a force as to wear a person down and crush him, especially since nothing seems lighter or softer than words. But Solomon notes that a great weight inheres in them, either for preserving us in life or for punishing us in death.

Moreover, when he says that a person excessive in speech brings crushing upon himself, this is not without emphasis. It is as though he should say that it often happens that even those in whose ears the words resound perish through harmful speech. But if that does not happen—as sometimes it does not—those who transgress in speech nevertheless never fail to bring destruction upon themselves. The same thing must also be understood in the former half-verse: even if through moderate use of speech we do not benefit others, we will nevertheless deliver our own souls. See Ezekiel 3:19 and 18.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 202 | printed columns 378–379]

Finally, it must be observed that nearly all who are a little more judicious labor diligently at this and apply effort vigorously enough, lest anything escape them in speech that would make them liable to human beings. Yet few are found who, when they present themselves for conversation with God or are admitted to it, are concerned to govern their words prudently and cautiously before his majesty. This must be done especially, whether we consider the majesty of God or ponder the gravity of the danger that flows from such rashness and thoughtlessness in speech. Therefore Solomon in Ecclesiastes 5 especially rouses us to that caution in speech. It must be maintained altogether that the wisest and best-advised persons in human judgment sometimes sin toward human beings in speech in such a way as to bring grave harm upon themselves, because in praying to God they had been so undisciplined and inattentive.

Verse 4. The soul of the sluggard is moved by desire, but nothing [will be present]; the soul of the diligent will be made fat.

In נפש (*soul*) there is a synecdoche of the whole, for the whole soul is put for the soul’s faculty of desire. In the verb תדשן (*will be made fat*) there is a metaphor from bodily obesity to the soul’s satiety.

From the latter half-verse it is understood that his desire is occupied with food and delicacies, although it is not inappropriate if we understand here a synecdoche of species. By the word עצל (*of the sluggard*) is denoted one who exercises himself in no lawful occupation, whether he devotes himself to no honorable craft or, having professed a craft, neglects to practice it. Therefore the idle are not only those who sit doing nothing, but also those who, as busybodies, run here and there and make themselves troublesome by doing other people’s business and πάρεργα. In 1 Timothy 5 these same women are called ἀργαὶ and περίεργοι. What sort of class of people this is we learned above from chapter 6, where most of both its effects and its adjuncts are graphically depicted. In this passage, however, he attributes this effect to it: that it is frequent and excessive in seeking or desiring. The causes of this are indeed not to be despised, because when labor is cast off the desires are let loose, though they would be restrained and, as it were, reduced to order by doing some work. For since people are so constituted that they must do something, it comes about that, when they cast away their labors, they rush forward to frequent feasts. Next, when those wearied by heavier occupation contract a hunger by which they find black bread and all the cheapest foods welcome, the idle who grow torpid in leisure have a prostrate appetite and therefore seek more exquisite foods. Hence the prophet Ezekiel joins fullness of bread and gluttony with the leisure and idleness of the Sodomites. And the Apostle in 1 Timothy 5 charges with wantonness the young widows who wandered about idly. And certainly, if we attend even a little to the behavior of the idle and are willing to confess what is true, we will observe no people who reveal this disease of gluttony by their talk and foolish wishes more than the idle do. For tasteless wishes often burst out from them: “O that we had this or that food or drink! O how fortunate were the Israelites, for whom manna was sent down from heaven every day and who were showered with quail!” They also belch forth wishes concerning other good things—for example, that they were learned or knowledgeable, or skilled in this or that craft—but they nevertheless relate all those wishes to the belly or the throat, so that they may stuff themselves that much more conveniently with food and drink, not because they seek those excellent gifts of God for their own sake.

The effect of this desire is then taught: namely, that no good comes from it to fulfill their desires, but rather a great deal of discomfort and torment. For just as desire fulfilled is to a person

[1632 witness | PDF p. 203 | printed columns 380–381]

is most pleasant; so a desire in which a person feels himself frustrated torments his soul with anguish. The idle man’s having nothing comes about for a twofold reason. First, neither bare desire, as in this passage, nor words and speeches alone, as later at 14:23, contribute any more toward acquiring sustenance than if someone expected offspring or children from the wind, or seed-corn from chaff. Hence the proverb commonly celebrated among us: “Wishers and woulders are no good householders.” Second, God’s command supports this doctrine: “He who does not labor, let him not eat,” 2 Thessalonians 3:10; and that statement in Genesis 3, “In the sweat of your face you shall eat here.” Finally, there inheres in the minds of most people a certain innate hatred of the idle, so that those who, out of love toward God, do not cut off sustenance from the idle will nevertheless do this out of a certain innate hatred of idlers.

What is said here about those who are sluggish and idle in procuring daily sustenance is said much more truly of those who delay and are idle in procuring the bread of life. No one is so estranged from his own mind that he does not desire to be saved and to attain heavenly life; and to obtain that thing he repeatedly hurls forth, not prayers, which he does not know, but wishes. Yet just as the idle man eagerly desires food and nevertheless shrinks from the means by which it is acquired, so it also happens in those who rot in their corruptions. To both groups the saying celebrated in the old proverb fits not unsuitably: the cat is held by a desire for milk or fish, yet will not endure wetting its feet either with milk or with water.

But the soul of the diligent, who persist attentively in invocation, will be made fat. There are many sayings in Solomon by which the diligence of the industrious is sharpened, as though by goads, for doing honest work. By this, as though by an argument from the contrary, the first part of this verse is also confirmed. For although this is established neither in anyone’s care nor in his diligence, but in God’s blessing, nevertheless God for the most part follows human labors with this blessing, even the labors of those who are estranged from the covenant, much more those of his own people who seek his glory.

But in those who are industrious and diligent in seeking the kingdom of heaven, this is found to be much more true. The blessing of God sometimes deserts those who are diligent and assiduous in preparing bodily sustenance; but it is never absent from those who pursue heavenly nourishment.

Verse 5. A righteous man hates a false thing, but it makes the wicked man stinking and ashamed.

In the word דבר (*word*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct, the utterance standing for the thing comprehended under the utterance. In יבאש (*will make stinking*) there is a metaphor transferred from bodily things that hasten putrefaction to incorporeal things, namely falsehood, which makes the people who employ it no less hateful than putrefaction makes food. In יחפיר (*will cast down the face*) there is a metaphor transferred from diggers to those who, suffused with a blush because of sin, lower their head to the earth, like those who dig the ground. A *false thing* signifies every departure and deviation from truth, whether by words or by deeds, brought out of the darkness of thoughts into the light. Solomon affirms that every such thing is hated by the righteous man, that is, by him who, freely justified by God, cultivates righteousness and upholds it according to his slender ability and manly share. For since God’s love has been poured into the hearts of the righteous, it cannot happen that, upon sensing God’s love toward themselves, a reciprocal love toward God is not immediately kindled in their souls; this love so binds them that everything opposed to God they

[1632 witness | PDF p. 204 | printed columns 382–383]

pursue with hatred. Hence that saying, “You who love the Lord, hate what is evil,” Psalm 97:10. Since, therefore, it is agreed that God is true and is truth itself, it necessarily follows that falsehood, which is hostile to him, is disagreeable and hateful to the righteous.

Here it must chiefly be noted that the sum of righteousness consists not only in someone’s restraining himself from falsehood, giving it no service, and expressing it neither by deed nor by word, but in his being so disposed in soul that he regards it as hateful and carries a mind, if not steeped in hatred for it, at least dyed with it to some degree. For there are many who refrain from frauds because they dread either eternal or temporal punishment, because they hope that the avoidance of falsehood will earn them the reward of eternal life as their due and just desert, because they fear disgrace, or because they court popular favor. But since all these things can be performed by one who does them without being moved by any hatred of falsehood, it follows most strongly that he is to be judged unworthy of the name of righteous. Therefore, to obtain the praise and true and genuine testimony of righteousness, we must labor above all that, kindled with love of the truth, we may detest and oppose falsehood, its contrary and enemy. Therefore let us search ourselves, and let us test ourselves by this Lydian stone, so that we may examine whether we are truly righteous or rest merely in a mask of righteousness.

I will say something further: namely, that a surer judgment of true righteousness may be sought from your hatred of falsehood, even though you sometimes stumble into it and do not always express that hatred in deeds, than if you always abstain from falsehood yet are not borne against it in hatred. David, who sinned in this respect not seldom and not lightly, must be preferred by countless parasangs to those who never transgressed in deed but did not restrain themselves because they were not moved by hatred of it. Hence the Apostle testifies that he did the evil which he hated, but did not perform the good which he desired. Provided, then, that we hate evil, stand armed and at war against it, and diligently turn aside either its ambushes or its assaults, it is not fitting for us to be dismayed if we are sometimes intercepted or circumvented by the snares and μεθοδείαις (*stratagems*) of sin and the devil. Nor, therefore, should we reckon our condition worse than that of unbelieving and ruined people, who in their own judgment and that of other people are free from those sins to which we have been liable through the weakness of the flesh.

But it must further be noted that this hatred of falsehood ought to be directed not only against the sins and crimes of others but also against our own. You may sometimes see people who immediately blaze up at the lapses of others but are excessively favorable and indulgent toward their own. See 2 Samuel 12:5; see Judah’s judgment against Tamar, Genesis 38:24. In the latter half-verse he confirms this very thing from the effects of the love of falsehood upon the wicked: the love of falsehood makes them stink like a privy and dung and, by that very fact, makes them ashamed. For just as flies blowing upon flesh make the flesh stink, Ecclesiastes 10:1, so sin by its stinking and impure breath makes the person who practices it stinking and festering. For just as the virtues of the godly are likened to beds of spices, Song of Songs 4, because they emit an exceedingly pleasing odor in the nostrils of God and of the blessed spirits, so, conversely, the stench of vices is disagreeable and hateful to God and all good beings. Indeed, there are some kinds of falsehood that are exceedingly displeasing even to the wicked themselves, in whom sparks of honesty survive in our corrupted nature. What is more, sin not only produces a figurative and transferred stench by its own foulness, Genesis 34:29, but a true, plain, and genuine stench when it brings on foul and malodorous diseases, as we read of David, Psalm 38:6 and following, and in Job 19:17. From this it also follows that the wicked

[1632 witness | PDF p. 205 | printed columns 384–385]

are suffused with a certain shame when they see that people shrink from them. Consider whether this word, which properly signifies digging the ground, does not by a metonymy of the adjunct signify shame, because one who is ashamed lowers his head and face to the earth as though about to dig the ground. But this shame is not always joined with true repentance; indeed, it often happens that it drives them headlong into despair, as may be seen in Saul, 1 Samuel 3:4, and in Ahithophel, 2:23. Now, since we so revere people—even wicked people—that we are ashamed for them to see our nakedness, how much more fitting it is for us to be ashamed under God’s gaze, before whom even our most deeply hidden sins are naked and laid open. From this it is also inferred that the pure and honorable conduct of life belonging to the righteous gathers favor and a kind of pleasing fragrance for them among people, and causes them never to be suffused with shame because of a life sincerely lived.

Verse 6. Righteousness guards the way of the upright; but wickedness overturns the sinner.

In צדקה (*righteousness*) there is a metonymy of the subject for the adjunct: God’s defense and protection, which is the inseparable companion of righteousness. Likewise in רשעה (*wickedness*) there is a metonymy of the subject for the adjunct: God’s vengeance and armed hand, which pursues the wicked.

People are wonderfully deterred from the pursuit of godliness and righteousness because they perceive in their minds that their integrity is exposed and liable to the hatred and ill will of many, and, together with hatred, to violence. For those who walk in darkness oppose the light of a piously and honorably ordered life, John 3:20; 1 John 3:12; Ephesians 5, since it tacitly reproves their foulness. And although this opinion does not deceive them, since all who desire to live godly in Christ Jesus will suffer persecution, 2 Timothy 3:12, nevertheless most or all of us sin grievously in this: when we consider the dangers and evils threatening us on account of godliness, we either do not recall God’s defense and the forces that aid us against the assault of the wicked, or certainly weigh them with too little attention and vigilance. What other cause should we assign for this carelessness and stupor of ours except that the eye of the flesh by which we see things that will harm us is sharper and keener in seeing than the eye of the mind and Spirit by which we ought to behold the hidden defense stored up for us in heaven? We see the forces drawn up against us; we do not continually discern the heavenly battle line that is on our side.

Solomon resists this temptation. He does so not by denying the danger born and flowing from righteousness, but at the same time adds that righteousness is not alone but attended by a great company of divine aid; indeed, righteousness itself is like garrison troops that keep watch lest the person who is upright and uncorrupted in every course of his life suffer any loss. Thus the righteousness which to us and in the judgment of our flesh seems death-bearing and destructive shines upon us as salutary and saving. Psalm 25:21: “Integrity and uprightness will guard me, because I wait for you.” For just as a well-equipped, numerous, and fortified bodyguard is assigned to a prince’s son sent out of his father’s house, and as in wartime a father does not send his own people out safely without a guard, so let us not fear that this bodyguard is insufficiently powerful and equipped to rout and put to flight every hostile force. See 2 Kings 6:16, 17; Psalm 34:8; Psalm 81:11. Bring in here those most generous promises which stand in Job 5, from verse 17 to 24. And although the scourge of the tongue is a grave thing and we are attacked by slanders, righteousness will vindicate us from their bites. Although for a time slander may spread darkness over us and our reputation, nevertheless righteousness, like the noonday sun, finally scatters all those little clouds of slander.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 206 | printed columns 386–387]

Therefore let us confidently and courageously enter the straight way and stand in it steadfastly and fearlessly. Let us not turn aside from the integrity once begun and undertaken, either to the right or to the left, because of any terrors. Let us keep the royal road and not allow ourselves to be led away into the byways and paths of wickedness.

But someone may object that by cultivating righteousness a person descends into conflict with many wicked people, with whom he will have to struggle anxiously, although he may be certain of finally fighting his way free. Consequently, the thought may quickly enter his mind that the life of the wicked is preferable and more desirable, as being free from those evils to which the godly are liable. Solomon meets this temptation when he says that wickedness alone—although there are many others besides who lie in wait for the wicked—will utterly overthrow the wicked. See Psalm 34:22: “The face of the Lord is against the wicked”; 1 Peter 3:12. Angels also drive onward those who stand upon a slippery way and pursue them, Psalm 35:5, 6. Indeed, although no one lays a hand upon them, they are nevertheless often dismayed by their own consciences and tremble at the fall of a leaf, Leviticus 26:36; Proverbs 28:1.

From this it is clear that the wicked call destruction upon themselves by their own agency and that they are the authors and parents of their own unhappiness. Although they usually divert their evils onto others, and indeed onto God himself, transferring the cause of their destruction to God’s decree and counsel, Scripture nevertheless teaches that it comes from nowhere but their own sins.

Verse 7. There is one who boasts himself rich, when all things are lacking; likewise one who pretends himself poor, whose substance is abundant.

Solomon censures the vanity of people destitute of the Spirit, or certainly only lightly imbued with him. The latter kind of person, though praised by most or certainly not hated or detested as he deserves, is just as hostile and displeasing to God as the former kind, who falsely pretends to have riches. Not unlike this is what he earlier set forth concerning those who conceal their anger and preserve their vengeance so as to execute it when the opportunity comes, and those who, being unable to restrain their anger, immediately disclose it. Although most people admire the former as prudent, God nevertheless hates him no less than the man who, through impatience, immediately breathes out threats and insults. Of the same kind is Proverbs 17:15: “He who condemns the innocent and he who justifies the wicked are both abominable to God.” The person who pretends to be rich when nothing is at hand for him lies; the person who represents himself as poor when he is rich does the same. The former continually lies against God by affirming that he received from God what God did not give; the latter lies against God by denying that he received from him what he did receive. Both are enemies of divine glory. The poor man is an enemy because he bears unjustly the condition assigned to him by God, though it is fitting that he should be grateful to God even for poverty; the rich man because he cannot praise God for this bounty if he does not acknowledge it. Each is injurious to other people: the former by contracting many debts that he cannot discharge; the latter by withholding from the poor, to whom something ought to be dispensed, from the magistrate, to whom taxes are to be paid, and even from God himself, whom it is fitting to honor and worship with riches. Finally, they harm themselves whether we consider this life or the future one. For each destroys his soul by a prolonged and continuous lie. When the former’s fraud is disclosed, as it cannot long be concealed, he incurs everyone’s hatred and contempt. The latter, in order more readily to hide and cloak his pretense, even cheats his own comfort and neither clothes himself

[1632 witness | PDF p. 207 | printed columns 388–389]

nor feeds himself as comfortably as his means allow. The source and origin of both evils is an immoderate love of riches and the fact that we esteem riches, which are least among God’s gifts, as great. The cause of the former is that he attributes so much to riches that he determines no life to be worth living without them. The cause of the latter is that through love of riches he separates himself from those duties of love and humanity for whose sake those riches were granted and bestowed on him by God, Luke 16. Therefore this love of riches must be diminished and brought back to moderation. That will happen if we acquire true riches, the fear of God, which will cause the lack of riches not to be grievous and burdensome to us and their abundance not to make us dry and barren for the duties of love, though it ought to make us ready and eager for them. For godliness is πορισμὸς μέγας (*great gain*), 1 Timothy 6:6–9. Likewise, let us consider and imitate that statement of the Apostle, Philippians 4:12, 13 and 2 Corinthians 6:10.

This proverb extends more widely, for it can be transferred neither unsuitably nor violently to spiritual riches. In these, pretense is no less common than in earthly riches and is equally hateful to God as that former pretense of which we have spoken—or is even more hateful by as much as the former riches must be preferred far above the latter.

People sin more rarely and sparingly by pretending spiritual poverty. Few are found who profess themselves less rich than they truly are. Yet some who incur guilt even here are not lacking. First, times arise when people, judging that danger is being created for them from God’s spiritual gifts, not only dissemble those gifts but display things entirely contrary to them. David was guilty in this kind when he feigned madness in the court of King Achish. Many also, exalted by ambition, for this reason deny their skill in heavenly matters, so that through this denial they may extort from others fuller and more abundant praise and commendation of themselves. There are also those who, spread through with envy, suppress their gifts and will not bear to contribute them to the common good. Further, there are those who, through laziness and flight from labor, conceal the gifts of mind they have received from God and allow them to gather rust. Finally, there are those who, deceived by error, think more abjectly and humbly of their gifts. They do this in two ways: either through a certain drowsiness and stupor of soul, because they have never examined God’s liberality toward them rightly and with just weights; or because they have been driven headlong into ὀλιγοψυχίαν (*faintheartedness*) by a tempest of temptation. Although the former people of whom we have spoken are more blameworthy, these latter are also not free from reproach. They all transgress that rule of the Apostle by which it is commanded φρονεῖν εἰς τὸ σωφρονεῖν, “to think toward sober thinking,” Romans 12:3. But among those who proclaim themselves richer than they are, one kind consists of those who are well aware of their own poverty, yet display their riches, stores, and resources in spiritual things; such people are almost infinite in number. For nearly everyone seeks the praise of godliness, though they wholly cast away godliness itself; and, what is worse, they prefer to seem rich rather than to be rich, contrary to what happens with earthly wealth. Indeed, not even the godly themselves are so free from the stain of this sin that they never proclaim more of their own godliness than is true. Others, however, think that they are rich, being deceived by error, although they are not. These include not only those who lie submerged in some heresy, but also those who profess the true religion. See Revelation 3:17, 18. The Apostle in Romans 12 convicts all these of sin when he exhorts them μὴ ὑπερφρονεῖν, “not to think too highly.” But for all of both kinds—both those who speak or think more abjectly of themselves than is fitting and those who are puffed up beyond measure in the mind of their own spirit—this treatment and medicine is from the Apostle.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 208 | printed columns 390–391]

it is prescribed that each person should think of himself according to the measure of faith he has received, and according to the gifts of the Spirit that have been given through faith.

Verse 8. A man’s riches are the ransom of his life, but the poor man does not hear rebuke.

Although, in the Proverbs from chapter 10 to chapter 30, I do not join the opinion of those who pursue a perpetual connection and continuity among the sayings, neither do I agree with those who affirm that all the Proverbs are mutually disparate and are joined by no affinity of consequence or connecting bond. For, as in some other places, so here it is more manifest than to require proof that the preceding proverb is held by some bond to the one we now have in hand. For since, through zeal for and love of riches, both the rich man and the poor man lie to themselves about their own condition, this proverb teaches how equably both the absence and the desire of wealth are to be borne, since riches are often possessed not without danger and desired without discrimination. It cannot be denied that riches have their advantages in this life, and poverty its disadvantages. But we err in this: to diminish our love of riches, we do not ponder among ourselves the disadvantages of riches; and, to bear the burden of poverty more equably, we do not consider the advantages of poverty. Instead, with all that we are, we are carried away either to calculate the advantages of riches or to weigh the losses of need. Therefore Solomon in this proverb meets our heedlessness in this matter and treats the disadvantages of riches together with the benefits of poverty. He does not, however, search out the disadvantages of poverty, which all people of their own accord turn over and scrutinize in their minds; thus he touches not upon the advantages of riches but upon their difficulties, just as he does upon the good things of poverty, leaving a fuller consideration of both to the meditation of the godly.

Christ’s testimony against the possession of riches is weighty: it is difficult for the rich to enter the kingdom of heaven, and therefore—if we assess the matter in human terms—it is ἐν τῷ ἀδυνάτῳ (*among the impossible things*). From this arose the Apostle’s statement that few rich persons belong to the hope of the heavenly kingdom, 1 Corinthians 1:26. That testimony should deservedly extinguish our ardor for riches, unless there are some who are not held by a desire for eternal life. And even if that were not so, yet if there is someone led by zeal for the present life who desires to pass it either long or peacefully—as is usually dear to almost everyone without exception—it would be advisable for him not to place much reliance upon riches for attaining that end. For it often happens that a rich man, brought into danger of his life, is ruined in respect of his goods, and what he gathered by wrongful labor is transferred to another possessor. In that danger the rich man will think himself well dealt with if he can redeem his life at the cost and loss of his whole substance, Jeremiah 41:8. Indeed, it often happens that snares for his life are set on account of his riches, and that wealth is like a trap treacherously placed to catch the rich man’s life. See 2 Kings 20:13, 17, and 1 Kings 21. We are taught the truth of this by common experience. For when the houses of the rich have been undermined or broken open by night thieves, their wealth and everything precious is carried away. Nor does this happen only when the bars of the laws have been broken. It often happens that those who abound in wealth and have made themselves distinguished by splendid buildings are, under the name and pretext of law and statutes, violated in the name of those same laws by people who envy them this abundance of every possession. They are not only stripped of their goods but are further deprived of life, lest perhaps, if they survived, they should seize an opportunity to recover their lost

[1632 witness | PDF p. 209 | printed columns 392–393]

wealth and dwellings. For just as thieves cut off a finger adorned with a gold ring when they cannot easily draw the ring from the finger, so rapacious and violent people, when they suppose that riches cannot be stolen from their rightful owners unless the owner is killed and crushed, enter into counsels for his ruin and destruction. And—to embrace briefly what could be discussed more fully—if a rich man stumbles or commits a crime punishable by death, inquiry is immediately made against him with exactness. But if he is free from crime, charges are nevertheless often fabricated, in whose name the rich are dragged to death unless they redeem their life with wealth. Indeed, the rich sometimes do not rescue themselves from death even by their riches, because violent people go so far as to ensure that the surviving owners never return to the wealth lost and taken from them. From these things, therefore, it is clear not only—as the Savior argues—that a man’s life does not consist in his having much, but that by having very much he often hastens the end of his life. Those who value riches so highly that they think no life can be worth living without them therefore act without due consideration. At the same time it follows from this that human desires fight and conflict with one another. For while people desire riches so as to live comfortably, it happens that through the heaping and amassing of riches they lose not only a comfortable manner of life but life itself. Therefore, if at some time the Lord strips us of riches through some mischance, it is some consolation that by this means the present life is, with some likelihood, to be prolonged for us. Yet let us remember Solomon’s saying that wisdom is good together with an inheritance, Ecclesiastes 7:11: some are safe beneath the shadow of riches, but the shadow in wisdom is more excellent, and wisdom preserves its possessor better than riches do. See also Proverbs 11:4.

But what is written in Psalm 49 seems to contradict this proverb. There the Holy Spirit expressly denies that anyone can redeem his life by wealth, even when infinitely increased; yet here Solomon declares that a person’s life can be protected by the aid of his riches. The answer, however, is readily at hand. David denies that anyone can exempt himself from death when God has summoned him to death; in this passage it is only indicated that, by paying a price, one can escape the death that people attempt to inflict. David says that death in general is inescapable; Solomon affirms that the danger of death can be turned aside for a time.

In the other half-verse we are taught that poverty and lack of possessions not only create no danger to life, but that the poor are generally free and exempt even from taunts and disgrace, or rebuke. First, the poor, by reason of their scantiness, are not so exposed to graver dangers. Next, no curious inquiry is made into the manner of life of the poor. Moreover, if they commit some offense before people who know of it, those people nevertheless make no great account of the matter. For they think the poor are already sufficiently wretched merely through their poverty, so that no further harm should be added to them. Hence it happens in the Scriptures and other histories that, when there is a universal falling away from duty, the leaders among the people are punished while the remaining guilty persons escape unpunished, Numbers 16:15. Finally, most people are moved by a certain perverse compassion and readily pardon them their crime. And just as the noble deeds of the poor are readily passed over, Ecclesiastes 9:15, so judgment upon their crimes is not so severe. For, like those situated in a valley, they are safe from the storms to which the rich are exposed. An excellent demonstration of this doctrine appears in the Babylonian captivity. When the leaders, the rich, and the craftsmen had been carried into captivity, poor farmers were left in their own country, so that their condition during the captivity was better than it had been when that kingdom had not been reduced to servitude. Therefore, the poverty imposed upon us

[1632 witness | PDF p. 210 | printed columns 394–395]

let us learn to bear moderately and patiently, since these good things are contained in it: it often prepares a way for us both to acquire the future life, Matthew 11:5, and to preserve the present one, as is confirmed by this proverb of Solomon.

Verse 9. The light of the righteous will be bright, but the lamp of the wicked will be extinguished.

In this proverb there is an allegory, or sustained metaphor. First it occurs in the words אור (*light*) and נר (*lamp*), by which a desirable condition of life is represented. Next it occurs in ישמח (*will rejoice*) and ידעך (*will be extinguished*), the former signifying continuation and increase, the latter decrease and destruction. Furthermore, in the verb ישמח there seems to be a metonymy of effect, as if *will rejoice* were put for *will gladden the righteous*. Of this kind is the expression *the meadows smile*, and most of the expressions near the end of Psalm 96 appear to be of the same kind.

The purpose of this proverb seems to be to teach that only by righteousness and innocence of life is a good and desirable condition of life made firm and stable. By this righteousness the good condition of a man, however slight and obscure at first, shines and increases more and more. By contrast, the light of the wicked, even if otherwise most brilliant, will be turned into unhappiness and darkness.

The light in this passage is the illumination of the soul by gifts of the Holy Spirit, especially those that are directed toward eternal life and glory as their end; next, it is the external goods of body and fortune. This light is nourished by innocence and incorruption of life as by the purest oil, so that it perishes neither by night nor by day and, as if by bellows or a fan, is made greater. For integrity of life, like a succulent root, transmits so great a power of moisture and richness that flowers and verdure which otherwise would wither and fall become, through this transmitted abundance of richness, like palms and olives, which retain their leaves in winter and do not permit their own greenness to perish or be scorched away. Hence it is that in Proverbs 4:18 the path of the righteous is said to be situated in shining light, a light that grows and advances as far as the noon of day—that is, until it reaches full and absolute perfection. This is completed when, having finished this life, they pass to the blessed dwelling places. And as for those gifts that are joined with eternal salvation, they possess such firmness that they never perish and do not grow old together with those who possess them. Instead, like palm trees, all who have been sown and planted in God’s house bear more fruit even in old age. Other goods—whether of soul, body, or fortune—when joined with innocence of life, last longer than those possessed by the wicked. Moreover, when they are removed and taken away from the godly, they have as their successor a supplement and aid of spiritual gladness by which that loss is amply compensated, [since all those things work together for good for those who love God.]

By contrast, every kind of the wicked’s light, within and without, melts like the fat of lambs. Hence it also happens that their condition is worse than if they had never been illuminated by any light. For just as those who walk at night, refreshed for a time by the aid of a light against the nocturnal darkness, become sadder after the light is extinguished, so do those who for a time used prosperity to soften life’s troubles when they are afterward deprived of it. And though they retain some light and gladness because they are alive, it is nevertheless taken away by death: for their glory does not descend with them, Psalm 49. See Job 18 and 21. Hence it is that in this passage also the happiness and gladness of the righteous is compared to the light of the sun, which endures; but the happiness of the wicked is compared to a lamp, which is quickly consumed.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 211 | printed columns 396–397]

Although what is written in Proverbs 4:19 seems to conflict with this proverb—for there all the wicked are said to be in darkness, while here a lamp is attributed to them, though only one extinguished in a brief moment—the answer is that these testimonies do not conflict. The light that is in the wicked is not true light; rather, in their greatest light they dwell in darkness. On this see 1 John 2:3: the one who does not have love walks in darkness. By contrast, light arises for the righteous in the midst of darkness, Psalm 97.

Verse 10. By pride alone a man produces strife, but wisdom is with the well-advised.

It is established that pride is in itself a grave and hateful crime, since it neither thinks honorably of God, whose gifts in oneself it does not acknowledge, and is insolently disposed toward other people. But beyond the evil it carries and cooks within itself, pride is inclined to inflict injuries upon others. From those injuries quarrels and strife arise, and from strife murders also spring forth. Therefore a proud person openly strikes against the sixth commandment, and whoever resolves in his mind to observe it must hate elevation of spirit. That pride produces quarrels and murders is clear from Zephaniah 2, where Moab and Ammon are said to have heaped curses and blasphemies upon the people of God from the pride of their own spirit. It is also clear that Haman’s insatiable thirst for blood—which he could quench only by the destruction of all God’s people—flowed from no other source than pride, Esther 3. See also Psalm 10:2, where the fact that a wicked person persecutes even one who is afflicted is attributed to pride. This was the cause and seed of both murders that Joab planned, one against Abner and the other against Amasa. This was also the cause of frequent disputes among the Apostles, Matthew 18:1, 2, 3, as is plainly established by the contrary medicine for it, namely submission of spirit. If you seek the cause of this matter, it is readily at hand. On the one side, the proud man makes almost no inquiry into his own sins and failings but is an excessively keen examiner of his virtues and gifts. On the other, he is found to be a severe judge of the sins of others and a scarcely equitable appraiser of the gifts with which they have been endowed. The cause of this perverse judgment both of himself and of others was assigned above: namely, too little diligent and constant exercise in the knowledge and study of the divine word, Deuteronomy 17:18, 19, 20.

But in the little word *alone* there seems to be no small force for magnifying the crime of pride, and it will be useful for us to weigh it. The sense is that pride alone and of itself, without any other person’s aid, will kindle contentions and strife. You may see very many who do not rush into strife and contentions unless they have been goaded to them by the sting of anger, overpowered by immoderate drink, provoked by injustice, or disturbed and disquieted in spirit in some other way. The proud man alone, when no occasion has been offered and no name or pretext has been put against him, rises in words to insults, from which quarrels and murders arise. The clearest example of this appears in the tribe of Ephraim, which, elated and swollen with pride in its own multitude, quarrels with Gideon over the very matter for which they ought to have been grateful to him, Judges 8:1. But it is clearer in chapter 12:1, 2, 3: when, though called to battle, they had not come, they nevertheless quarrel with Jephthah because they had not been called, though he could justly have reproached them for not coming when they were called. Therefore, although every wrongful contention is blameworthy, the one that flows from the swelling of pride merits graver rebuke, because, assisted by no incitements to strife,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 212 | printed columns 398–399]

it breaks forth, sounds the trumpet in the midst of deep peace, and calls people to arms. Since the Prophet David was well aware of this, he rightly resolved within himself that a proud person was by no means to be borne in his family, Psalm 101.

Therefore, so that we may pass a peaceful and quiet life, let us first take pains to drive this leaven of pride from our minds, and then resolve to drive far from our dwellings all who are suited to sowing strife.

In the latter half-verse it might have seemed proper to say, *But the humble and submissive in spirit avoid contentions or do not thrust themselves into strife.* Solomon, however, leaves this to the reader’s understanding, since the reader can hardly miss what is set before his feet, especially since Solomon had taught the same thing previously in chapter 11:2. In his wisdom he substitutes the effect and cause of a submissive spirit, so that the proverb might be rendered ἐπιστημονικώτερον (*more scientifically expressed*) and laden with the lessons of more recondite learning. First, therefore, he sets forth a noble effect of a humble and submissive spirit: a well-advised mind, that is, one which does not immediately seize an offered opportunity for dispute and, like smokeless wood, catch the very first spark of contention thrown at it, but debates and reasons within itself, first whether it is right to contend over the matter presented, and then whether it is expedient to do so. Moreover, it employs as counselor not only the civil laws—as most wicked people do—but those laws that David by his example taught us to employ, namely, God’s statutes, Psalm 119. An excellent example of this appears in Judges 20, where, although the Israelites were about to wage a just war against Benjamin, they nevertheless did not undertake that war until they had first admitted God into their counsel. See the example of Gideon, Judges 8:2, and of Abraham, Genesis 12.

From this it is clear that proud people are likewise rash and headstrong, indulge their passions, and do not govern the matter by counsel or take counsel from God or from well-advised persons.

But Solomon identifies wisdom as the cause of a humble and submissive spirit. Wisdom restrains luxuriant passions and administers its affairs by reason, not by a blind impulse of the mind; in this way Solomon not obscurely accuses the proud person of foolishness, just as in our own language we are also accustomed to call him *A proud foole*. Although by the word *wisdom* in this book is often understood a true knowledge of God, which is the mother of humility, that humility produces mature deliberation opposed to haste.

Verse 11. Substance from vanity will be diminished, but one who gathers by hand will increase it.

In הבל (*vanity*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct: *vanity* stands for a most vain craft, occupation, or exercise of the mind or body or of both.

In יד (*hand*) there is a metaphor of the cause for a useful and necessary craft and knowledge that is generally accomplished by the assistance of the hand. Although vain arts too are practiced largely by hand, this is nevertheless not done so much or so often, nor with so great a power and exertion of the hand.

By this word יד, therefore, we are taught that not only thefts and robberies are forbidden, but also those crafts which, although practiced by bodily labor and without violation of the civil laws, nevertheless contribute no labor and industry either to the honorable preservation of human society or to the support of the Church; indeed, they are often even found to be harmful. The Apostle expressly indicates this distinction among occupations in Ephesians 4:28, where he may appear to have alluded to this passage of Solomon. For he teaches that it is not enough that a person should not steal unless he also works something good with his hands. Although of these crafts

[1632 witness | PDF p. 213 | printed columns 400–401]

The number is almost infinite, yet it can be reduced to certain classes. First, then, there are the curious arts—not only necromancy, but also judicial astrology and the investigation of the philosophers’ stone—by which countless people have blown their resources away into smoke. Next there are the arts of luxury and artificial embellishment, to which people enslave themselves like abject bondservants in order to satisfy human desires. Of this kind are those who weave women’s cyclades and false hairpieces, actors, parasites, wandering musicians, those who carry wild beasts about for exhibition, tightrope walkers, and so forth. But the foremost and principal place among these is held by those who serve the worship of idols: painters and sculptors of images, and countless others concerning whom John speaks in Revelation 18:11 and Acts 19:24. Most people devote themselves to these arts. The first reason is that they are generally profitable. For people will pay more for the aids that serve to fulfill their base and dishonorable desires than for things intended for honorable use. Next, they are driven to them by love of leisure and devotion to idleness, because—as this proverb teaches—little of the trouble and labor which people flee is expended in practicing them.

Nor in this passage alone are magical and unlawful arts censured; those who practice honest arts but abuse them for dishonest purposes are also rebuked—for example, tailors of clothing who accommodate themselves to everyone’s wishes, and likewise painters, makers of statues, and so forth.

Now, as for the statement that what they acquired by smoke and false coloring goes away into smoke, God brings this about in many ways. For it often happens that through the proclamation of the word, as in Acts 19 and Revelation 18, the baseness of these things becomes known to people. Thus things which were purchased for a great price are accounted utterly worthless and either burned in the fire or buried in a dung heap. Sometimes it also happens that they are forbidden by the prescription of good and wholesome laws, so that the merchants of these wares are compelled to lament their lot. But the chief means, and the perpetual one, is that God by his curse scatters goods amassed unlawfully and crookedly. For if possessions acquired by lawful trades will not endure when they are accumulated with contempt for the worship of God, Haggai 1:2, or at other people’s expense, much less will those acquired unlawfully endure, which God permits neither to be contributed to his worship nor to be spent for the relief of human beings. And certainly, if we consult experience as our teacher here, she openly declares that craftsmen of this kind, although they gather much with very little labor, will always be poor, will often be pressed by want, and will even be utterly oppressed.

In the latter half-verse there is an outstanding promise by which the prophet strengthens and confirms hands that are slack and feeble for undertaking labor. This also receives force from the Apostle’s addition of a particular promise. For he teaches that the person who honestly seeks his livelihood with his own hands will advance so far that he can even relieve the need of others who suffer hunger. Ephesians 4. We have encountered promises of this kind before, at chapter 10:4, 12, 24, 27. See also the promise in Genesis 3: *You shall eat bread in the sweat of your face.* Therefore, in choosing a way of life, let us first consider whether the one we decide to profess is honorable and lawful. To do this the better, let us examine how useful it is to the commonwealth or the Church. Next, let us inquire which craftsmen are most scarce for maintaining the commonwealth and the Church, so that we may supply what is lacking. Finally, let it come especially into our deliberation whether, by reason of God’s inward as well as outward gifts, we are fit to practice the craft and occupation.

Verse 12. Hope prolonged makes the soul sick, but a desire that arrives is a tree of life.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 214 | printed columns 402–403]

In תוחלת (*expectation*) and תאוה (*desire*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct.

The sense of this proverb lies in common understanding and is set before our feet, so that it is unnecessary to labor to extract it. We must therefore see what use and fruit it has. First, then, we are taught not rashly and without counsel to promise ourselves good things for whose attainment there is no just cause, no certain reason, no base, and no foundation. For although at first a brief and momentary tickling of pleasure may enter the mind while we are soothed by some expectation of enjoying the thing desired, yet continual anxiety joined with grief follows that fleeting and momentary joy and makes the soul bleed. This anxiety grows greater day by day until, as time advances and we fall away more and more from hope, we at last cast away completely the hope we had conceived. Absalom and Adonijah, who set their hearts on obtaining the kingdom of Israel, doubtless experienced no moderate torments of mind, because, after being driven from their hope several times, they were finally disappointed in it. The same thing is readily evident in the godly and religious disciples of Christ when they sought primacy and chief rule. First, because, elated by this lust for dominion, they fell into frequent disputes, which bring grief with them, and that over things which would never be accomplished; and afterward, because they were disappointed in this hope—see Luke 24:17, 21. It will therefore be advantageous for us, in order to avoid sorrow and anguish and to fill our minds with solid joy and delight, to be occupied with expectation and desire for those things which God promises in his word. What he pronounces with his mouth he effects with his arm. Of this kind are God’s favor, the fruits of divine favor, and the destruction of the wicked—things which God most certainly performs and in whose performance he makes no delay. See Luke 18 and Habakkuk 2. In these matters no desire and no expectation, however vehement and intense, is blameworthy.

It remains that we take thought not only for ourselves and our own peace by restraining our minds from vain or vicious expectations and raising them to things that are certain and profitable, but also that we take care not to grieve others by defrauding them of things promised or owed. Therefore, in bestowing beneficence, we must beware lest we be like those clouds which produce a hope of rain but, because of their lightness, are driven about by the winds and vanish into nothing; see Proverbs 3:27–28. But we must especially take care to pay at the appointed and proper time those things which are justly owed and are therefore sought and expected by their rightful owners. Here it is especially necessary to see that the hired worker’s wages do not remain with you overnight, James 5:4. If you keep his pay with you, you not only fail to compensate his labor but also inflict upon his soul a pain more bitter than the labor itself. In all these matters, as in all other things of this kind which we have described, it is of no small importance—both for bridling vain desires and for expecting and seeking the things that belong to our duty—that the prophet says the soul is weakened by vain expectations, but, on the contrary, is refreshed and, as it were, brought to life by just and lawful desires. Thus one who makes vain promises is found to sin not only against the ninth commandment but also against the sixth, for the unbridled desires of the mind are like executioners by whom the human soul is tortured and made to bleed.

But if a deferred desire makes a person faint, how much more in hell will the desire of the wicked torment those who desire relief from evils which they will never receive.

Next, we must be occupied not only with those things

[1632 witness | PDF p. 215 | printed columns 404–405]

through desire and expectation for what we can attain; at the same time we must see that those things are good and lawful. Otherwise we will be tormented not only by delay and postponement of the thing; often, after obtaining and attaining it, we will be more distressed than we were when we did not yet possess it. For the bite of conscience, which follows sin as a shadow follows the body, brings more sorrow and anguish than the enjoyment of momentary pleasure can compensate—to say nothing of God’s dreadful judgment, which accompanies unlawful and forbidden pleasures. See the history of Amnon concerning Tamar whom he violated. See also how God rained quails upon the Israelites according to their desire, yet sent wasting leanness upon them, and so forth. For just as the insane and the fevered become worse through the use of the things they crave when they obtain them, so also those who seek things forbidden by God, after gaining and partaking of them, bring no small injury upon themselves.

Next, account must be taken not only of the desired things themselves, but also that desire should preserve due measure and not be too intense. For every swiftness is delay to an inflamed desire, and every delay, however long, is delay to one who hastens.

Verse 13. The despiser of the word—that man will be corrupted; but he who reveres the commandment is himself granted peace.

In the words דבר (*word*) and מצות (*commandment*) there is a synecdoche of the genus for the word and commandment of God. For that God’s word and commandment are meant here is gathered from the contrary reward of the one who reveres the commandment, since there is one God who blesses us with peace.

First in this passage it must be noted who is rightly and properly said to hold the word in contempt. A despiser of the divine word is anyone who turns away from observing it in order to obey himself and his own will. For although by violating the word he may provide for his advantage, his pleasure, or even his life, he must nevertheless be accounted among the despisers of the word or commandment. See Luke 14:18, 19, 20, 26. See also Genesis 25:31, 34, where it is plainly evident that Esau held his birthright in contempt, although he sold it to his brother so that he might keep himself alive; see Matthew 13:44, 45, 46.

Therefore those who for some slight little pleasure or a trifling three-obol advantage abandon their duty and cast the commandment behind their backs are deservedly held guilty of contempt for the word and commandment. One ought not readily to turn away from a prince’s decree unless this is done because of the oath that must be rendered to God; how much less ought one to turn away from God’s decree? Ecclesiastes 8. Next, it must be observed that he says the word must not be held in contempt and the commandment must be pursued with reverence. For not only is that person guilty who subordinates the whole law or all God’s commandments to his own will, but also one who subordinates even a single commandment or a single little branch of one commandment. See James 2:10–11. This refutes those who, in order to exempt themselves from the number of the wicked—although they give no consideration to some particular commandment—loudly claim their innocence in the other commandments.

Corruption shall be his; that is, he brings destruction upon himself. For if a king avenges the violation of his commands, how much more will God do this? And if a king does what he wills, how much more will the King of kings do this? And if wherever the king’s decree is, there are dominion and power, how much more will this be true in God’s word? See Ecclesiastes 8:3–4. Therefore, to the sword of a prince who commands unlawfully let us oppose God’s brandished, poised, two-edged sword, in comparison with which the swords of kings are rushes and reeds. Likewise, to the advantages of which kings may strip us, let us oppose the benefits of which God may deprive and dispossess us. But that such a person perishes, see Luke 14

[1632 witness | PDF p. 216 | printed columns 406–407]

and Genesis 29. For one who strikes against the word strikes against Christ, 1 Peter 2:8. Next, it must be noted that he perishes to himself. For although by rejecting the word they suppose that they inflict insult upon God, God’s ministers, or even the preached word, nevertheless what is said here is true: they bring destruction only upon themselves, as in Luke 7:30 and Jeremiah 7:19. Nothing departs from God’s word, nor do the ministers whom the wicked desire to afflict with insults lose anything, since they will receive just as great a reward for their ministry as if faith had been placed in them; much less is anything taken away from God. Rather, all their endeavors return upon them to their own destruction.

What it is to despise the word will become clearer from the antithesis of revering God’s commandment: we must place its observance before every threat of everyone and before all the flatteries and blandishments of whomever they may be; see Isaiah 66:2 and Ecclesiastes 9:4. By this we are taught how to obtain testimony of a true and unfeigned fear of God—namely, if we fear and revere the commandment.

But when people are driven by fear from obedience to the word, lest by obeying the word they fall from peace and prosperity, Solomon teaches the contrary: peace is established by reverence for the law. [There is no peace for the wicked,] Isaiah 57:23, 24, 25. To this also pertains what is so often repeated in the book of Deuteronomy: obedience to God’s commandments is therefore commanded of the Israelites so that it may be well with those who observe them.

Verse 14. The teaching of the wise is a fountain of life, for departing from the snares of death.

From chapter 10, verse 11, it is evident that the person here called wise is called righteous there. Thus one who, though justified by faith, does not practice righteousness is not to be regarded as truly wise. All the rest, whatever appearance or reputation for wisdom they may possess among any people, are not to be held in the catalogue or census of the wise, but rather must be numbered with the foolish and insane. All the more detestable, therefore, is our perversity: pursuing the reputation of painted wisdom proclaimed by vain people, we contemptuously pass by the wisdom which is true and genuine and approved by the wise God.

Since no one has speech so faultless and incorrupt, nor a mouth so circumcised, that he does not sin in words, the words of the proverb must be referred to the teaching which flows from a wise and righteous person insofar as he is wise and righteous. It is too well known to need proof that David, Israel’s sweetest psalmist, sometimes spoke unwisely and rashly blurted something out. And when Christ is set apart, no mortal will be found from the beginning of the world who has not repeatedly slipped with his tongue. Hence the person who does not offend with his tongue is accounted perfect and complete in every respect, James 3.

Next, it is worthy of observation that a man who lacks righteous and incorrupt speech is thereby convicted of not being righteous or wise. *I believed; therefore I spoke*, Psalm 116. As the man is, so is his speech, 2 Kings 9:11. For although all believers are not, as formerly, adorned with new tongues—that is, with the languages of various nations—nevertheless this much is found to remain true: all who have been imbued by God with true faith have imparted to them a new tongue, that is, purified speech, and cleansed lips sent down from heaven. Thus it may not inappropriately be said, *Show me your faith from your words.*

He illustrates the effect of pure speech by a metaphor drawn from living or perennial waters, whose sense is approximately this. Just as cold, perennial water wonderfully restores and refreshes one exhausted by thirst and heat, so the well-trained and fitting speech of a wise man refreshes both the speaker by the consciousness of duty performed, and the hearer by its sweetness and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 217 | printed columns 408–409]

wholesomeness, and cheers him. Hence it is not difficult to know how valuable a righteous man is in a city. Everyone proclaims the pleasantness and healthfulness of a city which abounds in fountains and aqueducts of perennial water; how much more that of a city which abounds in good and righteous men. This is especially worthy of proclamation if the place is deserted and thirsty. Therefore, in this world, as in a desert and wasteland, it is worth the effort to seek out good and righteous men. In southern regions people expend much and great labor and, at great cost, search out and dig down to springs. Why then should we not labor still more to seek out righteous and wise men? It was accounted rustic, uncivil, or barbarous when the Philistines stopped up Isaac’s wells with earth cast into them; much more so when wicked people endeavor to destroy righteous and wise men.

The excellence of the teaching of a righteous or wise man is established from its particular end or effect: turning us away from the snares of death which the world, the flesh, and the devil lay for us, and with which the ways of the wicked are in a certain manner paved, so that they cannot set down even one step free and clear from danger of a snare. Here, to add a spur to the embrace of this teaching, he indicates that no slight injury hangs over us—not captivity or bondage, not the dislocation or breaking of a foot or arm, not the loss of the head, but death itself; and not only momentary death, such as the separation of the body from the soul, but the most bitter death, by which a person, his entire self in every respect, is cast headlong in soul and body into the abyss of hell. Snares are set for us as for birds, and ambushes are laid daily. The same thing happens to us which is said of the thrush: the thrush excretes its own evil. For we are caught and held as with birdlime by the sins which we ourselves commit. Nor is there any art by which we may avoid sin’s enticements and the tricks and devices of the devil, that cunning fowler, unless we fly to the teaching of a wise and righteous man as to a fountain of life. One of these two things necessarily awaits us: either we perish in the snares of death, or, having become partakers of the teaching of the wise, we enjoy life. The teaching of the wise is fit not only to keep us safe and unharmed from the snares of death, but also to free and disentangle us when we have already become entangled and more tightly bound in them.

And it not only delivers us from harmful and dead works, but also renders us thoroughly equipped and animated for every good work. Hence the psalm sings that the righteous person, through continual meditation on God’s teaching, is like a tree planted beside streams and channels of waters which brings forth ripe and seasonable fruit, Psalm 1. For since in this passage the teaching of the wise is compared to a fountain of perennial water, it is no wonder if those who make it familiar to themselves—as though they moved the fountain close to themselves—bear fruit abundantly. See Proverbs 3:12, 16, 20, where God’s teaching is said both to rescue us from evil works and to drive us to good works, and thus to be a perfect rule of human life.

Verse 15. Favor gives good success, but the way of the treacherous is rough.

What *good understanding* is in this passage is evident from the opposite member, namely the way of transgressors. Corresponding to it on the other side is that understanding of the truth which is joined with the practice of virtue and piety, and which alone is to be regarded as good and salutary. For when the knowledge of heavenly things is separated from piety, it becomes deadly and destructive to the one who knows them. This understanding is commended by the effect it produces—namely, favor with God and man, as above at chapter 3, verse 4.

First, then, as far as God is concerned, this must not be understood as though God’s favor

[1632 witness | PDF p. 218 | printed columns 410–411]

and that grace should be called forth by any work of ours, since grace itself is the fountain and parent of all the good things in us; but rather that piety and sound understanding, taking their rise and origin from the grace of God, draw to themselves a new and more abundant grace—or rather, if we wish to speak properly, fuller and richer testimonies of the first and original grace, so to speak. This is the same as what is said in John 1, that in Christ we receive grace for grace. Here it should be noted in passing that the papists err by the whole breadth of heaven: they do not indeed deny that the first grace is gratuitous, but they say that what is afterward bestowed by God’s liberality is conferred upon us by our merit, because of the due and lawful use of the first grace. So far is this from being done by our merit that, by a metonymy of the cause, what is bestowed second retains the name of grace.

How great a thing it is for someone to be gracious in God’s sight is clear from the fact that seeking after it is commended so greatly in Scripture, and in so remarkable and unusual a manner, Psalm 27:8, 9 and 105:4. For the place it holds among the godly, see Psalm 4:7. It is also illustrated by comparison with a lesser thing, namely the grace of princes, which must be sought and pursued. Yet since princes are mortal, they are often unable to relieve our miseries even if they wish to do so. Indeed, we eagerly hunt not only for the favor of kings, but also for the favor of the man who is to succeed a king, as Solomon observes in Ecclesiastes, although, since he does not yet possess power, he can accomplish nothing on our behalf; and because of the perishing condition of human affairs, he will either have died before he handles the scepters or, if he survives the present king, will nevertheless never ascend the throne of the kingdom.

And just as it is of the greatest importance to obtain God’s grace, so it is by no means remarkable that, when we order our lives rightly, we should be acceptable to God, who embraced us with favor and grace even when, living from the flesh, we practiced feuds and enmities against him. But to enter into favor with human beings and preserve it once begun—especially with wicked people, who are enemies of piety and the true knowledge of God—seems difficult indeed, or rather incredible. Yet we must remember that all human hearts are in God’s power and that he leads and leads them back, this way and that, like channels of water. Therefore, since he has enriched godly and understanding people with promises of this kind, there is no more reason for us to doubt the truth and performance of the promise than if we were already holding it in our hands—especially since God expressly confirms that he will implant this disposition in enemies, so that they show favor and mercy to them, Psalm 106:46; Daniel 1:9. And he has proved it by countless, most illustrious examples from every age and century: in Joseph, who was valued wherever he went, even among the most wicked; and in the Israelites, whom the Egyptians, their sworn enemies, embraced with such favor that they freely granted them their jewels and property, or twentieth-part gold, and would moreover—as is credible—have plucked out their eyes to give them, if the Israelites had asked it of them. To this should be referred the accounts of the generosity of Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes toward the people of God, who were most hateful to them both in nation and in religion. See the example of David in Saul’s ruined court. See in Acts 27 how φιλάνθρωπος (kindly disposed) Julius was toward the Apostle, and in Acts 28 what kindness the barbarians showed to Paul and his companions.

This is illustrated from the contrary: the way and manner of life of transgressors is hard and rough—that is, inhuman and merciless—and by this very fact it causes them to be received harshly and hatefully both by God and by human beings. See the example of Joseph’s brothers, who on account of their own sin were harshly and roughly received even by their own brother, though he loved them deeply. See how often God set the wicked, though most closely joined among themselves, against one another, so that they

[1632 witness | PDF p. 219 | printed columns 412–413]

rushed upon one another in mutual slaughter. See the example of the Midianites and Syrians; see Judges 9:20, 23.

*Verse 16. Every prudent man acts with knowledge, but the stupid man spreads out folly.*

In the word יפרש (*spreads out*) there is a metaphor taken from curtains which were at first rolled up but are afterward wholly unrolled and spread out for all eyes to see.

In this passage the skillful or prudent person is one who wisely adapts himself, through the use of circumstances, to the affairs that must be conducted. Since, however, in this book the word is taken only in praise, as has been said, it follows that it is lawful for a godly person to employ prudence and caution in managing his affairs. He should not grasp simplicity and an open manner of acting with one hand in such a way that he allows prudence and circumspect caution to slip from the other, but should know how to join to the simplicity of the dove the prudence of the serpent, which is more cunning than all the other brute animals. This refutes the ignorance of those who suppose they can never sufficiently prove themselves Christ’s sheep unless they put their throats into the gullet and maw of wolves; and who dream that they can never rightly be regarded as Christ’s doves unless they surrender themselves to the talons of hawks and other birds of prey and make themselves their quarry—as if piety made us inferior to brute animals, to whom nature dictates that they guard and defend themselves and their lives against oncoming attacks; or as though piety extinguished the light of reason granted to the human race, by which we foresee evils that are going to happen long beforehand so that we may flee, whereas piety instead strengthens and confirms every honorable and lawful avoidance of dangers. Hence in Deuteronomy 4 it is said that God’s people, by keeping his commandments, will gain the name and reputation of wisdom even among an unbelieving people. See the example of David, who in the judgment of the courtiers is said to have managed everything prudently and is called exceedingly prudent by Saul, while the testimony of Scripture everywhere confirms that God was with him. There is certainly no need to howl among games, nor are we permitted to repay evil with evil. The godly therefore have their spies under Moses, they have their stratagems, as is clear in the history of Joshua, and they have their ὠτακουστάς (eavesdroppers), and so forth.

The effect of this skillful or prudent quality is judged by Solomon to be that one manages one’s affairs knowingly. This knowledge, as is gathered from the contrary member, chiefly consists and exercises its power in this: that the prudent person does not pour out everything at once, all together, and everywhere, but says or does some things in such a way that he omits other things that could be said and done, though the law of truth does not forbid him to say or do them. Thus he considers not so much what is lawful or unlawful as he aims at what is expedient or inexpedient; and he does and says certain things in a definite place and time and before definite persons which he would not do indiscriminately. Examples of this appear in Esther, who, instructed by Mordecai, kept silent about her race and origin; in Nehemiah 2:3, where, in place of the site of God’s worship—which would have been distasteful and hateful to the unbelieving king—he substituted the place of his ancestors’ tomb; in Ezra 8:22; in Paul, Acts 23:6; and everywhere in Christ, but especially when he was brought before Pilate and the chief priests.

The fool, on the contrary, pours out everything without regard to the circumstances of times, places, and persons. On this matter see chapter 12:13 above. For as long as he is silent or abstains from conducting affairs, his foolishness is kept like a curtain rolled and folded up, so that it escapes human eyes; but when he puts himself forward in either words or deeds, his foolishness is immediately exposed. Indeed, as long as he lives in silence, he is often considered wise.

*Verse 17. A wicked messenger*

[1632 witness | PDF p. 220 | printed columns 414–415]

*falls into evil, but an ambassador of excellent fidelity works healing.*

A messenger is an agent who bears definite commands in another’s name. Therefore a wicked messenger is one who executes commands without lawful authority, or lawful commands unlawfully; a faithful ambassador, on the contrary, is one who, furnished with lawful commands, puts them into effect with the utmost fidelity. Now, an error has become deep-rooted among almost everyone and has nearly passed into a proverb: that all the blame, as also the praise for a thing well done, rests with the author and commander, while the messenger is always free from fault. Solomon wished by this proverb to expose and correct that error, sharing the blame for a fault and the praise for an excellent deed with the messenger or ambassador. Hence we are deterred from presenting ourselves as agents and suppliers of unlawful things, and from the other side we are incited to advance honorable things by our service, even if that service is servile and menial.

But a wicked messenger exists in two kinds, with respect either to

{
    a calling,
    a false commission or its execution.
}

In the former kind are those who, furnished with no lawful calling, leap forward as self-appointed magistrates and πολυπράγμονες (busybodies) to undertake an embassy. Many of this kind have been found in the ministry of the word and are found even now: pseudo-Apostles and pseudo-Prophets who boast that they are sent immediately by God. See both Epistles to the Corinthians and Revelation 2, the letter to the Ephesians; such were those who declared that they had been sent by the Apostles, though they had not been sent by them, as in Acts 15; and such are those who are equipped with a calling from human beings, while their God does not add his testimony in their support. All these, whatever message they bear, are wicked messengers.

Next, those who report impious or unjust things, or who corrupt a lawful message commanded to them by their principals through addition, subtraction, or alteration, must be reckoned among wicked messengers. God’s curse is directed not only against wicked authors and princes, but also against their agents. See Isaiah 10:1. Examples of this appear in 1 Samuel 22:17, 18, joined with Psalm 52; 2 Kings 1:9, and so forth; Acts 22:18, 19; and Matthew 28:4. It is true that their masters and princes assure them with loud voice and mighty lungs that they will vindicate their servants and followers from every danger, as is clear from 2 Samuel 13:28; but they do not therefore carry off their crime unpunished. Indeed, it happens that agents often suffer a heavier punishment in this world than the authors themselves, as in Matthew 28:4 and 2 Kings 19. But if agents who are under the yoke of others and are fastened to their masters by the tightest bond of subjection suffer punishment, surely those who of their own accord devote themselves to perpetrating impious enormities will suffer heavier punishment.

*But the most faithful ambassador works healing.* Who this ambassador is—with respect to his calling, the substance of his embassy, and his manner of executing it—is sufficiently clear from those things that are contrary to what was said before. The first and most evident rule for such a person is set forth by the Prophet: *Do not withhold a word that is with you*, Jeremiah 1:17; Ezekiel 3:17. Many excellent examples appear in Jeremiah. See the example of Moses, who was unwilling to leave even a hoof in Egypt. See the words of Elijah to Ahab, of John to Herod, and of Nathan to David.

The former passage in the Prophet teaches that he works healing, for there he commands him not to omit even one little word, “if perhaps,” he says, “they should repent,” and so forth. See what power the words of Gad to David and of Nathan to the same man possessed. See Job 33:23. That, however, they bring no healing

[1632 witness | PDF p. 221 | printed columns 416–417]

to the reprobate must be understood in this sense: they exert their power upon the wicked by taking away every excuse from them. Next, from the antithesis in the former verse it must be understood both that the ambassador himself will be advanced and that he acquires greater health for himself than he brings to the reprobate. Likewise, in the latter half-verse it must be understood antithetically that a wicked messenger calls forth loss and destruction not only for himself but also brings them upon those to whom he brings a wicked message, unless they act cautiously and prudently extricate themselves from the snares that the messenger sets.

*Verse 18. Poverty and disgrace [shall be] to him who withdraws himself [from instruction: but he who observes correction shall be honored.*

How necessary chastisement is for all who derive their origin from Adam is proved by the illustrious statement in Job 11:11, 12, where all human beings are expressly called empty and devoid of all understanding, to such a degree that they are regarded as wild asses rather than as human beings. In the same place the purpose of divine correction is said to be that human beings—that is, tame creatures—may be made out of wild asses. As young horses, therefore, are first handed over to trainers so that we may afterward use them conveniently for journeys or for other uses of life, so we are all no less unfit to render worship to God or to live usefully with human beings until, tamed by God’s scourges, we submit our necks to Christ’s yoke. In Proverbs 22:15 it is affirmed that folly is bound to the heart of a boy, but the rod of correction will drive it away. And the Apostle teaches that he who does not partake in chastisement is a bastard, not a son, Hebrews 12:8. From this the truth of this proverb shines forth: one who withdraws himself from correction is perverse, whether a boy or anyone else whose age enables him to judge concerning chastisement. For if chastisement is so necessary in little children, in whom sin has not fixed and confirmed such deep roots, how much more is it necessary in adults and those more advanced in age? And if correction is needed to fit a human being for civil society, for the shaping of which certain excellent beginnings remain implanted within each person’s mind, how much more is reproof needed to maintain and cultivate a holy society, since not the smallest seed or slenderest spark of it was left surviving from that πανωλεθρία (utter ruin) which overthrew the human race through the stain of sin?

We read that David’s sons Absalom and Adonijah perished for this reason: they lived with the utmost freedom and at the decision of their own will, without the bridle of chastisement, to the point that they were not wounded even by a word. But if those to whom no chastisement was applied because of their parents’ foolish indulgence rightly perish, how much more justly will those perish who reject and repudiate the chastisement and severity imposed and commanded by those whose concern it is? For when Scripture exhorts us to receive chastisement meekly, it means not only that which is sent from heaven by God directly, which no one openly rejects unless he is monstrously wicked—as those do of whom John writes in Revelation 16:8, 9—but also that which God inflicts upon someone through the household, the Church, or the commonwealth, even if it is sometimes unjust when you consider those who apply the rod, 1 Peter 2:18, 19, 20.

But if those in an inferior place are bound to receive chastisement and bear it meekly, those who preside are under no less obligation to exercise just severity against offenders.

The punishment of those who withdraw themselves from the discipline of the household, Church, or commonwealth is poverty and disgrace. The means and agent of this poverty are often the indignation and anger toward their subjects of those who lawfully apply correction. For just as

[1632 witness | PDF p. 222 | printed columns 418–419]

it is well in this life with those who obey their parents, so, on the contrary, it is ill in this life with those who do not acquiesce in their authority. Then there is also this further support for the punishment: those who despise discipline learn no honorable art by which a person may sustain life.

Nor does he threaten poverty alone, but disgraceful poverty. For there is a certain glorious poverty, such as that of Christ, the Apostles, and the faithful, who for Christ’s name suffered the loss of their household estate and patrimony. Nor is the poverty of those who maintain this life with scant resources but nevertheless lawfully dishonorable. And this punishment of disgrace seems to be attached because the wicked, the refractory, and the restive for the most part flee stricter discipline, since they think it will be dishonorable to them. Solomon meets this temptation with this recompense when he teaches that disgrace is drawn on by refusing correction, not by embracing it, as human beings suppose. A remarkable picture of this matter appears in the prodigal son, Luke 15, who, impatient with the domestic discipline that flourished in his father’s house, withdrew himself from it and joined himself to gluttons and spendthrifts.

Eli and David suffered the same penalty for neglecting their duty to chastise their sons. For Eli’s posterity was for this reason reduced to a morsel of bread, and David was for this reason compelled to flee from Absalom’s face and for a time to be supported and fed by his own subjects.

In the latter half-verse, the one who observes reproof is opposed to the one who withdraws himself from chastisement. A person is said to observe chastisement not only when he admits it meekly and endures it, but when, mindful of it, he withdraws from those sins on account of which either he himself felt the scourge or he observed that others had felt it. See Joshua 22:20. See the example of Jacob’s sons, who by the harsh treatment and agitation with which their brother Joseph received them were immediately brought to the memory and sense of the sins committed long before. See 2 Kings 1:13, 14. For the contrary, see Ezekiel throughout chapter 19, where the princes of Judah, succeeding one another, were not recalled from the same crimes into which their predecessors had fallen by the chastisements of those predecessors.

He teaches that the one who observes reproof is to be adorned with the reward of true honor, contrary to the common opinion of those who regard it as a disgrace if someone patiently and calmly bears the chastisement imposed upon him. But it is asked why he does not also say that this person will be heaped with the gift of riches, just as he said that the one who withdraws from chastisement will be pressed by poverty. The answer is that by this manner of speaking he would teach that riches are not always conferred upon those who are godly, but only insofar as it seems good to God; and by the same fact it must also be understood that the one who rejects chastisement is not always pressed by poverty.

*Verse 19. Desire accomplished [will be sweet to the soul] of this one, ] but [it is] an abomination to fools to depart from evil.*

In נפש (*soul*) there is a synecdoche of a member for the whole person. In תועבת (*abomination*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct.

By nature all people rejoice and exult when the things they eagerly desire in their minds fall out according to the wish of their own mind. Up to this point the impulse of nature is placed neither in praise nor in blame. For this reason, when we seek those things that are suitable and adapted to nature—such as food, drink, sleep, and so forth, provided that in seeking them we do not pass beyond measure and reason, we are free from fault. But in truth, from the stain and corruption of our nature through sin, it comes about both that we desire lawful things more ardently and vehemently than is permitted and that we prefer unlawful things forbidden by the divine law. On the contrary, reborn by the Holy Spirit through the seed of the divine word

[1632 witness | PDF p. 223 | printed columns 420–421]

we also desire those things that savor of piety and righteousness. In the first half of this verse he seems to speak of the last two of these desires of our corrupted and reformed nature. For there is no reason here to undertake a discussion of natural appetite, which is accounted neither praise nor blame. And although scarcely any trace appears of desires that are measured by the rule of the divine law, nevertheless in this first part that desire is inferred from the contrary desire of the wicked, in a manner suited to the compressed and concise nature of a proverb.

But why did he express the desire of the wicked rather than that of the godly? The reason seems readily apparent: the desire of the wicked is more intent and inflamed toward evil than that of the godly toward what is good. The wicked are carried toward evil by desire with the whole impetus of their nature, as though with horses and sails, and sometimes are also incited by Satan's impulse; whereas the godly person's wishes for what belongs to the saints are retarded by the remnants of corruption and held back as though by fibers that still cling to them.

The sense of the former half-verse, therefore, is this: the unlawful desires of the wicked and the lawful desires of the godly, when fulfilled and perfected, drench the mind with a most delightful pleasure. In the appetite or desire of the ungodly their unhappiness and accursed condition shine forth, since they are nearest to hell when all things flow to them according to their will. Thus it is much better for them to be tormented by grim pains because they are deprived of the things they covet than to exult joyfully in obtaining their wishes. Hence no judgment more severe and more exquisite is exercised by God against them in this life than when he indulges them in the things they have wickedly desired. See Romans 1:24 and Psalm 81:13. And in Jeremiah the people's utmost wickedness is designated by *walking according to the impulse of their own heart*. In this manner there is in some measure fulfilled what is said in the Psalm: *their table is turned for them into a snare or net in which they are held entangled*.

In the latter half-verse the Prophet teaches that the wicked utterly reject and remove everything hostile and inimical to the fulfillment and satiation of their desire. Thus, however excellent it may be in itself, they not only hate but even abominate it. For they are like a she-bear robbed of her cubs, and therefore they charge and burst upon everyone they encounter. See 1 Kings 22 concerning the deadly hatred toward Micaiah, who always opposed his desires; for the same reason he calls the Prophet Elijah *his enemy*, 1 Kings 21:20. Thus Saul was about to kill his own son, and indeed his eldest, because he had opposed Saul's desire and appetite to destroy David.

By contrast, as the godly generally desire good things, because the very things they desire they ask from God in prayers—before whose majesty they are ashamed to pray for futile things, much less impious ones—so they dearly embrace all those things that serve to fulfill their honorable desires or furnish help toward them, even if otherwise they are bitter. Hence that saying of Psalm 141:5: *Let the righteous strike me; let him not withhold it*, and so forth. But if they eagerly desire something that departs from reason and God's law, they are nevertheless readily driven from it by the warning of others, as was seen in David, 1 Samuel 25; or they cast it away of their own accord, as with the water of Bethlehem.

Verse 20. He who walks with the wise will become wiser, but he who associates himself with fools will become worse.

In the present-tense participle הלך (*walking*), he indicates continual and constant companionship, as in Genesis 4:17, where Cain is said to be בונה (*building*); thus in Luke 10:17 they were

[1632 witness | PDF p. 224 | printed columns 422–423]

eating and drinking, and so forth. This is also plain even now from the corresponding expression *companion* in the opposite member. For one who has business or commerce with a fool is not immediately a fool; for then, as the Apostle says, *one would have to depart from the world*, 1 Corinthians 5. Rather, it is one who lives joined with him by a closer bond of fellowship.

How great and how necessary is the use of a friend in this perishing life. See Proverbs 17:17 and 27:9–10. It is so great that not even Christ himself, who for our sake renounced all the advantages of this life, was willing to lack this comfort so necessary to human life; rather, he admitted his servant to the right and fellowship of friendship instead of consenting to be without a friend. Since the entire human race is divided in this very verse into two parts, namely the wise and fools—that is, according to the form of speech in Scripture, especially in David and Solomon, the upright and the wicked—it is manifest that we are held and bound by this rule: we are to admit into the fellowship and communion of friendship those who are godly, unless we wish to pay the penalty for our rashness in choosing others. This must be understood not only of friends, but extends further to all those with whom the necessity of habitual and familiar association is imposed upon us; of this kind are all members of a household. Among them a spouse holds the chief place, because a husband is not permitted to separate himself from her for perversity of religion or conduct, with only the crime of adultery excepted. Next, servants of this kind are to be sought who are imbued, or at least tinged, with the fear of God. See Psalm 101. For just as one who walks continually in the sun is colored by its rays, and as Moses through his fellowship and conversation with God acquired the gleaming splendor of his face, so by constant association with people we are conformed to them in mind and manner of life. Hence the Prophet, intending to confine himself within the circuit of obedience to God and professing himself enclosed within the barriers of divine obedience, declares that he will be the companion of those who fear God. Psalm 119, section 8. For the prudent man saw that, however advanced and confirmed in piety he might be, without this help of the fellowship and communion of the godly he could not persevere in the course of piety he had begun, much less make progress in it without the company of the godly. Hence the monastic institution is refuted, especially that of the anchorites. Those are particularly censured who take no great care in choosing the companions of their life, being little conscious either of their own weakness or of the force that habitual association with others exerts in either direction. But the danger is greatest if someone not only does not abandon, but even neglects, the assemblies of the Church, Hebrews 11:25. See what a grave fall Thomas suffered because he was absent from the Church's sacred assembly on only one Lord's Day.

In depraved and perverse companionship there is still greater power to infect us than there is in association with the good to conform us to their piety, because corrupted nature has a propensity toward every vice and an aversion from virtue. Although this has been somewhat diminished and worn away in ungodly people, it has certainly not been conquered and subdued. Beasts and people infected with some taint transmit its contagion to others who are their companions, but they do not affect others with their own health. So also in this spiritual contact a good person is easily made worse by the companionship of an evil one, while an evil person is scarcely, or not even scarcely, made better by that of a good one. See 2 Kings 18:17 and 2 Kings 10:11. By the former of these is confirmed the sense of the proverb indicated by the verb ירע, since it signifies becoming evil and wicked; by the latter testimony the other sense receives confirmation, which is indicated by the verb רעע, signifying a wearing down. Since both senses suit this passage well enough, nothing prevents us from embracing both,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 225 | printed columns 424–425]

as in the word זונה, which signifies both an innkeeper and a prostitute.

Verse 21. Evil pursues sinners, but good recompenses the righteous.

There is personification in this Proverb. For the more vivid and forceful affecting of people with both hatred of sin and love of righteousness, life and judgment to discern between good and evil are attributed alike to punishment and reward, so that each may come upon people according to how they have conducted themselves. For since the wicked easily slide into evil through hope of impunity, while the godly have difficulty persuading themselves of a happy outcome for godly deeds, this Proverb addresses both: it announces undoubted destruction to the wicked, but certain safety to the upright, however long either punishment for the former or reward for the latter may be deferred.

By the word חטאים (*sinners*) are understood not those who violate God's commandments through the weakness of the flesh—in which class all of us, as many as we are, are sinners—but those who practice sin as an art. Of these it is said in John 9, *God does not hear sinners*, and in the following verse; and they are very often called *workers of iniquity* in the Scriptures. Evil, or God's vengeance, pursues them as a shadow does a body, as a hunting dog does prey or game, and as under the law an avenger does the person who killed one most closely connected with him. Since this vengeance receives from God feet like those of deer, or rather wings like those of eagles, the hope with which sinners flatter themselves—that they will escape the punishment justly due to their sin—must be vain and false. This is especially so because no city of refuge, no asylum or sanctuary, has been assigned by God to those who sin maliciously, as one has to those who depart from the way through weakness. Thus, though the wicked flee on horses from the punishment due to their sins, God's vengeance will nevertheless overtake them on dromedaries. But if they seize raven's wings by which to hasten their flight, vengeance equipped with eagle's wings will outstrip them. See Zechariah 1:5–6. Even if they fly up to heaven, descend to hell, or cross the sea, God's vengeance nevertheless accompanies them. Therefore, though they have no guards of the Prince pursuing them and no lictors of the Praetor, and so forth, and though they prevail in power and know how ingeniously to conceal a crime, they will nevertheless at last bear away the penalty due to sin.

From this we learn how momentary the delights of sin are and how dearly we buy their enjoyment. Even if they were bounded by the same span as life and closed only with the breathing out of the soul, they would nevertheless be momentary and have almost no future duration. But now, since most often in this life they are not merely interrupted but are mixed and tempered with the bitterest seasonings of God's scourges, what degree of madness will it finally be accounted to pursue them so greedily and thirstily?

The other half-verse embraces truth no less certain and indubitable: the good will fare well, and God's kindness and blessing match the righteous in diligence and speed, as though they were at their feet, and follow them closely in their tracks, just as evil pursues the wicked. For since vengeance on the one hand, and God's kindness and blessing on the other, are in a manner messengers sent out by him to their appointed works of wrath and mercy, and since he inclines more toward kindness than toward severity and is said to be drawn almost unwillingly to vengeance, it certainly cannot be doubted that good is no less ready and swift upon the road to reward the good than evil is to punish the evil. To this is added that, while the wicked flee from evil with their utmost effort, the righteous by their prayers draw down and bring near God's kindness and blessing. And since kindness,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 226 | printed columns 426–427]

and God's blessing have blessed in this life the wicked descendants of godly people on account of their parents' piety, much more will they load the godly worshipers of God themselves with good things. What is more, the wicked have been adorned and enriched with God's external benefits, both in themselves and in their posterity, merely because they rendered an obedience to God that was approved to some degree in appearance and by human judgment, as in Jehu and his descendants. How much more will he do this for those who are truly and sincerely godly? The godly do not so much doubt whether God will grant them the crown of eternal life; what is debated is whether he will bestow favor upon them in this age. Therefore, let us fortify ourselves with these reasons against this temptation.

Verse 22. A good man hands down an inheritance to his grandchildren, and the wealth of sinners is stored up for the righteous.

In בני בנים (*grandchildren*) there is a synecdoche of the part, grandchildren, for great-grandchildren, later descendants, and so forth—that is, for the whole posterity.

Care and anxiety about the present life exercises and torments many people greatly, so that provision may be made for them of those things by which they may sustain this life honorably. But desire to acquire an estate for posterity immeasurably increases this care and anxiety about their own life. For first they labor greatly to propagate offspring and progeny, in whom the dead may in some manner live again. But when by God's liberality they have obtained offspring, they immediately raise their minds to acquiring wealth and landed properties. By these three cares—maintaining their own life, producing offspring, and leaving an inheritance to their children—human minds are held so fast in the pursuit of earthly things, as though by a threefold cord, that they are scarcely, or hardly even scarcely, allowed to look up to heaven or entertain a thought of God. To obtain these things they rise early and lie down late, eating bread only with grief and anxiety; and yet very often they fall away from their hope and, with the utmost exertion of mind and body, do not obtain even one of these things. But in this passage the Prophet instills the hope of attaining all these things into those who cultivate righteousness. For when he affirms that a good man transmits an inheritance to his grandchildren, he affirms, first, that the righteous man has enough with which to preserve his own life; second, that posterity will not be wanting; and finally, that the father's inheritance will remain for his descendants. If someone objects that these things do not always and consistently fall to the righteous, the ready reply is that those who pass righteousness by and investigate and most zealously pursue every road and approach to these things attain them still less often. It is therefore far better to enter upon this shortcut of piety and righteousness, by which these things are acquired more often and more certainly, than to make them depend upon the devising and contrivance of our own reasoning, deliberation, and wicked toil. For granted that by his own industry a person may, while alive, provide to some extent for himself the things by which he sustains himself and his life, it nevertheless does not lie in his power to receive children, who are called God's reward in Psalm 127; nor, once dead, does it lie in his power to bring it about that the acquired inheritance passes into the hands and possession of his descendants, even if he disposes all things most circumspectly by will and fortifies the right of inheritance on every side by municipal law.

But God, the patron of the godly, lives forever. He takes the orphans of righteous people under his protection and easily provides for them, though all people oppose it. And although the wicked both abound in wealth themselves and enrich their children with estate and inheritance, nevertheless they very rarely transmit their inheritance to their great-grandchildren. Therefore, let us firmly hold what the Apostle teaches: that piety is useful for all things, and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 227 | printed columns 428–429]

to have promises both of the present and of the future life, 1 Timothy 4:8. And let us correct the error innate in us by nature and confirmed by the corruption of manners and imitation of examples, by which we allow ourselves to become persuaded that almost all the labor we place in divine worship and exercises of piety is lost, and that as much is taken from the hearth as is spent upon the altar—as though being thrifty and being devoted to divine worship were things so opposed and ἀσύστατα, or incompatible, that they could not stand together. This goes so far that most people find it burdensome and troublesome even to observe the Sabbath, or Lord's Day, religiously, because on it cessation from gainful occupations is commanded.

It is also significant that a good and godly man is said to hand down an inheritance to his descendants, although it proceeds, as does *every good gift*, James 1, from God and his undeserved kindness. To this is referred what is expressly said in the fifth commandment of the Decalogue: parents in some manner cause obedient children to lead a long life in this age. The Holy Spirit indeed presents this for that reason, so that he may add spurs to parents' piety by admitting them into partnership in this liberality toward their descendants; in just the same way he applies torches to ministers of the Church so that they may perform the ministry faithfully, because in this way they will save both themselves and their people, 1 Timothy 4.

In the latter half-verse there is a prolepsis, or anticipation. For here a question arises: how does it happen that a righteous man acquires so great an abundance of wealth that he provides so plentifully both for himself and for his descendants? Although this difficulty can be answered from other passages of Scripture, as first from Job 22:23–25, this might seem incredible because of the world's hostile hatred toward the righteous and because of the righteous person's devotion to piety, by which it happens that, being dedicated to heavenly things, they have less leisure for heaping up riches. The second answer is that God's blessing makes a person rich, Proverbs 10:22; third, that *the little which a righteous man has is more than the abundance of many wicked people*, Psalm 37:16. Nevertheless, it is answered from this very passage, namely, that the riches of the wicked have been stored and laid up for the righteous. This is especially supported by what is written in Job 27:16–17. Isaiah 61:5 wonderfully amplifies this passage when it says that the wicked and those alien to the Church will serve the godly as shepherds, farmers, and vintners. It is as though the possession of the land belonged to the wicked, while the godly are nevertheless to be regarded as its usufructuaries, so that it can truly be said of them that they have flocks and herds, meadows, crops, and vineyards while possessing nothing, in agreement with the Apostle's saying, *having nothing and possessing all things*.

Next, it must be observed that he says the wealth is stored up. By this he means not only that the wicked are separated from those treasures, but further that there are means by which the righteous are sustained with the supports of life required to preserve it, although they can neither see this with their eyes nor grasp it in their minds by rational calculation.

Verse 23. There is much food for poor people who break up fallow ground, but there is one who consumes himself through idleness.

In ניר, *plowing*, there is a synecdoche of the part for every lawful occupation.

For the sense and treatment of the former half-verse, see chapter 12:11 and 12:24. Its sense seems to come back to the same thing that is commonly declared by everyone: *A trade sustains the craftsman.* But, that this may be so, there is also a noteworthy enrichment of the former proverb. For when he affirms that poor people obtain food most abundantly by breaking up fallow ground, he teaches that no one is prevented by the slenderness of his fortune from having enough to maintain life, provided that he bends his sinews to this end and diligently applies his hand to the work. This

[1632 witness | PDF p. 228 | printed columns 430–431]

The poor especially do this, who, because their field is narrow, apply themselves more diligently to cultivating and subduing it. For there are many who, struck down by the deepest and sharpest sense of their poverty, utterly cast away the hope of procuring sustenance and either grow sluggish in idleness, give themselves to begging, or apply their minds to frauds and dishonest arts of acquiring riches. See Proverbs 10:15. Since the truth and trustworthiness of this proverb concerning an abundant return from labor has been found true among those who are strangers to the Christian commonwealth, how much more will it hold firm in Zion and among the people and covenant-members of God, whose poor are endowed with a most ample promise and, as it were, a privilege? Psalm 132:15; see Psalm 37:25. But if they do not obtain this by strenuous labor because God withdraws his blessing, first let them console themselves with the consciousness of righteous labor. Then let them consider within themselves that, by this withdrawal of his blessing, God purposes to train them toward humility and lowliness of mind, while they are compelled to seek and beg from others the things that sustain them.

In the latter half-verse another virtue is required: the prudent and cautious ordering and stewardship of things laboriously acquired. Unless this virtue is present, everything, however great it may be, easily slips away. For it is no lesser virtue to preserve what has been acquired than to acquire it. We must therefore make calculations so that expenses are not greater than rents and revenues; so that we do not turn our minds to building before we duly settle those things which are outdoors and in the fields; so that either the magnificence or the spaciousness of buildings is not ill suited to the meadows or plowed lands; so that the fruitfulness of one year is employed to compensate for another year's barrenness; so that each thing is seasonably adapted according to its nature to domestic uses; so that a table is furnished for strangers and members of the household according to each one's condition and rank; and so that prudent foresight guards against the corruption and deterioration of all things at home and abroad. And just as the sons of this age attend to these things, so they are especially performed by the godly, since God polishes and enlarges by his grace and by wisdom drawn from the word this natural prudence, which he bestows even upon the wicked. Psalm 112:5, where the same word employed here occurs, so that it may be easier to understand that this is the genuine sense of the present passage which we have followed, whenever other senses are advanced. To this must be referred the fact that each one of us is appointed by God as a steward of the things we possess, so that we may use them conveniently and in due measure for the benefit of those whose interest is greatest, since prudence is required in stewardship. Examples of such prudent stewardship, or οἰκονομίας, are found in Joseph, who adopted the plan by which the fertility of seven years compensated for the barrenness of seven other years; next it shone wonderfully in Solomon, who arranged with fixed order and method the management of his whole household both in food and in clothing; finally, an example of this matter appears in Christ the Lord and Savior, who could not be induced by God's bounty to allow the loss of even the smallest fragment or morsel of barley bread, or of the smallest little fish. Matthew 14:20; John 6:12. But when he teaches that judgment must be employed in the ordering and distribution of property and the household estate, let us understand that this is said not so much with a view to thrift as to generosity and beneficence by which we assist others. Otherwise it will come about through excessive attention to property and stinginess that, by God's just judgment, it will be transferred to one who is moved by compassion for the needy. The same may not inappropriately be said of the spiritual harvest: although we carry in and heap up a plentiful harvest, yet unless it is digested and dispensed by godly meditation according to a certain measure and method, it is rendered fruitless and useless to us.

Verse 24. He who withholds his rod hates his son; but he who loves him procures instruction for him at the proper time.

In שחרו, *he dawns upon him*, there is a metaphor from the dawn for a prompt and timely manner of acting; here childhood is also compared with the dawn of the day, just as the whole of human life is compared with the span of a day. In שבט, *rod*, there is a synecdoche of the part for every kind of correction, whether by words, by blows, by inflicting punishment, or by withdrawing benefits. In בנו, *his son*, there is likewise a synecdoche of the part for subordinates of every kind.

By this saying the Prophet marks the error of nearly all subordinates, especially those who are of tender age, and also of some parents. The former shrink from every correction as though it proceeded from their parents' hatred; the latter, through excessive indulgence toward their subordinates—especially their children—do not apply correction, because they judge it to be foreign to the mind of loving parents.

The necessity of correction for children was discussed at verse 18 of this chapter. This is understood even more clearly from the fact that, since all are children of Adam, it is plain that something of native corruption resides in their souls. For even if, having been regenerated by God, they have put on the new man, fibers and remnants of the old Adam nevertheless remain; these must be torn away by proper and prudent instruction. How much more must this cure, which is effected by correction, be applied when they have not yet cast off the clothing of the old man? To affirm that reproof is unsuitable for a child would be equivalent to denying that he is a child of Adam. What field is so fertile that it needs no weeding? What foal is by nature so tame and gentle that there is no need to break it? All certainly are either, so to speak, *Ephorini* or *Theopompini*, so that they must be either urged by spurs or restrained by bridles. See Ephesians 6:4; Hebrews 12. *What father is there who does not correct his son?* All parents willingly constitute themselves debtors to provide food and clothing for their children. Yet there ought to be no less care that they be trained by due correction, or indeed greater care, because by the one they are advanced toward life, but by the other toward a godly and honorable life.

But where this reproof is lacking, its cause is assigned to the parents' love toward their children—falsely, however; for the Holy Spirit locates the true cause and seed of this excessive parental indulgence in the parents' hatred toward their children. Thus in its own opinion and in that of their foolish children, let it be paternal love and kindness; yet in the judgment of the all-wise God it is plainly hatred and grave enmity. For since the fruits of that mad love are harmful, destructive, and plainly hostile, bringing ruin upon body and soul after this life and very often upon the present life as well, why should that not truly be accounted hatred which produces fruits and effects no less destructive than those that could proceed from a most sworn and bitter enemy? Nor does it help that they wish them well, since their will toward them is not in accord with right reason. In the same way, those who burn with a certain zeal for God, yet stray as far as heaven from earth from the true worship of God prescribed in the word, are not therefore to be accounted worshipers and lovers of God. And the Holy Spirit preferred to use this word *hatred* so that he might drive parents and children alike toward the proper and just pursuit of correction, unless children wish to regard their parents as enemies, and parents wish to be regarded as haters of their own children—a monstrous thing. And although foolish parents and children alike may take pleasure in this laxity for a time, parents, despised by kicking and refractory children, experience the fruits of their hatred toward their children at the great price of their children's reciprocal hatred toward them. And children, commonly dragged away to the gallows, often rail against indulgent parents

[1632 witness | PDF p. 230 | printed columns 434–435]

as against enemies, since they refer their deadly calamity to them as its authors and begetters through excessive indulgence, so that they proved to be no less the parents of their destruction than of their persons. Hence it is clear in what place all shameful love must be held, whether that of fornicators or of those sinning in any other kind of uncleanness; and therefore that kisses, embraces, and indecent touchings are so many testimonies of virulent hatred.

It must not be passed over that he does not say, *he who withholds a rod*, but *he who withholds his rod*; that is, the rod that is suitable for his son. For this seems to be the genuine sense of the proverb: the one who keeps away his rod—that is, the rod suitable for his son—hates his son. Or if we refer the pronoun to the parents, it amounts to the same thing and the sense comes back to the same point: the son is to be corrected with the rod that befits the parent. They are therefore taught here that he is to be struck with a rod suited not indiscriminately, but to the son's age, disposition, offense, or error. Thus one who must be beaten with blows ought not to be corrected only by countenance and words, as is seen in Eli's example in 1 Samuel; nor, conversely, should one who must be reproved with a switch broken or cut from a willow be corrected with a whip or cudgel; nor should one who must receive a few strokes be afflicted with many; nor should that which ought to be employed more rarely, like medicine, be applied continually. To this pertains the provision in the law that, even for the greatest crime punished and expiated by flogging, the number of forty strokes must not be exceeded. For just as one sins against health if the medicine is either milder or harsher than the nature of the disease requires, so also one sins against the health of the soul if correction and punishment are not proportionate to the fault. Here it is required above all that νουθεσία, *admonition*, be joined to every reproof, as the Apostle counsels. For human beings are not to be struck like beasts; yet this is what happens when one is not held convicted of an admitted sin and when exhortation to repentance is not applied. And in this matter people are sometimes fairer to dogs than to human beings. For when a dog has killed a sheep, they beat it in the presence of the sheep so that by this means, insofar as it can be given, the dog may understand what it is for which it is beaten. And not only must νουθεσία, *admonition*, be applied, but νουθεσία κυρίου, *the admonition of the Lord*, drawn, that is, from the teaching of Holy Scripture. Parents commonly sin in this respect when they lead their children away from error by empty terrors, but not by threats drawn from the divine law.

In the latter half-verse he supplies another precaution for legitimate correction: it must be done promptly, as soon as the depravity of childhood even begins to sprout. They must not be spared because of the softness of the still-tender little body from having at least some moderate penalty inflicted for an admitted error; otherwise, if the sin puts down deeper roots, the one who could have been healed with a few blows, had they been applied at the proper time, may not be recalled to good behavior even by many and more severe blows. It is well known that, as long as a shoot is tender, it can be bent and bent back this way or that with little effort; but when it grows into a tree, it is more readily broken than bent. Next, unless nettles and thorns are uprooted early, they choke the grain. This is illustrated by foals and young cattle: unless they are broken and subdued before they become grown, they will sooner suffer their necks to be broken than allow themselves to be tamed. Hence Jeremiah professed that it was good that he bore the Lord's yoke from his earliest age. Conversely, Ecclesiastes 8:10 says that people become bolder for crimes because they are not speedily punished. How much better it is, by moderate correction early and at the beginning of life, to purge vices while preserving the children, than for the children themselves to be cut off early by God's just vengeance once their vices have gathered strength! Verse 25. The righteous person eats to the satisfaction of his soul, but the belly of the wicked suffers want.

In אכל, *eats*, there is a synecdoche of the part for all things pertaining to the maintenance of life.

It was said at chapter 12:26 that the righteous person, by the very fact that he is righteous, excels his neighbor who is not righteous. In the present passage his superior condition is declared even in those things which pertain to this life, because he is more abundantly provided with food and clothing than the wicked. Although this is sufficiently attested by other sayings, and particularly by verse 22 of this chapter and 10:3, God nevertheless, who knows well the depth of our distrust in these matters, is not content to set it forth generally unless he explains it part by part. Nor does he repeat it only once, but more frequently for greater certainty of the matter, just as Joseph clearly teaches the use of repeating a single matter.

How disinclined our minds are to admit this persuasion concerning God's care and provision for us is sufficiently declared by Christ the Savior's lengthy and closely reasoned sermon in Matthew 6. See Isaiah 65:13, where confirmation of both parts of the proverb is found.

If we investigate the causes of this satisfaction attributed to the righteous person, we will not find them all enclosed and located in harvests or in an abundance of riches. Rather, they lie partly in the fact that righteous people are satisfied with fewer things and with more sparing food than the wicked person, who employs scarcely any measure or restraint in eating and drinking; next, in the fact that God's blessing attends their provisions in a more abundant measure than those of the wicked—on which see Psalm 37 and the example of Daniel, Daniel 1; but especially in the fact that God's wonderful providence keeps closer watch over feeding them than over feeding the wicked. A wonderful example of this is found in Genesis 47, where the Israelites ate to satisfaction while the native Egyptians raged with hunger. It is also memorable that Elijah was fed by the most ravenous birds every morning and evening for an entire year. 1 Kings 17. Christ sealed this doctrine by many miracles, even among those who were not righteous but made a show of righteousness and of seeking the kingdom of heaven. Matthew 14:15; John 6. On the contrary, concerning the wicked see Psalm 59:15–16 and Micah 6:14. The want of the wicked arises not only from poverty and scarcity of resources, but also from their insatiable desire; next, because God withdraws his blessing (Micah 6); and lastly, because by his wonderful providence God brings it about that they lack even what they have. See 2 Kings 7:14–15.
