VERSE 2. Do not be hasty with your mouth, and do not let your mind hurry to
bring forth anything before God himself. For God himself is in heaven, but you
are upon the earth; therefore let your words be few.

After speaking about the chief and primary part of divine worship, namely the
hearing of the Word, he proceeds to the speech which we pour out before God.
He first treats in general every prayer which we direct to God; afterward he
brings his discourse down to one kind of speech, namely a vow.

Here the opinion of the papists must be diminished. In their prayers—some
recited and muttered, others chanted—they place the sum and head of religion.
But the error of some of our own people must also be diminished, for they
place more reliance for obtaining salvation in prayers than in the preaching
of the Word.

In our speech directed to God, however, he commands us to beware lest anything
be said too hastily with the mouth or resolved upon in the mind. He presents
the mind's unpremeditated and sudden resolve and determination as the seed and
cause of rash speech. Therefore, so that rashness may be absent from our
speech, we must certainly set a guard upon it, lest whatever first comes to
mind be immediately transferred to the mouth and directed to God. Thus,
whatever desire or fleshly wisdom suggests and supplies for us to say must not
at once be brought forth with our lips before God. It must be brought back to
the standard of the divine Word and weighed by it as by a goldsmith's balance.

Hence Jephthah made an ill-considered vow to God in Judges 9, because his mind
brought forth with his mouth what fleshly wisdom had dictated would be
pleasing to God. What is more, men of outstanding godliness and learning have
provoked God against themselves through excessive haste, blurting out with
their mouths whatever first came to mind. Of this kind are those statements
of David in Psalm 39:4–6. Not a few statements of the same kind are found in
Job, by which he summons God to court and, because of his grief, wishes
suddenly to perish. See also Jeremiah 15:10, 18 and 12:1–2, and so forth;
Jonah 4. If men so great and excellent stumbled by hurrying their speech, how
ready and prone are we to stumble at the same stone unless we exercise great
caution? For here, if anywhere, the common proverb is true: haste brings
devastation with it.

The reason for this accompanying caution is taken from God's place, heaven.
He is said to reside there because the splendor of his glory shines forth
there most especially, though he is equally present in every place. By this
form of speech Solomon wished to adorn and set before our eyes God's supreme
loftiness and majesty. Hence Christ, when preparing us to pray to God with the
highest reverence in Matthew 6, employs the same argument Solomon uses here.

It is customary for men, when they approach those who are in high station, to
exhibit honor and reverence in proportion to the rank in which those persons
are placed. Indeed, to signify their rank, such persons are raised upon thrones
and higher places. Since, then, God infinitely surpasses even the most august
of them in greatness and majesty, no submission can be so great as to
correspond to his loftiness. See the example of Elijah in 1 Kings 18:42, and
the example of Christ, the Mediator and Son of the Most High, in Matthew
26:38.

Moreover, we see how idolaters abase themselves before idols, the papists in
particular. When about to adore the cross, they approach barefoot, on bended
knees, crawling reverently from one end of the church to the highest part, so
that they may take away a kiss from it. To see what the ungodly infer from the
fact that God sits in heaven, look to Job; but what the godly ought to gain
from it is described in this passage.

He magnifies the caution which he commands us to exercise in prayer from the
opposite condition, the abasement of men, which he deduces from the place of
their habitation: men crawl upon the ground like worms. For God cast men down
to dwell upon the earth in order to teach them humility. When a prudent head
of household thrusts his guest down to the lowest place at the table, he
reminds that guest of his low condition. In the same way, by casting us down
to inhabit the earth together with brute beasts, God has prepared an antidote
for us against the poison of pride. See Jeremiah 22:29. If to this is also
added

the fact that we not only crawl upon the earth but are earth and dust—Genesis
18—and, above all, that through the intervention of sin we are rottenness, it
ought to beat down the raised eyebrow of a swollen mind. Therefore, whenever
we address God, let God's loftiness on the one hand and our abasement on the
other come into our minds, so that, by due consideration of each, we may
approach the throne of grace with the greatest caution.

Another caution in speech addressed to the Lord is that the person who will
speak before him should avoid prolixity. The princes of this age demand
brevity from their petitioners. The King of heaven demands the same from his
people, but with a very different end and purpose. Princes demand it so that
their petitioners do not cause them annoyance and so that they may have time
for the other duties of administering the commonwealth. This consideration
does not apply to God. As far as annoyance and fatigue are concerned, it is
all the same to him whether you finish in a moment or speak night and day for
an entire year. Princes, when they press for brevity, seek relief from their
own labor. God, when he commands concision, consults our good.

This must not be understood as though every lengthy speech to God were
rejected, for one finds that Christ spent a night in prayer. Rather, the
repetition and continual reiteration of one and the same thing is displeasing
and hateful to God, as though God were somewhat deaf or slow of understanding.
This is precisely what Christ condemns in Matthew 6, where he censures vain
repetitions as an imitation of a foolish poet. The Lord's Prayer refutes such
foolish repetition, for it is set forth as the standard to which we ought to
conform the speech we address to God.

It must not be forgotten, however, that such repetition is sometimes not only
free from blame but is even counted praiseworthy: first, when the thing
repeated is of the greatest concern either to us or to the glory of God; and
second, when the doubled utterance doubles the zeal and ardor of the mind,
without which repeated words are reckoned like a clanging cymbal. Another
reason employed by the Savior is to be taken from Matthew: the Father knows
all the things we need.

It is clear from this that papist worship is hateful to God, both public
worship in churches—especially when it is performed in violation of this
caution—and private worship in homes. It should be noted that one and the same
reason is assigned for these two cautions governing speech to God, and for
that reason is inserted between them as equally common to both.
