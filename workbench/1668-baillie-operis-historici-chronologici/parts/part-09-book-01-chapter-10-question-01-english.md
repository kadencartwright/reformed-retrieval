## CHAPTER X

### Questions and doubts concerning the fifth epoch

### QUESTION I

#### How many years this epoch contains from the destruction of the first temple to the death of Alexander, and how the number of those years is established?

Answer. In all the preceding epochs, from the beginning of the world to the
destruction of Solomon’s temple, we confidently gave the totals of the years;
and we demonstrated them all from explicit testimonies of Scripture, to the
great confirmation of faith and much consolation. But the case of this and the
following epochs is different. Scripture is more often silent in them, and
there is great disagreement among human writers, both ancient and recent.
Therefore, what can be advanced here must necessarily be handed down with less
certainty and confidence; and in that there is no serious disadvantage.

> Marginalia, printed p. 143: *In the later epochs the particular numbers of
> years are not as clear as in the earlier ones*.

By the sure and clear thread of Scripture, the Holy Spirit leads us from the
beginning of the world to the head of the present epoch, and from there,
especially through Daniel’s weeks, to the death of the Messiah. Almost this
entire epoch before us has reference to those weeks. In some parts of the
history of those weeks it seemed good to the Holy Spirit to be silent, just as
in the entire history that follows those weeks—excepting the events of a few
years recorded in the Acts of the Apostles and the Apocalypse. For God’s
purpose in sacred chronology is to lead the faithful, by divine faith, from
the creation of the world to Christ. After his death comes the final point of
time; enclosing it in his own hand, he willed it to lie hidden from mortals,
so that with more watchful diligence they might await, and with more ardent
longing await, the Lord’s return to judgment. It is enough for the faith and
consolation of the godly if the total sums of the periods from the beginning
of the world to Christ are evident from Scripture, even if some parts of those
sums cannot be shown clearly from Scripture. This is especially so in the
later divisions of time after Malachi and the last of the prophets. For then
the writings of the Old Testament were closed, and both the events and the
sequence of time in those centuries have been handed down abundantly by many
authors sufficiently worthy of human trust. It was otherwise in the earlier
centuries, where no human writings whatsoever survive, so that both the events
and the sequence of their times can be drawn from Scripture alone and from no
other source whatever.

As for the total of years now in question, although there is great discrepancy
among various authors, nevertheless those whom we usually follow differ from
one another by only a few years, and scarcely by more than four. Ussher and
Emmius agree upon the number 266. The former places the beginning of the
epoch—the destruction of the temple—in 588 before Christ, and its end—the
death of Alexander—in 323 before Christ. The sum that intervenes here,
including the endpoints, is 266. The latter, however, places the beginning of
the epoch in the year of the world 3360 and its end in 3626. The intervening
years yield the former total of 266.

> Marginalia, printed p. 143: *Among chronologists of the first rank,
> disagreement in this epoch is slight and amounts to only a few years*.

Capellus adds two, for he places the beginning of the epoch in 590 before
Christ and its end in 323 before Christ; the interval between them yields 268.

Henricus Philippi adds four, since he establishes the beginning as the same as
Capellus, the year 590 before Christ, but the end as 325; their interval is
270.

Petavius goes only one year beyond Henricus, but Calvisius goes beyond Emmius
by several, for he establishes Alexander’s death in 321 before Christ. And
although, with others, he places the subjugation of the Babylonians by Cyrus
in

[Printed p. 144 | IA leaf 174 | access PDF 174 | OCR object 173]

536 before Christ, he nevertheless postpones Cyrus’s edict concerning the
return of the Jews by a full seven years, to 529; by what reasoning he was
moved, I cannot ascertain.

We shall see afterward which of these totals we should choose, when their
members and parts have been explained. Nor can the latter part of the question
be properly answered until we have unraveled the various questions concerning
the different members of those totals. Three matters in particular must be
elucidated. First, what is the true head, and what is the foot, of the seventy
years of the Babylonian captivity? Second, what is the beginning and what the
end of the Persian monarchy? Third, how long did Alexander live after the
Persian Empire came to an end? These and other matters we shall endeavor to
explain, partly from the Scriptures and partly from human writings.

Here, however, we shall first state that we place by no means as much weight,
in establishing the limits and marks of these ancient times, upon eclipses of
the sun or the moon, or upon the motions of the fixed stars observed by the
Chaldaeans and others in the Persian and Greek Empires, as I see assigned by
various chronologists, Protestant as well as Roman Catholic, especially by
Petavius after Scaliger. Certainly, if perfect tables of the motions of the
fixed stars and planets from the beginning of the world to the present day
were at hand, and thus the precise times of all eclipses were placed before
our eyes—which would not be very difficult for the skilled mathematicians of
the present day to produce—and if Ptolemy were found to have correlated a few,
and other trustworthy ancient authors many, eclipses of the sun or moon with
the deeds of kings and commonwealths, we would admit that from this could have
been derived a most elegant, clear, and certain method of fixing times that
are otherwise most fluid. But since an interval of a single half-hour—not to
speak of a day, month, or year—produces a great discrepancy and a very
different appearance in the motion and position of the heavens, the stars,
and eclipses; and since very few chronologists, if indeed any, can be found
who do not differ from others by whole years in their calculation of times;
and since even the recorded eclipses that are joined with historical events
are read of only very rarely: in this celebrated argument from the motions of
the heavens, we indeed judge that by no means as much weight as many boast
should be placed on it for establishing times.
