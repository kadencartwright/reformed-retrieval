# Response to the Questions of Giorgio Biandrata

John Calvin — Geneva, 1558

This is a new English translation made directly from the normalized Latin in
`latin.txt` and checked sentence by sentence against the 1870 *Calvini Opera*
facsimile. It translates the complete response at CO IX 325–332. CO's title
leaf, source note, corrective note, and variant apparatus are documented in
the README but are not presented as Calvin's words. Biblical quotations are
translated from Calvin's Latin rather than replaced with the wording of a
modern English Bible. See the translator's notes after the text.

**[CO IX, p. 325]**

When we profess that we believe in one God, by the name *God* we understand
the one and simple essence, in which we comprehend three persons or
hypostases.[^1]

Accordingly, whenever the name *God* is used without personal qualification,
we believe that it designates the Son and the Spirit no less than the Father.
But when the Son is joined to the Father, a relation then comes into view, and
so we distinguish among the persons.

Because the properties in the persons carry an order with them, so that the
principle and origin are in the Father, whenever the Father and the Son are
mentioned together—or the Spirit as well—the name *God* is assigned
especially to the Father.

In this way the unity of the essence is retained and the order is taken into
account; yet this detracts nothing from the deity of the Son and the Spirit.

That the essence of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit is one is established
by clear testimonies of Scripture. Certainly Jehovah is one, and that name is
common to the Son with the Father.

If anyone objects that this designation is transferred to Christ because he
arose from the Father, so frivolous a quibble is easily refuted, because
Jehovah is one.

Now when the apostles assert that the Son of God is the one whom Moses and
the prophets testified to be Jehovah, it is always necessary to come back to
the unity of the essence.

For this reason, to call the Son another God than the Father is detestable
sacrilege to us. The simple name *God* admits no relation, nor can God be said
with reference to himself to be this one or that one.

**[CO IX, p. 326]**

When the term *person* is ascribed to Christ, however, it can be used in two
ways. The Word begotten from the Father before the creation of the world is a
person in the eternal essence of God. And just as Christ—God manifested in
the flesh—is one, so the two natures thus united constitute one person.
Therefore, it is one thing to speak of God's eternal Wisdom before he put on
flesh, and another to speak of the mediator from the time he was revealed in
the flesh.

The title *Lord* properly belongs to this person of the mediator, insofar as
he occupies a middle rank between God and us. Paul's statement applies here:
“One God, from whom are all things, and one Lord, through whom are all
things.” In the same sense we receive the statement, “The Father is greater
than I,” and the other things about the mediator that are read in John.

But where Christ is called God simply, and the name of the Father is not set
over against him, the whole essence of God is comprehended. Thus, when it is
said in John chapter 1, “That Word was God,” the unity of the essence must be
maintained. Similarly, where Paul says that God is blessed for ever, it is not
right to imagine another God than the one who declared through Moses that he
is the one and only God, and who swore through Isaiah that he would not give
his glory to another. John therefore, not content with the name *God*, speaks
more expressly at the end of his canonical epistle: “This is the true God and
eternal life.”

That the name *Jehovah*, taken without personal qualification, applies to
Christ is also evident from Paul's words: “For this reason I entreated the
Lord three times,” since he adds a little later, “so that the power of Christ
may dwell in me.”

It is no wonder that Christ, when speaking of the Father, distinguishes him
by the name *God*, because we must consider what role Christ sustains. Insofar
as he is mediator, he places himself in a lower rank with respect to his
office, so that he may lead us gradually to the Father.

**[CO IX, p. 327]**

In this respect the Son himself is subject to the Father in the age to come,
because the very perfection of the deity will then be visible to us, whereas
now it shines upon us in an image according to our capacity.[^2]

And yet Christ, though rarely, speaks of God without personal qualification,
as when he says in John 4, “God is Spirit,” which applies no less to his person
than to the Father's. It is no objection that he immediately expresses the
name of the Father, because after delivering a general doctrine he confirms
the same doctrine in keeping with his office as mediator.

In this way Christ manifested no God other than the Father. He himself had to
descend to us so that he might raise our faith to the glory of deity.
Meanwhile, his statement should be noted: “Do you believe in God? Believe
also in me.” It signifies that he himself is the God in whom faith ought to
rest.

When he gathers up the sum of the law, however, he detracts nothing from
himself by using the name *God* without personal qualification; he is simply
content with the accepted form of speech. So too his question, “Why do you
call me good? One God is good,” is not directed toward casting the glory of
deity away from himself. Rather, he intimates that he is good in a different
way from what the young man had understood when he honored him with a
designation of human goodness.

Similarly, when reproaching the Jews for boasting that God was their God, he
refutes their vanity because they nevertheless rejected his Son. They did not
acknowledge the mediator who was near at hand, while under the deceptive
pretext of God's name they soared grandly above the clouds.

Although Paul, in chapter 10 of First Corinthians, expressly sets the one God
over against idols, he nevertheless names the Father, “from whom are all
things,” not without reason, because immediately afterward he adds the
mediator in a relational contrast.[^3] But elsewhere (Ephesians 4:6 and the
following verse), when teaching that there is one God just as there is one
baptism, he surely understands one Jehovah. And although a little later he
expressly names the Father, he does this because he wished to descend to
Christ, and therefore such a distinction had to be employed. For when he
joins the unity of God and of baptism, he certainly does not exclude Christ,
in whose name we are baptized. The simple and unqualified designation of God
and the distinction of God the Father from Christ the mediator therefore
agree perfectly with one another.

The fact that at the beginnings of his epistles Paul places God the Father
first and then joins Christ to him proceeds from the same reason. He
establishes deity under the Father's name, and then interposes the mediator
who lifts us up to God. Yet in the same epistle in which he had spoken in this
way, he does not hesitate to call Christ “God blessed for ever” (Romans 9:5).

**[CO IX, p. 328]**

It follows that the God whom we must seek is the one in whose essence three
persons are comprehended. And yet, when Christ comes forward, the Father of
Jesus Christ is truly and properly called God, because Christ then occupies
the rank of mediator, so that he may raise us to God and thus to himself.

As for the passage in John 20:17, “I ascend to my Father and your Father,” it
confirms and declares more openly what I have already said. When Christ speaks
in the person of the mediator, he descends in a certain sense beneath God so
that he may be nearer to us. But this does not prevent him from also being our
true God. For what Thomas says he had learned from no other source than
Christ's own mouth: “My Lord and my God.”

I answer briefly concerning the term *person*: it was introduced by necessity
to expose the frauds of those who were craftily attempting to overthrow this
first principle of faith. Meanwhile, it is a foul quibble to object with
statements in which Christ, distinguishing himself from the Father, refrains
from this word. We do not use this formulation in ordinary speech—“The
person of the Father conferred salvation upon us through the person of the
Son”—but use it when we wish to express the distinction between the Father
and the Son.

As for Christ's statement, “This is eternal life, that they may believe in you
as the true God,” and so forth (John 17:3): if on its pretext true divinity had
to be restricted to the Father alone, John would have lied when he said,
“This is the true God and eternal life” (1 John 5:20).

But it is utterly false that Christ did not disclose the one essence in three
persons. When he willed us to be baptized in the name of the Father, the Son,
and the Holy Spirit, he certainly set out these persons distinctly, and it is
an offense to seek them outside the essence of God.

Now when John says that the Word was God from the beginning (John 1:1), he
certainly does not tear apart the essence of God; rather, he plainly teaches
that the Word was one and the same God with the Father. But when he
immediately adds that the Word was with God, the distinction is displayed
even to the blind. Likewise, when Christ himself asks that the glory he had
with the Father at the beginning, before the creation of the world, be
manifested (John 17:5), he certainly attributes to himself a distinct person,
and at the same time claims for himself the same essence as the Father,
because God's glory and majesty could not be separated from his essence.

Similarly, when Peter says to Ananias, “Why have you lied to the Holy Spirit?
You have not lied to men, but to God” (Acts 5:3 and the following verse), he
openly marks a distinction. And under the name *God*, which is contrasted
with men, Christ himself and the Spirit are included no less than

**[CO IX, p. 329]**

the Father. So too, when the same Peter and John answer that God must be
obeyed rather than men (Acts 4:19), they certainly do not exclude Christ.
Rather, because they use the name *God* without personal qualification, they
designate God manifested in the flesh no less than the Father.

If the term *person* displeases someone who is excessively fastidious, the
apostle in Hebrews 1 (verse 3) certainly means nothing by *hypostasis* other
than what we call a person. And that is the relation over which contentious
people quarrel, as if it were a human invention.

As for the form of prayer, the ordinary form prescribed for us in the gospel
is that we address our words to the Father in Christ's name.

But where no mention of Christ is made, prayer under the name *God* is
directed no less to the Son than to the Father, as is clearly evident from
that passage of Paul: “For this reason I entreated the Lord three times, and
he answered me, ‘My grace is sufficient for you,’” since Paul immediately
adds, “I will gladly glory in my weaknesses, so that the power of Christ may
dwell in me” (2 Corinthians 12:8 and the following verse). It follows that
Christ is the God whom Paul had entreated.

Moreover, it is frivolous and childish to restrict the word *Lord* to the
person of the mediator, since the apostles everywhere use the name *Lord* for
Jehovah. And so that an example need not be sought far away, Paul prayed to
the Lord in no other sense than the passage of Joel cited by Peter: “Whoever
calls upon the name of the Lord will be saved” (Acts 2:21).

Similarly, when the disciples pray in this form, “You, Lord, who know the
hearts of all” (Acts 1:24), they do not turn their address separately to
Christ, because the designation would not fit him in separation; rather,
their faith looks simply to the essence of God. In short, where there is no
relation, the name *God* extends to the Son and the Holy Spirit.

If it is asked whether the safer form of prayer is not to invoke the Father
in the name of the Son, I answer, as I have already said, that this is the
ordinary and more familiar form, one better suited and more useful to our
weak understanding. But to condemn other forms that were also used by the
apostles and that the Holy Spirit dictated to them would be sacrilegious
rashness. Nor is the objection valid that God is invoked in vain without the
mediator. Although the principle stands fixed that we are not heard except
through the mediator's grace, the mediator does not always have to be
expressly present to our thought.

This question—“When praying to the Father, do we pray to the true God?”—arises
from ignorance of the first principle. The epithet *true* does not distinguish
the Father from the Son, because the name *God* designates that essence which
embraces three persons. And so among them

**[CO IX, p. 330]**

there is no antithesis with respect to the one deity.

Furthermore, when praying to the Father we also direct our prayers to the
deity of the Son, although he himself comes between us. Different respects
must be observed here. Although what is proper to the Father resides in his
person and does not belong to the Son, God nevertheless remains whole and
entire in his essence. Consequently, these things do not conflict: that the
Father is prayed to with respect to the Son, and that God is prayed to in his
undivided essence, so to speak. This is not because the essence is divisible
in any way, but because there is nothing absurd when we conceive the deity as
common to the Father, the Son, and the Spirit, if contemplation of the Son
turns our minds back to the person of the Father.

Now although Christ is mediator insofar as he is God manifested in the flesh,
and therefore according to both natures, nevertheless, with respect to the
human nature, the office of interceding is properly attributed to him. This
serves as a support for our sluggishness.[^4] Hence Paul, when presenting him
to us as advocate, calls him man rather than God (1 Timothy 2:5). And surely,
just as he discharged the priestly office in the flesh when he offered his
flesh as a sacrifice, so too he intercedes for us.

When Christ himself is invoked—something Ananias testifies is done by all the
godly (Acts 9:14), and something Stephen's example establishes is rightly and
holily done (Acts 7:59)—this honor is not rendered to a man in himself or by
reason of his humanity, but to the eternal Son of God, who put on our flesh
to acquire salvation for us. For prayer is a sacrifice that God claims for
himself alone. It is therefore no more lawful to transfer it to a mere man
than to transfer the other parts of divine worship. Christ is thus invoked as
God, who is in himself the author of every good thing; and Stephen adorns him
with the full power of deity when he commends his spirit into Christ's hands.

The question whether God's eternal Word is something substantive and
essential is ambiguous and can therefore be captious. Concerning the term
*substance*, we must hold that it is not properly essence but hypostasis. If
someone prefers to take it otherwise, I readily concede the point. Thus the
Word has a hypostatic import. But since the Word himself is truly God, the
term also designates essence. We must therefore hold that the Word is
essentially God, and yet that in this same expression there is also a
designation of person. A person, however, subsists in the essence.[^5]

It is easy to infer from the preceding discussion that, if anyone

**[CO IX, p. 331]**

believes sincerely and without pretense or deceit in God the Father, in the
Lord Jesus Christ, and in one Spirit, that is sufficient. At the same time, we
may infer from it that those who profess to believe in Christ while denying
his eternal deity are acting deceitfully. The same is true of those who
profess belief in the Spirit but do not think him to be God if the Father is
God. It is plain that such people tear apart the essence of God and do not
retain its unity, since they cannot assert the Father's divinity without
taking it away from the Son and the Holy Spirit.

Those who spitefully slander the orthodox faith on the pretext that inquiry
about one essence and three persons is useless reveal their wicked malice. We
do not speculate beyond the height to which Scripture lifts us, but render
its genuine and straightforward meaning. No one will acknowledge Christ from
the heart as his God unless he apprehends the distinct persons in the unity
of the essence, for the plurality of gods that some fabricate is an
execrable delusion.

Those to whom this distinction appears excessively subtle must be admonished
to consider more attentively how deadly their counterfeit simplicity is. For
once they have confessed that Christ is God, they will never extricate
themselves from Satan's snares in any other way than by interposing this very
distinction from which they flee.

The Nicene Council had this in view when it asserted that Christ is God from
God. It did not mean to fabricate two gods, but to assert that, although the
Son is distinct from the Father, he is nevertheless true God and the same God
as the Father, apart from the designation of person.

So too, when Athanasius and the other ancient writers said that the Son is
coessential with the Father, they meant nothing other than that he is of the
same essence, because the essence is one and indivisible. But those who make
the gods two in number will never be able to escape from this absurdity
without making the essence of God divisible.

Thus they spoke of three coeternal persons and nevertheless of one eternal
God, because eternity resides only in the one God, although it is

**[CO IX, p. 332]**

common to the three persons. And just as two natures constitute one Christ,
because the same one who is God and man consists of two natures, so three
persons constitute one God, because his essence, which is one and simple,
consists in three persons.

When Scripture speaks about Christ, it attributes to him now what is proper
to the human nature, now what pertains to deity alone; more often, however,
it embraces both natures and describes the person of the mediator. We must
therefore consider carefully the designations that belong neither to the man
nor to God separately, but to God manifested in the flesh. Of this kind are
most of those read in John's Gospel from chapter 3 through chapter 18.

Now things predicated of Christ sometimes have a special reference to one of
the natures. Thus, the name *Christ* belongs not only to the man but to God
manifested in the flesh, while the anointing is properly referred to the
humanity. The mediator, God and man, is truly the Son of God according to both
natures by reason of their union, but properly so with respect to his deity,
because he is the Word begotten from the Father before the ages. For the Word
is said to have become flesh because he put on flesh, so that the one who was
eternal God might also be mortal man. This was not merely so that the humanity
might be the temple of deity, but so that, when the two natures had been
united in one person, the one through whom we had been created might also be
our redeemer.

It would take too long to examine all the forms of expression used by the
ancient writers. This ineffable mystery will never be explained properly and
in a manner worthy of it by any human words, and each writer has his own way
of speaking, so that even in the fullest agreement one speaks differently
from another. Justin and Tertullian understood the matter piously and rightly,
and neither intended to teach anything other than what we confess. But since
the former was not so practiced, while the latter everywhere uses a harsh and
thorny style of speech, it is no wonder if expressions occur here and there
that are vulnerable to quibbles. They are nevertheless easily defended, if
only the passion for wrangling and slander will depart.

## Translator's notes

[^1]: Calvin uses both Latin *persona* and the Greek accusative plural
    *hypostaseis*. The translation retains “hypostases” because the equivalence
    and distinction of these technical terms become part of his argument.

[^2]: The future subjection of the Son alludes to 1 Corinthians 15:28. The
    qualification “in this respect” is essential to Calvin's distinction
    between the mediator's ordered office and the undiminished divine essence.

[^3]: CO's main text says chapter 10 of First Corinthians. Its editors add a
    separate corrective note, “rather, 1 Corinthians 8:5 and the following
    verse.” The translation preserves Calvin's transmitted citation error;
    the quoted formulation itself corresponds to 1 Corinthians 8:6.

[^4]: CO prints *ratione et proprie* (“with respect ... and properly”) in the
    main text. Its apparatus reports that a corrector's hand in the manuscript
    changed *et* to *ei* (“to it”). The translation follows the grammar of the
    CO main reading without silently substituting the correction.

[^5]: *Substantivum*, *essentiale*, *hypostaticum*, and *essentialiter* are
    deliberately represented by related technical English terms. Smoothing
    them into unrelated paraphrases would conceal the terminological
    distinction Calvin is making.
