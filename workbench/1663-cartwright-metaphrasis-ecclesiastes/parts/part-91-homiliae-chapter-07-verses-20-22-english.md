VERSE 20. Although no one is righteous on earth who does good and does not
sin.

In the former part of the exhortation a reason is proposed, drawn from the
adjuncts: imperfect righteousness, innocence, which attaches to everyone,
even the most righteous. Here it must be noted that the Prophet does not deny
that someone is righteous on earth, nor that anyone does good; rather, he
denies that anyone does not sin. From this it follows that in this place the
Prophet is speaking about those who, regenerated through faith in Christ, are
justified. For concerning those who have not been regenerated, Scripture
categorically denies that anyone is righteous or does what is good.

Therefore, both this passage and many other testimonies of Scripture teach
that those justified through Christ by grace infused through Christ are not
perfectly righteous, but repeatedly turn aside from the way of God's
commandments. 1 Kings 8:40; Psalm 143:12; 1 John 1:8. From this it is clear
that no one is justified by the works of the Law that he himself performs,
both because he repeatedly violates the Law of God and because, even in the
things he does uprightly and with praise, he nevertheless does not fulfill the
perfect and exact righteousness of the Law.

This axiom refutes the Papists, who attribute the first justification to the
death of Christ but place the second, and principal, justification in infused
grace. It likewise refutes the Anabaptists, who profess themselves pure from
every sin. Nor does this conflict with what is in 1 John 3, that the one who
abides in God does not sin. For John means that they do not sin as the wicked
do, in whom sin predominates, nor do they sin unto death, as in 1 John 5:17.

VERSE 21. Also, do not apply your mind to every word that is spoken, since you
ought not to hear even your servant cursing you.

From this Solomon draws the exhortation that the mind must not be applied to
everything that is said or done. For in the word הדברים, “the words,” there is
a Synecdoche. Then, by warning that one should not apply the mind to those
things, he signifies that we ought not to direct our mind toward an injury
inflicted by word or deed in such a way that we pursue it. With this meaning
Abigail urges David in 1 Samuel 25:25 not to direct his mind toward the insults
and reproaches that her husband Nabal had hurled at him. See the similar
expression in 2 Samuel 9:20, where it also explains expressly what it is to
apply the mind to an inflicted injury.

For if someone seriously applies his mind to weighing all the circumstances
of the injury with which he is afflicted, in order to sharpen himself for
revenge, it immediately happens that the mind devises a course and method of
retaliation; the mouth is unbridled for insults; the teeth are sharpened and
gnash; the feet take to the road to hurry to carry it out; the hands are
raised and the fists clenched to strike; and servants and dependents are
summoned to contribute their labor and strength in repaying vengeance. Thus
the shortest way of restraining all these things is for the mind to be calm
and tranquil.

If someone has provoked another by inflicting an injury, and fears that the
other person may bring equal or greater harm upon him, nothing is readier or
more prepared upon his lips than immediately to set before that person the
stain common to all people because of sin and their propensity to sin, so that
he may recall the person provoked by the injury to moderation and gentleness
of mind. But when he himself is injured, he does not think of this even in a
dream, though Solomon teaches that it ought most especially to come into his
mind.

How unjustly matters are arranged when you refuse to measure out to another
the same measure by which you desire him to measure to you. Here too that
saying of Solomon has a place, Proverbs 20:3, 23: “A measure and a measure are
an abomination to the Lord.” It is therefore detestable to wish your brother
to use clemency when he sins against you, while you are implacable toward him
when he harms you: provoked by one little word,

you flare up and return an insult, and not infrequently you even employ
violence; but when you assail another, you decide that the matter is unworthy
to be passed over in silence. Serious reflection on that saying in Proverbs
20:3 will correct this perversity of mind, especially among those who leap
toward vengeance, lest they cast themselves into the contempt of others. Let
David be an example: he did not apply his mind to Nabal's insults and Shimei's
curses. See also 1 Samuel 10:27.

When Solomon forbids the heart to be applied to all injuries, he seems to
permit as just and allowable that someone should retaliate evil for repeated
and doubled injuries. Yet this is prohibited by Christ, who commands us to
forgive a brother who sins against us even if he offends us seventy times
seven in one day. We must therefore understand that Solomon's precept does not
conflict with Christ's command. For Solomon admonishes us here that we should
sometimes not pursue a loss done to us in order to have it repaired, but
should pass it over in silence just as if it had not been inflicted. Christ,
however, prohibits vengeance alone, yet does not prevent someone from pursuing
his grievance or right arising from an injury, provided that his mind is
averse to vengeance.

Thus Solomon, in exhorting us here to overlook certain things, does not
prevent someone who has been repeatedly provoked from punishing a servant,
seeking legal redress against another, or obtaining compensation for damage
to his reputation or wealth, provided that he does this to protect himself
and not to harm another.

So much for the former part. The second part follows, concerning moderation
of mind toward the lowest, even servants. It was not so necessary to admonish
servants to overlook their masters' curses, or subjects not to answer curses
to rulers who curse them—for there is a Synecdoche of a part for all those
over whom supreme power has been given to us. Besides the fact that they are
prevented by duty from doing this, they are restrained from this offense by
the penalties and punishments with which those who are so insolent toward
their superiors are customarily bound.

But this needed to be enjoined upon masters and Rulers, who hold in their
hands the power to harm with impunity. Therefore, to set forth a τεκμήριον, a
proof, of patience and moderation of mind, the Prophet calls for a test of a
master's equanimity toward his servant. What he says about a master not
hearing his servant curse him is a metonymy of an adjunct: hearing stands for
the pursuit of vengeance that ordinarily follows the hearing of a curse. For
it is not contrary to duty to understand with what disposition a servant and
subject regards him, or to know and perceive the impatience of a servant or
subject. Rather, he means that every injury is not to be pursued, but that
some must not only be forgiven but wholly overlooked.

This is not to be understood as though one ought not to rebuke a person who
hurls insults to one's face, for that would be attributed to a master's
ignorance rather than to patience. Rather, one should sometimes overlook an
injury when, without the servant's knowledge, one hears him cursing or learns
of it from elsewhere. The Prophet teaches here that the duly perceived and
adjudged sins of slaves and bondsmen, even the most abject, must sometimes be
overlooked, just as God overlooks our sins.

People have been found among the profane nations who calmly and patiently
endured the insults of their male and female servants with the purpose of
fishing out what they desired to know. How much more must we do this in order
to render obedience to God and give evidence of so distinguished a virtue,
one proper to the pious? In order to prove to everyone his fairness in
governing his household, Job professes that he permitted his male and female
servants to dispute their case with him freely and fearlessly. He did this
both from a consciousness of duty and from fear of divine wrath, Job
32:13–15. By doing so he indicts all masters who,

moved by impatience, do not admit their male and female servants to a lawful
defense of themselves. If they themselves were not given the opportunity to
appear in court before their own people, Rulers, or judges, to defend
themselves, and to plead their case freely, even if they did not dare complain
that they had been treated most unjustly, they would certainly settle the
matter firmly in their own mind.

How unjust an appraiser of affairs you are, then, when you regard something as
unjust in a Ruler toward a subject but practice it yourself toward a servant
as though it were just and allowable. We must also fear lest those who deny
their servants this opportunity to argue their case should sometime be
compelled, by God's just judgment, to experience the same injustice and
impatience among others who are their superiors. Thus Job asks God to allow
him to defend himself without fear, and not to overwhelm him with terror and
thereby take away his ability to speak and dispute, Job 9:35–36.

Rulers and masters must likewise take care not to deprive their subjects or
servants of the ability to speak—before their case has yet been argued—by
threats, terrifying words, and thunderings. Authority and power alone more
than sufficiently terrify people of the lowest rank; if reproaches, insults,
and an angry countenance are added, people are so dismayed that they are
scarcely themselves. Nor does the Apostle's prohibition against servants
answering their masters, Titus 2, conflict with this teaching. It is one thing
to answer back, another to answer and state a just case modestly.

If it belongs to masters and Rulers to bear calmly the curses of servants and
subjects, even when undeservedly hurled at them, how much more must subjects
and servants do this when masters and Rulers load their servants or subjects
with unprovoked insults.

VERSE 22. For your mind is also conscious that you too have often cursed
others.

The reason for this exhortation is attached from the accompanying
consciousness of an injury that one has inflicted upon others. The Apostle
uses this argument in Titus 3:3 to exhort the pious to calmness and gentleness
toward everyone, even the lowest: “For we too,” he says, “were once foolish,
disobedient, wandering, serving various desires and pleasures, living in
malice and envy, hateful and pursuing one another with hatred.”

For a serious awareness of our sins instructs us in gentleness and humility,
and it is a signal benefit that we are permitted to receive from such
destructive fruits. Those, therefore, who are proud and cruel toward others
who offend are touched by no awareness of their own sins. Someone proves
especially unjust if, though he often sins against others, he is nevertheless
not bent toward this patience and gentleness. If an offense committed against
one's neighbor ought to instruct us in humility and gentleness, how much more
should those things undutifully committed against God do so?

It must further be noted that a person conscious of his own offenses ought to
be indulgent toward those who sin against him, even if other people are
unaware of his offenses. For he would seem absurd and shameless if, when his
wickedness becomes known to others, he did not use clemency toward other
people who sin in the same kind.

The Apostle uses another argument in Galatians 6:1–2 by which he bends people
toward this gentleness toward those who offend. For we must spare others who
sin not only because of our consciousness of our own sins, but also because
of the very fact that we can sin through the viciousness of our nature, even
though we have not yet fallen into some disgraceful offense. We must fear that
if we have been more severe toward others, we may experience the same severity
from others. Indeed, we must fear that, by God's just judgment, we may be
handed over to a reprobate mind and commit the same things, or worse ones,
against which we rage so fiercely.
