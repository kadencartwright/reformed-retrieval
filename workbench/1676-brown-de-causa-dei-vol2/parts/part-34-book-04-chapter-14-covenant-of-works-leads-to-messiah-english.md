## The covenant of works was proposed to the Israelites so that they might be driven to the promised Messiah

Although above, in chapter 10, where we undertook to prove that the covenant
made with the Israelites was not the covenant of works, we conceded that the
covenant of works was then proposed to them—though in a very different manner
from that in which it had been proposed to Adam in the state of innocence—and
showed that this was done so that, convicted of their sins, they might flee to
the promised Messiah to obtain salvation, nevertheless, for the sake of
certain Anti-Sabbatarians, I wish to explain and confirm this somewhat more
fully.

It cannot be denied that God set the covenant of works before the Israelites,
for the Apostle expressly testifies in Romans 10:5 that *Moses describes the
righteousness which is from the law* in these words: *The person who performs
these things will live by them*—namely, Leviticus 18:5. Thus Moses says in
Deuteronomy 27:26, *Cursed is everyone who does not continue in all the things
written in the book of the law, to do them.* Paul shows in Galatians 3:10 that
this is the tenor of the covenant of works. Dr. Burmann himself, in *Synopsis*,
book 4, chapter 6, section 9, acknowledges that the Decalogue, armed with those
two declarations, exhibited a certain form of the covenant of works and was a
reminder of the violated covenant of works. [He says: *Not that God at any
time established or renewed the covenant of works with a sinner, for that was
impossible. But the Decalogue, armed with those two declarations, exhibited a
certain form of the covenant of works and was an ἀνάμνησις, a reminder, of the
violated covenant of works and a daily reproach for their faithlessness. Thus
the Israelites were to recognize their sin and curse and the impossibility of
righteousness by the law. Driven away from that hope, they were to thirst for
the righteousness of their Redeemer and be led by the hand to Christ; for
Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes*
(Romans 10:4).]

But in section 7 of the same place Dr. Burmann denies that this was the form
of the Decalogue at its first promulgation, for he says in section 6 that at
its first promulgation it contained nothing but commands to faith and
repentance. From where, then, did the change arise? [*A certain change was
made*, he says in section 8, *after the people's apostasy and sin concerning
the golden calf* (Exodus 32). *When the tablets of the covenant were broken
by Moses and the people were rejected by God for a time, they were afterward
treated harshly and burdened with the yoke of ceremonies* (Jeremiah 31:32).
*Only then were those two declarations heard which did not stand in the
Decalogue: “The person who does these things will live by them,” and “Cursed
is everyone who does not continue in all things”* (Leviticus 18:5;
Deuteronomy 27:26). *These exhibited in themselves the contract and
stipulation of the covenant of works. It is under this aspect that the Apostle
considers the law when he presents it as the formula of the covenant of works*
(Romans 10:5; compare Galatians 3:10–11) *and opposes it to the promise and to
faith* (Romans 4:14; Galatians 3:12).]

These claims, however, rest on no solid foundation. For, 1. We nowhere read
in Scripture that such a change was made in the Decalogue because of the
people's apostasy. We know that the tablets of the covenant were broken by
Moses, but we also read that they were afterward restored

[Printed p. 154 | IA PDF 162 | IA leaf 162 | IA decausadei02brow]

and that the same words which were written on the former tablets were
engraved on them by the finger of God. 2. We grant that those two declarations
were heard afterward. But we maintain that words exhibiting the contract and
stipulation of the covenant of grace were also heard afterward—for example,
Deuteronomy 30:11–14: *For this commandment which I command you today is not
hidden from you, nor is it far away. It is not in heaven, that you should say,
“Who will ascend into heaven for us, take it, bring it to us, and declare it
to us, so that we may do it?” Nor is it beyond the sea, that you should say,
“Who will cross the sea for us, take it, bring it to us, and declare it to us,
so that we may do it?” But this word is very near you, in your mouth and in
your mind, that you may do it.* The Apostle testifies in Romans 10:6–10 that
these words contain the righteousness which is from faith: *But the
righteousness which is from faith speaks in this way: Do not say in your
heart*, and so forth. Moreover, in the same chapter there is a promise which
is purely evangelical and belongs to the covenant of grace. Verse 6: *And
Jehovah your God will circumcise your mind and the mind of your seed, that you
may love Jehovah your God with all your heart and with all your soul, so that
you may live.* 3. It is true that those two declarations did not stand in the
Decalogue, but neither did they stand in it afterward, even when they had been
pronounced. 4. They rebelled against God more than once. Was a change in that
administration of the covenant therefore made each time? Indeed, before the
law was given they transgressed God's commands (Exodus 17:7; Deuteronomy
9:22). Why does it not follow from this that the Decalogue exhibited the
stipulation of the covenant of works when it was first promulgated? 5.
Although we do not read the same words before that apostasy, we nevertheless
read words of equal force. What else do these words in Exodus 19:5 mean—*If
you diligently attend to my voice and keep my covenant, you will surely be my
peculiar possession above all other peoples*—than *The person who does these
things will live by them*? And these words in Deuteronomy 5:28, 29, 33: *Oh,
that they had such a mind as this, to fear me and observe all my commandments
all their days, that it might go well with them and their children—walk in all
that way which Jehovah your God has commanded you, that you may live and that
it may go well with you.* Likewise Deuteronomy 6:18, 24: *That you may do what
is right and what seems good in the eyes of Jehovah, that it may go well with
you—and Jehovah commanded us to perform all these statutes, to fear Jehovah
our God, that it may go well with us all our days and that he may preserve us
alive, as today.* And 8:1: *Take care to observe every commandment which I
command you today, that you may live.* And verses 19–20: *For if you utterly
forget Jehovah your God and follow other gods—I testify against you this day
that you will surely perish. As these nations which Jehovah destroys before
you, so you will perish, because you did not listen to the voice of Jehovah
your God.* Are these not equivalent to *The person who does these things will
live by them* and *Cursed is everyone who does not continue in all things*?
6. The passage in Jeremiah 31:32 proves more strongly that the Decalogue at
its first promulgation exhibited a certain form of the covenant of works than
it proves the contrary. For God promises that under the gospel he will make
something different from that covenant which they made void. Therefore, under
that covenant the Decalogue contained something other than commands to faith
and repentance. If this opinion of Burmann were true, the Lord would rather
have said that he would make a covenant different from the one—not which he
made with their ancestors on the day he took them by the hand to lead them out
of the land of Egypt, and which they made void, but—which had later been
broken and then renewed with the addition of new declarations exhibiting the
stipulation of the covenant of works. Indeed, there

[Printed p. 155 | IA PDF 163 | IA leaf 163 | IA decausadei02brow]

are some who expressly say (see Ball, *On the Covenant*, English edition, page
96 and following, where he merely presents this opinion of certain people)
that the moral law, as first proposed, had the form of the covenant of works,
but afterward had evangelical ἐπιείκεια, or leniency, mixed with it. They wish
to prove this from the fact that before the law was first proposed such great
and strict preparation was required of the people: they were forbidden to
touch the mountain, and so many terrible signs of terror were added, and so
forth. But when the tablets were broken, this arrangement vanished. Moses
interceded for the people (Exodus 32; Deuteronomy 9) and was commanded to
ascend to God (Exodus 34). At that point a very great alteration appeared.
Moses himself was commanded to prepare tablets, so that they might understand
that God now wished to employ the work of a mediator more than before. No
preparation was required here, and God produced no signs of terror. Moses was
commanded to prepare the ark, not only to preserve the tablets but also to
cover wrath and the curse. The people were not permitted to look at the
tablets themselves (1 Samuel 6:19), but at the copy belonging to Moses the
mediator, together with its explanations and mitigations. When Moses appeared
with the tablets which he had prepared, God openly declared himself merciful
and longsuffering, and so forth (Exodus 34:5), and then announced the covenant
to Moses (verse 27). When Moses came down from the mountain, he covered his
face, signifying by this act that the curse was covered (2 Corinthians 3).
Such is their view. This opinion is indeed much more probable than that of
Burmann and the others. It nevertheless does not please me, both because of
what has already been said and because Scripture mentions a covenant of one
kind made with the Israelite people, not covenants of different species.

Nor do I sufficiently understand what he intimates here and afterward states
more plainly, namely in chapter 7, sections 9–10: [*After the sin of the calf*,
he says, *the condition of the church changed and became worse. God had until
then regarded them as sons and, having freed them from the Egyptian prison,
had received them into his household. But now he resolved to treat them more
harshly, like servants who had unlearned the servility contracted in Egypt,
and exercised dominion over them* (Jeremiah 31:32). *As the Apostle explains
the Prophet's words in Hebrews 8:9, he began to disregard them. A large part
of them perished in that long wandering through the deserts. The others,
burdened with various ceremonies, were commanded to serve God under stricter
bondage and, spending their lives in servile ministries, to approach and
worship God not as Father but as Lord. Ezekiel 20:25–26 teaches this most
clearly, where it says that because of the sin committed in the wilderness
God gave them statutes which were not good. It also says that he declared
them unclean by their offerings and, passing over the firstborn and taking
the Levites in their place, kept them from the altar. Likewise Paul says in
Galatians 3:19 that the law was added because of transgressions. Hence, when
God's covenant was renewed and Moses received the second tablets, which were
armed with more severe words, he finally imposed the entire apparatus of
ceremonies and its yoke on the nation*, and so forth.]

These things seem to me not at all sound. For, 1. Scripture nowhere says that
after the sin of the calf the condition of the church changed and became
worse, or that God resolved to treat it more harshly, whatever he may have
threatened against the rebels. Indeed, the course of the history plainly shows
that God instead treated them better as regards their ecclesiastical state
and ecclesiastical ordinances. 2. Scripture does not say that God gave them
the yoke of ceremonies because of this sin of the calf. As for Ezekiel 20:25,
we demonstrated fully above in book 1, chapter 13, how wretchedly this text is
distorted here. Indeed, this fact alone can suffice to expose the misuse

[Printed p. 156 | IA PDF 164 | IA leaf 164 | IA decausadei02brow]

that those statutes which were not good were given not to the fathers who
first rebelled
against God in the wilderness itself, but to their children. The Lord had
said to them in verse 18, *Do not walk in your fathers' statutes or observe
their ordinances.* Nevertheless, according to verse 21, they rebelled against
God, so that God said he would pour out his burning anger on them, but at that
time he spared them (verse 22). We therefore cannot understand by those
statutes which were not good the apparatus of ceremonies which, immediately
after the covenant was renewed, had been given during the lifetime of those
very fathers who broke the covenant. Rather, they were plagues, by a not
unusual antanaclasis, or repetition of a word in a different sense. See
Jeremiah 23:2. The words in verse 26 also have a far different meaning from
the claim that, after setting aside the firstborn and removing them from the
altar, he took the Levites in their place, as the words themselves show even
at first glance. For the Lord defiled them—or, as the Dutch annotations have
it, declared them unclean—by making them desolate and so giving them over to
their iniquities that they caused everyone who opened the womb to pass through
the fire. The words *through the fire* must be supplied from verse 31, as the
Dutch annotations state; they further show that these words are also omitted
in Leviticus 18:31. See also Ezekiel 16:21. Moreover, the Levites—or at least
the priests—had been appointed before that time (Exodus 28). 3. Before that
sin was committed, very many ceremonies had already been instituted, as can
be seen in Exodus 20:22 and following, immediately after the covenant was
made. See also chapters 22 and 23. The tabernacle and all its furnishings were
appointed to be made in chapters 25–27. Suitable craftsmen were prepared to
make the tabernacle in chapter 31. The order of the priesthood, with all its
ceremonies, was instituted in chapters 28–29, and the altars and duties to be
performed about them in chapter 30. Thus, even before that sin was committed,
they were commanded to confess their sins, to inscribe them on the altars by
sacrifices and the blood of livestock, and to pour out at the tabernacle the
groans and sighs of a conscience which labored and was never sufficiently
glad. This was a perpetual and public reproach for guilt which still remained,
which they themselves had not only to bear but also to carry out, as he says
above about the ceremonies instituted afterward. 4. Indeed, after that
covenant had been sanctioned and solemnly confirmed with blood (Exodus
24:3–8), Moses ascended the mountain, where he remained for forty whole days
and forty nights, to receive from the lawgiver all those statutes which
concerned divine worship (Exodus 24:12–18; 25 and following). 5. The Vulgate
edition and certain others render the words of Jeremiah 31:32, ואנכי בעלתי בם,
*I exercised dominion over them*, or *I was their lord*. Münster renders them
in this way: *Even when I had dominion over them, as a husband.* Junius and
Tremellius: *And I had remained joined to them in marriage.* The Dutch version
has *hoewel ick getrouwt hadde*, that is, *although I had married them.* The
English version has *Although I was an husband unto them*, that is, *although
I was their husband.* See the English and Dutch annotations on the passage.
The French version of 1570 has *combien que je leur fusse mari*, “although I
was married to them,” in the same sense. See also the parallel passage in
Jeremiah 3:14, כי אנכי בעלתי בכם, which the Vulgate version renders, *because I
am your husband*, and Junius and Tremellius, *for I am joined to you in
marriage.* The verb signifies *to take a wife, to become a husband*
(Deuteronomy 21:13; 24:1). See also Genesis 20:3; Deuteronomy 22:22; Isaiah
54:5; 62:4–5. It is true that the Apostle, like the Seventy, renders these
words differently, namely, *I disdained them*, or *I disregarded them*. Yet it
does not follow from this that after that time the Israelites were commanded
to approach and worship God not as Father but as Lord. For afterward we often
read that they approached God

[Printed p. 157 | IA PDF 165 | IA leaf 165 | IA decausadei02brow]

as Father and were commanded to approach him as such (Jeremiah 3:4, 19; Hosea
2:16; Isaiah 63:16; 64:8; Deuteronomy 32:6). Indeed, in all those ministries
believers were bound to approach God as their Father in Christ, who was
foreshadowed by those types. 6. When Paul says in Galatians 3:19 that *the law
was added because of transgressions*, he speaks of the law both before and
after that sin of the calf, because he speaks of the law which appeared 430
years afterward. More than this, he speaks of the law which was ordained
through angels by the hand of a mediator (verses 17, 19, 20).

It is now acknowledged that the covenant of works was proposed in that Mosaic
economy—not so that the Israelites might be justified by it, as the carnal
Jews dreamed (Romans 9:31–32; 10:3), but so that, renouncing their own
righteousness, which from the proposed covenant they could readily discern to
be very imperfect, they might betake themselves to Christ, in whom they were
to possess a righteousness most perfect and absolutely complete in every
respect. As is evident from what has been said, no solid foundation has yet
appeared for distinguishing between the law at its first promulgation on
Mount Sinai and the law after the rebellion committed in making the calf. We
know that the Mosaic economy was not completed on its very first day, but it
can by no means be proved that so many varied and distinct states of that
church ought to be invented. Nor do we see by what authority it can be said
that the state of that church was free after the law was given, but that after
the law and testament were violated the yoke of ceremonies succeeded it and
the state of the church, free until then, began to be servile, as Dr. Burmann
says in *Synopsis*, book 4, chapter 26, section 4. Indeed, in chapter 25,
section 1, he himself says that the economy of the Old Testament was called
legal because it consisted of a threefold law—moral, ceremonial, and forensic.
Consequently, that legal state of the church began with the promulgation of
the moral law, the first institution of ceremonies, and the enactment of
political laws; throughout that economy it was one and the same, not one thing
and then another.

In section 2 of the same place he says that *this economy of the Old Testament
differed from the Old Testament itself, of which it was merely an adjunct.* I
wish he had explained this, for his meaning is not sufficiently clear to me.
I know how that economy differs from the testament or covenant of grace
itself, of which it is merely the administration and an adjunct. But I do not
yet see how it is distinguished from the Old Testament, for the administration
of the covenant under the law by promises, prophecies, sacrifices,
circumcision, the Passover lamb, and other types and ordinances delivered to
the Jewish people, all of which foreshadowed the Christ to come—or the
covenant thus administered—is called the Old Testament.

Nevertheless, the distinction which he proposes in the same place must be
maintained: namely, between the law, or the covenant of works itself, and that
legal economy. For this legal economy was nothing other than a certain economy
of the covenant of grace under which the law of works was repeated.

We must also maintain the distinction between the law taken broadly and the
law taken strictly. The law taken broadly is that whole Mosaic economy of the
covenant of grace, or the Old Testament as opposed to the New Testament—that
is, to the evangelical dispensation of the covenant of grace. The law taken
strictly, however, is the covenant of works itself, which is opposed to the
covenant of grace.

That this law taken strictly was proposed not so that by observing it they
might pursue righteousness

[Printed p. 158 | IA PDF 166 | IA leaf 166 | IA decausadei02brow]

before God with a view to justification, but so that they might be
led to Christ, the mediator of the covenant of grace, can be proved by various
testimonies of Scripture, namely:

1. Romans 10:4: *For Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone
who believes.* By *law* here he also understands the moral law, or every law
by which the Israelites sought to establish their own righteousness (Romans
9:31–32). This law was given to the Israelites for this end, that they might
be led to Christ, although many did not perceive this end. See Calvin on the
passage.

2. Verses 6–8 of the same chapter, compared with Deuteronomy 30, where Moses
set before them life and good, death and evil. He commanded them to love God
with all their heart and all their soul and to observe his commandments,
statutes, and judgments, that they might live, increase, and be blessed by God.
If they were unwilling to listen, he declared that they would perish.
Moreover, he called heaven and earth as witnesses against them that he had set
before them life and death, blessing and curse (verses 15–18), things which
sufficiently savor of the covenant of works. In the same chapter he also
describes to us the righteousness which is from faith when he says, *This
commandment which I command you today is not hidden from you*, and so forth
(verses 11–14), according to the Apostle in Romans 10:6–8. [*It is therefore
evident*, says Beza on the passage, *that the Apostle interprets Moses' words
in such a way as to teach that Christ is the end of the law—that is, that in
him alone, apprehended by faith, what Moses said about the law is fulfilled:
namely, that if it were performed, it would pacify consciences.*]

3. Second Corinthians 3:13. Even that law which was the killing letter and
the ministry of death and condemnation had an end on which the Israelites
could not fix their eyes, because their minds had grown callous. That is, on
account of the blindness of their hearts they could not see that goal toward
which the law looked, namely Christ. Nevertheless, Christ was exhibited there
in such a way that he could be seen in that dispensation, although it was
obscure. Therefore the law in some manner intended Christ, and Christ was the
goal of that law. Calvin says that the words in verse 16, *to Moses*—that is,
to the law—must be understood in this way: [*For since its end is Christ, to
whom it ought to have been referred, it was twisted in another direction when
the Jews excluded Christ from it. Therefore, just as they wander through
byways in the law, so the law is wrapped up for them like a labyrinth until
it is referred to its end, which is Christ. Therefore, if the Jews seek Christ
in the law, God's clear truth will be opened to them. As long as they wish to
be wise apart from Christ, they will wander in darkness and never arrive at
the law's true sense.*]

4. Galatians 3:17–18. That law which is opposed to the promise—so that if the
inheritance were from it, it would not be from the promise—did not abolish the
promise but was added because of transgressions. From this it is evident that
this law of works was given for a special end: namely, that they might see
their guilt more clearly and fix their eyes on the seed who was to come.
[*Because of transgressions*, says Beza on the passage, *that is, so that when
their transgressions had been disclosed, people might understand that they
were saved by the grace of God alone, which he had revealed and promised to
Abraham, and thus that everyone should look to Christ.*]

5. Verse 23 of the same chapter: *They were kept under the law's guard, shut
up unto the faith which was afterward to be revealed.* Therefore, that law
(under which the covenant of works may be included insofar as it formed a
part of that economy), under whose guard they were kept, looked toward that
faith—or Christ, who is apprehended by faith—which

[Printed p. 159 | IA PDF 167 | IA leaf 167 | IA decausadei02brow]

was afterward to be revealed. This law had, as it were, confined them in a
prison, shutting them up under sin, as verse 22 says, so that, constrained by
necessity, they might embrace Christ by faith. [*For the law*, says Calvin on
the passage, *did not keep them away from faith, but kept them within the
bounds of faith so that they would not wander outside them. There is an
elegant allusion here to what he had said before, that Scripture shuts up all
things under sin. Therefore, just as they were besieged on every side by the
curse, so they had a guard set against this siege which protected them from
the curse.*]

6. Verse 24 of the same chapter: *And so the law was our pedagogue leading to
Christ.* [*For by manifesting God's righteousness*, says Calvin on the
passage, *the law convicted people of their own unrighteousness. In God's
commandments, as in a mirror, they could see how far they were from true
righteousness. Thus they were warned that righteousness must be sought
elsewhere. The legal promises had the same function, for they had to reason in
this way: “If you cannot obtain life by works otherwise than by fulfilling the
law, you must seek a new and different way, for the weakness within you will
never permit you to rise so high.” On the other side, the threats pressed and
urged them to seek an escape from God's wrath and curse. Indeed, they did not
allow them to rest until they had spurred them to seek Christ's grace.*] See
the same author in *Institutes*, book 2, chapter 7, section 11. No one should
suppose that this law is only the ceremonial law. Throughout this entire
epistle the Apostle also argues against the covenant of works, or the moral
law when it is employed in this abusive manner. In this same chapter he also
understands this law, because it is the law meant in verse 10, where everyone
who does not continue in all the things written is pronounced accursed. In
verse 11 he likewise understands the law whose declaration is, *Whoever does
these things will live by them*, and in verse 17 the law which *was given 430
years after the promise made to Abraham*, and which *was ordained through
angels*, and so forth, in verse 19.

7. Galatians 3:16. The promises were spoken to Abraham and to his seed, not to
seeds. But the promises would have been spoken to seeds if the law had then
been proposed so that they might be justified by it and not by faith in
Christ, as Beza shows on the passage. And that seed, insofar as it concerns
the Israelites, would not be Christ.

8. If that law under which the Israelites lived, and from which they were at
last redeemed by Christ—made of a woman, made under the law—had not been
proposed for this sole end, that they might flee to the Messiah for
redemption, they would not have been heirs who are lords of all, nor would
they have been merely under tutors and guardians until the time appointed by
the Father, contrary to what Paul teaches in Galatians 4:1–5. For they would
then have had nothing in common with Christians, but would have been obliged
to expect the inheritance in a very different manner.

9. This is why Moses expressly acknowledged that the Israelites could not
observe the law perfectly when he called them a stiff-necked people
(Deuteronomy 9:6) and said in Deuteronomy 29:4 that *God had not yet given them
a mind to know, eyes to see, and ears to hear.* Therefore, he could not have
given them the law so that they might claim a right to eternal life by
observing it exactly. Rather, it was so that, perceiving their inability to
observe the law and the impossibility of obtaining life in this way, they
might betake themselves to Christ.

10. Moreover, the words of Moses in Deuteronomy 30:1–2 prove plainly enough
that the law was proposed for an evangelical end. *For it will happen*, he
says, *when*

[Printed p. 160 | IA PDF 168 | IA leaf 168 | IA decausadei02brow]

*all these things come upon you, this blessing and curse which I have set
before you, if you call them back to mind and, turning all the way to Jehovah
your God, listen to his voice according to all that I command you today*, and
so forth. These words speak of repentance and of reception into favor after
repentance. But repentance has no place in the pure covenant of works. Nor
would he have exhorted them to repentance if he had proposed the law as a
pure and simple covenant of works then in force, rather than to lead them to
Christ.

11. Otherwise, to what purpose does he mention the Prophet whom Jehovah their
God would raise up from among them and whom they were to hear (Deuteronomy
18:15), since this Prophet was Christ himself, as Peter testifies in Acts
3:22–23? [He says:] *For Moses said to the fathers, “The Lord your God will
raise up for you from your brothers a Prophet like me. You will hear him in
all the things he speaks to you. And it will happen that whoever does not hear
that Prophet will be destroyed from among the people.”* And to what else do
all the prophecies of the prophets tend? *For all the prophets, from Samuel
and those who came afterward, as many as spoke, also foretold these days*—that
is, the days of Christ—as the same Peter testifies in verse 24. Peter also
testified to Cornelius that *all the prophets bore witness to Christ, namely,
that everyone who believes in him will receive remission of sins through his
name* (Acts 10:43). Paul expressly declared that *he said nothing beyond those
things which the prophets and Moses foretold would happen: namely, that Christ
would suffer and, as the first from the resurrection of the dead, would
proclaim light to the Jews and Gentiles* (Acts 26:22–23). Indeed, Christ
himself asserts that Moses wrote about him (John 5:46).

12. If not, what do Paul's words in his defense before King Agrippa mean in
Acts 26:6–7? *And now I stand called to judgment because of the hope of the
promise made by God to our fathers, to which promise our twelve tribes,
diligently worshiping God night and day, hope to attain*, and so forth.

13. Finally, this can be proved by a sound argument. We must not believe that
God wished to mock this people, whom he regarded with so great a love, by
proposing to them, for the purpose of obtaining eternal salvation, a covenant
which it was impossible for them to observe, unless he had annexed some remedy
to which they might flee when reduced to straits.
