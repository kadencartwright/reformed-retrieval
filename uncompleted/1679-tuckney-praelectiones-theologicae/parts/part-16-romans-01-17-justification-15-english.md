What does Bellarmine answer to these things? He gives three replies.

1. Paul excludes works that Abraham could have done apart from faith. For
even those who have faith sometimes act apart from faith, as when they sin
or perform purely moral works without reference to God.

We demonstrated earlier at length how inept and absurd this answer is, and
how unworthy of Bellarmine's acuteness both as a Jesuit and as so great a
cardinal. We therefore dismiss it here for what it is.

2. Bellarmine says that when Paul declares Abraham justified by faith and not
by works, he does not reject works done from faith, but affirms that those
works were not done without faith, since, had they been such, they would
never have justified him.

I answer:

1. Paul could quite consistently do both things. He could affirm at once
that works done without faith and absolutely all works are excluded from
justification, and invincibly prove the former through the latter. The Jews
and Judaizing false apostles, enemies of faith, contended that a man was
justified by works of the law done without faith. Paul refutes them with a
most effective negative argument from genus to species: A man is justified
by no works whatever, but by faith alone; therefore he is not justified by
works of the law done without faith. Faith is necessary for justification;
therefore works alone do not justify, as the false apostles wished. Faith
alone justifies; therefore no works at all justify, as Paul proves.

2. The Roman Catholics overturn the entire course of Paul's dispute, as
though his task were to prove that no one is justified by works without
faith. Yet he assumes this in order to prove that no one is justified by any
works at all, but by faith alone—or that everyone is justified by faith
alone without works—as appears from his whole dispute and its proven
conclusion in Romans 3:20, 28.

3. My third answer is that throughout Paul's dispute about justification it
is not works without faith and works from faith that are contrasted, but
works with faith, or faith with works. But one may say: faith is contrasted
only

[1679 printed p. 116 | IA leaf 124]

with works of the law. Works of the law are not works of faith, and so
excluding works of the law does not simultaneously exclude works of faith.
I answer:

1. Works of faith and works of the law ought not to be torn so far apart.
Works that are works of faith in respect of the principle from which they
flow may truly be called works of the law insofar as the law commands them.

2. Although justification is repeatedly denied to works of the law, Paul
nowhere ever attributes justification to works of faith, as the Roman
Catholics desire. He is utterly silent about that. What occurs in James has
an entirely different sense, just as its occasion was entirely contrary;
this is not the place to discuss it.

As for Romans 4 and its dispute about Abraham's justification, the question
is not by what kind of works he was justified—whether works preceding or
following his faith—but whether he was justified by works at all. The
question under dispute is contained, as usual, in the antecedent clause of
the hypothetical syllogism in verse 2: “If Abraham was justified by works.”
It says simply and universally “by works,” not specifically by works that
preceded or followed his faith.

When the assumption of the same syllogism denies that he was justified by
works, it means all works, and especially works following his faith, by which
our adversaries wish him to have been justified. This is evident from five
points.

1. If we look to the works Scripture records Abraham doing before his faith,
they were idolatrous deeds meriting condemnation (Joshua 24:2, 15). An
opponent who dreamed, much less asserted, that such works justified would be
too foolish to deserve an apostolic refutation.

2. Paul denies Abraham justification by works of which he could boast before
men. But these were the deeds most pleasing to God and men, for which
Hebrews 11 and Genesis so famously celebrate him—deeds he performed after
he had been regenerated, made righteous, and justified.

3. In Paul's argument the justified man is treated as the one who does not
work in verse 5: “To him who does not work, his faith is reckoned for
righteousness,” namely, so that he is justified by it. Therefore, by Paul's
purpose and meaning, he is justified by no works, not even those springing
from faith. This becomes still clearer because:

4. Paul draws his argument from the wage owed to works in verse 4: “To the
one who works, the reward is not reckoned according to grace, but according
to debt.” This embraces all works, including those arising from faith and
grace. Suppose two hired laborers dig your field, one with his own spade and
the other with a spade you gave him: you will owe the latter his wage no less
than the former. Even if the gift of the spade makes some difference, this
argument from a due wage nevertheless embraces works from faith more
particularly, because on Roman Catholic principles a wage is due to such
works by the strict rigor of justice, while works not done from faith have,
by their own admission, nothing to do with merit.

5. This is clearer still because faith and grace are substituted for works,
or rather are opposed to works in the matter of justification. If a thing is
removed not only by an argument from the nature of the matter at issue, but
also by placing something contrary to it in its place, the former is denied
more openly and strongly on two grounds.

[1679 printed p. 117 | IA leaf 125]

For example, if I say that Titius will not enter the inheritance because he
is a complete foreigner, and also because Sempronius stands ready as the true
heir, I exclude Titius from the inheritance all the more strongly. Precisely
this occurs in Paul. The question is whether the inheritance, or
justification, is obtained by works. Paul says no: he drives away the worker
seeking the inheritance as a foreigner, because he demands a wage from his
work, which is utterly alien to justification, since justification is wholly
gratuitous. At the same time, as Jehoiada once presented Joash, Paul presents
the believer in Christ as the true heir of the kingdom, who relies upon
faith and grace alone, things inseparably joined with true evangelical
justification. Thus let the Babylonian Athaliah go to destruction, while the
true believer obtains justification and the kingdom by faith alone through
Christ alone, the heir of all things.

3. Bellarmine's third reply is that Paul speaks conditionally: If Abraham was
justified by works that did not proceed from the grace of faith, as those
believed who attributed righteousness to their own powers, then certainly he
had glory, but not before God. Since it is sufficiently evident that Abraham
had glory even before God, Paul concludes that he was not justified by works
without faith, but by faith, from which good works truly proceed.

I answer:

Chamier charges Bellarmine here with unprofitableness because he speaks
conditionally according to his adversary's hypothesis. I instead charge him
with a badly formed argument and a false exposition of Paul's argument,
utterly contrary to Paul's meaning. For:

1. Bellarmine's reasoning is far too tangled, as is customary with him. By
loosely presenting his arguments he everywhere plays the orator more than
the disputant, enveloping everything so that, if reduced to form, it would
immediately vanish into smoke.

2. Suppose someone says the argument is sufficiently clear and casts Paul's
reasoning in this form: If Abraham had been justified by works done without
faith, he would have had glory before men only, but not before God. Yet he
had glory even before God. Therefore he was not justified by works done
without faith.

I answer:

1. What have works done without faith to do with this argument of Paul? He
speaks simply of all works insofar as they demand a due wage, and especially
of Abraham's most excellent works, which for both reasons must be understood
as works done from faith.

2. I grant that nearly all Roman Catholics and some of the Fathers cast
Paul's argument in this form. Chamier also adds Peter Martyr and Piscator
from among our writers, though their commentaries do not make that so clear
to me. Louis de Dieu, however, plainly seems to hold that the whole of verse
2—“If Abraham was justified by works, he had something in which to boast,
but not before God”—belongs to the syllogism's major premise. The implied
minor premise would then be: But Abraham had something in which to boast
even before God. Therefore he was not justified by works.

Calvin and Beza, the Arabic interpreter, and most of our writers instead

[1679 printed p. 118 | IA leaf 126]

cast Paul's argument so that “but not before God” is not part of the major
premise but itself the minor premise: If Abraham was justified by works,
then he would have something in which to boast, not only before men but also
before God. But Paul assumes that, even if he perhaps had something in which
to boast before men, he certainly did not before God; therefore he was not
justified by works. The two forms of the syllogism thus contain directly
contrary assumptions. One says, “But Abraham had something in which he could
boast”; the other entirely denies this and says, “But Abraham did not have
anything in which to boast.”

The latter, which is ours, is truer and more consonant with Paul's mind and
argument both here and elsewhere.

1. In the preceding chapter, verse 27, Paul had said that all boasting is
excluded from justification. Paul did indeed have his own boasting, as they
object, insofar as the term signifies rejoicing rather than boastfulness in
the proper sense: in infirmities (2 Corinthians 11:30), not in excellent
deeds; in sincerity of heart (2 Corinthians 1:12), not in the merit of a
work; in hope of glory freely conferred (Romans 5:2), not in works to which
it is owed by justice; and against boastful, slandering false apostles to
vindicate his apostolic authority (2 Corinthians 10:8), though in this he
counts himself a fool (2 Corinthians 11:16). He did not boast before God,
since, as Paul himself testifies, Christ has been made all things for us so
that no flesh may boast before God (1 Corinthians 1:29–31).

I acknowledge that Peter Martyr says Paul intended those who are justified
to have boasting before God, and that Bucer uses still greater boldness when
he says: “If Abraham obtained nothing in which he might boast before God,
what is more absurd than to say that he was justified?” Yet they intend
anything but boasting in oneself and one's works. Bucer immediately explains
that by this boasting before God he means God's approval of the person.
Peter Martyr likewise explains it as obtaining righteousness from God and
not from one's works. This is the true, serious, and solid boasting of the
justified: while they have nothing in themselves, they possess everything
from Christ, according to Paul's words, “We boast in Christ Jesus and put no
confidence in the flesh” (Philippians 3:3); “Let the one who boasts boast in
the Lord” (1 Corinthians 1:31); and “Far be it from me to boast except in the
cross of our Lord Jesus Christ” (Galatians 6:14).

If with de Dieu you say that Paul speaks of this boasting in Christ and thus
frames his argument as follows—If Abraham was justified by works, he has
something in which to boast, but not before God, that is, he has only carnal
boasting that is worthless before God. Yet Abraham had something in which to
boast before God, that is, a boasting valid and pleasing before God.
Therefore he was not justified by works and did not find righteousness
according to the flesh. For the flesh has nothing in which to boast before
God, but faith does: having embraced God's grace and the death of his Son,
faith rightly boasts that it has found eternal redemption. The preceding
chapter therefore excluded carnal boasting from works, but here Paul does
not deny Abraham this boasting of faith in Christ alone, since God so highly
commends his faith; for we are commanded to boast in God, and Christ is said
to have been made righteousness for us so that we may boast in the Lord

[1679 printed p. 119 | IA leaf 127]

(1 Corinthians 1:31)—these things are true and pious in substance. Although
this form of Paul's argument agrees with the Roman Catholics, its teaching
is utterly opposed to their justification by works. Yet besides my first
difficulty, two more make me doubt whether this truly is Paul's form of
argument.

2. Their assumed premise, “But Abraham had something in which to boast
before God,” appears nowhere here; rather, the contrary seems to be said.
The alternative form gives us a complete syllogism: If Abraham was justified
by works, he had something in which to boast. But he did not, at least not
before God. These two premises occur in verse 2, while the conclusion—he was
not justified by works—occurs in verse 1 in the question, “What shall we say
that Abraham our father according to the flesh found?” The answer is:
nothing. He attained no true righteousness, for “to find” is to attain, and
“according to the flesh” and “by works” are taken here for the same thing, as
appears from the connection of the two verses, which are joined not so much
syllogistically, as Piscator wishes, as causally. As for Piscator's objection
that if the verse coheres with the preceding one causally rather than by
prolepsis, Paul would have arranged his words, “For if Abraham was justified
by works, he has a boast before God—but he does not,” there is no absurdity
in saying with Chamier that “before God,” expressed once, is understood as
repeated in the former clause. It is no harsher to supply that phrase than
to supply an entire assumed premise, as they do in their form of Paul's
syllogism.

3. Verse 3 also seems to support this. After saying at the end of verse 2
that Abraham had nothing in which to boast before God, Paul immediately adds,
“For what does Scripture say?” and proves that denial from Scripture.
Abraham had nothing in which to boast before God because God freely imputed
his faith to him for righteousness; had it been from works, it would not
have been gratuitous but a debt in which he could boast before God.

I grant that on Louis de Dieu's view verse 3 may readily be drawn in to
confirm it. If one supplies their assumed premise, “But Abraham had something
in which to boast before God,” one can easily reason: Either he did not have
this boasting, or he had it through faith. But he had it. Therefore he had
it through faith. Paul then proves this from Scripture in verse 3. Whether
that sequence of reasoning flows as correctly and clearly as the former, I
say with the learned de Dieu: Let the learned judge.

However that may finally be, to return at last to the Roman Catholics,
nothing here supports their view that this passage means works preceding
faith.

Perhaps they will say it supports their view very strongly. If the passage
means works done before faith, the reason for Paul's inference is plain: If
Abraham was justified by works done before faith, he would have glory, but
not before God—that is, before men, who see only outward things, but not
before God, who searches hearts. Such works are empty of faith, without
which they cannot please God. But Abraham had glory before God. Therefore

[1679 printed p. 120 | IA leaf 128]

one concludes that he was not justified by works done by free choice and his
own powers before faith. One cannot infer, however, that he was not justified
by works flowing from grace and faith, because these are done not only
outwardly before men but inwardly before God, who is himself an eyewitness;
in respect of their principle they flow from faith, please God, and give a
man something in which to boast before him.

I answer:

1. I will not repeat anything about their affirmative premise, “But Abraham
had something in which to boast,” which has already been sufficiently
examined.

2. As to their major premise—If Abraham was justified by works done in his
own powers before faith, he would have had glory from men, but not before
God—I ask, why not before God as well? Indeed, to that extent he would have
had even greater boasting equally before God and men from works performed by
his own strength than from those in whose performance he was aided by faith
and God's grace.

They say that works performed by one's own powers cannot please God because
they are done without faith, and so afford nothing in which to boast. I
answer that this is perfectly true according to gospel truth and something
all Christians must confess. But the Jews against whom Paul disputes, at
least in part, cared little for it. They denied that faith in Christ was
necessary for a work to be considered good, provided it was good in itself.
The more they performed it by their own powers unaided by faith and God's
grace, the more they regarded God as indebted to them and rightly boasted in
this and in his justice.

One point remains in explaining and vindicating our third argument. The
Roman Catholics, following their old translator, rendered Paul's word
*kauchēma* as “glory”: “If Abraham is justified by works, he has glory, but
not before God.” This makes it easier to concede the affirmative premise
they wish to supply in Paul's argument: “But Abraham had glory before God.”
And indeed he did have glory before God: that glory of which Paul had said
all men were deprived through sin in Romans 3:23, “For all have sinned and
fall short of the glory of God.” We gladly acknowledge that in justification
this glory of God, lost to everyone through sin, was restored to Abraham
through faith.

But we must observe that this “glory of God” means nothing but God's approval,
as in John 12:43. Such glory greatly differs from the boasting now under
discussion. The glory of God, taken actively, is God's approval of us; this
boasting is our commendation—or at least approval—of ourselves before God,
the very thing Paul wanted to be utterly excluded from justification in the
preceding chapter, verse 27. Toledo's subtlety is no help. He distinguishes
*kauchēsis* from *kauchēma*, saying that the former signifies boasting and
bragging, but the latter only praise and approval. Thus Paul excluded
*kauchēsis* from justification there, but here attributes *kauchēma* to
Abraham in his justification. This distinction is far too curious and
plainly false. The words have the same root and the same meaning, except
that *kauchēma* signifies material for boasting and *kauchēsis* the act of
boasting. Both

[1679 printed p. 121 | IA leaf 129]

come to the same thing in justification: just as all material for boasting
is absent from it, so every act of boasting must remain far from it. Since
Paul excluded every *kauchēsis*, every act of boasting, from justification
in the preceding chapter, he consistently asserts that Abraham found no
*kauchēma*, no material for boasting, even in his most illustrious works
flowing from faith, and concludes that he was not justified by them.

Chemnitz writes: “This testimony is so clear, illustrious, and firm that
Andradius himself confesses that some exceptionally learned Roman Catholics
stick at this difficulty and cannot extricate themselves.” He therefore
rightly adds, “I now love and esteem this testimony all the more because I
see that the Roman Catholics cannot evade it even with any semblance of an
answer” (*Examination*, part 1, p. 152).

But we have lingered far too long over this third argument, as effective as
it is. I will be briefer in what follows.
