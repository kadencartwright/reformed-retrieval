#### Bellarmine's arguments from Paul's epistles

49. Bellarmine. 1 Corinthians 10 says, “The cup of blessing which we bless.”
These words signify that consecration is necessary for making this sacrament.
Yet it would not be necessary if the sacrament were only a figure or
representation of the Lord's blood. For the manna, the water from the rock,
and the paschal lamb required no consecration in order to be figures or
sacraments; nor is a blessing necessary upon the water of Baptism in order
for it to be a sacrament.

Protestant reply. 1. The conclusion of this argument—“therefore this
sacrament is not only a figure or representation”—in no way strikes or
touches us. 2. A blessing is just as necessary in the sacrament of Baptism as
in the Eucharist. 3. The manna and the water from the rock required no solemn
blessing by any minister because they were extraordinary sacraments and were
presented immediately by God. The paschal lamb always had its own solemn
blessing. Bellarmine himself has just said that Christ's blessing in Luke
22:17 pertained to the paschal lamb.

Bellarmine. Christ's institution itself suffices to constitute the sign.

Protestant reply. 1. It also suffices for every presence of Christ which he
instituted it to furnish. But in order that this generally instituted sign
may be designated in a particular instance with the religion due to it, and
that the institution may be suitably applied to it, a blessing is necessary
according to Christ's example and that command: “Do this.” 2. If the
institution of the sign renders a blessing superfluous, then the papists are
certainly exceedingly foolish: they bless not only the water instituted by
Christ for Baptism, but in their own idle institutions they most intemperately
abuse both leisure and good words in blessing water, oil, salt, chrism,
candles, ashes, and infinitely many other things of that kind. Bellarmine, *On
the Worship of the Saints*, book 3, chapter 7.

50. Bellarmine. In the same passage we read, “And the bread which we break.”
This breaking is the same as immolation, which is not the immolation of wheat
bread but of Christ.

Protestant reply. Breaking in this passage is not the same as immolation or
oblation.

Bellarmine 1. In 1 Corinthians 11:24, “which is broken for you,” “to be
broken” is the same as “to be immolated.” Therefore it is so in this passage
also, for it is a word of the same author, in the same epistle, and treating
the same thing.

Protestant reply. 1. There is no consequence. In *On Purgatory*, book 1,
chapter 6, Bellarmine makes a threefold sense of one and the same word,
“fire,” not only in the same epistle but also in the same sentence, 1
Corinthians 3:13–14, where he also adds that it is not unjust for blessed

[1632 witness | printed p. 89]

Paul to use the same name variously in the same sentence. Yet in this very
chapter 11 he does not concede that “bread” signifies the same thing in verse
23, “he took bread,” and verse 26, “as often as you eat this bread.” 2. We
nevertheless concede that there is a fitting correspondence of signification
between the breaking of the bread and the breaking of Christ's body in his
passion and oblation, and we say that this is the reason for the sharing of
this phrase.

Bellarmine 2. Paul describes the cup by words of consecration, not of
distribution: “which we bless.” Therefore he ought likewise to have described
the bread by words of consecration, not of distribution. Consequently, “we
break” does not signify “we distribute,” but “we offer and immolate to God.”

Protestant reply. 1. We answer in Bellarmine's words from chapter 11: “Who
imposed upon Christ (Paul) a law that he must always speak in the same
manner?” 2. Paul changed the phrase with the strongest reason. For just as in
the cup blessing and distribution are understood synecdochically through the
word “blessing,” so also in the bread blessing and distribution are
understood through the word “distribution” or “breaking.” 3. Bellarmine
absurdly confuses consecration with immolation. 4. In the proper sense, he
attributes breaking, which is suffering, to Christ existing in the Eucharist.
This is blasphemous.

Bellarmine 3. This argument cannot be answered unless a double equivocation is
admitted, one in the word “we break” and the other in the noun “bread.”

Protestant reply. 1. It is impudence more than sophistical to call every
variety of signification in which any word is used in Scripture an
equivocation. 2. What Bellarmine in *On Purgatory*, book 1, chapter 6,
fruitlessly employs to excuse his tortured interpretation of the threefold
fire named in 1 Corinthians 3 fits this passage especially well: “It is not
properly equivocation, but an elegant allusion in Paul's words.” For Christ's
body is said to be broken by allusion to the breaking of the bread.

51. Bellarmine. In the same passage the bread and cup are called the communion
of Christ's body and blood. But this communion occurs corporally, really, and
properly.

Protestant reply. We do not deny that this communion is real and proper, but
the text itself denies that it occurs in a bodily manner. 1. For in verse 17
it proves, as Cajetan rightly observes, the conjunction of Christ's body with
the receiver from the commonality of Christ's body in relation to the
receivers. That commonality and communion of all the faithful among themselves
is only spiritual, not bodily. 2. The sacrament is called a communion of
Christ only for those who are one through communion with Christ. But the
wicked, who have no communion with Christ, are just as much participants in
every bodily presence as the faithful. Therefore this communion is not
bodily. 3. It is exceedingly absurd to say, without

[1632 witness | printed p. 90]

any trope, that the bread is corporally a communion—that is, that a substance
is an action and a subject is an adjunct in its bodily essence.

Bellarmine. The apostle's intention was to turn the Corinthians away from the
sacrifices of the gentiles and from eating immolated meats. He turns them away
by showing them a far more excellent table and sacrifice. Therefore true
flesh and true blood are upon the altar; otherwise the apostle would have
accomplished nothing.

Protestant reply. The apostle's intention and the force of his argument are
indicated clearly enough in verses 20–21: “I do not want you to be sharers
(*koinōnous*) with demons. You cannot drink the Lord's cup and the cup of
demons. You cannot be participants at the Lord's table and at the table of
demons.” Therefore, just as communion (*koinōnia*) with demons at the table of
demons did not require the substantial presence of demons in or under the
species of idolatrous foods, so neither does communion with Christ at the
Lord's table require the bodily presence of Christ under the species of bread
and wine.

51. Bellarmine. In 1 Corinthians 11, some are said to receive Christ's body
unworthily unto judgment. But they do not receive it by spirit and faith;
therefore they receive it by the body alone. Therefore Christ's body is
present and is received by the bodily mouth.

Protestant reply. Those who receive the sacrament unworthily are guilty of the
Lord's body because they injure the sacred symbols which represent and present
the Lord's body.

Bellarmine 1. Those who destroy an image of Christ sin against Christ.

Protestant reply. Those who treated unworthily an image sanctified by Christ
through his institution would sin grievously. But the rationale of the
papists' idols is different.

Bellarmine 2. Injury is not considered to be done to an image unless someone
violates the image with the intention of doing injury to the one depicted.
But those who handle the Eucharist unworthily are guilty of the Lord's body
even if they did not approach with the intention of violating the Eucharist.
Therefore the conclusion follows.

Protestant reply. 1. The proposition is manifestly false. For the Philistines
did the gravest injury to the ark of God when they brought it into the temple
of Dagon, their god, and placed it near Dagon, not with the intention of
violating it but of honoring it, 1 Samuel 5. The Beth-shemites likewise did
so when they looked into the ark, chapter 6, verse 19. Indeed, David himself
and Uzzah were judged to do injury to the ark when nothing was further from
their thoughts, 2 Samuel 6:2. Bellarmine ought to have remembered that in
Scripture a voluntary injury is considered to involve the intention of doing
injury interpretatively. Thus by his wicked deed David is said to have
despised the Word of God and God himself, 2 Samuel 9:10.

Bellarmine 3. If those who receive the Eucharist unworthily sinned for this
reason, that it represents Christ, a sinful person would not be permitted to
hear the Word of God.

[1632 witness | printed p. 91]

Protestant reply. No one is permitted to hear the Word of God unworthily. This
consequent, however, would have been a part of Bellarmine's argument if he had
observed the rules of the syllogism.

Bellarmine 4. The good and the bad, the worthy and the unworthy,
indiscriminately received the manna and the water from the rock. Yet none were
said to be guilty of the Lord's body or to have eaten judgment to themselves.

Protestant reply. 1. In clear, though not identical, words, Paul says in 1
Corinthians 10:5 that most of them ate judgment to themselves. 1. It is plain
that under the Old Testament a defiled person was not permitted to eat from
the meats of the presentation, 1 Kings 21.

Bellarmine. This was because those things were certain types and a figure of
the sacrament of the Eucharist.

Protestant reply. 1. Bellarmine is exceedingly ridiculous here, since he
concedes that a figure of the Eucharist could not be handled unworthily
without sin and yet denies that those who handle a figure of Christ
unworthily sin. 2. Someone who receives the water of Baptism without the due
disposition sins against the grace of God.

Bellarmine. That is indeed true because he places an obstacle before the
grace of God, not because that material signifies or represents something
sacred. But in the Eucharist a certain singular and special reverence and
purity is also required.

Protestant reply. Bellarmine's and the other papists' supreme perversity must
be attentively observed here. They teach that images of Christ and the saints
are worthy of veneration and religious honor because “in them there is a
likeness to sacred things and a dedication or consecration for divine
worship.” Bellarmine, *On Images*, book 2, chapter 21. They teach the same
concerning an image and the sign of the cross in that work, chapters 27–29,
and concerning the temples themselves in Bellarmine, *On the Worship of the
Saints*, book 3, chapter 5. Yet in this passage the same Bellarmine does not
hesitate to maintain that no special reverence is owed to Baptism and that no
sin is committed in violating it by reason of its signification and
representation of God's divine grace in Christ, so that when Baptism is
brought low, the Eucharist is not adorned with its spoils but is transformed
into an idol.
