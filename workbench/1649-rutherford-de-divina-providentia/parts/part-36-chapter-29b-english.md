13. Our theologians argue thus: *One who foreknows that a sin will occur, yet
does not will to prevent it by efficacious grace when he could, wills that the
sin occur with himself permitting it. But God does not will to prevent sin by
efficacious grace when he could. Therefore, and so forth.* Arminius replies

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 496; IA PDF page 553; BSB canvas 548]

*that: But I deny that the event of sin is rightly inferred from
the refusal to prohibit or prevent it. An affirmative cannot be concluded from
a negative act. 2. It is a fallacy,* says Arminius, *from what is said with a
qualification to what is said simply. GOD wills to prevent sin in a qualified
manner, as is fitting—namely, by an ethical and resistible action—but not by an
omnipotent and physical action,* as he says elsewhere. *3. I argue thus: one who
hates sin and wills to prevent it by establishing a law and giving sufficient
grace does not will sin to occur; but GOD does this. Therefore.*

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Examination of Perkins*, p. 699.]

I answer: 1. An affirmation follows from a negation which includes an
affirmation. For GOD’s efficacious non-prevention of sin, together with certain
knowledge of its futurition and concurrence toward the sinful act, imply that
God wills sin to occur. Indeed, as Bradwardine rightly says, *with respect to
anything whatever, GOD’s permission of it is his actual volition of it.*
Scripture concludes an affirmative from a negative willed by God. The soldiers
did not break CHRIST’s bones; therefore Scripture was fulfilled and hence
God’s decree that CHRIST’s bones not be broken. So the scholastics rightly teach
that both negations and affirmations are positively decreed by God. For example,
there are not three worlds; therefore it is not GOD’s will—indeed, God decreed
that there would not be three worlds. Judas was not saved; therefore God decreed
that he would not be saved. More than twelve legions of angels did not deliver
CHRIST from death; therefore God decreed that the angels would not deliver
CHRIST from drinking the cup. This is clear from the Savior’s prayer: *Not my
will, but yours be done.* The Arminians and Jesuits also inflict an atrocious
injury when they make God behave negatively and wholly idly toward things which
do not occur, such as the non-obedience of apostate angels and reprobate human
beings. An affirmative act is not logically enough inferred from a negative act
of creatures, but from a negative act of GOD one rightly infers GOD’s positive
will concerning what is denied to occur. Ahab does not return home in peace;
therefore his non-return is positively decreed by GOD. The Canaanites did not
stand in battle against GOD’s people; therefore God positively decreed that
they would not stand. The faithful would have no consolation and no confidence
in GOD if evils which never occur were not held off by his positive will. If
God decreed that there should be 17984590co good angels, beyond doubt he
decreed there should be no more; if he decreed that the number of apostate
angels should be 871378359000, beyond doubt he positively decreed

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 33.]

[Marginal summary: From GOD’s will of sign the morality of a thing—its equity
or iniquity—is inferred, not the thing’s existence; but from the will of good
pleasure one does not infer the good or evil morality of a thing, but very well
its existence or nonexistence.]

[Marginal summary: Arminius acknowledges no prevention of sin except a moral,
ethical, resistible one consistent with the eternal ruin of all mortals.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 497; IA PDF page 554; BSB canvas 549]

that there would be no fewer. 2. Arminius attributes to God
no prevention of sin other than a moral and resistible one; this alone, he
thinks, befits God. But he plainly raves. 1. A resistible prevention of sin can
stand together with the eternal ruin of every elect angel and human being. But
God neither has nor decreed to have a providence consistent with the destruction
of all rational creatures. This prevention can also stand with the eternal
salvation of all rational creatures; but in the first case the glory of sparing
mercy would have lain hidden, in the latter the glory of punitive justice, and
in both cases the glory of CHRIST the God-man would have been hidden forever.
Because such prevention can be resisted by created free choice, it utterly
overthrows GOD’s dominion over good and evil acts; destroys fervency in praying
that God not allow us finally to fall away; weakens the confidence by which we
believe that God will, by his sheer grace, preserve us from apostasy; takes the
glory of holy abstinence from sin away from God and sacrilegiously transfers it
to the proud freedom of man; and wholly empties gratitude to God for the grace
of perseverance in which we stand, supported and strengthened by God. 2. If it
does not befit GOD to prevent sin by a physical and irresistible action, neither
would it befit GOD physically to work faith in us, especially irresistibly in
CHRIST, the Blessed Virgin, and John the Baptist—something the adversaries do
not dare assert. 3. GOD would have sinned against what befits him if he
infallibly converted and saved everyone, and human beings who prayed that God
keep them undeviatingly in obedience would pray that God sin against what
befits him. 4. It is not foreign to what befits GOD that he confirm and
establish some in grace until the day of CHRIST. Therefore he efficaciously
prevents sin in them. Therefore, in those in whom he does not prevent sin, he
wills their sins to occur. 4. Arminius’s argument presupposes that through the
law and the gift of sufficient grace God decreed to preserve all mortals immune
from sin and fall, if only they wished to preserve themselves. Such a decree is
plainly invisible in the sacred oracles, as though God said: “From eternity I
decreed that the Jews should not kill CHRIST, and I never decreed the redemption
of the world through that killing.” Let those who wish believe such a decree of
God; they will not have my vote. And if God prevents sin only ethically, all
grace is mere ethical persuasion and teaching sounding externally—behold
Pelagianism.

14. *But GOD either does not prevent sin because he does not will sin to occur*

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 498; IA PDF page 555; BSB canvas 550]

*, which which is false; or because he does not care, which is Epicurean; or
because he wills it to occur, which is what was to be proved.* Arminius replies:
*The reason GOD does not prevent sin is neither that he wills it to occur nor
that he wills it not to occur. What he wills to occur he efficaciously effects;
what he wills not to occur he efficaciously prevents. Rather, the reason he does
not prevent sin is that he permits the creature freely to will and not will.*

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Examination of Perkins*, p. 700.]

[Marginal summary: A demonstration against the adversaries’ Epicurean
permission.]

I answer: 1. For Arminius, not preventing sin is the same as permitting sin. The
question is why God permits, or why he does not prevent, sin; and Arminius
answers that the reason God permits sin is that he permits sin. But I bind the
argument more tightly. GOD either does not efficaciously prevent sin because he
does not efficaciously will sin to occur, or he efficaciously prevents sin
because he wills sin to occur. For every divine prevention of sin is either
efficacious or inefficacious; these are contradictory opposites. Therefore the
reason why he does not efficaciously prevent sin is either that he wills sin to
occur—which is what was to be proved—or that he wills it not to occur, which is
absurd. For according to Arminius, not efficaciously preventing sin is to
procure that it occur; and thus God does not prevent sin because he procures
that it occur, which is monstrous. Or, third, God does not prevent sin because
he does not care whether it occurs or not, which is Epicurean. The argument
gains strength when permission includes GOD’s concurrence.

15. Objection 6. *An evil will,* says Arminius, *is named from an evil volition;
an evil volition is one borne toward an undue object. But an undue object is
evil. Therefore an evil volition is one by which someone wills evil and wills
evil to occur. Accordingly, it belongs to an evil will to will that evils occur,
whether that will delights in evils or wishes to use them for good; for evils
must not be done, indeed must not even be willed, so that goods may result. The
first iniquity is in the volition of evil, the second in its perpetration.* So
also Bellarmine, who falsely says that Calvin absolves God of crime because God
wills evil for a good end.

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Examination of Perkins*, p. 701.]

[Marginal reference: Bellarmine.]

I answer: 1. Without impropriety of speech, an evil volition is not named from
a volition of evil. 2. Doctor Twisse rightly denies that a volition is properly
called evil because it is borne toward an undue object. One should speak not of
an undue object, but of an undue bearing and motion. That great man rightly
observes this, for laws which constrain by a bond and obligation of debt are
properly laid upon rational creatures. No legal obligation of debt rests on
brute

[Marginal reference: Doctor Twisse, *Vindication of Grace against Arminius’s
Examination of Perkins*, book 2, section 10, charge 3.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 499; IA PDF page 556; BSB canvas 551]

and irrational objects. No law is laid upon another person’s gold, but
upon a human being not to covet or seize that gold; not upon the fruit, but upon
Adam not to eat the fruit; not upon the Amalekites’ herds, but upon King Saul,
that he not possess those herds, while Adam not eat the fruit. Nor is the
volition undue because its object is formally undue, but because the volition
itself is forbidden by GOD’s law. 3. For GOD to will that evil be done by
another who commits it and is subject to law, with himself permitting, is not
evil, nor is it repugnant to his holy nature. The great theologian whom our
bishops in Scotland and England imagined Arminius to be ought to have proved
this. I admit that to will the evil of fault by approving it as an honorable
good, or to will or do it contrary to a law to which one is subject, is evil.
For one subject to law to will evil to be done by himself is to will himself
and approve himself as evil in willing and doing it iniquitously; in this manner
God by no means wills evil to be done. But to will that evil be done, not by
oneself but by another, is not always an evil volition even in a human being,
much less in GOD, who is above every law laid upon creatures. Peter holily
willed that the Savior be crucified by Pilate and the Jews, but he could not
holily will that the Savior be crucified by himself. Peter willed this holily,
I say, κατὰ τὶ, insofar as it was a means of redeeming the human race, for the
Savior rightly rebuked him for not willing it to occur. GOD’s martyrs holily
will themselves to be burned by tyrants as a testimony to the truth, but they
could not holily will themselves to be burned alive by their own act. GOD’s
martyrs are bound by no law to refuse to suffer burning by tyrants; rather, they
were bound submissively and peacefully to acquiesce in such injuries. But by
the sixth commandment they are strictly forbidden to be burned alive by
themselves. Therefore even among human beings, willing the evil of fault to be
done by others is not always unlawful, much less in GOD. These two things
therefore stand well together in GOD: to permit evil—that is, to will and decree
that evil be done by others, while he disapproves and forbids it—and for GOD
neither to commit nor love that evil. Hence it follows that not every volition
borne toward an object undue for another is evil, unless it is undue for the
one who wills. 4. What Bellarmine and Arminius object against Calvin—that by
this reasoning GOD becomes a doer of evil so that good may result—ought not
frighten us in the least. The same was formerly objected by the Libertines
against the apostle Paul, let alone Calvin, in Romans 3:4–6: if our lies conduce
to GOD’s glory, then we ought to lie so that God may be

[Marginal summary: To will that evil be done by another, in a qualified
respect, is not evil even in a human being.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 500; IA PDF page 557; BSB canvas 552]

glorified. This is sophistically objected against Calvin here, as though
these were the same: doing evil and sinning for a good end; and permitting, or
permissively willing, that evil be done by others without oneself co-effecting
or co-approving it. The former is doing evil so that good may result, which must
by no means be said of GOD. The latter is neither doing evil nor an evil
volition. But is Calvin rightly accused here? Let us see. Calvin says, I admit:
*When human beings commit either theft or homicide, they sin because they are
thieves and murderers, for in theft and homicide there is a criminal design.
GOD regards something altogether different: he wills to chastise one person
and to exercise another’s patience, and thus he never departs from his nature,
that is, from perfect rectitude. But because an evil deed is assessed by the
end toward which each person tends, it is plain that GOD is not the author of
sin.* To omit other things, I say that if the act itself as to its substance,
which God effects, were evil, then GOD’s intention of a good end would by no
means absolve God from association in the crime. But because for God
permissively to will evil to be done by others is in itself an indifferent act,
it has from GOD’s free will that it is good. Thus, although a good intention
never makes an evil act good, nevertheless an intention of a good end can make
that indifferent act good, or make a good act better; and Calvin speaks in this
sense. 2. I admit that diversity of ends in God and a human being is not the
adequate cause why God is not a cause of sin while a human being is. But Calvin
attributes to the human being an action which not only has an evil end but is
also, as to its substance, an act forbidden by GOD’s law. Calvin often explains
himself thus. Therefore the adversaries do not prove that we teach there to be
in GOD an evil volition or perpetration of evil so that good may result. Much
less will they ever prove that we teach God is not involved in blame because
God does evil so that good may result.

[Marginal reference: Calvin, *Institutes*, book 1, chapter 17, section 5.]

[Marginal summary: GOD does not do evil so that good may result; this is falsely
imputed to Calvin.]

16. The adversaries fail to understand that sin has the character of evil for
the creature, not for God—that is, not before God as decreeing. Indeed, God does
not even permit evil as evil, because permission includes some volition of GOD.
Therefore GOD’s permission no more than his volition tends toward evil as evil;
evil is only the material, not the formal, object of GOD’s volition. Indeed,
martyrs who desire death from tyrants do not desire evil as evil, but as good.
Arminius rises against this: *I deny,* he says, *that*

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Examination of Perkins*, p. 702.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 501; IA PDF page 558; BSB canvas 553]

*sin has the character of good before GOD as decreeing. For GOD’s acts
concerning sin altogether declare that GOD considers sin not under the aspect
of good but under the aspect of evil. He permits sin, but effects good; he
punishes sin, and punishes evil as evil; he remits and pardons sin, and what is
pardoned is considered evil by the one who pardons it; and sin is not good in
itself, but accidentally.* It is false, he continues, *that GOD does not permit
sin as evil, for the proper object of divine permission is evil as evil and
indeed as evil considered by GOD, although the reason he permits sin is not
evil, but that,* as he has elsewhere, *he knows how to elicit good from it,* as
Corvinus also says.

[Marginal reference: Ibid., p. 703.]

[Marginal reference: Corvinus against Molina, chapter 14.]

I answer: 1. Arminius’s first reason by which he proves that sin does not have
the character of good before GOD as decreeing—namely, because GOD effects good,
whereas GOD cannot effect sin but only permit it—is false. God does not effect
every good, even every useful good. The adversaries would not say that God
crucified his own Son, though the crucifixion of the Son of GOD is a useful
good; nor can a human being lawfully do every good that he wills. CHRIST rightly
rebuked Peter because he dissuaded the Savior from being crucified by sinners.
For Peter was bound lawfully to will the Savior’s crucifixion insofar as it was
a means for redeeming the human race, but he could not himself lawfully crucify
the Savior. 2. It can also rightly be said that the good which is in sin is from
God, although not by efficiency properly so called, while sin itself neither is
from God nor is effected by him. For it is from God that such an orientation
and relation toward God’s glory is present in sin; this good is from God,
although the sin underlying such goodness is not from GOD. 3. What GOD punishes
and pardons is dishonorable evil to GOD as pardoning and punishing, but it is
not the dishonorable evil of the one who punishes and pardons, because it is
another’s evil and is considered by God as another’s evil. Yet what God
punishes and pardons is not useless evil, but a useful good conducive to the
illustration of divine glory. Hence the inference from the affirmation of one
species to the negation of another is vain: Arminius is a father; therefore he
is not someone else’s son. It does not follow. This is like it: what God
pardons is dishonorable to the one who pardons; therefore it is not a useful
good. Rather, the contrary inference is good: because sin is not an honorable
good to God as pardoning, therefore it is a useful good for the glory of

[Marginal summary: Sin is dishonorable evil insofar as it is a useful good from
God as pardoning.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 502; IA PDF page 559; BSB canvas 554]

the one who pardons, by which mercy is declared. 4. It is false that sin is not from
itself and its nature a useful good. Rather, the worse and more dishonorable a
sin is, the more punishable and pardonable it is, and consequently the more it
serves to illustrate GOD’s glory. Hence, because it is worse in itself in the
class of dishonorable evil, it is more useful in itself in the class of useful
good. 5. Arminius says that God permits sin because he knows how to elicit good
from it, as the Jesuit Lessius also says; but for Lessius this knowledge is not
a final cause, but only a condition without which it does not occur. Yet, first,
GOD’s bare speculative knowledge is not the reason God permits sin. For God
knows how to elicit good from the possible sins of infinitely many human beings
and angels who never exist, yet he does not permit such sins. If, however,
GOD’s practical knowledge is the reason for which God permits sin, then God
first intends and wills to elicit good from sin, and because he intends this he
therefore permits sin and wills it to occur—which is our position. Further, God
either knows it to be better to permit sin or not to permit it. If not, then
permission of sin itself will be at least comparatively evil or ineligible, yet
it is chosen by God, which is absurd. For God knowingly and willingly permits
sin, as everyone teaches. But if it is better to permit sin, then God either
chooses it under the aspect of the better or does not. If the former, then God
intends to permit sin as better and more conducive to the promotion of his
glory. Arminius therefore falsely said that *sin has the character of evil
before GOD as decreeing, but not of good.* And it follows that before GOD it is
better and more useful for the declaration of his glory that sin exist than
that it not exist. But if God does not choose to permit sin under the aspect of
the better, it follows that by a physical will without any election God wills
something comparatively evil and ineligible, which is absurd. 6. It is false
that evil as evil is the formal object of GOD’s permission. Indeed, because
permission is a slow and remiss volition, by Arminius’s judgment, it follows
that GOD’s permitting will does not will evil as evil. Further, for evil as evil
to be permitted by God can be understood in two ways. First, logically: thus
malice as malice is only permitted because GOD neither effects nor approves it.
Second, physically: thus malice as evil is not permitted by God, but as
conducive to the promotion of GOD’s glory. Therefore GOD’s volition always
terminates upon conduciveness toward God’s glory arising from the guilt of

[Marginal reference: Lessius, *On the Divine Perfections*, book 11, chapter 6,
figure 27.]

[Marginal summary: Evil as evil is not the object of divine permission.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 503; IA PDF page 560; BSB canvas 555]

sin, not upon sin as sin. But I hear Arminius objecting here: *Sin is not at
all conducive to GOD’s glory except insofar as it is sin, because it is
conducive only insofar as it is punishable or pardonable. Therefore if GOD wills
sin insofar as it is conducive to his glory, he wills sin as sin.* I answer that
by the same reasoning one could conclude that every means is desired as a
means; thus a human being would desire the cutting of a vein insofar as it is
hostile to a nature abounding in blood and humors, because only thus is it a
means of health. Yet sound philosophers have always taught that venesection is
desired not as hostile to nature but as productive of health. Accordingly, I
deny this inference: *Sin as sin is a means of declaring GOD’s glory. Therefore,
if GOD desires sin insofar as sin is a means of illustrating GOD’s glory, he
must desire sin as sin.* To ground the character of a means in sin, it is enough
that it be dishonorable evil, from which its conduciveness results. But to
ground the reduplication, sin must formally be desired under the aspect of
malice and not be referred to something else under the aspect of a means. Yet
in this way God does not even will obedience insofar as it is obedience, but
only insofar as obedience is a means of declaring GOD’s glory. Nor does
Gamachæus rightly say that goods annexed to sin are diametrically opposed to the
nature of sin and must be attributed only to God and in no way to sin. For I
admit that the goods annexed to sin—namely, its punishment and merciful
pardon—are attributed to GOD alone in the class of efficient cause; yet this
does not prevent them from being rightly attributed to sin in the class of
material, demeritorious, and occasional cause. Nor is this inference valid:
good is elicited from sin by GOD’s wise ordering; therefore that good is annexed
to sin wholly by accident. For good follows obedience by GOD’s wise ordering,
yet it is not inferred from this that obedience is a means of good outcome by
accident. Although by GOD’s ordering alone sin is actually punished and
pardoned—for by GOD’s absolute power it can be neither punished nor
pardoned—nevertheless sin is from itself and its nature punishable and
pardonable, even when every free decree of God is set aside. For sin does not
have from God’s decree that it is punishable and pardonable; antecedently to
every decree of God, it is in itself and through itself

punishable and pardonable.

Objection 7. *That upon whose supposition another thing necessarily follows is
the cause why the other follows. But upon the supposition that GOD decreed Adam
would fall, Adam’s fall necessarily follows. Therefore GOD’s decree is thus the
cause of sin.* And it is false, says Arminius, *that if no intermediate cause
intervenes between the antecedent upon whose supposition the consequent follows
and the consequent, then the antecedent is not a cause of that consequent.
Neither does the antecedent therefore cease to be the cause of the consequent,
even if some intermediate cause intervenes. For Satan is the cause of eating
from the forbidden tree, even if the human being is its proximate and immediate
cause. But if you can show,* he says to Perkins, *that these two can stand
together—that GOD can will sin to occur and that nevertheless a human being
sins by free will—then you have won the case. The decree is the deficient cause
of sin and the energetic principle of all things.* And Bellarmine says: *From
Calvin, GOD decreed and willed from eternity that Adam would fall, not after
foreseeing a predetermination of the human will, but from his absolute good
pleasure, so that he might have something in which to display the glory of
justice and mercy which he had decreed to declare. Therefore, lest GOD’s decree
be frustrated, GOD necessarily efficaciously procured sin and imposed upon the
human being a necessity of sinning.*

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Examination of Perkins*, p. 704.]

[Marginal reference: Ibid., p. 705.]

[Marginal reference: Bellarmine, *On the Loss of Grace*, book 2, chapter 4.]

I answer: 1. If the maxim, “That upon whose supposition another thing
necessarily follows is the cause of what follows,” holds of a moral cause, its
conclusion is not against us, because GOD’s decree is not a moral cause of sin.
If it holds of a natural cause, it does not harm our case either. For sin as sin
reduplicates a moral mode of causing, by which it arises from a moral cause
subject to law, as the Jesuit Mendoza rightly teaches. 2. The major is denied
simply. As Perkins rightly says, upon the creation of the world, time and place
are necessarily posited, yet the creation of the world is not the cause of time
and place. So when GOD’s foreknowledge is posited, Peter must deny the Lord, yet
GOD’s foreknowledge is not the cause of sin. If I see you writing, it very well
follows by logical consequence, “Therefore, you write”; but my sight is not the
cause of your writing. The argument proceeds from logical consequence, as the
most distinguished Doctor Twisse observes, whereas the present question
concerns real consequence. 3. Arminius’s statement that *what is the cause of
the antecedent is the cause of the consequent, even if a free cause intervenes
between antecedent and consequent,* is irrelevant, because there is no causal
connection here between antecedent and consequent. The maxim holds where there
is a real connection. If fire is the cause of glowing iron

[Marginal summary: The statement “That upon whose supposition another thing
necessarily follows is the cause of what follows”; or, “What is the cause of
the antecedent is also the cause of the consequent,” is explained.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 505; IA PDF page 562; BSB canvas 557]

and glowing iron is the cause of a burned hand, it rightly follows that fire is
the cause of the burned hand. But in our proposition the antecedent—namely,
GOD’s decree—is not at all the cause of sin, remotely or proximately, nor does
it flow into sin by any causative force. We admit that GOD’s decree does not
cease to be a cause because a free cause intervenes between antecedent and
consequent—namely, between GOD’s decree and sin—for it is not a cause at all,
and therefore we give no reason why it ceases to be the cause of sin. 4.
Arminius says that Perkins wins the case if he shows how these two stand
together: *that GOD can will sin to occur and that nevertheless a human being
sins by free will.* Hence it is paradoxical to Arminius for GOD’s will to be an
energetic cause of a free act, because he imagines that this overthrows freedom;
yet this reasoning holds equally in good and evil acts. Therefore I say that the
Arminians will win the case if they show how these two stand together: *that GOD
can will the final obedience of all elect human beings to exist, and that
nevertheless all the elect finally obey most freely.* Thus the controversy
between us would cease if we conceded to Arminius that GOD’s efficacious will is
the cause of no free act, so that the glory of every supernatural act would be
taken from God. 5. It is not truly said that GOD’s decree is a deficient cause
of sin; but the Arminians prove it thus: because God decreed to deny human
beings the cause necessary for avoiding sin, and when that grace is denied by
God’s decree, they say that according to our position sin follows just as
necessarily as a stone’s motion in the air when an impediment is removed. But
this has been answered abundantly above. Arminius himself cannot avoid this pit,
for he teaches that sin necessarily follows from God’s permission, although
unknowingly and imprudently, in these words: *It is necessary,* he says, *that
the will be persuaded by none of those arguments* which incite to a good act *to
refuse it; otherwise it will not have been permission.* Likewise: *Permission
of the sin of ignorance arises from suspension of the revelation of GOD’s will
and of sin’s malice, and from suspension of the act by which the heart’s
perversity is corrected and changed,* and so forth. Hence I argue thus. The act
by which the heart’s perversity is corrected is either absolutely necessary for
avoiding a sinful sin, or necessary only in a qualified way so that sin is
merely more easily avoided. If the former, then God in permitting the sinful
sin will necessitate human beings to a sinful sin, for God denies an act
absolutely necessary for avoiding a sinful sin. Arminius condemns this

[Marginal reference: Arminius, ibid.]

[Marginal summary: What Arminian permission of GOD is—namely, Pelagian,
altogether removing every necessity of grace.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 506; IA PDF page 563; BSB canvas 558]

in our theologians. If the latter is said, then the gracious act of GOD which he
suspends and denies in such a case is only Pelagian grace, required not for the
being of obedience but for its easier being; for a human being could avoid a
sinful sin even if GOD did not suspend the act by which the heart’s perversity
was corrected, because that act is not simply necessary for avoiding a sinful
sin. Thus Augustine says: *Pelagius asserts that grace is given for this
purpose: so that the things which people are commanded to do by free choice may
be fulfilled more easily through grace.* But Corvinus, Arminius’s follower,
rejects this position: *We are sufficiently far from Pelagianism as long as we
acknowledge as necessary the inward aid of grace by which the will is prepared
for supernatural acts.*

[Marginal reference: Augustine, *On Heresies*, 88.]

[Marginal reference: Corvinus against Bogerman, part 2, chapter 7, p. 204.]

Further, although God suspends efficacious grace, it does not follow that he is
the cause of sin, even in the manner of one who removes a preventative, as the
absence of the sun is the cause of darkness. It follows only that God is the
cause of non-obedience, which is reduced to a physical cause—whereas sin as sin
has no physical cause—not of disobedience, which has no cause except a moral one
obliged not to cause sin. I know Arminius can answer cunningly that efficacious
grace is considered materially insofar as it gives the ability, or formally
insofar as, administered according to GOD’s foreknowledge, it gives actually to
avoid sin. In the former way, permission is not suspension of grace; in the
latter way it is, and therefore human beings are not necessitated to sin through
suspension of grace. Our permission, however, is suspension of grace which gives
the ability to act well and the ability not to sin, and therefore our
permission simply necessitates sinning. But besides the fact that Arminius’s
disciples desert their teacher here concerning congruous grace—which is the
empty dream of the more recent Jesuits—the efficacious grace which gives the
ability to act well, insofar as it does not have GOD’s foreknowledge of the
event joined to it, is for Arminius sufficient grace, not efficacious grace. But
then permission will be suspension only of GOD’s foreknowledge, which has no
activity toward acts, whether good or evil, but presupposes grace’s actual
efficiency. Therefore permission must be the negation of grace with respect to
the substance of the act and not with respect to bare foreknowledge; otherwise
God would be said to permit sin because he suspends foreknowledge of the event,
but nothing more absurd than this can be said. 6. No necessity of sinning flows
from the permissive decree. Yet when the decree is posited, I admit that it
follows by modal necessity that sin will

[Marginal summary: What necessity flows from the decree.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 507; IA PDF page 564; BSB canvas 559]

freely occur. So far is the freedom of the act from being removed when the
decree is posited that without the decree’s being posited its freedom would not
stand; for not only is the act decreed by God, but the mode of the act—namely,
freedom—is also absolutely appointed by GOD. For God decreed that human beings
would will and act freely.

To Bellarmine I say that Calvin would be orthodox in this matter if he had said
that GOD willed sins not absolutely and before the foreseen determination of
free will, but conditionally. For let Bellarmine say to what end and with what
intention God permits sin. If to illustrate his glory, then, for him to attain
this end, it was necessary for sin to occur by God’s permissive will. If for no
end, then God’s permission of sin will be a rash action, not the work of a free
and rational agent. He also speaks falsely, and to stir up ill will, when he
says that *through his decree GOD imposed upon a human being a necessity of
sinning.* For to impose necessity is causatively to make something necessary.
But GOD’s decree is neither the efficient cause, end, matter, nor form of sin.
This objection has equal force in good acts. The answer received by all
theologians is that GOD created certain angels and human beings to glorify
himself and decreed that such glory would be illustrated through their free
obedience. Therefore, lest this decree of GOD be frustrated, GOD had
efficaciously to procure their free obedience toward that end. God therefore
procured the free acts of sinners as acts in only the same manner and by equal
necessity as God procures the final obedience of the elect. 3. The controversy
would be ended if we held that sins occur with GOD neither willing, caring, nor
decreeing, but with the creature sinning or not sinning by its own absolute
freedom, while God has no fixed and immovable decree concerning his glory
through either the creature’s salvation or destruction, except as it pleases
the free creature to act this way or that. But if God absolutely wills
contingency to exist, he wills this contingency to exist; if he absolutely
wills the act—which cannot be denied without injury to providence—he absolutely
wills the act’s mode. For by the same act and equal necessity God wills a human
being to exist and to be capable of laughter; the mode of a being is an
attribute of that being and is identified with the being.

17. Objection 8. *For something to be rightly willed,* says Smising, *on account
of another thing, the other thing must be rightly willed, and what is willed on
account of it must have a connection with it in the character of cause or*

[Marginal reference: The Jesuit Smising, volume 1, theological disputation,
tractate 3, disputation 3, *On the Will of GOD*, question 3, figure 99.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 508; IA PDF page 565; BSB canvas 560]

*effect, because there is no other mode of participating from another in the
character of desirability. Since sin is neither the means nor cause of any good,
but rather destroys through itself the order of good, insofar as it is a
disordering from the supreme good from which all participation in created good
is taken; and since it is not the effect of any good, because it is caused not
by efficient action but by deficient action, it follows that sin cannot be
rightly willed even accidentally.*

I answer: 1. The evil of punishment is a means to some good—namely, the display
of justice—but you yourself teach, with your Scotus in figure 100, that sin is
formally the evil of punishment insofar as it is a defect of the good of
justice received in the will. Therefore, by your account, sin can be a means
rightly willed. 2. It is false that sin is not a means to good, for it is a
useful means. 3. Sin is not a disordering from the supreme good simply, but
only insofar as God is Legislator; with respect to GOD insofar as he is the
universal Administrator of the world, it is a means conducive to glory. To
that extent it is not a disordering from the supreme good.

18. Objection 9. *Sin cannot be desired unless it is joined with a good more
excellent and eminent than the good which it privatively removes. But no good
joined with sin is more eminent or desirable than the good which sin
privatively removes. Therefore sin cannot be desired for the sake of that good
joined with it, and consequently not as a means of declaring GOD’s glory.* The
minor is proved: *Sin, so far as lies in itself, grieves GOD and deprives him of
his happiness; indeed sin is opposed to the infinite good—namely, justice,* say
Corvinus and Philip Gamachæus, *and to GOD’s will.* Thus Christopher Scheibler,
Gregory of Valencia, and Balthasar Meisner argue.

[Marginal reference: Corvinus against Molina, chapter 3, section 9.]

[Marginal reference: Gamachæus, on I, disputation 6, question 9, point 2.]

[Marginal reference: Scheibler, *Metaphysics*, book 2, chapter 3, title 5,
point 3.]

[Marginal reference: Valencia, volume 2, disputation 6, question 9, point.]

[Marginal reference: Meisner, part 1, *Philosophical Sobriety*, book 2,
question 5, section 499.]

I answer: 1. I deny the major. Although it is true of human beings, whose will
is moved and enticed by good—more efficaciously by a greater apprehended good
and less by a lesser—it does not hold of God, whose holy will is not enticed by
good, nor does any good exercise an act of final causation upon GOD’s infinite
will as it does upon a created appetite. For GOD can permit and will a greater
evil for a lesser good, and God can will a lesser good while rejecting a
greater good. God did not will to create a more excellent world, although he
could have; for the scholastics rightly say that no external good

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 509; IA PDF page 566; BSB canvas 561]

limits or circumscribes GOD’s will. 2. The major is as true of permission as of
volition, but the conclusion would be blasphemous. For if GOD’s will were led
by a good outside God, natural reason would declare that GOD could not permit
sin unless it were joined with a good more eminent than the good which it
privatively removes. Nor would an earthly father knowingly and willingly permit
his son’s eternal ruin unless that ruin were annexed to a good far more eminent
than his son’s eternal happiness. 3. The minor also is false. The good annexed
to sin—namely, the manifestation of divine glory—is a good far more eminent
than the obedience of the reprobate which their sin privatively removes,
because the end is more eminent than the means which leads to the end. Obedience
is the means; the manifestation of GOD’s glory is the end. 2. Although, in the
class of offense and dishonorable good and with respect to the sinner, sin is a
greater evil—namely, moral and illegal, or ethical—than the manifestation of
divine glory is a good; and although in itself it is better and more desirable
for Judas the traitor not to betray the Savior than for the glory of justice to
be declared in punishing and avenging that betrayal eternally; nevertheless,
the display of the glory of divine justice and mercy, simply and with respect
to the universe in the class of physical good, is a greater and more eminent
good than the obedience of Judas the traitor. For more, and something more
illustrious, accrues to God’s glory from the declaration of justice and mercy
than if all the reprobate and elect had committed no sin at all and all human
beings had finally persisted in entirely angelic holiness. The glory of GOD as
universal administrator of the world is a greater good than the glory fittingly
declared in him insofar as he is legislator, because the more universal a good
is, the more eminent it is. Add that goodness must be measured by divine good
pleasure. Therefore it is better and a more eminent good for the glory of
justice and mercy to be declared in CHRIST the Mediator—because God so willed
from his wise good pleasure—than for human beings never to sin but all finally
to obey GOD. 4. It is also false that sin is privatively opposed to GOD’s
happiness or will, for sin privatively removes no attribute in GOD; rather, I
admit, it removes and takes away the created honorable thing which God as
legislator approves by his free will. But none of GOD’s beatitude departs from
him on this account. The subtle Jesuit Theodore Smising clearly

[Marginal summary: The good annexed to sin is far more eminent than the good
which sin privatively removes.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part B; printed page 510; IA PDF page 567; BSB canvas 562]

labors to refute this, but in vain. He says: *It is false that sin is a lesser
evil than to be deprived of certain goods which GOD’s goodness bestows on the
occasion of sin, because denial of these goods is not evil, since it is not
denial of a good which is due, but of one bestowed from GOD’s sheer mercy and
liberality. Even if it were evil, it would not be moral but natural evil; and
one venial sin is a greater evil than the natural death of all human beings,
than deprivation of the vision of GOD, or than the divine Word’s not being
incarnate, because some good end, natural or moral, could have been intended by
GOD from those evils, whereas no good end can arise from that evil.*

[Marginal reference: Smising, theological disputations, volume 1, tractate 3,
*On the Divine Will*, question 3, figure 99.]

I answer: 1. It is not true that *sin is a greater evil than the declaration of
GOD’s glory is a good*; in this way the Jesuit charges God with ignorance. Nor
does he prove what he so boldly asserts. For the non-declaration of GOD’s glory
is therefore an immense evil because the declaration of GOD’s glory is an
immense good and the ultimate end of all GOD’s external actions. This is clear
from Proverbs 16:4, Revelation 4:11, and Romans 11:36. It is also an end which
God efficaciously procures to occur. 2. Nor does the non-declaration of the
glory of justice and mercy cease to be some evil because it is *not the
negation of a due good but of one bestowed from GOD’s sheer mercy.* For then
almost none of the things in the world would be good: God created the world,
angels, human beings, and his creatures under no debt or bond of obligation,
but solely from his good pleasure; he could have refrained from creating them
if it had seemed so to him. 3. It is also false that GOD declares the glory of
his mercy from his mercy alone, because solely from his good pleasure he
declares to his creatures the glory of his mercy, justice, and all his
attributes. 4. I acknowledge that *for the sinner any venial or light sin is a
greater evil than the natural death of all human beings,* for the sinner’s sin
is an extreme misery which makes him far most unhappy and deprives him of
eternal beatitude. But simply, with respect to the universe and GOD himself,
sin is not a greater evil than the death of all human beings, because God
elicits from it a far better end. Indeed, sin is occasionally a most useful
good.

19. Objection 10. *What occurs with GOD willing occurs with GOD as author and
with GOD approving. But sin occurs neither with GOD as author nor with GOD
approving. Therefore.* So Aquinas and Durandus. I answer: 1. I deny

[Marginal reference: Aquinas, I–II, question 79, article 3.]

the major, for it is not universally true. 2. I distinguish the major. *What
occurs with GOD willing occurs with God as Author:* if “author” is taken
catachrestically for one who permits and orders, the major is true, but the
conclusion is not against us. If *what occurs with GOD willing occurs with God
as Author* means with God as procurer and efficient cause, the major is false
and the conclusion will be vain. 3. *What occurs with GOD willing,* that is,
approving, *occurs with GOD as Author*: the major is true, but in that sense the
minor is most false. *What occurs with GOD willing,* that is, decreeing that it
be done by another who is subject to law, *occurs with GOD as Author*: in that
sense the major is false. 4. *What occurs with God willing occurs with GOD
approving.* In that sense the major is false, for when a genus is affirmed the
species is not affirmed. It is an animal; therefore it is a human being. This
does not follow. The approving will is, as it were, one species of will, while
the decreeing will is another species contradistinguished from it. And God’s
will is, as it were, a genus comprehending both wills.
