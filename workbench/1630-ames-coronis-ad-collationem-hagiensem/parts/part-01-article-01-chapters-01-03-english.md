ARTICLE ONE

ON ELECTION

From Foreseen Faith

CHAPTER I

The state of the question is elicited.

The position of the Remonstrants is:

THAT GOD, by an eternal and immutable decree in Jesus Christ his Son, before
the foundation of the world was laid, determined to save—from the fallen
human race, liable to sins—those who believe in that same Son by the grace of
the Holy Spirit and who, by the same grace, persevere to the end in that faith
and in the obedience of faith, in Christ, because of Christ, and through
Christ.

This ear of grain must be rubbed a little so that, once all the coverings of
ambiguity have been removed, it may be understood more easily what grain it
contains and what the matter under dispute is.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 14 | printed p. 2]

In this decree of election, then, I seem to myself to have observed that the
controversy has five divisions. Accordingly, I think that those who wish to
descend into the inner recesses of the case must consider the same number of
parts diligently: 1. the act itself—the “who,” or what sort of decree it is;
2. the object—the “what,” or of what kind it is; 3. the terminus—the “to
which,” or what it is; 4. the condition and rationale—the “by which,” or what
it is; and 5. the foundation—the “upon which,” or of what kind it must be
posited.

As for the first, Arminius posited two decrees in his *Declaration*, p. 47.
Either one of them can be designated by these same words because of that
equivocation which Arminius makes latent in the word *election* (*Response to
the Synodical Questions*, question 1). The first is that general pronouncement
by which God determined within himself to bestow salvation upon no class of
human beings except those who believe and persevere to the end in the
obedience of faith. The second is that particular or singular decree by which
God decreed to save certain individual persons whom he already foresaw would
believe and would persevere to the end.

The Remonstrants commonly distinguish both of these decrees by the name
*predestination*, although the former is only a prior conception and
prescription of the duty owed and of the condition which God would require in
those who are to be saved. Arminius himself describes it in this way in
*Private Theses*, disputation 41, thesis 1. It should no more be called
predestination than that statement of the law, “Those who do this will live,”
or those opposite statements, “I will condemn unbelievers,” and, “I will
condemn those who do not do this.” Thus, if this account of predestination is
admitted, all mortals would be said to be destined twice to life and twice
also to death, yet not so destined to life that they could not all be
condemned, nor so destined to death that they could not all be saved. The
Article, wrapped in ambiguity of this kind, also entangled the disputation
itself to some extent and did not allow it to be distinct. Thus, the reverend
Brethren who stood on the op—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 15 | printed p. 3]

—posite side seem to have taken that thesis as though it contained only that
general declaration and nothing more. For that reason, in their first writing
at the Conference, they requested a more distinct declaration of it. The
Remonstrants themselves also sometimes want it to be understood that way. In
the very formulation of the state of the question which they delivered to the
States at the end of this Conference, p. 489, they call this predestination
which they had set forth in this Article the one extreme or the terminus from
which one must begin, because it is the cause and fountain from which grace
and the other things follow. These statements can in no way be understood of
that singular predestination which I mentioned above. It is not first of all,
but is the last of the four things which Arminius posits as decrees
(*Declaration*, p. 47); nor is it the fountain from which grace and the other
things follow, but it follows grace itself and our faith. Arminius puts it
this way: “It rests upon foreknowledge of faith and perseverance.” They should
therefore accuse themselves rather than accuse others—as they nevertheless do
most unfairly on pp. 86–88—if some arrows do not aim with sufficient certainty
at so changeable a target.

II. Concerning the object, the reader must know that by “those who believe”
not all who are true members of Christ are understood here. First of all, they
seem to remove infants from this roll, so that no account is taken of them in
election. Nicolaas Grevinckhoven was so unwilling to concede this that he
accuses of extraordinary rashness the person who posited as granted and
acknowledged that, even on their position, there is some election of infants.
Indeed, he says on p. 150: “For the sake of inquiry, I asked you to prove this
very point—that there is any election of infants.” Nor do they include all who
are engrafted into Christ through true faith, but only those who have already
completed their lives. Thus speaks Nicolaas Grevinckhoven, the best interpreter
of this position, on p. 136: “Whenever we establish believers as the object—”

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 16 | printed p. 4]

—“of election, we always join faith and perseverance in faith.” Therefore a
person must persevere to the final terminus of life before he can be capable
of any act, fruit, or perception which might flow from this election. And so,
since they are believers before they are destined, they should not be called
*predestined* believers, but rather *postdestined* believers, although they
are predestined to be blessed. The same man distinguishes—I admit—between a
certain incomplete election which he makes common to all believers and this
complete election which regards no one except a dying person. See
Grevinckhoven, p. 137. But this is a division so crude and so thoroughly
rejected elsewhere that, when served a second time, it would bring at least
the greatest nausea, if not death itself. Nevertheless, it will be worth the
student of truth’s effort to compare it with Socinus’s distinction in chapter
13 of his *Theological Lectures*, where he makes one election weaker and the
other firmer.

III. The terminus toward which they want this predestination to tend must also
be observed by the studious reader. They teach that it must be placed in the
single salvation or glory which follows this life. They say that believers
are predestined to glory alone (Grevinckhoven, p. 132), contrary to the
testimony of Scripture, Ephesians 1:4–5, and contrary to the nature of
salvation. The right to salvation, which belongs to adoption; its judicial
award, which belongs to justification; and even the part or degree of it
which is bestowed in present sanctification cannot flow from a source other
than glory itself. Nor should they be separated from that glory, with which
God has joined them.

Nor should it be concealed that Arminius himself (*Private Theses*,
disputation 41) recognizes a certain predestination to faith, while
Grevinckhoven (pp. 116–17) moreover makes it particular and comparative, so
that Paul rather than Caiaphas would be given this grace. But 1. the proposed
Article does not recognize such a predestination, and yet they think it
unlawful to ascend beyond that Article (p. 493). 2. The predestination to—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 17 | printed p. 5]

—faith which Arminius grants is the Huberian predestination of the whole human
race. It contains nothing but a decree concerning the sufficient
administration of the means of faith to everyone, arising from some affection
and will—I know not what—to bestow eternal salvation upon the whole world.
Accordingly, it is not elective predestination but a certain common ordering
of every human being. It does not bring about the certain generation of faith
but can coexist with the final unbelief of every human being. 3. The election
which Grevinckhoven imagines—“I will reveal the mystery of faith to little
children”—is also something general, designating one person no more than
another, and contains not the gift of grace but its offer alone. I need not
say that this is a plainly foolish invention, as I have sufficiently shown
elsewhere. It therefore cannot be denied, but must be acknowledged as true,
as I said, that they refer glory alone, not grace, to divine predestination.

Nor should this point be passed over: they deny that salvation itself or glory
is destined under the aspect of an end (Grevinckhoven, pp. 129–30), but only
as a wage or reward. If this aberrant consideration is properly weighed, it
further entails that not human beings themselves but that reward must
necessarily be established as the first and proper object of this election
and predestination. A human being is not destined to a reward; rather, a
reward is destined to a human being. But what we infer from the Scriptures
with the most certain reason is different: namely, that *proorismon*
(προωρισμόν, in the witness's spelling) denotes a certain deputation,
ordination, and dispatch toward a
fixed end, and that three things concur in it—what is ordained, through what,
and toward what. The first is the human being; the second, grace; and the
third, glory. Yet glory also includes grace as the means to the end.

IV. Concerning the condition, one must know that when they honor faith with
this title, they understand not every true and living faith, but only
consummated faith. They posit it—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 18 | printed p. 6]

—not as a condition which, while preceding salvation, follows election (a
point which our side has never denied but has constantly handed down with the
greatest religious care), but as one which precedes election. Nor do they
posit it as a bare antecedent or terminus from which—as they nevertheless
often seem to present the matter when they say that we make sin the cause of
election just as much as they make faith its cause—but as an antecedent
prescribed and demanded by God. Once it has been furnished, not only salvation
as its reward, but also the willing and determination of salvation, follow.
Nicolaas Grevinckhoven has taught us in these words on p. 24 that a condition
of this kind has the same nature and force which we commonly attribute to a
true cause: “I contend that it is entirely fitting to the nature of laws and
prescribed conditions that the will of a judge should be moved to the reward
by the condition demanded and furnished.”

Nor should it be thought that faith alone is raised to this dignity by them;
works are raised together with it. Thus Grevinckhoven, p. 137: “Peremptory
election requires beforehand in its object the whole, consummated obedience
of faith, according to Paul’s statement, ‘I have fought the fight,’ etc.” Thus
the sons of Arminius, in the epistle preceding his dissertation on Romans 7:
“It must be maintained from the Scriptures that true conversion and the
performance of good works are conditions required before justification.” If
they are before justification, then they are also before election, for the
decrees of election and justification are “uniform and conformable”
(Grevinckhoven, p. 111). Finally, in *Private Theses*, disputation 39, thesis 4,
Arminius himself describes the condition of the new covenant: that a person
should believe in God through Christ and in Christ, and should render to both
love, worship, honor, fear, and complete obedience according to the
prescription. Indeed, on the very threshold of their response, p. 89, these
Remonstrants say that the quality taken into account in election consists in
“obedient humility by which they demonstrate their faith,” not in faith
alone.

V. As for the fifth point, we do not deny that the force and dignity of the
foundation in the decree of election may be attributed to Christ, if this is
done in the right sense—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 19 | printed p. 7]

—namely, that he is himself the head of the elect and the origin or fountain
of all the good things destined for them in election. But what the Remonstrants
intend and parade is something else, and it involves more than one serious
error. 1. They establish Christ’s mediation as the *prokatarktike*
(προκαταρκτικήν), or externally moving or provoking, cause with respect to the
divine willing in the act of election (Nicolaas Grevinckhoven, p. 103). This
accords neither with the nature of God nor with the nature of mediation, as I
have shown elsewhere, nor with the various testimonies of Scripture which
teach that God’s will was the cause why Christ became our Mediator, and not,
conversely, that his mediation properly moved God to will our salvation. 2.
Nevertheless, they commonly distinguish that willing in such a way that it
cannot in any way be referred to Christ as its cause. They say that it must be
divided into an intention of procurement and an intention of application
(Grevinckhoven, pp. 4, 7–9). The matter itself plainly teaches that the former
intention, namely that of procurement, precedes Christ’s death as its
foundation and is not founded upon it. The latter, however, does not follow
from Christ’s death as its effect (in Grevinckhoven’s judgment, pp. 8–9); it
still remained undetermined whether God would will that redemption to be
applied to anyone or not. 3. They seem to make Christ the foundation of
reprobation just as much as of election to life. For they say that Christ is
the foundation of that entire decree by which God determined to elect
believers and reprobate unbelievers (Grevinckhoven, p. 107; Arminius,
*Declaration*, p. 47). 4. They say that it is not simply Christ himself as
Mediator who possesses that causal force in the election of individual human
beings, but Christ apprehended by faith (Grevinckhoven, p. 108). Accordingly,
they maintain that a person must be—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 20 | printed p. 8]

—lieve and also persevere in faith to the end before God posits the proper
foundation of his election. Indeed, the foundation of election itself—namely,
faith—is posited by the human being rather than by God, for faith is a human
act. This is what Arminius plainly said: “Election rests upon foreknowledge of
faith” (*Declaration*, p. 47), where nothing other than foreseen faith can be
understood by “foreknowledge of faith.” For Arminius thinks it absurd to
distinguish the permission of the fall from the permitted fall when the fall
is considered insofar as it was permitted (*Examination of Perkins on
Predestination*, p. 24). Therefore, in the judgment of Arminius and his
disciples, whoever tried to distinguish foreknowledge of faith from foreknown
faith would deservedly incur a similar rebuke—especially in this matter,
where foreknowledge has no place except insofar as it is directed toward
faith. Foreknown or foreseen faith, then, is on the Remonstrants’ position the
proper foundation of that election by which God decreed to save these
individual human beings rather than others.

From these points the attentive reader can now see clearly enough both the
innermost meaning of this thesis and the genuine state of the whole
controversy. Our men did not pursue all these matters one by one at the
Conference. Nevertheless, I wanted to set them out together so that the
substance of the case might be understood more easily and fully. In the
disputation which follows, however, I shall press only those tracks in which
our men went before me at the Conference.

CHAPTER II

On the Division of the Case

In that Article proposed by the Remonstrants, their opponents challenged two
things above the rest: 1. the object, and 2. the condition or attached cause.
The Remonstrants do not approve this division; indeed—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 21 | printed p. 9]

—they report on p. 88 that it had been made plain that it must not be admitted.
But they would have acted much more fairly if they had made the cause of their
censure no less known than the censure itself. For my part, I cannot discover
any just cause, not even by reflection, why any material and common object
cannot be considered separately from that formal rationale by which it is
subjected to a specific act. Can it not be shown separately—indeed, is it not
customarily shown—what is visible in the nature of things before any mention
is made of color or light? Certainly Paul himself gives a rather long
description both of election itself and of other blessings before he thought
faith should even be named, Ephesians 1:1–11. But it is enough to have
reproved that accusation in a word; let us approach the matter itself instead.
Here, however, the reader must again be warned that the Remonstrants’ thesis
was posited in words so ambiguous that it gave their opponents just occasion
to take its meaning as though it contained nothing beyond that general
declaration, “I will save those who believe and persevere in faith to the
end.” With Arminius this belongs to the second decree (*Declaration*, p. 47),
but it would not also include the purpose—which is Arminius’s fourth decree—of
saving certain particular persons. In the first part, therefore, our men
opposed this and confirmed by many arguments that election is of certain
individual human beings. The adversaries answer, not ineptly, that this
statement, posited so generally, does nothing against them. “But if the
meaning is,” say the Remonstrants on p. 89, “that God’s election to salvation
concerns certain individual human beings as individual human beings—that is,
as destitute of qualities and accordingly outside Christ and faith in
him—this we deny.” Indeed, I say, if the meaning is that this election
concerns individual human beings even while they are still destitute of that
final perseverance in faith and obedience which can—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 22 | printed p. 10]

—be possessed by anyone only at the final terminus of life, you and that
thesis of yours which I described above deny this too. Whether it can be
proved by the proposed arguments will therefore become clear from the
following comparison of them with the opposing responses.

CHAPTER III

The First Argument

Page 86

The first argument is taken from the words *election* and *elect*, which are
used so frequently in the Scriptures. The reasoning is firm. For if
perseverance to the end were a condition required beforehand in the object of
election, as the Remonstrants maintain, then election would have no object;
that is, no one would be elect before the end of life. Nor should anyone be
called actually elect while he is alive, because no act of election is yet
exercised concerning him and God as elector has no relation to him. But the
Scriptures protest everywhere against this paradox when they so often call
people who are still living here *elect*. The former assertion must therefore
also be rejected.

The Remonstrants say something about each testimony of Scripture, but nowhere
do they offer anything in favor of an election so late.

Matthew 20:16: “Many are called, but few are elect.” They say, “Here a quality
is posited on the part of the elect, and it consists in obedient humility, by
which they did not murmur against the master of the household.”

But 1. that quality is not posited as the cause or rationale of election but
as an effect. This cause is assigned to the constancy of certain people in
God’s grace: they are elect. 2. That quality is neither restricted nor
extended there to the final end of life. 3. If the rationale of election were
to be placed in—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 23 | printed p. 11]

—obedient humility, then those who remained obedient in the work for a whole
day would seem to have deserved election more than those who remained for one
hour. 4. The comparison is not established by the Holy Spirit as though the
obedient humility of these last workers were exalted there and distinguished
them from the first. For what excellent or memorable thing did they do if they
did not murmur against that dispensation of the master of the household, in
which they had an equal lot with the first, although they went into the
vineyard when a single hour of work remained? 5. This one consideration
supplies the strongest argument against foreseen faith and perseverance. If
faith constitutes the formal rationale in the object of election, and does so
not by its own simple nature but as it has perseverance attached to it, then
the duration of this perseverance must surely be so determined that it does
not depend upon the utterly uncertain end of life, for the rationale of every
certain thing is fixed and certain. But if it is determined to some fixed span
of duration, then reason and necessity urge that greater account be taken of
the person who remained in obedience for a long time, and that his final
defection be forestalled in some way, than that such a great privilege in so
weighty a matter should be granted to a single little hour at the final
terminus of life—a moment not brought to maturity by any special strength but
then arriving perhaps by chance or accident.

Matthew 22:14: the same words are again read as appended by Christ to another
parable.

The Remonstrants respond: “1. As this parable pertains generally to the Jews
and Gentiles, so Scripture plainly teaches that the Jews were rejected
because of unbelief, while the Gentiles stand through faith, Romans 11:20.”
Quite right; but meanwhile they ought to observe, even from these words, that
unbelief bears one relation to rejection and faith another relation to
election. They are rejected because of unbelief; they are not elected because
of faith, but stand through faith.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 24 | printed p. 12]

Scripture never teaches that any people or any human being was ever elected
by God because of faith. Moses frequently used to press the contrary in
Deuteronomy 7, 8, 9, etc., as did the prophet in Ezekiel 16. The matter itself
cries out that a nation does not first become God’s people through faith and
then become elected by God, but becomes his people through God’s election.
“Jehovah your God has chosen you so that you may be a peculiar people to him
above all the peoples who are under the whole heaven,” Deuteronomy 7:6. God
first says, “He is my people,” and then that people itself says, “Jehovah is
my God,” Zechariah 13, final verse. From this, therefore, they might be taught
what the true election of believers is: namely, one which does not find
believers but makes them. “For he who caused a world to be elected out of the
world did not find what he would elect.” Augustine on John 15.

They respond: “2. As this parable is applied to individual human beings, it
attributes the wedding garment, which is faith, to the elect.” This too is
true, at least in part. But I ask further: was not this garment a pure gift of
God? Was it not then given by God to his people when they differed in nothing
from other mortals, except that they were among the most miserable? Both are
plainly taught in the Scriptures, Ezekiel 16:3–4, 8–10. If this is true, then
it is plain that election precedes these garments and does not follow them.

Matthew 24:22: “For the sake of the elect the days will be shortened.” The
Remonstrants answer that Mark 13:20 expounds this in the words “the elect whom
God elected,” which signifies “the elect together with their faith, since God
delights in faith alone.” But I wonder how “faith” is inferred from Mark’s
words “whom God elected” as something antecedent to election any more than
from Matthew’s simple title “elect.” If Mark had said, “who elected God,” the
inference would not be so meager; but it cannot be elicited from the words
that are there. “God delights in faith alone,” they say; “therefore, he elects
only together with faith or from faith.” I answer briefly in God’s very own
words: “Jehovah inclined in love—”

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 25 | printed p. 13]

—“toward his people because of the love of Jehovah by which he loves them,”
Deuteronomy 7:7–8.

God does not find the material of love in his people, but brings it in; and he
delights no less in the very act and purpose of bringing it in than in the
thing itself once bestowed.

The same chapter, verse 24: “So that they might seduce even the elect, if it
were possible.” The Remonstrants respond: “No one is seduced except from
faith; therefore, the elect there are believers.” But if election is the
rationale which prevents seduction, then 1. perseverance to the end is not
required before election; rather, election is required before perseverance.
2. Then the unequal lot of certain human beings in comparison with others,
even in this life, also flows from election.

Romans 9:11: “So that God’s purpose according to election might stand.” The
Remonstrants respond: “This election is said in the same place to have been
made from the One who calls—that is, according to faith from grace.” This is a
mockery of a most forceful passage. For 1. it does not say there that election
is made from the One who calls, but speaks of the purpose according to
election. 2. It does not say that this purpose is *made* but that it *stands*,
or stands firm. Therefore, both election and that purpose which was according
to election preceded this calling as the principle which now began to be
brought to completion through this means. 3. “From the One who calls” can
properly be expounded by those words which they use here, “from grace
according to faith,” so that Grace is the divine mercy shining through in
election and faith is its effect. But taken this way, it profits their Sparta
nothing. If, however, they expound it with Arminius as “from the One who
calls,” that is, “from the faith by which the one who calls is obeyed,” or as
“from the one called,” they have that distortion sufficiently demolished by
the most learned young man Jodocus Larenus in his *Response to Arminius’s
Analysis*, p. 44.

Romans 11:5: “There is a remnant according to election.” The Remonstrants
respond: “The Apostle adds that this election is gratuitous, not from works,
and consequently from faith, Romans 4:16.” They speak truly: in the text it is
called “the election of grace,” but not passively, as though grace were
elected by—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 26 | printed p. 14]

—a human being, but actively, because God elects a human being from grace. “But
if from grace,” they say, “then from faith.” Is that really so? Calling is from
grace; is calling then also made from faith, rather than to faith? Christ
Jesus, our blessed Savior, was himself also given, promised, and sent by God
the Father from grace. Was that then from faith? I grant that the Apostle’s
words are, “Therefore from faith, so that it may be through grace.” Hence one
may reasonably infer that what is from faith is through grace. But to deduce
conversely that everything which occurs through grace is from faith would be
backward and perverse.

Ephesians 1:4: “God elected us in Christ,” and verse 11: “In whom we were
elected.” The Remonstrants respond: “No one is elected in Christ unless he is
in Christ, but no one is in Christ unless he is a believer. Therefore, no one
is elected in Christ unless he is a believer.” I too will be permitted to
syllogize in exactly the same manner and by the same means. All who are called
or even redeemed are called and redeemed in Christ. In 2 Corinthians 5:18—to
go no farther—we are blessed “with every spiritual blessing in Christ,”
Ephesians 1:3. The Remonstrants themselves say in Article 3 that it is
necessary that a human being “be regenerated [the source misprints
*regignatur*] by God in Christ” in order to believe, or to do or think
anything good. And in Article 4 they say that all good works which can be
imagined—and accordingly the very beginning both of faith and of
repentance—must be attributed to God’s grace in Christ. But no one is called
or redeemed in Christ unless he is in Christ, and no one is in Christ unless
he is a believer; therefore, no one is either called or redeemed in Christ
unless he was already a believer. Such is the force of this argument. But the
matter itself stands thus: “in Christ” must in no way be referred here to the
object of election, or to the human beings who are said to be elected, as
though they had previously been in Christ. Instead, it must be referred to
God’s action, just as the words themselves indicate clearly enough. For it
does not say “being in him” (ὄντες ἐν αὐτῷ), or “those in him” (τοὺς ἐν αὐτῷ),
but “he elected—”

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 27 | printed p. 15]

—“us in him” (ἐν αὐτῷ), that is, through him, just as verse 7 has “in whom”
(ἐν ᾧ), that is, through whom we have redemption. So also Colossians 1:16: “in
him” (ἐν αὐτῷ), or through him, all creatures were made—not because even
inanimate things believed in him. Thus here too, we were elected “in him” (ἐν
αὐτῷ), or through him. Nevertheless, this is not meant so properly that he
would be posited as the cause of the principle in this action, but only of the
terminus—that is, of grace both to be prepared and to be bestowed. Yet it is
in such a way that we may truly be said to have been in Christ even then, when
we were elected: not by actual inherence, which is through faith, but by
virtual containment, so to speak, which has no other rationale than the
divine purpose itself.

2 Timothy 2:19: “The Lord knows those who are his.” The Remonstrants respond:
“God knows as his own those who devote themselves to the righteousness which
is from faith.” Everyone knows that God knows such people. But the further
question is whether he did not know them before they persevered to the end in
that righteousness. If they think he did not, they plainly contradict the
Scriptures. Galatians 4:9: “But now you know God, or rather are known by God.”
Indeed, God knows as his own all those in whom he decreed to kindle the faith
and zeal which he knows to be proper to his own. “I say,” says Augustine on
John 10, “the Lord knows those who are his. The sheep themselves sometimes do
not know themselves, but the shepherd knows them according to the election of
the sheep before the foundation of the world.”

John 10:3: “He calls his sheep by name.” The Remonstrants respond: “The sheep
are considered here as they hear their Shepherd’s voice.” But the question
still remains whether they are sheep because they hear the Shepherd’s voice,
or whether they hear because they are his sheep. I hope they will not say
that essence flows from operation, but rather that operation flows from
essence. According to God’s predestination, many sheep are still wandering
outside who will afterward hear the Shepherd’s voice. Augustine on John 10.

John 13:18: “I know whom I have elected.” The Remonstrants respond: “Christ
knew whom he had elected as his Apostles. What is sur—”

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 28 | printed p. 16]

—“prising about that?” Nothing whatever, to be sure. But it is nevertheless
surprising that the most keen-sighted Remonstrants see no election here except
to the apostolate, when Christ makes an open distinction between Judas the
Apostle and the others: “I do not speak of all of you.”
