Verse 28. Do not move the age-old boundary which your fathers set in place.

In עלם (*of an age*) there is a synecdoche of the species. For an age, which comprises about fifty years, is put for a long period of time. In אבותי (*[your fathers]*) there is likewise a synecdoche of the species, standing for ancestors.

The purpose is to exhort everyone to possess what is his own in peace.

The establishment of boundaries seems, in the first place, to rest upon the law of nature. For God seems to have separated and distinguished peoples and kingdoms from one another by the sea, rivers, and mountains, so that neither these should cross the boundaries set for them, nor those invade others' possessions by leaping over their own limits. But the people whom he himself chose, and for whom he undertook particular care, he wished to enclose within limits all the more precisely, the more clearly he had given testimony of his love toward them. For this reason Scripture itself describes the boundaries of the Jews with the greatest exactness. All the regions, southern, northern, eastern, and western, are carefully and minutely delineated, and it is expressly shown where each should begin and where it should end, Numbers 34. In the book of Joshua, moreover, the boundaries of the individual tribes are explained separately and at greater length. This is evidence that under the Gospel, when God undertakes the greatest care of his Church, this doctrine ought to remain in force against the Anabaptist community of goods.

Since, therefore, the demarcation of boundaries is protected by natural and divine law, it follows that those who break through them and remove them violate not only human law, but also natural and divine law. And since living in peace is of the greatest importance for maintaining a holy society, it is worthwhile, indeed necessary, to embrace this doctrine concerning the establishment and preservation of boundaries. It has the greatest weight both in reconciling those who have become estranged and in cherishing and strengthening in concord those who are united with one another.

The Papists are accustomed to seek from this passage a defence of their errors, which have prevailed for several hundred years. But they do so ineptly. For besides the fact that they twist Solomon's words from their natural sense to an allegorical one of their own invention, it can easily be shown that the doctrine of the Gospel which we profess is much more ancient than their fictions, since it has been received from the very cradle of the Church and from the beginning of the world. Consequently the novel and, as it were, yesterday-born religion of the Papists ought to rise in reverence before its grey head.

Of equal weight is what they are accustomed to bring forward and press from this passage in favour of the writings of the fathers. For we may answer, first, that the fathers frequently disagree with the Papists, and sometimes with themselves; secondly, that all the Prophets and Apostles stand on our side, in comparison with whom even the most venerable of the fathers is beardless.

### Translator's note

In the second lexical note the witness prints אבותי and the Latin gloss *partes tui*, although the verse lemma has *patres tui* (“your fathers”). The translation supplies “[your fathers]” as the evident intended gloss; the paired transcription preserves both anomalous printed forms. The first Hebrew headword is printed עלם, without a vowel-letter *waw*. The closing images of a grey head and beardlessness continue the argument from antiquity: Cartwright represents the apostolic doctrine as older than the papal claims and the later church fathers.
