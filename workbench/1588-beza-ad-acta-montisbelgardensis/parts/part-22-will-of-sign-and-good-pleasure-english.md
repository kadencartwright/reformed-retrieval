On the distinction between the will of sign and the will of good pleasure

Although the Lord our God is no hypocrite but judge of hypocrites, and his will is not manifold but simple and one, this does not prevent us from considering it in two ways according to our capacity: first, insofar as he reveals something to us; second, insofar as something remains hidden in him, sometimes agreeing with what he reveals and sometimes differing from it. Hence the scholastic distinction, not to be rejected if correctly understood, between the will of sign and the will of good pleasure—that is, the revealed and secret will. I will explain it more fully because I see some unjustly censure it through misunderstanding.

From the beginning of sin it was revealed that God would save human beings through the woman’s seed by crushing Satan’s head. When and how he would save was revealed gradually. God promised Abraham, and Abraham believed, that all nations would share his blessing; yet the calling of the Gentiles was delayed for many ages (Acts 14:16; Romans 16:25–26). It is revealed that the Lord will come on the last day to judge living and dead, yet God alone knows that day. Countless examples can be produced from Scripture and daily experience. Behold one—not manifold—will of God, partly open and partly hidden. But what if we say God conceals something contrary to what he presents and reveals? Will he not appear to disagree with himself and act as a hypocrite? By no means. The vice of hypocrisy includes the evil deception by which hypocrites seek to harm good people; not every kind of simulation does so, for some deception is good—

By the will of sign, outwardly revealed, God not only wills but commands Abraham to kill Isaac. By the will of good pleasure, hidden from Abraham for a time, he willed only to test Abraham’s faith through such remarkable obedience. Speaking to Moses in Exodus 32, the Lord says, “My wrath will burn against this people and I will consume them.” He commands Isaiah to announce Hezekiah’s death and announces Nineveh’s destruction within forty days, yet executes none of these. Was he thinking differently from what he spoke, like hypocrites, or did he later change his judgment? I think our adversary will dare say neither. Did God place the truth of his decree in the discretion of the people, Hezekiah, or the Ninevites? That cannot be said truly or piously. Their repentance, the means through which God did not fulfill his announcement, was graciously given them by the very one announcing these things. He would not have given it had he willed to execute the announcement, and had he willed it, he certainly would have done so immutably.

What, then? With respect to human beings, the will here was twofold: the revealed will of sign opposed to the secret will, which appears only from the outcome. With respect to God, however, he never willed anything except what he also did. Yet he said he would do what he did not will done—not like hypocrites or with falsehood, but announcing threatened punishment while concealing the condition of repentance, as though the punishment were decreed and immutable. He did so for the good of those whom in his anger he did not will to destroy, so that by terrifying conscience he might open for those whom he secretly decreed to save as repentant the way to receive the gift of repentance.

Precisely the same judgment applies conversely when the Lord willed to exercise his just judgments by a will partly revealed and partly secret until he revealed it—two wills appearing diametrically opposed. By the open and common will of sign he made the covenant simply and without distinction with Abraham and his offspring, yet by the will of good pleasure rejected Ishmael. He deprived Esau, though gifted by birth with primogeniture, and his offspring of the covenant’s blessing. Many have long been and daily are outwardly called by the common preaching of Word and sacraments, from whom only those elect by saving good pleasure are saved. This must also be maintained here whether one considers the Father or the Son speaking as God. If we consider these things’ nature and what they reveal even to the unwilling, toward what but this gathering and salvation did Christ’s teaching and works invite every hearer and spectator? But why did he not will to gather all? Had he willed it, he would certainly have given everyone faith, through which alone all sheep are joined to this head—

—and if he had given it, all certainly would have been gathered. Why did he not draw them too? Because, being debtor to none, he did not will to draw them, by the hidden will eventually manifested in the outcome. Far from the Son opposing this will—which he attributes to the Father’s *eudoxia*, good pleasure—while by the open will of sign willing indefinitely to gather everyone, and by the will of his own good pleasure distinguishing everywhere from them only those given him by the Father, he instead thanks the Father for this distinction in Matthew 11:25 and does not pray for them in John 17:9 and 16, whom as God he also knew in John 6:64.

Thus with respect to humanity this will was twofold: one open, visible in the Savior’s power and goodness in his teaching and works, but concealed from people blind by nature and against their own conscience; the other hidden, which they finally experienced in their just destruction. But in the Father and Christ there was one will: outwardly, to convict them by presenting these things and increasingly remove every pretext of ignorance; inwardly, justly to harden them and finally execute in fact the secret decree concerning their destruction through their own fault and impenitence.
