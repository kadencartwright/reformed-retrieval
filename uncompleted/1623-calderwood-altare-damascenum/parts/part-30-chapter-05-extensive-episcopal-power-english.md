[1623 printed p. 318 | IA leaf 330 | PDF p. 330 | begins at `Quod ad quartum`]

As to the fourth, namely the extensive power of bishops, the English dioceses
are spread more widely than permits either the lawful and ordinary government
of any particular church to be preserved in them through a common presbytery
(for the hierarchists say that all the parishes of a diocese constitute one
particular church), or the extraordinary government of the whole province
through provincial councils. *It is evident from this,* Parker says, *that not
even the provinces of the ancient metropolitans, though they had many
episcopates under them, were so extensive as the dioceses of the English
bishops are now. For who would not think it burdensome for even an entire
English diocese to be convened twice in a year?* The same writer says, *The
circuit of these men,* namely the English archbishops and bishops, *is
immoderate, so that it sometimes extends beyond 40 English miles.*

[Marginalia: *De Politia Eccl.* li. 3. ca. 19. sect. 26. pag. 293.; sect. 23. pag. 287.]

Camden reports the following about York. *From that time ecclesiastical
dignity became established in this city, and, after a pall was sent by Pope
Honorius, it was made a metropolitan city, which, besides twelve episcopates
in England, was to exercise primatial right over all the bishops of Scotland;
but five hundred years ago Scotland departed from its metropolis, and the
metropolis itself so devoured the other neighboring episcopates—admittedly
slender and almost shadowy ones—that now it has only four in its diocese,
namely Durham, Chester, Carlisle, and Man or Sodor on the Isle of Man.*

[Marginalia: *Camd. Brit.* edit. Francof. in 8. pa. 570.]

Thus one episcopate, York, devoured seven others. They were indeed slender if
compared with the episcopates that are now in England, yet not slender if you
consider the government of the church. *Human affairs would be happier if all
kingdoms were small and rejoiced in harmonious proximity,* Augustine says.
So also the affairs of the church would be happier if one episcopate were
divided into several. The apostles instituted no particular churches other
than small ones, enclosed within narrow bounds and rejoicing in harmonious
proximity.

[Marginalia: *De civit. Dei* lib. 4. cap. 15.]

Camden writes as follows about the diocese of Lincoln: *Their diocese, not
content with those narrow bounds with which in the early Saxon church the
bishops of Sidnacester, who presided over this region, were content, embraced
so many regions that it labored under its own size. And although Henry II
detached the province of Ely and Henry VIII detached Peterborough, nevertheless
in jurisdiction and number of counties it is still accounted by far the
largest in all England, and it has 1,247 parochial churches in its diocese.*

[Marginalia: *Britann.* pa. 430.]

And Lyndwood says, *When chrism is prepared in the cathedral church, which is
perhaps a hundred miles or more from the place of baptism—as one may posit, for
example, in the diocese of Lincoln—it is not to be imputed to the presbyter or
curate if on Holy Saturday itself he does not have fresh chrism, since it would
be impossible, and no one is bound to the impossible.* You see that the statute
of John Peckham cannot be observed, *that presbyters, either by themselves or
through deacons or subdeacons, should be bound each year before the solemnity
of Easter, or*

[Marginalia: *Provinciale Lyndewode* l. 1. titulo de sacra unct. cap. Cum sacri verb. vel quanto citius.]

[1623 printed p. 319 | IA leaf 331 | PDF p. 331 | ends before `Sunt & alia praeter quatuor`]

*as soon as it can be done, to seek sacred chrism for the individual churches
from the bishops of their places,* because of the extent of certain dioceses.
Are even the institutions of Christ and the apostles, by which all presbyters
ought to be participants in common government, to be violated rather than the
territories of the bishops diminished? In his eighth response to Augustine,
bishop of the English, Gregory says, *We wish you to ordain bishops in England
in such a way that the bishops themselves are not separated from one another
by a long interval, so that there is no necessity whereby they cannot come
together at the ordination of some bishop. For a bishop's ordination ought not
to be performed without three or four bishops assembled.*

[Marginalia: Dist. 80. ca. 6.]

It should also have been added that the other ancient canons of the church, if
not the institutions of the apostles, may be preserved. *England has two
provinces and likewise two archbishoprics, namely Canterbury, the primatial and
metropolitan see of all England, and York; under them are 25 bishops, 22 under
Canterbury and the remaining three under York,* as Camden says in his
*Britannia*.

[Marginalia: Pag. 109.]

Most English episcopates comprise several counties; six counties bound
Lincoln, the greatest of them all. Francis Godwin, bishop of Llandaff, in his
commentary on the prelates of England, numbers 52 ancient episcopates and 26
modern ones in the index. See also, reader, what we brought forward from Mucket
above at pp. 30–32. We treated the disadvantages and injuries of extensive
power in the preceding chapter.
