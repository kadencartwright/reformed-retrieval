XXIV. *[What prevents us from being able to see through things*, he says on
page 64, *is a certain corruption and depravity of our minds, which the Holy
Spirit corrects by his power.]* Answer: You rightly say that our minds are
corrupt and depraved and that the Holy Spirit corrects them by his power. But
the question is, what corruption do you acknowledge? An innate and implanted
incapacity, blindness, and death? And what power of the Spirit? The power which,
by infusing new light, removes this blindness and death and raises the mind and
renders it fit to perceive those supernatural things? What follows will teach
us what these things are. *[But this corruption is situated chiefly in two
things. For the mind's eye is not guided correctly; and even if it were guided
on the right path, obstructed by many impediments, it still could not see
sharply. The former condition involves a great calamity: for our sluggish
minds, enfeebled by the languor of sin, are not aroused of themselves; nor,
when they begin to act, do they discern where they ought to tend; nor, even if
they see this, do they grasp the path by which they ought to proceed.]* Answer:
Are you orthodox, you who assert that the whole corruption of our intellect
consists in these two things? O how great is the blindness of your mind, when
you do not perceive any greater corruption! But what do you say here that any
Pelagian or Socinian would not likewise say? You say that the mind's eye is not
guided correctly; you therefore suppose that the mind's eye is sound and has
in itself the faculty and power to perceive supernatural things, and that
man's mind is not altogether blind or dead. The Apostle therefore spoke badly
in 1 Corinthians 2:14: *The natural man is not capable of the things which are
of the Spirit of God.* You say that man is obstructed by many impediments; yet,
despite all these impediments, the intellect

[Page 114 | ONB canvas 168 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000168]

can possess a natural power or capacity to apprehend distinctly all those supernatural
things, just as someone shackled in fetters or stocks has the power to walk and
run. The mind of man therefore remains sound and whole with respect to its
inward power and capacity to understand and perceive those spiritual things.
Is this your orthodoxy, *Wolzogen*? Even in acquiring sciences—physics, for
example, metaphysics, mathematics, logic, and the like—the mind's eye is not
always guided correctly in everyone; and even if it were guided on the right
path, obstructed by many impediments it still could not see sharply. For our
sluggish minds are not always aroused of themselves; nor, when they begin to
act, do they discern where they ought to tend; nor, even if they see this, do
they grasp the path by which they ought to proceed. What? Is not our blindness
and incapacity to perceive the sacred mysteries greater than our incapacity
for Aristotle's logic, physics, or metaphysics? What believer does not
certainly know and acknowledge that his mind's eye is often not guided
correctly, and that even if it were guided on the right path, obstructed by
many impediments it still could not see sharply? Do they not freely confess,
and often perceive with grief and complain, that their sluggish minds,
enfeebled by the languor of sin, are not of themselves aroused to good, and
that when they begin to act, they do not discern where they ought to tend? Is
not the incapacity of the unregenerate to perceive supernatural things greater
than that of those born anew of the Spirit? You now see, *Wolzogen*, where your
doctrine tends; or if you do not see it, others do. Perhaps what follows will
explain these things. Let us listen. *[Just as in grasping the sciences,
although a sound mind is believed to have been distributed equally among
mortals, nevertheless, because they use different paths and methods, you may
see some far surpassing others in wisdom; so, although all men are equally
endowed with their mental faculties, the one who is led by the Spirit of God
advances in one way, and the one who is destitute of him in another.]* Answer:

[Page 115 | ONB canvas 169 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000169]

Therefore, just as men have a mind capable of the sciences, so they all also
have faculties equally fitted in themselves to understand spiritual things.
What would Pelagius himself find fault with here? Then, because man needs no
new light to be rendered fit to perceive those sciences, since that sound mind
which is distributed equally among all is sufficient, neither does man need
any new spiritual light by which he might become fitter to perceive spiritual
things and the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, or what the hope of his
calling is and what the riches of the glory of the inheritance among the
saints are. For that faculty of the mind with which all are equally endowed,
whether godly or ungodly, is sufficient. And just as that which causes one man
to surpass another in wisdom is not any better disposition of their minds but
the use of different paths and methods, so one man, but not another, advances
in spiritual things because the former is led by the Spirit and the latter is
not, but without any infusion of new light or healing of innate blindness. O
how crudely these things have been said! What cruder thing could have been said
by a Pelagian, Arminian, or Socinian? *[For even someone who is not governed by
the Spirit can sometimes attain the meaning of the scriptures* (he thus
confirms the preceding statements), *because natural light is sufficient for
him to attend to the things upon which understanding depends. But because he
does not attend in this way, does not search, and is not moved—not having been
aroused, taught, or led by this Spirit—it consequently happens that he does not
make such progress and cannot always attain the truth of the interpretation.]*
Answer: All these things are from the same bran. For you suppose that natural
light is also sufficient for understanding other things; indeed, you
acknowledge no other light. You do not say that another light is needed, but
only that a languid and sluggish man should be aroused by the Spirit. It is
clear that, according to your position, no new principle by which one
understands is needed. Natural light is always sufficient as the principle by
which one understands; and with the Spirit arousing, leading, and teaching by
moral persuasion, that is, or by the clear presentation and elucidation of the
object, they make progress. Therefore, the godly's knowledge of supernatural
things is also of the same nature

[Page 116 | ONB canvas 170 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000170]

as the ungodly's, and is distinguished only in degree. This is also what your
elder *Velthuysen* teaches us—an orthodox man? No, a Pelagian and Socinian—in
*On the Use of Reason in Theology*, page 8 and following. I do not think it is
my task to pursue these things at greater length or refute these errors. It is
enough to show what *Wolzogen* thinks; how little he agrees with the orthodox
is evident from what has been said and will be further evident from what is to
be said. *[We therefore attribute this power here to the Spirit*, Wolzogen
continues, *that he arouses the inactive mind, leads it to the true goal, and
leads it along those paths by which it may at last arrive at the saving truth
of interpretation, and that he does all those things required for this action,
as it were, of the soul. For the other thing which is lacking in us seems
capable of being referred to some passive condition of ours, and it comprises
all the impediments which can intervene to prevent us from seeing things
thoroughly as they are. I could not enumerate all of them here, because they
are innumerable; nor, even if I could, would it be necessary, provided that it
is sufficiently understood that when the Spirit removes those impediments, he
does only this: with the clouds dispersed and the shadows scattered, I may
then clearly perceive those things in scripture through which it is granted
to penetrate their meaning.]* Answer: These, therefore, are all the things
which must be attributed to the Spirit in this business. And unless my mind
deceives me, any Pelagian or Socinian would say all these same things. For you
do not say that God infuses a new, heavenly and supernatural light into minds
or implants a new principle by which they understand. You say that he *arouses
an inactive mind*, not that he *makes a dead mind alive*; that he *leads it to
the true goal*, not that he *gives it a supernatural faculty or power of seeing
or understanding*. What else, then, do you wish to attribute here to the
Spirit except what can be ascribed to assistant teachers when they teach
grammar, logic, rhetoric, or the like? Do they not also in their own way arouse
the inactive minds of their pupils, lead them to the true goal, and lead them
along those paths by which they may at last arrive at *knowl-*

[Page 117 | ONB canvas 171 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000171]

*-edge* of that science or art? You will perhaps say that you also *attribute
to the Spirit all things required for this action, as it were, of
the soul.* But what are all these things? Do you include among them the
infusion of new life, of a principle by which one understands, and of
supernatural power? Why do you not expressly indicate this? Why, having
enumerated the other things, are you silent about this operation of God? But
perhaps by *all these things* you understand nothing other than God's
concurrence and influx as the first cause. Yet the same thing is required for
all natural acts. What distinctive thing, then, do you understand here? Or
what do you say from which a Pelagian or Socinian would recoil, and to which
you would not find him assenting? What follows, which concerns the passive
condition, will not free you from this charge. For by these impediments you
understand nothing that concerns the constitution of the mind itself, but only
those external things which obscure the object, or which lie between the
object and the organ and so impede perception; and you assert that these are
innumerable. Therefore, by them you cannot understand the implanted
incapacity, unfitness, blindness, or death from which our minds now suffer.
Moreover, you yourself call those impediments *clouds and shadows*. For when
God removes those impediments, he only disperses these clouds and scatters the
shadows; and when they have been scattered and dispersed, he causes the mind
to be able to perceive the object. What could have been said more clearly to
express the Pelagian, Socinian, and Arminian mind? Who cannot now clearly
perceive—with the clouds dispersed by *Wolzogen's* own aid and the shadows
scattered—how *Wolzogen* Pelagianizes? For, as regards the passive condition
of the soul, or its disposition and constitution, nothing else is required in
order to perceive a supernatural object except that the intervening clouds be
dispersed. When they have been scattered, the mind can clearly perceive the
things which are in Scripture, through which it is granted to penetrate their
meaning. That is, nothing else is required than a clear presentation of the
object for *o-*

[Page 118 | ONB canvas 172 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000172]

*-ur* intellect to be spiritually illuminated. But let us leave *Wolzogen*
stuck in the Pelagian mire and proceed to other matters.
