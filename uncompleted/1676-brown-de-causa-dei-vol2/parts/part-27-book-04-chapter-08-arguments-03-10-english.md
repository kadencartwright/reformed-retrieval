# CHAPTER VIII (continued)

*Third*, that law is moral whose summary, given by Christ himself, is entirely
moral. The law of the Decalogue is such a law. Therefore, and so forth. The
major premise is certain, for Christ knew the nature of the divine laws
perfectly. The minor premise cannot be denied, for *to love the Lord our God
with all our heart, with all our soul, and with all our mind, and to love our
neighbor as ourselves* is without doubt entirely moral. These two things form
the summary of the whole law of the Decalogue, as Christ himself testifies in
Matthew 22:37–39: *But Jesus said to him, You shall love the Lord your God*,
and so forth. *This is the first and great commandment. But the second is like
it: You shall love your neighbor as yourself.* See the same in Mark 12:29–31,
where Christ further adds, *There is no other commandment greater than these.*
So too Luke 10:17–18. Hence Paul says in Galatians 5:14 that *the whole law is
fulfilled in this one saying: You shall love your neighbor as yourself.* For
love of one's neighbor presupposes love of God (1 John 4:21–22; 5:1). Paul has
similar statements in Romans 13:8–10, where it is also plain that he speaks of
the law of the Decalogue, for he enumerates almost all the precepts of the
second table. This summary is also called the royal law in James 2:8.

*Fourth*, that law is moral upon which the whole Law and the Prophets depend.
The law of the Decalogue is such a law. Therefore, and so forth. The minor
premise is established from Matthew 22:40: *On these two commandments*—Christ
says—*depend the whole Law and the Prophets.* The major premise is certain,
because by the Law and the Prophets he here means all those precepts concerning
the manner of living justly and worshiping God which are commanded in the Law
and the Prophets, as Pareus testifies on the passage. Therefore, the law of the
Decalogue is the summary of the teaching of the prophets, who explained and
applied it in their teaching.

*Fifth*, that law is moral which is neither judicial nor ceremonial, but far
more excellent. The law of the Decalogue is such a law. The major premise
needs no proof, since no fourth kind of law has yet been given. The minor
premise is established from Mark 12:33, where a certain scribe testifies that
*to love God with all one's heart, with all one's understanding, with all
one's soul, and with all one's strength, and to love one's neighbor as
oneself, is more than all burnt offerings and sacrifices.* Christ plainly
declares that he

[Printed p. 102 | IA PDF 110 | IA leaf 110 | IA decausadei02brow]

answered wisely when he said this, and thereby approves the statement. Here,
therefore, the law of the Decalogue—whose summary is to love God with the
whole heart and one's neighbor as oneself—is distinguished from the
ceremonial law and preferred far above it. And no one says that the Decalogue
contains purely judicial precepts.

*Sixth*, the law greater than which none is given is moral. The law of the
Decalogue is such a law. Therefore, and so forth. The major premise is certain,
for the moral law always holds first place and must be preferred far above the
ceremonial and civil laws, which were given only for a time. The minor premise
is proved from the same passage, namely Mark 12:31. *There is no other
commandment greater than these*, Christ says.

*Seventh*, the law which, after Christ's death and consequently after the
abrogation of the ceremonial laws, continued to bind the Gentiles—to whom
properly judicial laws were never proposed for observance—is moral. The law
of the Decalogue is such a law. Therefore, and so forth. The major premise
needs no proof, since no fourth kind of law which could bind anyone has yet
been alleged, and it is certain that the Gentiles were not bound by Jewish
laws, whether civil or ceremonial. The minor premise is proved from Romans
13:8–10. There the Apostle speaks of the law which is fulfilled by the person
who loves another, or whose fulfillment is love (verses 8, 10), and of the law
of which these commandments form a part: *You shall not commit adultery; you
shall not kill*, and so forth. But this law is the law of the Decalogue, and
the Apostle intends the Romans themselves to be bound by it. For in addressing
them he says, *Owe no one anything except this, that you love one another; for
the person who loves another has fulfilled the law.* The Apostle's argument
would have no force, however, if this law had not bound them. A similar
argument can be drawn from Galatians 5:14. There too he speaks of the law of
the Decalogue, and by the force of this law he exhorts even the Galatians to
serve one another from love. It can also be drawn from Ephesians 6:1–3, where
it is perfectly clear that the Ephesians too were bound by the law of the
Decalogue. For this commandment occurs in that law: *Honor your father and
mother*, with the attached promise, namely, *that your days may be prolonged
in that land which the Lord your God gives you.* Or, as the Apostle has it
there, *that it may go well with you and you may live long upon the earth.*

*Eighth*, that law is moral which forbids everything opposed to sound doctrine
and to the doctrine which accords with the glorious gospel of the blessed
God, and which we may now, under the gospel, legitimately use as good. The law
of the Decalogue is such a law. Therefore, and so forth. The major premise is
proved from the fact that the ceremonial law has now been abrogated under the
gospel, that the judicial law properly so called does not now properly bind
anyone, and that neither of these laws forbids everything opposed to sound
doctrine. The minor premise is plain from 1 Timothy 1:5, 8–11. For the law of
the Decalogue is meant there: its *end is love from a pure heart, a good
conscience, and an unfeigned faith* (verse 5), and it is called *good* (verse
8; compare Romans 7:12). It is not laid down for the righteous, but for the
lawless and insubordinate, the ungodly and sinners, the unholy and profane,
those who kill fathers and mothers, murderers, fornicators, men who lie with
males, kidnappers, liars, and perjurers (verses 9–10). We know that all these
wicked people and all these vices are condemned by the law of the Decalogue.

*Ninth*, that law is moral which is the royal law, which commands us to love
our neighbor as ourselves, which does not permit us to show partiality, and
which God

[Printed p. 103 | IA PDF 111 | IA leaf 111 | IA decausadei02brow]

himself specially pronounced, so that violation of one commandment convicts
the sinner of all and of contempt for divine authority—and does so in a
special manner—and which bound the Jews themselves after the ceremonial laws
had been abolished. The law of the Decalogue is such a law. Therefore, and so
forth. The major premise is certain, for the law is called *royal*, according
to Pareus on James 2:8, because it is universal and binds everyone, like a
royal road which everyone travels. Moreover, we showed above that love is the
summary of the moral law and that God pronounced this law in a special manner.
The minor premise is confirmed from James 2:8–11. There James speaks of the
law which God himself pronounced in a special manner, which commands us to
love our neighbor as ourselves and contains the commandments *Do not commit
adultery* and *Do not kill*. He thereby speaks of the law containing those
commandments which constitute one indivisible rule of righteousness and
holiness, are joined to one another by a firm bond, and must be received not
separately but jointly and as a complete whole, as an absolute law, all of
whose provisions were enacted with equal strictness. No one will deny that
this is the law of the Decalogue.

*Tenth*, that the law of the Decalogue is moral is proved by the testimonies
of theologians—not to mention the scholastics with Thomas, 2:2, question 100,
article 2—who expressly assert that it is moral, or that it is a summary
restatement of the natural law and consequently moral, or something else
which proves as much.

Let us first hear the testimony of entire churches.

The *Helvetic Confession*, chapter 12: [We teach that God's will—what he wishes
or does not wish us to do—is set forth for us in the law of God. Therefore we
confess the law to be good and holy. At one time this law is said to have been
written by the finger of God on human hearts and is called the law of nature;
at another, to have been engraved by his finger upon Moses' two tablets and
expounded more fully in the books of Moses. For the sake of clarity we divide
it into the moral law, which (note well) is contained in the Decalogue or the
two tablets and expounded through the books of Moses, and so forth.]

The *Württemberg Confession*, chapter on the law: [We acknowledge that the law
of God, whose epitome is the Decalogue, commands works which are best, most
just, and most perfect; and that a person is not only bound to obey the moral
precepts of the Decalogue, but also that if he performed the works of the
Decalogue with the perfection and integrity which the law requires, he would
truly be accounted righteous before God by his works and would obtain eternal
salvation by his merits.]

The *Heidelberg Catechism*, received and approved by the Dutch Church,
recognizes no law other than the law of the Decalogue as the standard according
to which good works can be done. See questions 91 and 92.

The *British Confession*, chapter 19, section 2: [This law—namely, the law God
gave Adam as a covenant of works—did not cease after the fall to be the most
perfect rule of righteousness. Under this designation it was delivered by God
on Mount Sinai, written on two tablets, and comprehended in ten commandments
(James 1:25; 2:8, 10–12; Romans 13:8–9; Deuteronomy 5:32; 10:4; Exodus
34:1–3). Besides this law, which is commonly called moral, and so forth.] And
in the *Larger Catechism*, question 99, it is framed in this way: [Where is the
moral law summarily comprehended? Answer: The moral law is summarily
comprehended in the ten commandments of the Decalogue, which were delivered
by the voice of God on Mount Sinai and inscribed by him on two stone tablets
(Deuteronomy 10:4; Exodus 34:1–4), but are set down in writing in Exodus 20,
and so forth.]

As for individual theologians, it would take too long to relate the
testimonies of them all here. We shall present only a few.

Pareus on the *Catechism*, question 92: [It should be noted in passing, he says
on my p. 675, how the moral law, the natural law, and the Decalogue differ. The
Decalogue is (note well) the summary of the moral laws which are scattered
throughout the whole Scripture of the Old and New Testaments. The law of
nature and the moral law were the same in uncorrupted nature. In corrupt
nature, however, a good part of the natural law was obscured by sin, and only
a small part of it concerning obedience to God remained in the human mind
after the fall. For this reason (note well), God repeated and declared the
complete sense of his law in the Decalogue in the Church. The Decalogue,
therefore, is (note well) the renewal and restoration of the law of nature.]

Maresius in *Systema*, locus 7, section 33: [The moral law—to pass now to its
parts—consists of two tablets written by the finger of God (Exodus 31:18), on
each side, or on their two faces. *Those tablets were God's work, and the
writing engraved upon the tablets was God's writing* (Exodus 32:15–16).]

Polvander, in *Synopsis of Purer Theology*, disputation 18, theses 34–38, not
only asserts this but also proves it.

Tilenus, in *Syntagma of Theology*, part 1, disputation 32, sections 27–28:
[It is plain that the moral law belongs to the natural law, because its
precepts form morals and direct them according to the rule of right reason.
The summary or epitome of the moral law is the Decalogue, whose commandments
God himself spoke in his own voice and engraved by his finger upon stone
tablets, and so forth.]

Altingius, *Common Places*, part 1, locus 7, on the law of God, p. 102: The
moral law, however, is presented κατ᾽ ἐπιτομὴν, *by way of epitome*, in the
Decalogue, and so forth.

Polanus, in *Syntagma of Theology*, book 6, chapter 10: [The moral law is the
law which commands what each person ought to be in inward and outward conduct,
so that his whole life may conform to God's will. Its summary is comprehended
in the Decalogue. The Decalogue is the brief summary of the moral law,
containing ten commandments, and so forth.] He later proves, among other
things, that the Decalogue pertains to us Christians by this argument: the
Decalogue [is nothing other than the summary of the natural law repeated by
God after the fall of man, by which all people are bound.]

Why is more needed? Dr. Burmann himself, in *Synopsis*, book 4, chapter 2,
sections 3–4, admits this: [From this also depends, he says, the division of
the Mosaic laws. For some belong to natural law, such as (note well) the moral
law or the Decalogue. The law of the old covenant or Testament was threefold:
1. The Ten Words, which contain the commandments which, if a person does them,
he lives in them (Ezekiel 20:30, 11), or the precepts of the covenant of works.
Hence the Decalogue is commonly called the moral law.] And in chapter 5, on the
Decalogue, section 1: [This law, he says, delivered by God consists of ten
commandments, from which they are called the Ten Words (Exodus 34:28;
Deuteronomy 4:13), corresponding to the Greek name δεκάλογος, *Decalogue*.
They are a compendium of the moral law and of all holiness and virtue.] Thus
in chapter 6, section 6, he says—though falsely, as we shall see later—that
[the Decalogue comprehends nothing except commands of faith and repentance,
such as we read were given to Abraham: *I am God sufficient; walk before me
and be blameless* (Genesis 17:1).] Yet he himself, as we saw above in book 1,
acknowledges that faith and repentance are moral.

And Dr. Cocceius, *On the Covenant*, and so forth, section 13, grants us the
same thing, saying: [Meanwhile, nothing is contained in the tablets of the
covenant and the book of the law except what the law of nature commanded
uncorrupted man,

at least with respect to the substance of worship and as, so to speak, the
source of a more specific precept. That all things are contained radically in
the law of nature is clear from the fact that Moses and the Apostle attach the
life promised to uncorrupted man to works according to the written law, and
denounce death and the curse—into which we fell in Adam and from which we are
delivered through Christ—upon people as those who do not perform the written
law. For it is necessary that the one law of works be the cause either of life
or of death. Therefore, the law of nature and the Decalogue, or the written
law, are the same.]
