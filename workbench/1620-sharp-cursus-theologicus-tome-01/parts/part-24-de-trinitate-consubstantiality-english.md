# Second Controversy: On the Consubstantiality of the Persons

[1620 Tome I | col. 220 | BSB frame 00126]

SECOND CONTROVERSY

On the *homoousia* of the Persons

So that this question may be understood more easily, and so that the known
terms may be dis—

[1620 Tome I | col. 221 | BSB frame 00127]

—cussed, certain things must first be laid down as foundations.

The word “person” is taken in three ways.

1. For a quality and outward appearance, Matthew 22:16.

2. For a substance, or for a thing subsisting by itself in an individual but
finite manner. Thus angels and human beings are persons.

3. For a substance subsisting by itself in an individual but infinite and
immense manner. Such are the persons in the divine essence: they are indeed
distinct, but cannot be separated, because they are of the same nature and
essence; indeed, they themselves are the essence.

The word *ὁμοουσίαν* is not found syllable for syllable in Scripture, nor is
*οὐσία* found in the sense in which we use it in this disputation. The reality,
however, the sense, and synonyms of this word are found there, Romans 1:20. For
God’s divinity is nothing other than his *οὐσία*, Philippians 2:7; 2 Peter 1:4.
It is also found in its concrete form when he is called *ὁ ὤν*, “the One who
is”; and it is certain that both abstract and concrete terms are rightly said
of God. Similar names derived from *οὐσία* are found in Scripture, as
*ἐπιούσιον* in Luke 11:3 and Matthew 6:11, and *περιούσιον* in Titus 2:14. Thus
Ambrose, book 5, chapter 7 of *On the Faith*, says: “We rightly call the Son
*ὁμοούσιον* with the Father, because that word signifies both the distinction
of the persons and the unity of the nature.” Finally, the heretics who shrink
from this name use the term *ὁμοιουσίας*. Why, then, may the orthodox not use
the word *ὁμοουσίας*?

*Οὐσία* is taken in two ways.

1. For a nature communicable to many things differing in number, as human
nature belongs to human beings. The divine *οὐσία* is not such a nature,
because it is most simple.

2. For a spiritual, simple, indivisible, and truly one substance, in which,
as in a whole, its essential parts wholly subsist, as in a soul. The divine
essence is in some manner like this. Hence,

[1620 Tome I | col. 222 | BSB frame 00127]

*Ὁμοούσιον* is taken in two ways.

1. For that which has the same nature as another but is numerically another.
In this way one human being is *ὁμοούσιος* with another.

2. For that which not only has the same nature, but subsists with another in
the same *οὐσία* and is not outside the other’s *οὐσία*, because they
inseparably subsist in the same *οὐσία*. Thus the persons in the divine nature
are *ὁμοούσιοι*.

It follows from these things that the distinction of persons in general is
twofold.

1. One is not only hypostatic but also essential. This occurs when the
substances of the persons are divided. In this manner human persons are
distinct from one another, but the divine persons are not.

2. The other is hypostatic only, when the persons are distinct *τῷ τί* and in
reality, so that one is not predicated of another and one is not in another,
although the *οὐσίαι* are not divided from one another. In this manner the
faculties of the soul are in the soul. Yet the distinction of persons in the
divine *οὐσία* is far more excellent, because they are not distinct from one
another in *οὐσία*, and nevertheless one is not predicated of another, nor is
one in the other as an accident in a subject, but *ἐνυποστάτως*.

“One” is taken in six ways: 1. in continuity; 2. in subject; 3. in genus; 4. in
species; 5. in definition; 6. in a simple and indivisible essence. The
*ὁμοουσία* of the persons must be understood in this last manner. In this way
God is also called one, Deuteronomy 6:4; Isaiah 43:10—namely, in the most
perfect kind of unity. There cannot be many with respect to essence, because
to be one and many with respect to the same thing and in the same respect
would imply a contradiction. Therefore the many in God are many only with
respect to the persons. The three persons are in one *οὐσία*, not as accidents
in a subject, a form in matter, integral parts in a whole, or a body in a
place, but as the essential parts of the soul are in the soul *ἐν τῷ
ὁμοουσίῳ*. For the faculties of the soul do not differ from the soul itself
*τῇ οὐσίᾳ*, but subsist in the one

[1620 Tome I | col. 223 | BSB frame 00128]

*οὐσία* of the soul so as to be its very *οὐσία*.

The name “God” is taken in three ways in Scripture.

1. Indefinitely, and thus *οὐσιωδῶς* and simply for the divine essence. This
occurs when it is not restricted to a particular person, either expressly by
the names of the persons or consequently by some evident circumstance of the
text, as in Matthew 4:10, John 5:7, and throughout Scripture.

2. Definitely, when a particular person is indicated either expressly or by
the circumstances. It is then taken personally, as in John 1:1, 3. *Κατ᾽
ἐξοχήν* it is said of the Father, because he is the first person in order,
having his origin from no one: John 20:17; Ephesians 1:3; 2 Corinthians 1:3;
John 17:3.

The first signification answers the Sabellians; the second, the Tritheists;
the fifth, the sophisms of Arius.[^p24-1]

The name “Father” signifies three things.

1. It is taken for the Father of Christ and thus signifies the first person of
the Trinity.

2. It is taken for the Father of the elect. In this sense the Son and Holy
Spirit, no less than the Father of Christ, are called our Father, because the
work of election is common to the three persons, although with primary regard
to the order of acting the Father of Christ is called Father.

3. It is taken for the Father of all creatures and signifies the same as
“author,” as in Job 38:28; Isaiah 64:8; Hebrews 12:9. When “Father” is taken for
the Father of Christ, it is taken in two ways.

1. As he is the eternal Father *τοῦ λόγου*, of the Word; and thus the Son is
*ὁμοούσιος*, John 3:13.

2. As he is the Father of Christ insofar as Christ is the man conceived by the
Holy Spirit, Luke 1:35.

The name “Son” signifies two things: 1. a natural son; 2. an adopted son. A
natural son, however, is of two kinds.

1. One who is born from a father in such a way as to receive a part, not the
whole, of his substance. Thus we

[1620 Tome I | col. 224 | BSB frame 00128]

are the natural children of our parents.

2. One who is born in such a way as to receive the whole essence. Thus Christ
alone is the natural Son of God.

Furthermore, the name “natural Son” is said of Christ in three ways.

1. Insofar as he is begotten from the substance of the Father; thus he is
*ὁμοούσιος*.

2. Insofar as he as man was begotten from the substance of the virgin, indeed,
but by the power of the Most High, that is, of the Father.

3. On account of the grace of union, by which the human nature was united
*ὑποστατικῶς* with the divine *λόγῳ*. In these latter ways Christ is not
*ὁμοούσιος* with the Father, but less than the Father, and thus is said to have
ascended to the Father, to have prayed, and so forth.

The names *Λόγου* and *σοφίας* are taken in two ways.

1. For a created thing and gift of God in human beings, James 1:5.

2. For a substantial thing and person, Proverbs 8:22; John 1:1.

“Holy Spirit” is taken in two ways.

1. For the power of God—that is, his gift, action, and *ἐνεργείᾳ*. In this
manner it is not in God, as the Servetians dream, for then it would be an
accident in God. Rather, it is in the human beings in whom God works.

2. For substantial power endowed with judgment and will, that is, for the
third person of the Trinity, John 3:7; 1 Corinthians 2:12.

“Another” is said in two ways. 1. *Οὐσιωδῶς*: namely, one who is other than
another’s *οὐσία*. In this way one human being is other than another, but the
Son is not other than the Father.

2. *Ὑποστατικῶς*: namely, one person who is other than another person, but not
in *οὐσίᾳ*. In this sense the Father is other than the Son, the Son other than
the Father, and the Holy Spirit other than both.

“Alone” and “one” are each taken in two ways.

1. Exclusively, insofar as all others are excluded from the one under
discussion, as in Luke 18:19: “No one is good except God alone.” There the name
“God” is taken essentially, and therefore

[1620 Tome I | col. 225 | BSB frame 00129]

the particle “alone” excludes only all creatures. So also in 1 Timothy 2:5 and
Isaiah 43:10; and the Father alone begets.

2. Inclusively, when all who cannot be separated from the one under discussion
are included in the matter being considered. Thus Christ alone is called the
Savior of the world, 1 Corinthians 8:6; John 17:3.

“To beget” can be distinguished in three ways.

1. Under the first distinction, it is taken in three ways. 1. *Φυσικῶς* and
*σωματικῶς*. This is the generation of creatures, not of God. In this manner
the begetter is prior in nature and time to the begotten, and thus Christ as
man was begotten from Mary.

2. *Ὑπερφυσικῶς* and *πνευματικῶς*, as when God the Father is said to have
begotten the Son. This is nothing other than his having communicated his whole
*οὐσία* to him without movement, passion, or alteration.

3. *Μεταφορικῶς*, as in Acts 13:33 and Colossians 1:18.

2. Under the second distinction, it is taken in two ways.

1. It signifies a generation outside the one who produces, such as the
generation of creatures. In this manner God produced all created things.

2. It signifies a generation that takes place within the one who begets, as
the soul produces a *λόγον* within itself. Thus the Father produces the Son,
and both produce the Holy Spirit.

3. Under the third distinction, the verb “to beget,” as it is transferred to
God, is taken in two ways.

1. Improperly, signifying simply to communicate. In this sense Augustine,
Ignatius, and others call the essence itself begotten, insofar as it is
communicated by the Father to the Son.

2. Properly, signifying to beget a Son for himself by communicating the
essence in an incomprehensible manner. Thus Christ as God is not properly
begotten, but he is begotten as Son, because as Son alone he was begotten by
the Father; with respect to the essence, however, he was not. Since precisely
the same essence is in the three persons, the Son is rightly called
*ἀγέννητος* with respect to the essence.

*Ἄναρχος*, “without a beginning,” and *ἀναίτιος*, “without a

[1620 Tome I | col. 226 | BSB frame 00129]

preceding cause,” are taken in two ways. 1. *Οὐσιωδῶς*. Thus with respect to
the essence Christ is rightly called *ἄναρχος* and *ἀναίτιος*.

2. *Ὑποστατικῶς*. Thus the Father alone is such.

*Αὐτὸς Θεός* is taken in two ways. 1. *Οὐσιωδῶς*. Thus Christ with respect to
the essence is such.

2. *Ὑποστατικῶς*. Thus the Son is begotten by the Father, receiving the whole
essence, which in itself is *αὐτὸς Θεός*. In this sense the Fathers said that
Christ is true God from true God.

“To create” is taken in two ways. 1. To make something from nothing. In this
way the Son is not created.

2. To establish and beget, as we speak of creating a magistrate and begetting
children. In Proverbs 8:25 the Son of God is called created in this sense.

“Principle” can be distinguished in three ways. Under the first distinction,
when it is said of God it is understood in two ways.

1. For a principle of the divine essence in some person. In this manner there
is no principle in God, because the same eternal and most simple essence is in
the three persons. Therefore those who wish there to be an “essentiator” and
an “essentiated” in God err. If this were so, the Son would have a principle
of his essence, which is blasphemy. For either the Son’s essence is different
from the Father’s or it is the same. If different, it was either made by the
Father, as Arius wished—and thus God would have created God—or begotten from
the Father’s essence. The latter cannot be said, because then the Father’s
essence would be divisible. Therefore it is the same; and if it is the same,
it could not have had a principle in the Son without also having had one in
the Father. Consequently Christ as God is said to be without a principle.

2. For the principle of a person, from which that person has his origin. Thus
the Father is the principle of the Son, and both are the principle of the Holy
Spirit. This principle is not one of time but of origin, not of *οὐσίας* but of
*ὑποστάσεως*.

[1620 Tome I | col. 227 | BSB frame 00130]

2. Under the second distinction, “principle” is also taken in two ways. 1. For
the principle of those things which are in the divine *οὐσίᾳ*. Thus the Father
alone is the principle of the Son, and both are the principle of the Holy
Spirit.

2. For the principle of those things which are outside the divine *οὐσίαν*.
Thus the three persons are the principle of all things.

“To receive” is taken in two ways. 1. To have from without, from an extrinsic
principle, those things which we need. Thus all creatures receive from God
what they have. In this sense the Son as God receives nothing from the Father.

2. To have from within, from an intrinsic principle, as in generation the
begotten receives from the begetter everything essential. Thus the Son as Son
has everything he possesses from the Father as an intrinsic principle in his
eternal generation.

General rules by which the opponents’ sophisms are answered

1. In all arguments drawn from properties to the essence—as from denying the
Father’s property in the Son to denying the Son’s deity—not only the sophism
of equivocation but also the sophism of non-cause for cause is committed, as
in this argument:

It is proper to God that he be *ἀγέννητος*. But the Father alone is
*ἀγέννητος*. Therefore the Father alone is God. Here non-cause is put for
cause, because the cause why the Father is God is not that he is *ἀγέννητος*,
but the divine essence. Therefore attributes of the essence alone necessarily
establish that someone is or is not God; attributes of the persons do not.

2. In all arguments drawn from the generation of animals to deny Christ’s
generation, every imperfection must be purged away: that such generation
occurs in time, outside the begetter, with motion or change, and without the
communication of the whole

[1620 Tome I | col. 228 | BSB frame 00130]

essence, and so forth. These apply only to creatures, not to God.

3. Arguments drawn solely from the principles of nature and philosophy must
be rejected as the work of a syllogizer, because on the testimony of the
philosophers themselves one must not pass from genus to genus. Therefore the
apostle rightly calls the wisdom of this world foolishness with God, 1
Corinthians 3:18.

4. One must not argue from things said improperly of God as though they were
said properly.

The *ὁμοουσία* of the Son and Holy Spirit is demonstrated

Since the deity of the Father has never been doubted, it will not be necessary
to prove that the Father is God. That the Son and the Holy Spirit are true God,
*ὁμοούσιον* with the Father, is therefore proved by these arguments.

1. He who is equal to God the Father is God no less than the Father. But the
Son and Holy Spirit are equal to God the Father, John 5:18; 16:15; Philippians
2:6; Colossians 2:9. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

2. Those to whom God’s essential name is truly attributed are truly and
essentially God. But God’s essential name, Jehovah, is truly attributed to the
Son and Holy Spirit. To the Son: Isaiah 20:6; Jeremiah 23:6 and 33:16;
Zechariah 2:10–11; Genesis 31:11, 13; Hosea 12:5; Exodus 3:2, 4; Judges 6:12,
where the one called the Angel of Jehovah is himself called Jehovah in verses
14 and 16. Likewise, in 1 Corinthians 10:9 Christ is said to be the one whom
the people tempted in the wilderness; but the same one is called Jehovah in
Numbers 14:26–27 and 21:2, 5; Psalms 87:18 and 95:9; and so forth. To the Holy
Spirit: Acts 1:16; Hebrews 3:7; 1 Peter 1:11; 2 Peter 1:21. Therefore, the
conclusion follows.[^p24-2]

3. He who is explicitly and properly called God in Scripture is truly and
essentially God, *ὁμοούσιος* with the Father. But the Son and Holy Spirit are
explicitly and

[1620 Tome I | col. 229 | BSB frame 00131]

properly called God. The Son is so called in Psalm 45:7, which the apostle
interprets of Christ in Hebrews 1:8; likewise Isaiah 9:6 and 25:9; John 1:2 and
20:28; Acts 20:28; Romans 9:5; 1 Timothy 3:16; 2 Peter 1:1; 1 John 5:20–21. He
is called the great God in Titus 2:13. The Holy Spirit is so called in 2 Samuel
23:2; Acts 5:3–4; 1 Corinthians 3:16; 6:19; 12:6, 11, 28; Acts 4:24–25; and
Acts 1:16. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

4. Those to whom God’s essential properties are attributed are truly and
essentially God and *ὁμοούσιοι* with the Father. But God’s essential
properties are attributed to the Son and Holy Spirit. To the Son: eternity,
Proverbs 8:22; Isaiah 9:6; John 1:1; 8:58; 17:5; 1 John 1:2; Revelation 1:17;
Micah 5:2; ubiquity, and thus also immensity, Matthew 18:20; 28:30; John 3:13;
14:23; the last verse of Mark; Ephesians 4:10; omniscience, Matthew 9:4; 12:35;
John 2:25; 10:15; 16:30; 21:17; Revelation 2:13; omnipotence, John 3:35; 5:19;
Philippians 3:21; Revelation 1:8; 4:8. To the Holy Spirit: eternity, Hebrews
9:14; Isaiah 40:13; ubiquity, Psalm 139:7; Romans 8:9; 1 Corinthians 3:16;
6:19; 2 Timothy 1:14; omniscience, John 14:26; 16:13; Acts 1, verses 16, 20,
23; 1 Corinthians 1:10; 1 Timothy 4:1; 1 Peter 1:11; omnipotence, Psalm 91,
verses 3, 4, 8; Matthew 12:28; 1 Corinthians 12:6; Hebrews 3:17. Therefore, the
conclusion follows.[^p24-3]

5. Those to whom works proper to the one God alone are attributed are truly,
properly, and essentially God and *ὁμοούσιοι* with the Father. But works
proper to God alone are attributed to the Son and Holy Spirit: creation,
preservation, searching hearts, justification, salvation, and judgment of the
living and the dead. To the Son: Proverbs 8:27; Psalm 102:26; Hebrews 1:2; John
1:3; 5:17; Ephesians 3:9; Colossians 1:16; Jeremiah 17:10; 1 Corinthians 6:11;
Acts 10:42; Matthew 7:22; 13:41; 2 Timothy 4:1; Matthew 25:31; Revelation
19:11; 22:12; Romans 2:6; 3:6. To the Holy Spirit: Genesis 1:2; Job 26:13;
33:4; Psalms 33:6; 104:30; Isaiah 40:12–13; Luke 1:35. Therefore, the conclusion
follows.

6. Those to whom honor and worship proper to God alone are attributed are
truly and pro—

[1620 Tome I | col. 230 | BSB frame 00131]

—perly God and *ὁμοούσιοι* with the Father. But honor and worship proper to
God alone—such as invocation and being believed in—are attributed to the Son
and Holy Spirit: Deuteronomy 6:13; Matthew 4:10; Psalm 86:8–10; Isaiah 42:8;
Jeremiah 17:5; Hosea 13:4; Matthew 8:2; Luke 24:52; John 5:23; 14:1; 16:27;
Revelation 1:6; 5:13; Hebrews 1:6; Philippians 2:10. This is also evident from
the Apostles’ Creed, in which we are said to believe in the Holy Spirit and the
Son no less than in the Father. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

7. He into whose name we are baptized is truly and essentially God. But we are
baptized into the name of the Son and Holy Spirit no less than into the
Father’s, Matthew 28:19. Therefore the Son and Holy Spirit are God in essence
no less than the Father, and consequently are *ὁμοούσιοι*.

OBJECTIONS

1. He who has an origin is not the true God existing from himself. But the Son
and Holy Spirit have their origin from the Father.

Response. This is a fallacy of equivocation in the word “origin.” The
proposition is true only when it speaks of the origin *οὐσίας*, that is, of the
substance; but the assumption concerns the origin of the person.

2. The true God is from himself, not from another. But the Son and Holy Spirit
are not from themselves, but from the Father. Therefore, the conclusion
follows.

Response. This is a fallacy of equivocation in the word “God.” The proposition
speaks of the essence: the true God is from himself with respect to essence.
The assumption, however, concerns the persons. Furthermore, it is an inference
from particulars, for the proposition taken universally is false.

Counterinstance. The persons of the Son and Holy Spirit are not from
themselves. But the persons are the essence. Therefore the essence of the Son
and Holy Spirit is not from itself either.

Response. This is a fallacy from what is said with a qualification. Moreover,
the assumption taken simply is false, because the persons are not simply the
essence, as if there were no difference between person and essence. The
essence is considered absolutely, but the person is consid—

[1620 Tome I | col. 231 | BSB frame 00132]

—ered relatively. Likewise, the essence is communicable; the person is
incommunicable.

3. He who has all things from another is inferior and less than the one from
whom he has them. But the Son and Holy Spirit have all things from the Father.
Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. The proposition must be distinguished. It is true of one who has
all things from another in such a way that he does not have everything the
other has, and who has them by grace, not by nature: he is inferior to the one
from whom he has them. But it is false of one who has them from another in
such a way that he has by nature all the things the other has. Thus a prince by
right of succession has everything his predecessor had, yet is not inferior.
So Christ has everything the Father has, John 16:15, and has it by nature
through generation; and the Holy Spirit has all things by nature through
procession from both, John 16:7 and the following verses.

4. He who acts not from himself but from another is inferior. But the Son acts
from the Father, John 5:19, and the Holy Spirit from the Father and Son, John
16:13. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. The proposition must be distinguished. It is true of one who does
not do all the same things, but false of one who does all the same things.
Furthermore, although they do not do all things in the same manner
*ὑποστατικῶς*, they do them *οὐσιωδῶς* and naturally, John 5:19.

5. He who is sent is inferior to the sender. But the Son is sent by the Father,
and the Holy Spirit by both, John 5:35; 14:26; 15:26. Therefore, the conclusion
follows.

Response. The proposition is denied, because an equal can be sent by an equal
and, by common consent, execute a lower office. Thus Christ, Philippians 2:6.
Furthermore, an order of acting among the persons is indicated, not a degree
of dignity. Cyril therefore rightly says, “Mission and obedience do not take
away equality of power.”

6. He who is sent changes place and therefore is finite. But the Son and Holy
Spirit are sent by the Father. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. This is a fallacy of equivocation in “mis—”

[1620 Tome I | col. 232 | BSB frame 00132]

—sion,” because it does not signify local motion, but God’s will acting and
effecting in the Son through the Holy Spirit, or the disclosure and execution
of the divine will in the Son through the Spirit.

7. He who is sent by Jehovah is not Jehovah. But the Son and Holy Spirit are
sent by Jehovah. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. This is a fallacy of equivocation in the word “Jehovah,” because in
the proposition it is taken *οὐσιωδῶς*, and in the assumption
*ὑποστατικῶς*. It is true that one sent by another is a person other than the
sender, but he is not another in essence, although among created things the
matter stands otherwise.

8. If the Father is called the one God alone, then the Son and Holy Spirit are
not God. But the former is true, John 17:3; 1 Corinthians 8:6; Ephesians 4:6.

Response. This is a fallacy of equivocation in the word “God,” because it is
not taken *οὐσιωδῶς* but *κατ᾽ ἐξοχήν*. There is also equivocation in the words
“one” and “alone,” because they exclude only creatures.

9. He alone is the true God whom Christ commanded us to invoke. But he
commanded us to invoke the Father alone, Luke 11:2. Therefore, the conclusion
follows.

Response. There is equivocation in the word “Father,” because it is taken
*οὐσιωδῶς*, not *ὑποστατικῶς*.

10. He who is not propitiated through the Mediator is not the true God. But the
Son and Holy Spirit are not propitiated through the Mediator. The assumption
is proved because the Son intercedes with the Father alone, not with himself.

Response. The assumption is denied, and its proof contains the fallacy of
non-cause for cause. Christ is not unpropitiated because he intercedes; rather,
in the work of mediation Christ bears the person both of the one who
propitiates and of the one propitiated. He is said to be Mediator only with the
Father because the Father is the fountain of deity, of all counsels, and of
grace. And just as the Father forgives sins on account of the Son, so the Son
does so on account of his own satisfaction.

11. If Christ is *ὁμοούσιος* with the Father, then

[1620 Tome I | col. 233 | BSB frame 00133]

he received either the entire essence or a part of it. If the whole, the Father
would lack the essence; if a part, the essence would be divisible. But both are
absurd. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. The first part of the assumption is denied. Although the Father
communicated the entire essence, he does not lack it; the same essence is
common to both, so that it remains in both.

12. If the Son is born from the Father, either he was begotten from eternity or
he is still being begotten. If the former, the generation is ended; but
whatever is in God is infinite God. If he is still being begotten, the
generation is not yet perfect. But both are absurd. Therefore, the conclusion
follows.

Response. God’s internal actions are not subject to time, but have been from
eternity and will be to eternity. Therefore Psalm 2 speaks of generation in
the present tense, because there is no prior and posterior in God. Thus God and
God’s actions are rightly called the most simple act.

13. He who is distinguished from the true God is not true God. But the Son is
distinguished from the true God, John 20:17. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. This is a fallacy from what is said with a qualification, because
Christ does not call the Father his God simply, but with respect to the human
nature and the office of Mediator.

14. Christ denies that he is good as God. Therefore he is not God.

Response. This is a fallacy from what is said with a qualification. He does not
simply deny that he is good, but speaks according to the opinion that the
young man had formed of him, for the young man thought him a mere man.
Ambrose, book 1, chapter 2 of *On the Faith*, therefore rightly says, “Christ
does not say, ‘No one is good except the Father,’ but ‘except God’; and ‘God’
is a name common to the nature.”

15. The Son of the Most High is not God, because no one besides the Most High
is God. But Christ is the Son of the Most High, Luke 1:32. Therefore, the
conclusion follows.

Response. There is equivocation in the expression “the Most High.” In the
cited pas—

[1620 Tome I | col. 234 | BSB frame 00133]

—sage it is taken personally in the assumption for the person of the Father,
but in the proposition it is taken *οὐσιωδῶς*. If it were taken personally,
the proposition would be false.

16. He who is subject to God the Father as his head is not God. But the Son is
subject to God the Father as his head, 1 Corinthians 11:30. Therefore, the
conclusion follows.

Response. There is equivocation in the word “God.” Furthermore, Christ is
subjected to the Father as Mediator and as man.[^p24-4]

17. He who alone dwells in inaccessible light alone is God. But the Father
alone dwells in inaccessible light, 1 Timothy 6:16. Therefore, the conclusion
follows.

Response. The particle “alone” excludes only creatures, and the name “Father”
is taken *οὐσιωδῶς*, not *ὑποστατικῶς*.

18. He who alone is known as the true God alone is the true God. But such is
the Father. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. This is a fallacy from the combined to the divided, because “alone”
does not refer to the substance but to the predicate. It is also a fallacy
from what is said with a qualification, because it does not exclude the Son or
Holy Spirit, but only creatures.

19. He who was promised and given, and who grew in wisdom, and so forth, is not
eternal and therefore is not God. But such is the Son, Luke 2:52. Therefore,
the conclusion follows.

Response. This is a fallacy from what is said with a qualification, because
these and similar things are said of Christ not as he is God, but with respect
to the office of Mediator or as he is man.

20. He who was created and made is not God. But Christ was created and made,
Proverbs 8:22; John 1:15; Acts 2:36; Hebrews 3:2. Therefore, the conclusion
follows.

Response. In Proverbs 8 the Hebrew verb signifies “possessed,” not “created,”
and in the Greek version *ἔκτισε* is corruptly put for *ἔκτησε*. Furthermore,
there is equivocation in the word “make,” because it does not signify to
produce from nothing, but to ordain to an office, as in 1 Corinthians 1:20 and
2 Corinthians 5:21.[^p24-5]

21. Christ is the firstborn of every creature. Therefore he is not God.

Response. The word “firstborn” has regard either to time or to dignity. Time
has no place in the generation of the Son, because it is eternal. The sense is
that he is the firstborn of every creature, that is, begotten from eternity
before every creature. As Mediator, however, he surpasses all creatures in
dignity.

[1620 Tome I | col. 235 | BSB frame 00134]

22. He who has life from another is not God. But such is Christ, John 6:57.
Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. This is a fallacy from what is said with a qualification, because it
is said of Christ only as he is man. Furthermore, if the proposition is
understood of Christ insofar as he is the Son of God, it is false. He receives
life from the Father in such a way that he has never failed to possess it in
himself, but has had it eternally from the Father as from a fountain.

23. He who acts with another’s will going before him is not God,
*ὁμοούσιος* with the Father. But such is the Son, Matthew 26:39; John 5:19.
Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. Christ acts with the Father’s will going before his, not in time but
in the order of acting among the persons. Furthermore, the passage in Matthew
speaks of his will as he is man and consequently establishes nothing.

Counterinstance. Then there will be two wills in Christ.

Response. Yes. There is in Christ the divine will, which is the same as the
Father’s will in essence and conformity. There is also the human will, which
is the same as the divine will not in essence but only in conformity.

24. He who does not do all things from himself is not omnipotent and therefore
is not God. But such is the Son, John 5 and 8:28.

Response. The Son acts from the Father not by reason of the essence but of the
person, and the assumption must be distinguished. Furthermore, the cited
passages speak of Christ as Mediator, not simply as God, and thus this is a
fallacy from what is said with a qualification.

25. He who receives from another is not omnipotent. But the Son receives and
the Father gives, Philippians 2:9; John 5:26. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. The Father gives and the Son receives in two ways: either eternally
through generation, in which case the proposition is false; or tem—

[1620 Tome I | col. 236 | BSB frame 00134]

—porally, as Mediator and as man, in which case the argument does not establish
what is in question.

36. He who delivers the kingdom to the Father is inferior to the Father and
therefore is not God. But the Son will deliver the kingdom to the Father, 1
Corinthians 15:24. Therefore, the conclusion follows.[^p24-6]

Response. This is a fallacy from what is said with a qualification. He will not
deliver the kingdom in such a way that he himself ceases to reign, because
there will be no end to his kingdom, Daniel 7:14; Luke 1:33; Micah 4:7. Rather,
this concerns the mode of reigning which he now employs in the church
militant. Only in this sense will he deliver the kingdom to the Father after
the day of judgment.

Counterinstance. But it is said that he will be subjected to the Father.
Therefore he is inferior to him and consequently is not God.

Response. He will be subjected insofar as he will deliver the kingdom—namely,
only as Mediator, not as God. Thus this is a fallacy from what is said with a
qualification.

27. He is less than the Father who prays to and honors the Father, who is
anointed and sanctified by the Father, and who is oppressed by hunger, thirst,
and so forth. But such is the Son, John 14:28; 11:41; 27:5; Mark 6:46; Luke
4:18; John 8:49. Therefore, the conclusion follows.[^p24-7]

Response. This is a fallacy from what is said with a qualification, because
these things are said of Christ not as he is God, but partly with respect to
his ordination to and execution of the office, and partly with respect to the
human nature.

28. Works *ad extra* are common to the three persons. But the incarnation is a
work *ad extra*. Therefore, if the persons have one essence, when the Son was
incarnate the Father was incarnate. But the consequence is absurd. Therefore,
the conclusion follows.

Response. The proposition is true of works which are simply *ad extra* and
which depend upon the divine essence, such as creation. But the incarnation
is not simply a work *ad extra*, for in the incarnation there are two things.

1. The creation of the human nature, which is a work *ad extra* and common to
the three persons.

2. The assumption of the human nature, which is not simply outside all the
per—

[1620 Tome I | col. 237 | BSB frame 00135]

—sons, but is a personal action of Christ.

29. One who produces is more perfect than one who does not. But the Father and
Son produce the Holy Spirit, whereas the Holy Spirit does not produce.
Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. The proposition is denied, for angels and souls do not produce, yet
they are more perfect than other things which do.

30. The Holy Spirit was not from eternity, John 7:39. Therefore, the conclusion
follows.

Response. This is a fallacy of equivocation in the word “Spirit,” because the
passage does not speak of the person of the Holy Spirit, but of his power and
mission.

31. The Holy Spirit is not omnipresent; therefore he is not God. The antecedent
is proved: he changes place, Luke 1:21. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. This is a fallacy of equivocation in the word “come upon,” because
it does not signify a change of place, but a particular manifestation in a
particular place.[^p24-8]

32. The Holy Spirit is not omniscient. The antecedent is proved: no one knows
the Father except the Son alone, Matthew 11:27. Therefore, the conclusion
follows.

Response. This is a fallacy of figure of speech, because the particle
*οὐδεὶς* does not exclude the Holy Spirit, who knows the Father perfectly, 1
Corinthians 2:10, but creatures.

33. The Holy Spirit is said to be created by God, Amos 4:13. Therefore, the
conclusion follows.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because “spirit” in that passage does not
signify the third person of the Trinity, but wind. It is therefore a fallacy
of equivocation in the word “Spirit.”

34. All things were made through Christ. Therefore the Holy Spirit is not God,
John 1:3.

Response. This is a fallacy from the combined, for the text adds that all
things which were made were made by Christ; but only creatures were made.

35. He who prays to God is not God. But the Holy Spirit prays to God for us,
Romans 8:26. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. This is a fallacy of figure of speech, because the passage does not
teach what the Holy Spirit immediately and of himself does, but what he
arouses and effects in us.
