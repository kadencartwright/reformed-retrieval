QUESTION XI.

THE CONCEPTION AND NATIVITY OF CHRIST.

*How was Christ conceived from the Holy Spirit and born of the blessed
Virgin?*

[Marginal heading: The first degree of Christ’s humiliation from the
conception.]

I. The first degree of Christ’s humiliation and emptying is to be sought from
his *conception and nativity,* which are the two parts of his incarnation.
Although there were no obscure indications of his glory in either, it is
certain that here the great

[Printed p. 299 | Internet Archive PDF 327 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

mystery of Christ’s emptying is now disclosed, when the eternal and most
glorious Son of God willed to lower himself so far that he did not disdain to
lie hidden within the confines of his mother’s womb and to assume weak and
mortal flesh.

II. The *conception,* or σύλληψις, *conception,* of which Luke 1:31 speaks and
which is also called γέννησις, *generation,* in Matthew 1:18, is that by which
Christ was invisibly formed in the womb of the blessed Virgin, by no operation
of man but by the power and overshadowing of the Holy Spirit alone: τὸ ἐν
αὐτῇ γεννηθὲν, ἐκ Πνεύματός ἐστιν ἁγίου, “what was begotten in her is from
the Holy Spirit” (Matt. 1:20). In it a twofold *principle* must be considered:
the Holy Spirit as the *active* principle and the blessed Virgin as the
*passive* principle.

[Marginal heading: Its active principle is the Holy Spirit;]

III. The *active principle* was not any man, as in ordinary generation,
because the conception had to be such that the blessed Virgin’s virginity
would remain whole and inviolate, and such that through it Christ would be
free from every stain of sin, which is customarily propagated and contracted
through ordinary generation. Rather, it was the Holy Spirit alone (Matt.
1:18, 20; Luke 1:35), not to the exclusion of the other Persons of the
Trinity, who concurred indivisibly in this work also, as in other external
works of the same kind, but *appropriately,* insofar as the completion and
fecundation of things are customarily ascribed to the Holy Spirit, just as
works of creation and government are ascribed to the Father, and works of
redemption and wisdom to the Son. Thus Christ’s generation and our
regeneration correspond to one another and have the same cause.

IV. Here, however, the *Spirit* acted not *materially* but only *efficiently:*
δημιουργικῶς, *creatively,* not σπερματικῶς, *seminally;* κατ᾽ ἐξουσίαν, *by
power,* not κατὰ συνουσίαν, *by intercourse.* Thus Christ was conceived from
the Spirit’s power, not from his substance; not by generation, but by blessing
and consecration, as the ancients used to speak. The preposition ἐκ, *from,*
therefore marks the efficient cause, as often elsewhere, such as Romans 11:36,
when *all things are said to be from God,* and 1 John 3:9, where the godly are
born ἐκ τοῦ Θεοῦ, *from God.* It does not mark the material cause, as when it
is attributed to the Virgin, from whom he is said to have been born.

[Marginal heading: Yet he cannot be called the Father of Christ.]

V. Hence an easy answer can be given to the question customarily raised here
by the Socinians: Is the Holy Spirit not rightly called the Father of Christ,
since Christ is said to have been conceived from him? Since the title Father
requires generation from the substance of the begetter and the generation of
a nature like his own, and neither occurs here, it is evident that the Holy
Spirit cannot be called the Father of Christ. Add that Christ is called
ἀπάτωρ, *fatherless,* with respect to his humanity (Heb. 7:3); that God the
Father alone is called his Father, and indeed his own Father (John 1:18;
5:17); and that the Father does not proceed, is not sent, and is not given by
the Son in the way the Spirit is said to proceed, to be sent, and to be given
by Christ. It is one thing to form something by one’s power from material
taken from elsewhere, but another to beget it from one’s own substance. The
Holy Spirit did the former, but not the latter, which belongs to the Father.

VI. The same men fare no better when, to destroy Christ’s deity, they maintain
that he is called the Son of God only because of his extraordinary conception
from the Holy Spirit. 1. As was proved in its proper place, the Son of God
already existed before his conception from the Holy Spirit (John 1:1–2; Ps.
2:7; Prov. 8:24), and because of him the faithful of the Old Testament were
already sons and heirs in actuality. 2. He is called the Son of God according
to the divine nature as opposed to the human (Rom. 1:3–4; 9:5; 1 Pet. 3:18).
3. Just as he is called the Son of Man because he was begotten from man, from
Adam’s race, so he must be called the Son of God, not because he was conceived
from the Holy Spirit with respect to his humanity, but because he was
begotten from God with respect to his deity. Otherwise, the perpetual and
immediate antithesis which occurs between these two names could not stand.

VII. Nor can this be established from Luke 1:35: “The Holy Spirit will come
upon you, etc., διὸ καὶ, *therefore also,* the holy thing begotten from you
will be called the Son of God.” For the particles διὸ and καὶ do not mark a
consequent, as though he were

[Printed p. 300 | Internet Archive PDF 328 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the Son of God because he was begotten from the Holy Spirit. For he is said
already to have existed and subsisted before he was conceived (John 1:2;
Phil. 2:6). Rather, they mark a consequence with respect to manifestation,
because from this his eternal sonship was subsequently disclosed. To this
belong: 1. The fact that it does not say ἔσται, *he will be,* but κληθήσεται,
*he will be called,* that is, he will be manifested and acknowledged to be
the only-begotten Son of God (John 1:18), as in John 1:14, *we beheld his
glory as of the Only-Begotten.* 2. The fact that it is not expressed in the
masculine gender, ὁ γεννώμενος, *the man being begotten,* but in the neuter,
τὸ γεννώμενον, *the thing being begotten.* This intimates that the man who
was to be born is not called *the Son of God,* but that something essential
in Christ is thus distinctly named, namely his divine nature or Person, which
is fittingly designated in the neuter as something ὑφιστάμενον, *subsisting.*

VIII. Nor, if believers are called sons of God because of the grace of
regeneration, does it follow that Christ is distinguished by that name because
of his miraculous conception. The account of believers is one thing: they are
called born of God only analogically because of their participation in moral
virtues. The account of God’s Only-Begotten is another: he was begotten from
God’s essence and is said to be equal to him in all things.

IX. Furthermore, this operation of the Holy Spirit in the Virgin is expressed
in Scripture by two words, ἐπέλευσιν, *coming upon,* and ἐπισκίασιν,
*overshadowing,* in Luke 1:35: “The Holy Spirit ἐπελεύσεται ἐπὶ σὲ, καὶ
δύναμις ὑψίστου ἐπισκιάσει σοι, *will come upon you, and the power of the Most
High will overshadow you.*” These designate the wondrous and most powerful
efficacy by which he accomplished that mystery. By ἐπέλευσιν is marked the
presence of the Holy Spirit and his operation in general. It is not the common
and ordinary operation which obtains in the conception of everyone (Job 10:8;
Ps. 139:15), but an extraordinary and heavenly one. In this way the Spirit is
said to come upon those appointed to a great and extraordinary work (Judg.
14:6), and the Spirit came upon the apostles when he was sent from heaven in
the appearance of fiery tongues to sanctify them and prepare them for the work
of the gospel (Acts 1:8). By ἐπισκίασιν is particularly marked the *manner of
this operation,* which was: 1. *Most powerful* for protection and defense, so
that the blessed Virgin would not be consumed by the divine majesty. 2. *Most
efficacious* for fecundation, so that without any help of a man she would
conceive in her womb. Thus a bird is said to cover, warm, and brood over her
eggs so that by her heat the chicks are begotten and hatched. This alludes to
the creation, in which the Spirit is said to have brooded over the waters
(Gen. 1:2), so that this offspring which was to arise might be said to arise
by the same power through which the world had its beginning. 3. *Secret and
incomprehensible,* so that it can neither be traced out by reason nor related
in speech. To this pertains the cloud’s overshadowing in the tabernacle (Ex.
40:35) and the cherubim’s overshadowing of the ark (2 Chron. 5:8). Thus here
God willed to draw over a shadow as a sign of the hidden and incomprehensible
ἐνεργείας, *operation,* lest we inquire too curiously into the manner of this
mystery. So the angel aptly answers the blessed Virgin’s question, since she
regarded the matter as impossible because she had not known a man: it ought
not seem astonishing to her, since it would not occur by human work but by
the power of God, to whom nothing is impossible, and in an altogether
wondrous manner, which ought to be admired, not searched out.

X. There were two principal operations of the Holy Spirit here: 1. *The
preparation of the material.* 2. *The formation of Christ’s body* from the
prepared material. First, he had to prepare by a suitable sanctification the
material separated from the blessed Virgin’s substance. He did this not only
by imparting power to it and raising it to the activity sufficient for
generation without a man’s seed, but also by cleansing it from every stain of
sin, so that it would be ἄκακος, *innocent,* and ἀμίαντος, *undefiled,* and
thus Christ would be born without sin. Hence there is no need to take refuge
in the immaculate conception of Mary. For although there is no creature,
however great its power, which can bring a clean thing from an unclean (Job
14:4), God’s power must not be measured by it, for nothing is impossible to
him, and he calls the things which do not exist.

XI. The second operation lay in *the formation of Christ’s body,* to which
belong its organization, animation, and the union of both body and soul with
the Word. The apostle expresses this by καταρτισμόν, *fitting,* in Hebrews
10:5: σῶμα κατηρτίσω μοι, “a body you fitted for me,” insofar as through the
incarnation a body was so formed for Christ and fitted by the Holy Spirit
that in it he could discharge the ministry of a servant, obey the Father, and
be sacrificed.

[Marginal heading: Was Christ’s body formed in a moment, or successively?]

XII. But if it is asked, as it is by the scholastics, how this was accomplished
by the Holy Spirit—whether in a moment or successively—a distinct answer can
be given, since three things occur here: 1. The preparation of the material
from which Christ’s body was formed. 2. The formation of his body from that
duly prepared material. 3. The perfection of that body as it was gradually
brought through its stages of growth to its proper size. With respect to the
first and third, everyone agrees that they were accomplished successively.
But with respect to the second, which concerns the formation of the body,
theologians dispute: some press for a momentary formation, others for a
successive formation. We join the latter.

XIII. 1. That momentary formation of Christ’s entire body and its conjunction
with the soul is imagined without Scripture, in which no trace of such a
miraculous formation exists. Indeed, intervals of time are expressly
mentioned in this matter, and the acts of conception, generation, and
nativity are distinctly recounted and ordinary time is assigned to them
(Luke 1:26, 38, 56–57; 2:6). 2. In his assumption of human nature Christ is
said to have been made like us, not even excepting infirmities, but sin alone.
Therefore, he had to be formed not in a moment, but gradually according to
the customary order. 3. He grew outside the womb in the manner of other
bodies. Therefore, the manner of his formation in the womb did not have to be
different, just as his gestation was successive and an ordinary nine months.
This would have been pointless if he had been formed and perfected in a
moment. For if Christ’s body was perfected in a moment, he could likewise
have been born at the same moment, and the blessed Virgin would not have had
to endure the discomforts and labors of ordinary gestation.

XIV. It should not be thought that this doctrine asserts anything unworthy of
the Holy Spirit. For although he could have fashioned Christ’s body without
delay and in a single moment, nothing prevented him from working successively
here, as he customarily proceeds from imperfect things to perfect ones in the
works of nature and grace. Nor is it of much importance to inquire too
scrupulously at what time the soul was united to the body and the Λόγος,
*Word,* to the flesh. It is enough for us to believe that from the moment the
human nature began to exist, it never existed separately from the Λόγῳ,
*Word,* but was assumed by him and united to him hypostatically. And if the
soul could not be infused into the body unless it was already organized and
fully formed—a matter on which physicians do not agree among themselves—it
does not follow that the Λόγον, *Word,* could not immediately unite the flesh
to himself, since his work cannot be confined by either the presence or
absence of the soul. Nor is it more absurd for Christ’s body, not yet
animated, to be united to the Λόγῳ, *Word,* than for that same body, once
lifeless, to remain conjoined to him in the tomb, as theologians acknowledge
happened in Christ’s death.

XV. From this miraculous conception of Christ from the Holy Spirit arises
Christ’s holiness of every kind and his exemption from all sin, both imputed
and inherent. Nor is it an obstacle that he is called the son of Adam (Luke
3:38), in whom all are said to die (1 Cor. 15:22). For communion in Adam’s
sin pertains only to his natural posterity, who arise from him in the
ordinary way, not to Christ, who descended from him in an altogether
extraordinary manner. And if the grace of the holy conception preserved him
from participation in the stain, why would it not free him from the guilt
which, according to God’s order, is customarily imputed only to Adam’s natural
heirs? And if Christ could be in the loins of Abraham

[Printed p. 302 | Internet Archive PDF 330 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

with Levi and yet not be tithed with him, what prevents him from having been
in Adam and yet not having sinned with him and in him?

[Marginal heading: Christ’s nativity was ordinary.]

XVI. Christ’s conception is followed by his *nativity* from the blessed
Virgin, which occurred at the fixed time of birth determined by nature.
Although this birth was miraculous because it was virginal, it is nevertheless
rightly called natural because she gave birth in the manner customary for
mothers. Hence she cannot be said to have given birth without pain. Since she
was not free from sin, she also had to bear in this respect the punishment of
sin and the primeval lot of mothers: “In pain you will bear children” (Gen.
3:16), and “When a woman gives birth she has sorrow” (John 16:21). Much less
should it be asserted with the Roman Catholics, in order to maintain from
this the penetration of bodies, that Mary gave birth with her womb closed.
Scripture expressly determines the contrary in Luke 2:22–23, where the
passage of Exodus 13:2 concerning the firstborn who opens the womb is applied
to Christ. The very nature of the thing likewise does not permit this
penetration of dimensions and the presence of two bodies in one place. Nor
is Mary’s virginity thereby called into doubt, because it consists not in
Christ’s being born with her womb closed, but in the Virgin’s having been
untouched by a man.

XVII. The passage in Ezekiel 44:2 concerning *the closed gate of the
sanctuary,* through which access is open to no one except the Prince, is
wrongly drawn into this subject. Apart from the fact that it is allegorical
and therefore, by the adversaries’ own confession, not probative, it is
foreign to the matter treated here. For the building shown to the prophet was
not a type of the blessed Virgin, but of the universal church, to which the
gate of heaven, closed because of sin, is opened and unlocked through Christ,
its supreme Priest and Prince.

XVIII. From this nativity Christ is rightly called the Son of Mary, just as
from eternal generation he is called the Son of God. Yet that twofold
filiation in Christ—the one with respect to his Father in heaven and the
other with respect to his mother on earth—does not constitute two sons, but
one Person to whom this twofold relation belongs, since one and the same
subject is capable of several relations.

XIX. Here two questions are principally asked: 1. Why did Christ have to be
born of a Virgin? 2. As she was a Virgin before and during the birth, did she
also remain such after the birth? To the first it is answered in general that
this was necessary to fulfill the ancient oracles which had expressly
foretold it (Isa. 7:14), and which therefore customarily described the
Messiah as the woman’s Seed and the fruit of the womb. But various particular
reasons are customarily assigned for this dispensation. 1. A new manner of
generation befitted the new man, and an extraordinary Person required an
extraordinary birth. First, the first man was created immediately by God;
afterward Eve was produced from a man without a woman; thereafter all men
are ordinarily born from a man and a woman. A fourth manner remained in
which God’s wondrous wisdom might be seen in the origin of men: that a man
would be born from a woman without a man. This was fulfilled in Christ alone.
Some refer to this Jeremiah 31:22: “Jehovah has created a new thing in the
earth: a woman encompasses a man,” namely in the womb. 2. He willed to be born
from a woman in the manner of other men, that he might prove himself a true
man. He did not will to be born from a man’s seed, that “we might believe him
also to be something other than man,” as Ambrose says in *On the Sacrament of
the Incarnation.* The former pertained to our necessity, the latter to his
dignity. 3. It was not fitting for the same Son to have two fathers. Rather,
just as he acknowledged no mother in heaven as the Son of God, so he would
acknowledge no father on earth as the Son of Man, and thus would truly be
ἀπάτωρ, *fatherless,* and ἀμήτωρ, *motherless.* 4. If he had been conceived
from a man’s seed, he could not be called conceived from the Holy Spirit, and
he would have been a natural son of Adam, and therefore an heir of all Adam’s
evils. So

[Printed p. 303 | Internet Archive PDF 331 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

that it might therefore be established that he had nothing in common with
sin, which is transmitted to us by ordinary generation, he had to be born in
an extraordinary manner.

XX. But if you ask with the Virgin, How could this happen? the angel’s answer
should suffice for us: “With God no word is impossible” (Luke 1:37). “If a
reason is sought,” says Augustine in Letter 3 to Volusianus, “it will not be
marvelous; if an example is demanded, it will not be singular. Let us grant
that God can do something which we confess ourselves unable to investigate;
in such matters the whole reason for the deed is the power of the doer.” Nor
ought this to seem absurd to either Jews or Greeks, since various things no
less absurd and impossible to reason are proposed among them. Cyril of
Jerusalem treats this superbly in *Catechesis* 12.

[Marginal heading: Did Mary always remain a Virgin, both after the birth and
before it?]

XXI. As to the second question concerning Mary’s virginity, the inquiry does
not concern the time of the birth or the time preceding it. For Christians
agree that the Virgin was such before and during the birth. Rather, the
inquiry concerns the time following the birth: Did she always remain a Virgin
after it? This is not expressly determined in Scripture, but is nevertheless
piously believed with a human faith from the consensus of the ancient church,
and it is not necessary to go farther for faith. For it is probable that the
womb in which our Savior received the beginnings of life, and from which he
entered this world as from a temple, was so consecrated and sanctified by so
great a Guest that she always remained untouched by a man and Joseph never
knew her.

XXII. Hence Helvidius and the Antidicomarianites—so called because they were
ἀντίδικοι, *adversaries,* of Mary—are rightly rebuked by the Fathers because
they denied that Mary was ἀεὶ πάρθενον, *ever-virgin.* They maintained that
after the birth she was joined to Joseph and even had children by him, as
Augustine notes in *On Heresies,* relying upon the flimsiest arguments. 1.
“Christ is called Mary’s *firstborn.*” For as Jerome rightly notes, he is so
called because no one was born before him, not because another came after
him. Hence among jurists, “He is first whom no one precedes; he is last whom
no one follows.” The Hebrews also customarily call only-begotten children
firstborn. In Exodus 4:22 Israel is called *God’s firstborn,* although it was
the only people chosen by God. Thus in Exodus 13:2, he “who is born in the
first delivery” is called “the firstborn holy to God,” whether others follow
or not. Otherwise, firstborn children would not have had to be redeemed until
another offspring had been born, which the law shows to be false, for it
requires them to be redeemed in the first month after birth (Num. 18:16).

XXIII. 2. They could not establish this any more soundly from the fact that
certain persons are called *Christ’s brothers* in the New Testament. For in
Scripture it is customary to designate by this name not only proper and full
brothers by nature, but also relatives and cousins, as Abraham and Lot, and
Jacob and Laban. Thus James and Joses, Simon and Judas are called Christ’s
brothers in Matthew 13:55 by kinship. For the Mary who is called their mother
by Matthew and Mark is called the sister of the Lord’s mother by John. The
statement in John 7:5 that “not even his brothers believed in him” must be
understood of more remote relatives.

XXIV. 3. Nor is it any better inferred from the statement that Joseph “did
not know Mary until she had borne her firstborn Son” (Matt. 1:25). For the
particles *until* and *up to* often refer only to the past and not to the
future. That is, they denote the preceding time, about which there could be
doubt or which it was especially important to know, without regard to the
future. There are various such passages (Gen. 28:15; Ps. 123:2; 110:1; Matt.
28:20, etc.). Thus it is shown what Joseph did before Christ’s nativity,
namely that he abstained from her; but it is not implied that he

[Printed p. 304 | Internet Archive PDF 332 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

behaved differently after the birth. Therefore, when she is said to have
been found pregnant πρὶν ἢ συνελθεῖν αὐτούς, *before they came together,* a
preceding union is denied, but a subsequent one is not affirmed.

XXV. Although conjugal union was absent from this marriage, it did not cease
to be true and valid, even if it was not consummated, because consent, not
intercourse, makes a marriage. It was therefore perfect with respect to its
form, namely the indivisible union of life and inviolate fidelity, but not
with respect to its end, namely the procreation of children, although it was
not lacking with respect to the upbringing of a child.

XXVI. Although her perpetual virginity is piously believed, the Roman
Catholics nevertheless wrongly fabricate a vow of virginity for her. For she
could not promise virginity to God and marriage to Joseph at the same time,
or vow ἀτεκνίαν, *childlessness,* as something pleasing to God when she knew
it to be cursed in the law (Deut. 7:14), foreign to the custom of her nation,
and denounced with the designation of disgrace by her relative Elizabeth
(Luke 1:25). Further, she could make the vow neither before her betrothal nor
after it. Not before, for if she had vowed it, why would she marry when she
could preserve her virginity more safely in another way? With what conscience
would she have abused the name of marriage to deceive Joseph and, by allowing
herself to be given to a husband, have spurned the holy covenant of marriage,
not without mockery of God? Not afterward, because a vow without the consent
of her betrothed would have been empty, and that consent cannot be proved by
any argument.

XXVII. Nor do Mary’s words in verse 34 prove such a vow: “How will these
things happen, since I do not know a man?” It is alleged that she indicated
her moral inability from a vow, not a natural one: *I do not know,* that is,
I am not accustomed to know, nor can I, and indeed not because nature or law
prohibits it, but because of a vow, as Maldonatus holds on this passage. But
these words are wrongly twisted to a vow of which neither rumor nor trace
exists here. They belong only to one who marvels at the matter’s novelty and
magnitude and asks about its manner. For it could not but seem astonishing to
the Virgin that an imminent conception and birth should be announced to her
just as if she were already married, with no mention of consummating the
marriage or of Joseph her betrothed, while she herself was conscious of her
virginity and had not yet engaged in conjugal relations with any man.

XXVIII. Furthermore, if it is asked why Christ willed to be born not only of
a Virgin but of a betrothed virgin, various reasons are advanced by the
Fathers with respect to Christ himself, his mother, and us. With respect to
*Christ,* it was so that no man could judge him illegitimate and born from
adultery; so that his genealogy could be known in the customary manner from
Joseph, who was thought to be his father, and it could be known from which
fathers he was born; and finally, so that in Joseph he would have a guardian
and caretaker while still an infant. With respect to his *mother,* it was so
that provision would be made for her reputation and safety, lest she be
regarded as an adulteress and stoned according to the law, and so that she
would have a husband by whom she would be cared for and defended and under
whose mantle she would be covered. With respect to *us,* it was so that our
faith concerning Christ’s birth from the Virgin would be more firmly
confirmed, since Joseph himself also bore witness to it concerning his wife.
