QUESTION IX.

ON THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN THE SACRAMENTS OF THE OLD AND NEW TESTAMENTS.

Does the difference between the sacraments of the Old and New Testaments
consist in this: that the former foreshadowed grace, while the latter contain
it; that the former had the figure, while the latter have the body itself? We
deny.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

I. This question depends upon the preceding one. In order that the papists may
exalt the efficacy of the sacraments of the New Testament all the more, and so
secure greater authority for sacred ministers, they disparage the sacraments
of the Old Testament. They maintain that the latter were only shadows and
figures of the grace that the sacraments of the New Testament confer and
effect. Hence Bellarmine contends that the difference between the sacraments of
the Old and New Testaments is abolished unless the latter effect what the
former signified.

II. To establish the state of the question, observe that the inquiry is not
whether there is some difference between the sacraments of the Old and New
Testaments, but what kind of difference and how great it is. For we acknowledge
that there are various and multiple agreements and differences. In both there
is the same efficient cause and author, namely God; the same thing signified,
Christ with his benefits; the same instrument of application, namely faith;
the same word of command and promise, although the words are not the same in
both; the same end, effect, and mode of operation, namely signification and
sealing. Yet they differ in various respects. 1. In

[1847 vol. III printed p. 316 | IA leaf 350 | PDF p. 350]

external signs and rites. 2. In number: the old were more numerous, both
ordinary and extraordinary; the
new are fewer, more restricted, and more excellent. 3. In ease of use, in which
the new surpass the old, as is evident even from comparing baptism with
circumcision. 4. In the mode of signification: the former looked toward and
foreshadowed Christ to come; the latter regard Christ as exhibited. 5. In the
extent of their object: the former bound only Abraham’s descendants; the latter
pertain to all nations. 6. In duration: the former were to last until the time
of reformation, or Christ’s advent; the latter until the end of the ages. 7. In
clarity and perspicuity, arising not so much from the material of the signs as
from the institution of the Word joined to them, which is far clearer in the
New Testament than it was in the Old. 8. In the measure and degree of efficacy,
which is far greater in the new sacraments than in the old, not in the
conferring and effecting of the thing signified, but in sealing it more
certainly.

III. The papists extend this difference further, so that it is not merely a
difference of degree with respect to the mode of signifying, but also an
essential difference with respect to the thing signified. Thus the old
sacraments were only shadows that signified grace, while the new are true and
real causes that effect it. The former conferred grace only *ex opere
operantis*, that is, on account of the recipients’ faith, obedience, and
devotion; the latter do so *ex opere operato*. Canon 11 of the Council of Trent
pronounces “anathema” upon “those who teach that the sacraments of the new law
do not differ from the sacraments of the old law except because the ceremonies
and external rites are different.” But Bellarmine acknowledges that this was
said too briefly and obscurely, because he knew that we posit other differences
between the old and new sacraments. The fathers, however, craftily left the
true difference undetermined because of the scholastics’ open disputes over
this subject. They remained with general statements that do not touch the
hinge of the question. But in the instruction to the Armenians at the Council
of Florence, Eugenius had expressed the common opinion more clearly: “The
sacraments of the old law did not cause grace, but only prefigured that it was
to be given through Christ’s passion; but ours both contain grace and confer it
upon those who receive them worthily.” Bellarmine, book 2, chapter 13, *On the
Sacraments*, acknowledges that the scholastics do not agree among themselves
about circumcision. Scotus and his disciples maintain that circumcision
conferred justification *ex opere operato*. Thomas and his school deny this
and assert that it justified not by its own power, but insofar as it was a
profession of faith. Bellarmine himself adopts the latter view, which is the
more common opinion among them.

IV. The question therefore comes down to this. Is the difference between the
sacraments of the Old and New Testaments properly located in the fact that the
former foreshadowed grace, while the latter contain it? Or did the old and new
sacraments have the same efficacy with respect to the thing, although
different in mode and degree? Or do they differ from one another in the thing
signified and in effect? The papists affirm the latter; we affirm the former.

[Marginalia: It is proved that the sacraments of the Old and New Testaments do
not differ essentially. 1. From 1 Corinthians 10:1–2.]

V. The reasons follow. First, Paul in his own way attributes our baptism and
Supper to the fathers (1 Cor. 10:1–4), certainly not with respect to the signs,
which were different, but with respect to the thing signified. The cloud and
the passage through the sea signified and sealed to the fathers what baptism
does to us, and the manna and water from the rock what the Eucharist does to
us. Hence they are said “to have eaten the same spiritual food and to have
drunk from the same spiritual drink, for they drank from the rock that followed
them, and the Rock was Christ.” Thus it is evident that they agree with ours in
the thing signified and in effect, and that ours have no prerogative over
theirs. Although those sacraments were not ordinary but extraordinary and were
to last only a short time, the argument is no less valid—indeed, it is
stronger. For if the same efficacy as belongs to the new is attributed to
those that were instituted for a time, much more must it be attributed to

[1847 vol. III printed p. 317 | IA leaf 351 | PDF p. 351]

the ordinary sacraments that would last as long as the economy of the Old
Testament church. Nor can it be said that
they were not sacraments because they were common to brute animals. On that
reasoning, Christ’s baptism and the eunuch’s baptism would not have been
sacraments, because brute animals could also drink from the waters of the
Jordan. But distinguish the common use of the signs, according to which they
are purely natural things that beasts can use, from their special or sacred
use, according to which they are sacraments. Thus manna and water in their
common use could have been shared with brute animals, but not with respect to
the sacred use at issue here. Hence Paul does not call the manna spiritual and
the water spiritual, but calls them spiritual food and spiritual drink. Manna
as manna, and water as water, were corporeal things available even to every
beast, but they were called spiritual only insofar as they were such food and
drink—that is, spiritually—in the same way that our sacraments are spiritual.

VI. Bellarmine wrongly adds that this food and drink are called spiritual not
because of their effect but because of their cause, since both were produced
miraculously by the work of angels, whence manna is called the bread of angels.
As though someone discussing the use or abuse of the sacraments would have been
content to indicate their efficient cause, about which there was no dispute,
while omitting their end and effect, which was the principal question.
Moreover, it is gratuitously assumed that the food is called spiritual from
its cause, since the following words make clear that it is so called from its
effect, or its signification of Christ. Paul adds, “The Rock was Christ”—not
that Christ brought streams out of the rock, but that the rock itself was
Christ. This can be said only spiritually and understood only significatively,
which is the effect of all sacraments. Otherwise Paul could not have said that
the Israelites drank from that rock which was Christ, if Christ was not
presented in it to be drunk symbolically. It is therefore called spiritual
food and drink because it was to be received not only corporeally by the mouth,
but also spiritually by faith. This end is as much more excellent than the
proximate and lowly end of nourishing the body as divine things are more
excellent than earthly ones. Because most Israelites regarded them no more
than brute animals did, God most severely avenged this contempt. Nor should it
be answered that if the food is called spiritual because it is understood
spiritually, then only the righteous would have eaten it, contrary to Paul,
who asserts that all the fathers ate the same food, even those with whom God
was not pleased. For it is one thing for food to have been understood
spiritually, another for it to have been meant to be understood spiritually.
The food is indeed called spiritual because by God’s institution it was meant
to be understood spiritually, not because it was immediately understood
spiritually by everyone. It could therefore be eaten indiscriminately by good
and evil people: by the former as understood according to God’s institution,
but by the latter, through abuse, without understanding it.

VII. Bellarmine’s objection is no more solid: “Paul” does not say “the
Israelites ate the same spiritual food with us, but with themselves and among
themselves.” 1. The apostle’s purpose is contrary to this. His purpose is to
show the Corinthians, by the example of the Israelites, that they must not
presume to despise God’s judgments out of confidence in external symbols. The
Israelites, although equal to us with respect to the tokens of salvation and
having the same sacraments as we do with respect to the thing signified, could
not escape God’s judgments. This argument would be entirely null unless a
comparison between the old and new sacraments were being made. (2.) The words
cannot bear this gloss, both because the name *baptism* is given to the passage
through the sea, which would be without reason unless they agreed in the thing
signified and in effect, and because the manna and water from the rock receive
the name of Christ, who is the thing signified in the Eucharist. Finally, there
was no occasion for Paul to admonish the fathers so seriously that they ate
the same manna and drank the same water among themselves, since no one ever,
even in a dream, imagined that various and multiple kinds of manna and water
were exhibited to the Israelites. John 6:58, which Bellarmine

[1847 vol. III printed p. 318 | IA leaf 352 | PDF p. 352]

adduces to establish his opinion, does not overturn ours, but distinguishes
the external symbol, or sacrament,
from the thing of the sacrament. Christ means that the fathers who clung to
that external husk and regarded nothing in the manna but corporeal food did not
draw spiritual life from it, but died. In the same way, those among the
Corinthians who did not discern the body of the Lord and approached the sacred
table with no greater reverence than a profane one were punished by God and
afflicted with illness and death (1 Cor. 11:30). Nor can Christ, in speaking
this way, be said to have fostered the Jews’ error. Rather, he recalled them
from the signs to the thing signified, that is, to himself, in whom there is
life, teaching that there is no sacrament when, with the spiritual food
excluded, only the symbols are received.

[Marginalia: 2. From circumcision, which was the seal of the righteousness of
faith.]

VIII. Second, circumcision did not merely figure grace, but conferred it in
lawful use. In Genesis 17:11 and Romans 4:11 it is called the seal of the
righteousness of faith. A seal does not crudely figure something, but puts one
in possession of the thing. Add that circumcision is called God’s covenant, in
which God promises to be the God of Abraham and his seed. This certainly
implies the communication of saving grace and embraces the special love of God
with which he attends the elect. Now the principle that applied to
circumcision applies equally to the other sacraments of the Old Testament. Nor
can it be answered that the promise in which God promises Abraham to be his
God was only temporal protection and did not extend beyond the limits of this
life. For the nature of the covenant and Christ’s reasoning invincibly show
that this promise extends to the immortality of the soul and the resurrection
of the body, that is, to complete blessedness. The patriarchs are therefore
said to have aspired to it as to a heavenly homeland (Heb. 11:16). To the same
point belongs God’s promise to be Abraham’s exceedingly great reward (Gen.
15:1). Who would say that the person whose reward is God has nothing but an
earthly reward? Finally, the righteousness of faith, of which circumcision was
the seal, includes the remission of sins and salvation. These are said to have
been sealed to Abraham through circumcision as one of the believers to whom
the same grace is promised and sealed in the sacraments of the New Testament,
namely, all those who follow in the footsteps of Abraham’s faith.

[Marginalia: 3. From the interchange of names.]

IX. Third, there is an interchange of names between the sacraments of the Old
and New Testaments, which are attributed indiscriminately to the faithful of
both Testaments. Just as circumcision and the Passover are attributed to us (1
Cor. 5:7; Col. 2:11), so baptism and the Eucharist are attributed to the
fathers (1 Cor. 10:2–4). This interchange would have no place unless there were
a parity and essential identity between the sacraments of both Testaments.
Since this is not found between the signs, it must be found with respect to
the thing signified and the effect.

[Marginalia: 4. From the word of promise.]

X. Fourth, the sacraments of both Testaments agree in the word of promise
(Gen. 3:15; 17:11; Acts 2:38–39; Rom. 4:10–11; Gal. 3:29). Therefore they also
agree in the thing signified, which is included in that word.

[Marginalia: 5. From the kingdom of heaven promised to the fathers.]

XI. Fifth, Christ promises to the faithful of the New Testament no other
kingdom of heaven than the one in which they recline with Abraham and the
other patriarchs (Matt. 8:11). Likewise, in the sacraments of both peoples he
exhibits and seals not another grace, but precisely the same one, because
Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever (Heb. 13:8). Abraham saw his
day and rejoiced, and he invites us in the Supper to communion in his body and
blood.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XII. The sacraments of the Old Testament were shadows and types of things to
come (Heb. 10:1; Col. 2:17) in

[1847 vol. III printed p. 319 | IA leaf 353 | PDF p. 353]

their legal relation, yet in their evangelical relation they also exhibited
and applied in their own manner the body itself, namely Christ with his
benefits. Moreover, they are called shadows not with respect to our sacraments,
but with respect to Christ, who is the truth of all types (John 1:17) and the
body of the shadows (Col. 2:17). If Peter calls them antitypes (1 Pet. 3:21),
this is not because they are types of our sacraments, but because they are
types similar and corresponding to ours, which were substituted for them.

XIII. If the expiation of sins is attributed to the sacraments of the New
Testament (Acts 2:38; 22:16), it is not the proper and real expiation that
belongs to Christ’s blood alone, but a moral and sacramental one. This is no
less attributed to the sacraments of the Old Testament (Lev. 1:4; 4:26),
although proper and real expiation is denied to them (Heb. 9:10).

XIV. The sacraments of the Old Testament are said to be nothing (1 Cor. 7:19),
to avail nothing (Gal. 6:15), and to be weak and beggarly elements of the
world. This is partly when they are considered simply in themselves as signs,
in opposition to the thing signified. In that respect, the same principle
applies to ours when they are considered solely in the external rite. Or it is
with respect to their abrogation after the rising of the sun of righteousness,
which dispelled those shadows. Or it is with respect to the abuse of people
who, contrary to the divine institution and their lawful use, by a deadly
error bound grace to them in themselves, although it was to be ascribed to
Christ’s death and the Holy Spirit.

XV. The promise of grace annexed to the sacraments of the Old Testament was
just as absolute as the promise added to the sacraments of the New, because
they were signs of the same covenant (Gen. 17). When the New Testament is said
to have been established upon better promises, this is understood not with
respect to the thing promised, which was the same in both, but with respect to
the mode of promising, which was clearer, fuller, and more efficacious. The
passages in Jeremiah 31 and Hebrews 8:8 do not establish some substantial
difference between the old and new sacraments, but institute a comparison
between the law and the gospel, and between the righteousness of faith and of
works. But just as the pious Israelites were partakers of evangelical grace
through faith, so they also received the pledge and seal of the same grace in
the sacraments.

XVI. In the sacraments, the material must be distinguished from the formal.
The material is the sign with the external rites, which do not justify. The
formal is the thing signified itself, Christ with his benefits, from whom alone
comes justification. The old sacraments were the same as ours not with respect
to the material, but with respect to the formal.

XVII. Although Christ’s flesh did not yet exist under the Old Testament, the
righteousness obtained through his flesh was sealed to the ancient fathers in
their sacraments no less truly than the promises of God are true and certain.
To God all future things are present, and he makes them in some manner present
and existent also to the faith of those who use the sacraments. Thus the whole
Christ and the whole mystery of our salvation were always present, not only to
God but also to the eyes of faith, according to their capacity and measure.
All the faithful of every age firmly and efficaciously embraced this mystery
both in the Word and in the sacraments and sacrifices. For the faith is the
same, whether it looks to Christ as one to be born, as did that of the fathers,
or as already born, as does ours.

XVIII. If Scripture in certain places diminishes the dignity of the old
sacraments, it does not do so to exalt the new sacraments above the old, but to
exalt the efficacy of Christ’s death above the rites and sacrifices of the
law. The efficacy of expiating sins or regenerating is not taken away from the
sacrifices in order to be claimed for baptism or the Eucharist, but in order
to be attributed to Christ’s death and the Holy Spirit (Col. 2:11; Heb. 9:12,
14; 10:4–5).
