QUESTION IX.

*On the nature and unity of Christ’s priesthood, and why is he said to be a
priest according to the order of Melchizedek?*

[Marginal heading: The twofold typical priesthood is a figure of Christ’s priesthood.]

I. The nature and dignity of Christ’s priesthood can be known in no better way
than by comparing the twofold *typical priesthood* by which it was shadowed
forth in the Old Testament, namely, the *Levitical* and the *Melchizedekian*.
There is no doubt that both had reference to Christ and obtained their
fulfillment in him, although a far more perfect image of him was exhibited in
the latter than in the former. Hence he is said to be according to the order
of Melchizedek, but not according to the order of Aaron.

[Marginal heading: Comparison and difference between the Levitical priesthood and Christ’s.]

II. For although the Levitical priesthood was instituted chiefly for no other
end than to shadow forth Christ’s priesthood, and a likeness may be observed
in various respects, yet it is certain that the latter is far more august and
perfect than the former. A difference appears in many things, whether account
is taken of the persons, the institution, the efficacy, the perfection, or
the duration.

[Marginal heading: 1. With respect to the persons.]

III. For as regards the *persons*, the Levitical priests were taken from the
tribe of Levi alone, but Christ arose from the tribe of Judah (Heb. 7:13–14).
They were mere men; he is the true and eternal Son of God. They were sinners
who needed to offer for themselves; he is holy and undefiled, who had to offer
for us alone and not for himself (Heb. 7:26–27). They differed from the
victims they offered and had to make expiation with another’s blood; he was
at once priest and victim, who delivered himself for us and carried his own
blood into the sanctuary, having obtained eternal redemption for us. They
were many because individuals could not fulfill all the parts of that office;
but because Christ most perfectly accomplished all things by himself, he is
the one priest who needs neither successor nor vicar.

[Marginal heading: 2. With respect to the institution.]

IV. 2. With respect to the manner of *institution*, although both were
instituted by God, yet the manner of their institution was very different:
the Levitical priesthood was instituted without an oath, but Christ’s
priesthood μετὰ ὁρκωμοσίας, “with an oath” (Heb. 7:20–21; Ps. 110:4), insofar
as the heavenly Father, employing the solemnity of an oath, promised Christ
an eternal and ἀμεταμέλητον, “irrevocable,” priesthood. The former was κατὰ νόμον

[Printed p. 359 | Internet Archive PDF 387 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

ἐντολῆς σαρκικῆς (Heb. 7:16), *according to the law of a carnal commandment*,
that is, with various external and bodily ceremonies employed, which were
changeable and destined to be abolished. But the latter was κατὰ δύναμιν ζωῆς
ἀκαταλύτου, *according to the power of an indissoluble life*: not under that
law and omen that, being shortly to die, he would transmit the priesthood to
his descendants, but consecrated by heavenly power through the Holy Spirit
and inaugurated into immortal life, so that he might be a priest forever.

[Marginal heading: 3. With respect to efficacy.]

V. 3. With respect to *efficacy*, the Levitical priesthood was ἀσθενὲς and
ἀνωφελές, “weak” and “unprofitable,” because by itself it had no power to
expiate sin and cleanse the conscience (Heb. 9:13–14; 10:3–4). Therefore the
law is said to have perfected nothing (Heb. 7:19). But what innumerable
victims repeatedly offered could not do, this one victim offered once
accomplishes (Heb. 10:10). Christ’s priesthood is most efficacious, both for
justification through the infinite merit of his righteousness and for
sanctification through the power of the Spirit. Hence, all the legal victims
having been rejected, God rested in this sacrifice alone (Heb. 10:6–8; 1 John
1:7).

[Marginal heading: 4. With respect to perfection.]

VI. 4. With respect to *perfection*, because the Levitical priesthood was
ineffectual, it was therefore also imperfect, to such an extent that the same
sacrifices had to be repeated yearly and daily (Heb. 10:11). But because
Christ’s priesthood was of infinite value and efficacy, it was therefore most
perfect and complete in every respect, so that the offering once made neither
can nor ought to be repeated, as the apostle teaches at length in Hebrews 10.

[Marginal heading: 5. With respect to duration.]

VII. Finally, with respect to *duration*, the Levitical priesthood was
temporary, both with respect to the order, which had to endure only until a
fixed time and afterward be abolished (Dan. 9:27; Heb. 7:16), and with respect
to the persons, who, since they were mortal, could not endure long but needed
successors to take the place of those who died, so that the priesthood was
transmitted from one to another. But as Christ is immortal and lives forever,
so he has an eternal and ἀπαράβατον, “intransmissible,” priesthood, in which he
had neither predecessor nor successor, and which he not only administered on
earth but also perpetually exercises in heaven, always living to intercede
for us (Heb. 7:24–25).

[Marginal heading: What was the Melchizedekian priesthood?]

VIII. The other typical priesthood, far more excellent than the preceding and
the one from which Christ’s priesthood receives its designation, is the
Melchizedekian. Moses briefly recounts its history in Genesis 14; David
touches on the type in a single phrase in Psalm 110:4; and the apostle explains
it more fully in the epistle to the Hebrews. Since this subject is
δυσερμήνευτον, “difficult to explain” (Heb. 5:11), and no light shines upon us
from elsewhere for attaining a knowledge of this mystery, we must undoubtedly
rest in those things which God wished to disclose to us in this passage.

[Marginal heading: Who was Melchizedek?]

IX. To say something about the person, although it is easier to say who he was
not than to define who he was, the silence of Scripture in some manner imposes
silence on us here and puts a clasp on our curiosity. First, κατ’ ὄψιν, “at
first sight,” we cannot agree with those who held Melchizedek to be not a man,
but either the *Holy Spirit*, as did certain ancient heretics who were for
that reason called *Melchizedekians* (concerning whom see Epiphanius,
*Panarion*, heresy 55, and Sixtus of Siena, *Bibliotheca*, book 5, annotation
90), or the *Son of God himself*, presenting himself to Abraham in this
prelude to the future incarnation, as Cunaeus held (*De Republica Hebræorum*,
3.3). For: 1. There is no greater reason for understanding symbolically
everything related about him in Genesis 14 than what is related about the
other Canaanite kings. 2. Moses removes every doubt when he describes him so
expressly by his name, seat, a place very well known in Palestine,

[Printed p. 360 | Internet Archive PDF 388 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

office, and actions. 3. Christ could not be said to be a priest according to
the order of Melchizedek unless he were distinguished from him; nor could
Melchizedek himself truly be said to be ἀφωμοιωμένος, “made like,” the Son of
God (Heb. 7:3), unless he differed from Christ as the type from the antitype.
Nor does his being called ἀπάτωρ and ἀμήτωρ, “without father and without
mother,” prove that he was not a mortal man, because this must be understood
not absolutely but comparatively—not so much *positively*, as if he were
simply such, as *negatively*, because Scripture is silent about him, as will
be said afterward. Nor should the statement in Hebrews 7:3 that he remains a
*priest* forever be understood otherwise: he was unique and had no successor.
Likewise, testimony is borne to him “that he lives” (v. 8) because Moses, in
writing neither of his birth nor his death, appears in a manner to present him
as immortal, so that true and properly so-called eternity might in some
measure be shadowed forth.

X. Second, neither can we join those who think that he was Shem, Noah’s son,
as most Jews hold and as, after Epiphanius, seemed right to Jerome and not a
few others. For he could not be called ἀπάτωρ, ἀμήτωρ, ἀγενεαλόγητος,
“fatherless, motherless, without genealogy,” when his genealogy, birth, and
death are recounted so carefully. Nor can any reason be given why Moses would
have changed his name. Further, Canaan did not fall to Shem as an inheritance,
but to Canaan (Gen. 10:15, 19). Finally, Melchizedek was to have nothing in
common with the Levitical stock; otherwise Levi would no less have received
tithes in Shem than given them in Abraham. Nor is it credible that a
grandfather would receive tithes in his grandson, or that anyone could be
called a stranger in his grandfather’s country and before his eyes. Hence
Josephus and Philo openly reject this opinion of their countrymen.

XI. Therefore the third opinion alone appears to be retained here: it holds
that Melchizedek was a true man, born of men, king of Salem, who lived in the
time of Abraham and united the royal office with the priestly. To inquire
further who he was is to inquire into what God did not wish us to know.
Indeed, if this were known and perceived, there would have been no room for
the type and mystery, the whole basis of which depends on God’s having chosen,
not without purpose, that the lineage, birth, and death of so great a man not
be designated. Elsewhere this is usually done diligently whenever public
persons, whether priests or kings, are treated. No other reason can be given
why it was not done than the special nature of the mystery which the apostle
pursues in the epistle to the Hebrews.

[Marginal heading: What τάξις Melchisedeci means.]

XII. This must now be weighed so that the true nature of this figure may be
known. For a clearer knowledge of it, we must first see what the psalmist in
Psalm 110 and, from him, Paul in Hebrews 7 understand by τάξιν Μελχισεδέκ. The
word דִּבְרָתִי found in the Hebrew is rendered by Paul as τάξιν, which denotes
*order, custom*, or *condition*, so that here י is paragogic, not a suffix, as
in Ecclesiastes 3:18, דִּבְרַת בְּנֵי הָאָדָם, *the condition of human affairs*.
Thus τάξις Ἀαρών designates nothing other than the condition of the Aaronic
priesthood prescribed by the law, as is gathered from verse 16, where what he
had called κατὰ τὴν τάξιν he interprets as οὐ κατὰ νόμον ἐντολῆς σαρκικῆς,
*according to the law of a carnal commandment*, and what he called κατὰ τάξιν
Μελχισεδέκ he explains as κατὰ δύναμιν ζωῆς ἀκαταλύτου, *according to the power
of an indissoluble life*. Thus nothing else is denoted than the condition of
the Melchizedekian priesthood and the agreement and likeness which Christ’s
priesthood bears to it in this respect, as the apostle expresses in verse 15,
κατὰ τὴν ὁμοιότητα, *according to the likeness*.

[Marginal heading: Christ agrees with Melchizedek:]

[Marginal heading: 1. In name.]

XIII. The apostle places this likeness in various things: 1. In *name*,
because he was called Melchizedek, king of Salem, which, according to the
apostle’s interpretation, signifies *king of righteousness* and *king of
peace* (Heb. 7:2). Both properly belong to Christ, who is *Jehovah our
righteousness* and the *Prince of peace*.

[Marginal heading: 2. In origin.]

2. In *origin*, because Melchizedek is described to us by Moses as ἀπάτωρ,
ἀμήτωρ,

[Printed p. 361 | Internet Archive PDF 389 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and ἀγενεαλόγητος (v. 3), not absolutely and simply, as if he were positively
such—which the common condition of men, from whose race he was said above to
have come, rejects—but *in a certain respect*, by reason of the Mosaic
description. Moses speaks of Melchizedek as if he had fallen from heaven,
making no mention of his birth, death, or parents, so that by such silence
Christ’s true eternity might in some measure be shadowed forth. Therefore he
is called ἀπάτωρ and ἀμήτωρ, not in *nature*, as a natural man and son of Adam,
but in the *economy*, as a mystical man and figure of the second Adam, as he
is considered here—not with respect to generation, but to commemoration,
because nothing concerning his parents is found in Scripture. Likewise, he
is said to *have neither beginning nor end of life* because Scripture is
completely silent about his birth or death. He is ἀγενεαλόγητος because his
lineage is nowhere described, *whose father and mother are not written in the
genealogies*, as the Syriac rightly explains it; that is, neither his
ancestors nor his descendants are recounted in Scripture, so that it might be
said from whom he arose or who arose from him. For γενεαλογεῖν is to recount
someone’s stock and ancestry, as γενεαλογεῖν θεούς in Justin Martyr means *to
compose the genealogy of the gods*. But the things which occur in him only
shadowily and imperfectly are truly and perfectly fulfilled in Christ,
whether with respect to person or office. For with respect to his *person* he
was truly ἀπάτωρ as man and truly ἀμήτωρ as God; with respect to his *office*,
he was born neither of a priestly father nor of a mother who drew her origin
from priests, nor is he read to have had any predecessor or successor in this
office. In this way he is truly ἀγενεαλόγητος.

[Marginal heading: 3. In duration.]

3. With respect to *duration*, because he is presented as immortal, since no
mention of his death is made by Moses (Heb. 7:8). But this is most truly said
of Christ because he is a priest forever according to the power of an
indissoluble life (Heb. 7:16).

[Marginal heading: 4. In office.]

XIV. 4. With respect to *office*, because the two offices, royal and priestly,
were united in him: the same man was king of men and priest of the Most High
God. This was not lawful in the Old Testament, where God circumscribed each
of those offices with its own boundaries so that one and the same person
could not ordinarily discharge both offices. But this was truly fulfilled in
Christ, who is described to us as both king and priest (Ps. 110).

[Marginal heading: 5. In dignity.]

5. With respect to *dignity*, because he is presented to us as superior to
Abraham by the fact that he is said to have blessed him, namely, with a solemn
and priestly blessing, according to which the lesser is blessed by the greater
(Heb. 7:7). Abraham himself publicly acknowledged this by paying tithes to
Melchizedek, which, it is agreed, were due to God alone and his ministers
(Gen. 28:22). Thus Christ obtained an office far more excellent than the
Levitical. Nor, if Christ arose from Abraham as Levi did, can he be said to
have been tithed in him as Levi was, because he was there only according to
the flesh and human nature, but not according to the person and divine nature,
which must chiefly be considered here.

[Marginal heading: 6. In unity.]

6. With respect to *unity*, because Melchizedek is presented as unique, having
neither predecessor nor successor. Thus Christ is the one priest of the New
Testament, who needs neither vicars nor successors, since he lives forever and
has an ἀπαράβατον, “intransmissible,” priesthood, in contrast to the Levitical
priests, who, because they were weak and mortal, had to be many (Heb. 7:22–23,
28).

XV. These are the chief heads of this likeness, which show that the priesthood
of Melchizedek is far more excellent than the Levitical. But the Roman
Catholics deny that the character of this order is to be established in these
things. They say it must instead be sought in the unbloody sacrifice of bread
and wine which is offered daily in the Mass, after the pattern of
Melchizedek’s offering, through the ministry of the priests of the New

[Printed p. 362 | Internet Archive PDF 390 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Testament, who were instituted by Christ for that end. For they say neither
the blessing and tithing can constitute this difference, since both were done
by the Levitical priests, nor can his unity and lack of succession, because no
one is read to have succeeded Abel either, nor is the lineage of Job or Elijah
recounted. But those who reason this way labor in vain. 1. Although the things
ascribed to Melchizedek might be found separately in others, they are nowhere
found conjointly except in him. 2. Blessing and tithing in general also
belonged to the Levitical priests, but not in the manner in which they are
ascribed to Melchizedek, since he is said to have tithed and blessed Abraham
himself and, in him, the Levites themselves, as a superior blessing his
inferiors. 3. Abel cannot be called ἀπάτωρ, ἀμήτωρ, ἀγενεαλόγητος, nor are the
lineages of Job and Elijah absolutely passed over in silence, besides the fact
that they were not the priests with whom we are dealing here.

[Marginal heading: Are there many priests according to the order of Melchizedek? Denied.]

XVI. But let us see further how falsely some priesthood of the New Testament
according to the order of Melchizedek is obtruded upon us, exercised by
Christ’s ministers in the sacrifice of the Mass. The contrary is gathered
clearly from various considerations. First, the apostle places the difference
between the Levitical and the Melchizedekian priesthood in this: the former
priests were many, both simultaneously and successively, but the latter is
one, who ought to have neither colleague nor successor. “Those indeed,” says
the apostle (Heb. 7:23–24), “were many according to the law because they could
not continue through death; but this one, because he remains forever, has an
ἀπαράβατον priesthood.” This certainly could not be said if the
Melchizedekian priesthood had many mortal and weak priests succeeding one
another, no differently from the Levitical priesthood.

XVII. Nor does it help to reply here with Bellarmine (*On the Mass* 1.25) that
“Paul indeed excludes priests of the same order and dignity, but not inferior
priests who in relation to Christ are truly ministers. No one indeed can
succeed Christ in the same order because he lives forever, but nothing
prevents others from being instituted under him as his vicars and ministers.
Although Christ is our one pastor and teacher (Matt. 23:8), there can
nevertheless be other inferior teachers and pastors without prejudice to
him.” 1. This distinction openly contradicts the apostle to his face when he
says that Christ’s priesthood is ἀπαράβατον (Heb. 7:24), that is, not only
perpetual but incapable of being transmitted to another. But if there were
many secondary priests, they would have a priesthood participated from Christ
and transmitted to them. 2. It also conflicts with the type. For
Melchizedek’s priesthood was such that he had no associates, whether as
successors or as vicars and secondary priests; it was included in the one
Melchizedek to the exclusion of every other. Therefore, since Christ is a
priest according to the order of Melchizedek, he must be one, so that no one
under any name or pretext can be joined to him in this office as colleague,
successor, or secondary priest. For if the figure is restricted to one person,
why not the reality by an even stronger right? And if from the fact that
Melchizedek had no successor it is rightly gathered that Christ has none,
why is it not likewise concluded from the fact that he had no inferior that
Christ likewise has none? This is confirmed by the fact that in the calling
of Aaron mention is always made of his sons and descendants, but with respect
to Melchizedek mention is made only of the person himself. This is certainly
for no other reason than to show that the Aaronic priesthood was destined for
one *family*, but the Melchizedekian for one *person*. 3. These secondary
priests are either of the same species as the primary priest or of a different
one, but neither can be said. Not the latter, because then the sacrifice they
offered would have to be different from Christ’s sacrifice, which the
adversaries

[Printed p. 363 | Internet Archive PDF 391 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

do not wish. Not the former, because then they would have to be kings and
priests, ἀπάτορες, ἀμήτορες, ἀγενεαλόγητοι, and to possess a perpetual and
ἀπαράβατον priesthood which would not be transmitted to another, in which
they had neither predecessors nor successors, and the like, in which this
order of Melchizedekian priesthood consists according to the apostle. Anyone
can see that these things cannot be said of them. Again, like Christ they
would have to be constituted with an oath and by the power of an
indissoluble life; to be pure and undefiled, needing to offer not for
themselves but only for the people; themselves to be both priests and victims;
to offer only once, not daily; not to be prevented by death from continuing;
and to be able to minister in the true sanctuary, namely heaven, as is said of
Christ. How foreign all this is to them is evident in itself. 4. If under the
New Testament there are no priests of the same dignity and order as Christ
because the reason for which many were appointed under the law no longer
exists—namely, the necessity of dying—so neither can there be many unequal and
secondary priests, because the reason for which there were many under the law
has ceased—namely, weakness, because one could not suffice for discharging so
many functions. Therefore he had to have many assistants to relieve him. But
neither reason has place in the immortal and most perfect Christ, who is
therefore neither prevented by death from continuing nor unequal through
weakness to so great an office. This is the apostle’s reasoning (Heb. 7:23–24,
28).

XVIII. Nor can the objection drawn from Christ’s prophecy prove the contrary:
it can admit secondary pastors and teachers although Christ always remains
the supreme pastor and teacher of the church. 1. The nature of prophecy,
which acts in God’s name before men, is one thing; the nature of the
priesthood, which acts in men’s name before God, is another. With respect to
the former, Christ, remaining in heaven, requires ministers who supply his
bodily presence, address men visibly, and teach repeatedly. But with respect
to the latter, since Christ is always present with the Father and the
sacrifice offered once is of infinite power, he does not require ministers to
sacrifice in his place. 2. Christ, who calls himself our supreme teacher,
instituted apostles and ministers to preach (Matt. 28:19; Eph. 4:11), but he
is nowhere read to have instituted inferior priests. 3. Inferior pastors
under Christ conflict with no type, as secondary priests do. 4. When Christ
is called the one teacher, it must be understood exclusively in relation to
all others because he teaches externally and internally and because he
brought us the gospel from heaven. Thus pastors teach nothing which they did
not receive from him, something which can belong to no mortal. But this cannot
be said of the priesthood because, on the adversaries’ hypothesis, the priests
must be of the same order as Christ.

[Marginal heading: 2. It was not instituted by Christ.]

XIX. Second, this priesthood is nowhere read to have been instituted in the
New Testament, either by Christ or by the apostles. Paul indeed enumerates
various orders of sacred offices, both ordinary and extraordinary (Rom. 12;
1 Cor. 12; Eph. 4), but among him there is complete silence about priests of
the New Testament. Is it credible that Paul, who so carefully explains
everything pertaining to the sacred ministry, even matters more minute and
less necessary, as appears in the epistles to Timothy and Titus, would have
omitted this species and part of that office, which is principal and primary
and in comparison with which the others appear of little importance? Thus
Christ indeed calls his own to the apostolate and ministry, that they may
teach, preach the gospel, and administer the sacraments; but he nowhere
commands them to perform a priestly office and to offer him daily as an
ἱλαστικόν sacrifice. Nor can his command to the disciples in the institution
of the Eucharist to do this in remembrance of him pertain to this, because,
according to Paul as interpreter, this concerns all the faithful (1 Cor. 11:

[Printed p. 364 | Internet Archive PDF 392 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

25), who explains it as eating the bread and drinking the cup: “As often as
you eat this bread and drink this cup, you will proclaim the Lord’s death,
etc.” Hence Bellarmine (*On the Eucharist* 4.16) acknowledges that this is
common to all Christians. Further, it is unheard of that the same words can
serve to institute two different sacraments, widely separated from each
other, as would happen here, namely, the institution of the Eucharist and of
the priesthood. 3. It is gratuitously supposed that τὸ *facere*, “to do,” is
placed here for *to sacrifice*. Nothing can be more foreign to the mind of
Christ, who is nowhere read either to have observed any sacrificial rite here
or to have commanded his apostles to do so, as will be shown more fully in
its place.

[Marginal heading: 3. This priesthood can belong to no man.]

XX. Third, this priesthood according to the order of Melchizedek is marked by
characteristics and signs which can belong to no one except Christ: the
priest is called and constituted by God with an oath (Heb. 7:21); he needs to
offer not for himself but for the people (Heb. 7:27); he had to offer not
repeatedly but only once, not another’s blood but his own, to take away sin
(Heb. 9:26); having accomplished eternal redemption by his own blood, he had
to enter the heavenly sanctuary (Heb. 9:12); living forever, he could save
perfectly those who come to God through him (Heb. 7:25); and finally, to
accomplish all these things, he had to be pure, undefiled, and separated from
sinners (Heb. 7:26).

[Marginal heading: 4. Because Paul does not speak of it.]

XXI. Fourth, the apostle, who deals so carefully with the agreement of the
Melchizedekian priesthood and Christ’s, says not a word about that unbloody
sacrifice or offering of bread and wine in which they wish this likeness
principally to be placed. He would not have omitted it if he had held the same
opinion as the Roman Catholics. Nor should it be said here with Bellarmine
that either doing this did not belong to the apostle’s purpose, which was to
extol the excellence of Christ’s priesthood above the Levitical—for if the
apostle intended to institute a comparison of these two priesthoods, could or
ought he have omitted its most important part?—or that the mystery of the
Eucharist was too deep for the Hebrews to understand, which the apostle calls
δυσερμήνευτον (Heb. 5). For if this reason were valid, he ought to have said
nothing about so many other mysteries no less arduous, indeed far more
difficult, than the mystery of the Eucharist, for example, Christ’s deity,
incarnation, sacrifice, and the like. Indeed, in Hebrews 6:1 the apostle
testifies that the Hebrews ought not to cling to the rudiments of religion but,
leaving the discourse of the beginning, to press on to perfection.

[Marginal heading: 5. Because the priesthoods of Aaron and Melchizedek do not differ in this respect.]

XXII. Fifth, in that case the Aaronic and Melchizedekian priesthoods would not
differ in this respect, although the apostle presents them as utterly
different, since in the former there was also an offering of bread and wine
(Ex. 29:24; all of Lev. 2; Num. 28:5, 12), where offerings of

[Printed p. 365 | Internet Archive PDF 393 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

fine flour are commanded, not only as some part or seasoning of another
sacrifice, but also as an offering in itself (Lev. 2:1–2). Indeed, the
Melchizedekian would be cast below the Levitical if what constituted a part of
the sacrifice for the latter constituted the whole sacrifice for the former.
Nor does it help to reply that the Melchizedekian priesthood differs from the
Levitical in this: the latter offered sacrifices of every kind, both bloody
and unbloody, but the former only an unbloody one. For so far is it from the
case that any excellence thereby accrues to the Melchizedekian that, on the
contrary, the Levitical would have to be judged far more excellent for this
very reason. Add that in this way Christ should be called a priest according
to the order of Aaron rather than Melchizedek, since in this sense he offered
both a bloody and an unbloody sacrifice: the bloody on the cross and the
unbloody in the Eucharist.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XXIII. There is nothing in the history of Melchizedek in Genesis 14 from
which it can be gathered that he offered an unbloody sacrifice of bread and
wine to God. Nor does the statement about bringing bread and wine to Abraham
as he returned from the slaughter of the kings pertain to this, because it
concerns not a priestly function but royal munificence, by which he wished to
refresh Abraham’s weary soldiers and receive his victorious friend with a
royal banquet. Fagius on Genesis 14 notes that the ancients had this custom:
to offer bread and wine to those returning from battle and possessing victory
in order to refresh them. An example occurs in Judges 8:5, where the
inhabitants of Succoth go out to meet Gideon with bread after the Midianites
have been defeated. Thus in Hebrew usage bread signifies every kind of food,
and to eat bread with someone is to sit at table and feast. That this and no
other is Moses’s meaning in this passage is proved: 1. From the verb הוֹצִיא,
which means to bring forth, bring into the midst, lead out, or draw out, but
not to offer. Hence the Septuagint everywhere translates it by ἄγειν, ἐξάγειν,
and ἐκφέρειν, and never by θύειν. Nor can it be taken in another sense in
Judges 6:18. Indeed, even if it were taken somewhere in Scripture for a
priestly action, it would not follow that it must be so taken here likewise.
2. The nature of the matter demands this, because a sacrificial offering
requires an altar erected for that end, of which Moses makes no mention. Yet
elsewhere Moses carefully relates this whenever he speaks about the sacrifice
of Noah, Abraham, Jacob, and others. Hence it is evident that this offering
was not made to God as a sacrifice, but to Abraham as a banquet. 3. Josephus
so explains it (*Antiquities* 1.11): ἐχορήγησε δὲ οὗτος ὁ Μελχισεδέκ τῷ
Ἀβράμου στρατῷ ξένια, καὶ πολλὴν ἀφθονίαν τῶν ἐπιτηδείων παρέσχε, *This
Melchizedek supplied gifts to Abraham’s army and furnished a great abundance
of necessary things*. The Septuagint renders it by ἐξήνεγκε, and in many
codices, especially the Complutensian, αὐτῷ, *to him*, is added by way of
explanation: ἐξήνεγκεν αὐτῷ ἄρτους καὶ οἶνον, *he brought out bread and wine
to him*. Thus Onkelos renders it. The Vulgate, which is authentic for the
Roman Catholics, has “brought forth.” Jerome, writing to Evagrius, and
Ambrose on this passage note that the ancient Hebrews so understood it.
Cajetan confesses that nothing is written here about sacrifice or offering,
but about the bringing forth and drawing out of bread and wine, which Josephus
says was done to refresh the victors.

XXIV. The objections brought by the adversaries do not prove the contrary.
1. They say the cause of the offering is immediately given from the
priesthood when it is added, *For he was priest of the Most High God*. But the
word in the Hebrew is not a causal but a copulative particle, as it is
rendered by Pagninus, Montanus, Vatablus, and the Septuagint, ἦν δὲ ἱερεύς.
Nor does it pertain to what precedes, as giving the reason why he *brought
forth* bread and wine, since he did that as a friendly and allied king, but to
what follows, that it might be understood by what right he blessed Abraham
and received tithes from him. Nor, if a soph pasuq is added, does it show that
the sentence ends, but only the verse, as happens more than once elsewhere
(Ps. 95:7–8; Gen. 23:17–18; Jer. 33:10–11). 2. They say there was no need to
destine the bread and wine for feeding Abraham’s soldiers because they were
already satisfied from the spoil (v. 24). But this does not follow, as
Pererius himself judges on Genesis 14: “There seems to be little strength in
this refutation, because either Melchizedek did not know this, or, if he knew
it, he nevertheless wished by this gift to declare his goodwill toward
Abraham and his own singular joy and delight at Abraham’s victory.” Add that
not all spoils are edible things capable of being swallowed. 3. They say that
nowhere else is mention made of Melchizedek’s sacrifice and its rite described
except here; therefore these words are rightly drawn to it, or, if they refer
to a banquet, it was a sacred and religious banquet to demonstrate their
communion in the worship of the true God, as was usually done after
sacrifices. But it was no more necessary for Moses to treat Melchizedek’s
sacrifice than his genealogy and parents. For besides the fact that

the priesthood extends more widely than sacrifice, since praying and blessing
belong to it, the purpose of the Holy Spirit was not to recount all the acts
of Melchizedek’s priesthood, but only those in which he excelled the Levitical
priests. This could not have been done by reason of sacrifice, which was
common to them. Indeed, Scripture’s silence in this matter, as in others,
could be mystical, so that it might be intimated that Christ had nothing
outside himself by which a sacrifice would be signified, because he had to be
both priest and victim. As for the addition concerning a sacred and religious
banquet which followed his sacrifice, so that the loaves were sacrificed
before they were set before Abraham, it is a mere supposition, of which not a
trace or vestige occurs in Moses. Indeed, it posits ἀσύστατα, “inconsistent
things.” For since, according to Bellarmine, sacrifice brings about the
destruction of the thing sacrificed, if the bread and wine were once destroyed
in sacrifice, how can they be fit to be shared by men? The flesh of cattle
could indeed be eaten after their immolation, in which they suffered their
destruction, that is, death. But the same cannot be said of inanimate things,
which cannot be destroyed unless they are either consumed by flames or poured
out, so that they can no longer be set out as food.

XXV. The eternity of the priesthood does not immediately infer the
perpetuity of offering a sacrifice, as if the priesthood were idle unless a
sacrifice were continually offered by it. It suffices that the sacrifice be
perpetual with respect to its efficacy and continual application. Thus the
sacrifice of Christ, offered once and ceased with respect to the act of
offering, nevertheless endures perpetually with respect to its eternal power
and value because his blood is always living and fresh before God. Therefore,
although the act of sacrifice ceased on earth, the priesthood is not thereby
idle, since a priest is instituted not only to offer but also to intercede and
discharge other functions. Indeed, if Christ were still on earth, he would
not even be a priest (Heb. 8:4). Nor must a priest always have something new
to offer (Heb. 8:3), provided he has offered at some time, just as the
Levitical priests did not cease to be priests when the sacrifice was
completed. Or, if some offering is required, it is not always a repetition of
the sacrifice, but its most efficacious representation through intercession,
after the pattern of the high priest, who, after completing the offering in
the tabernacle, carried the blood of expiation into the Holy of Holies to
intercede for the people.

XXVI. Since the apostle says that Christ is a priest forever without any
mention of sacrifice, the eternity of his priesthood cannot consist in it,
but is referred by the apostle to three other causes: 1. The *eternity of the
person*, because Christ as the Son of God lives forever (Heb. 7:8, 16–17). 2.
The *power and efficacy of the sacrifice*, which always endures and extends
to all believers of every time (Heb. 7:25; 10:10, 19). 3. The *perpetuity of
intercession*, because he always lives to intercede for us (Heb. 7:25).

XXVII. The Christian religion cannot exist without some external and visible
sacrifice by which God’s wrath was once appeased and sin expiated. But it is
not necessary that this external sacrifice be offered daily. It suffices that,
having been offered once, it possess eternal power and efficacy, which is
found in Christ’s sacrifice.

XXVIII. Wherever among the prophets priests and Levites are said to be future
under the New Testament, the passage refers either to the spiritual and
mystical priesthood common to all the faithful, by which, having been made a
royal priesthood, they must offer spiritual sacrifices pleasing to God through
Christ (1 Pet. 2:5; Rom. 12:1; Heb. 13:15). In this sense Isaiah 61:6 says that
the faithful will be called priests and ministers of God. Or the evangelical
ministry is understood, which is expressed in legal phraseology because of
the analogy between the two, since

God used the ministry of the priests to preserve the order of divine worship.
Thus in the Christian church he wishes to use the sacred ministry, by which
the elect are brought to Christ and, once brought, persevere steadfastly in
his worship. Thus Isaiah 66:21 must be understood, where God says that he will
take priests and Levites from the Gentiles, that is, sacred ministers. Just as
the Levites are not properly understood here as those from the tribe of Levi,
so neither must the word “priest” be understood properly, but mystically.

XXIX. If the ancients sometimes customarily distinguished ministers of the
gospel by the name of priests, they did not do so to signify that some real
and properly so-called external sacrifice besides Christ’s bloody sacrifice
offered on the cross exists in the church. For by that name they not
infrequently designated the faithful themselves as well. Rather they did so
chiefly for two reasons. First, ministers consecrate themselves to the work
of the ministry. Second, when performing the preaching of the gospel, they as
it were slay the people of God and offer them to God as a sacrifice
εὐπρόσδεκτον τῷ Θεῷ, “acceptable to God.” Paul has this in view in Romans
15:16, when he says that he is to ἱερουργεῖν τὸ εὐαγγέλιον, “perform the sacred
service of the gospel,” *so that the offering of the Gentiles might become
acceptable, sanctified by the Spirit*. Thus there is a certain sacrifice, but
one so called mystically and figuratively, not properly and visibly.

XXX. The distinction between a bloody and unbloody offering is ἀσύστατος,
“inconsistent.” For: 1. If Christ offered himself not only once in a bloody
sacrifice but also in an unbloody one and is offered daily, there are not one
but many sacrifices of the same Christ, contrary to the express passages of
Scripture (Heb. 9:25; 10:10, 14). 2. Thus the apostle’s argument falls, by
which he concludes that Christ would have had to suffer repeatedly if he had
needed to offer himself repeatedly. 3. It conflicts with the Pauline oracle
testifying that without the shedding of blood there is no remission of sin
(Heb. 9:22). Hence, since the proper quality of the sacrifice was to be
bloody, whoever posits some unbloody sacrifice of Christ thereby denies
Christ’s true sacrifice.

XXXI. The removal of the continual sacrifice treated in Daniel 8:11 must,
historically and according to the letter, be referred to the persecution of
Antiochus, who profaned the temple and caused the legal worship, especially
the continual sacrifice, to cease. But mystically, if it is referred to
Antichrist, the continual sacrifice cannot pertain to the Mass, because it was
a bloody sacrifice which was customarily offered twice daily and in only one
place, which cannot be said of the Mass. Rather, it pertains to Christ’s
sacrifice itself, which is continually present before God for propitiation and
before believers for consolation. It can truly be said to have been removed
by Antichrist through the institution of the sacrifice of the Mass, which is
ἀσυστάτου, “inconsistent,” with it.

XXXII. The *pure offering* of which Malachi 1:11 speaks can no more be referred
to a properly so-called sacrifice consisting in the offering of bread and wine
than can the *incense* mentioned in the same place. Just as it is agreed that
by this incense the prayers and praises of believers are mystically
understood, which are often designated by this word (Ps. 141:2; Rev. 5:8), so
by the pure offering nothing else is denoted than the spiritual worship of
God to be instituted among the nations through the preaching of the gospel,
in language accommodated to the legal worship, as frequently elsewhere (Rom.
12:1; 15:16; 1 Pet. 2:5; Phil. 4:18).
