Second, only those to whom Christ has given the keys have the power to ordain.
But these keys have not been given to the people. Ordination is an act of
power, and it ought to be performed by those who have this power and authority
from Christ. Private Christians as a body do not have it; otherwise all would
be governors and rulers and there would be no one to be governed and ruled.
The church would thus not be an organic body, contrary to 1 Corinthians 12.
Scripture, however, clearly distinguishes between pastors and the flock;
between those who preside and those who ought to obey them; between governors
and those who are governed; and so forth.

That the keys have not been given to all believers is clear from the following.
1. Not all are ministers, governors, stewards, dispensers of the mysteries of
God, and so forth, 1 Corinthians 4:1; 2 Corinthians 4:5; 1 Timothy 3:16.
Apollonius argues as follows in his *Consideration of the Questions*, page 62:
“That office which essentially includes the pow-

[Volume II; printed p. 161; ONB canvas 169]

er and exercise of the keys is not common to all believers in the church, but
belongs to certain persons specifically chosen for it, 1 Corinthians 12:28;
Ephesians 4:11–12. The power of the keys was delivered to apostles, pastors,
and so forth as such, John 20:21–23; 2 Corinthians 5:20; 1 Timothy 5:17. This
power therefore belongs to them alone and not to all believers. For God gives
the keys to those who are dispensers of the mysteries of God, 1 Corinthians
4:1; servants of God in God’s house by reason of a special office, 2
Corinthians 4:5; those who know how to conduct themselves properly in God’s
house, 1 Timothy 3:16; those who distribute the portion due at the proper time
to the members of the household, Matthew 24:45; and those who rightly divide
and distribute the word of God, 2 Timothy 2:15. For the keys are emblems of
entrusted power. Christ commits them to the stewards and overseers in his house
so that they may administer his power. In Scripture, therefore, ‘keys’
metonymically signifies the authority, capacity, power, and administration of
government which is exercised by commanding, forbidding, releasing, and
restraining. See Isaiah 22:22 with Isaiah 9:6; Revelation 1:18; 3:7; 9:1; and
Matthew 16:18–19. It denotes the power of ministers and full administration.
Those keys, therefore, were delivered to the overseers of the church, not to
everyone in the church.”

It is commonly conceded that *keys* signify authority and power. The Hebrew
word מַפְתֵּחַ, “key,” Spineler says in his *Lexicon*, “metonymically signifies the
authority, capacity, and power of every kind of government, by commanding and
forbidding, releasing and restraining.” Musculus, on Isaiah 22:22, says: “The
keys, the emblem of received power, are entrusted to the steward and overseer
of the house, so that he may administer his power.” So Calvin on the passage.
Gualter, in homily 114, says that the keys are “the symbol of power given to
kings,” and therefore were presented to kings. See Beza and Pareus on Matthew
16:19, and Whitaker, volume 2, controversy 4, chapter 5. Calvin, on Matthew 16,
says that Christ “is discussing Peter’s apostleship.”

The adversaries commonly object by citing Christ’s statement, “I will give you
the keys of the kingdom of heaven,” Matthew 16:19. They say that these keys
were given to Peter not insofar as he was an apostle or a presbyter, but insofar
as he was a believer. *Reply.* (1) By this argument it follows that the power
to proclaim the gospel was given to everyone no less than the power of
jurisdiction, for the Fathers, doctors, commentators, Belgic annotations, and
theologians understand by these keys both the power they call *of order* and
the power *of jurisdiction*. (2) If the keys were given to Peter insofar as he
was a believer and represented all believers, then they were given to all
believers—to women, children, and everyone who has faith—because an inference
from someone as such to every instance of that kind follows perfectly. But
this is absurd. (3) If they were given to believers as such, then the keys were
not given to any member of the church, whether an overseer or not, who is not
really and truly endowed with true and saving faith, because what does not
belong essentially belongs accidentally. Therefore these keys were not given
to Judas. See Matthew 7:22–23. (4) If the keys were given to the multitude of
believers as their first and principal subject, then, 1. there is no need for
ecclesiastical ministers, because all believers can and must formally exercise
all public offices, or all the power signified by the *keys*. What greater
absurdity can be imagined? If it is said that they can transmit this power to
others—pastors, for example—by whom it can be formally exercised, then I ask:
by what right will they prove that believers may entrust this power to others
to exercise? All to whom Christ grants ecclesiastical power ought to exercise
it; they have no right to divest themselves of it and transfer it to others.
Those in whose name the keys were given to Peter also ought to have bound and
loosed. 2. The church would then, says Baynes in the English-language work
titled *The Diocesans’ Trial*, question 3, conclusion 3, page 84, have true
dominion with respect to ministers,

[Volume II; printed p. 163; ONB canvas 171]

because it would not only call them but, by virtue of the power it has in
itself, would create them. So John Ball in the English-language work titled
*The Trial of the Grounds Tending to Separation*, chapter 12, page 252, and so
forth: “If,” he says, “spiritual and ecclesiastical power resides in the church
or community of believers, the church will not only call ministers but will
make them by virtue of the power it has in itself, and will have true dominion
with respect to them. For just as one who wishes to transmit authority into the
church makes himself the church’s lord, so, if the church transmits authority
into the church’s ministers, it makes itself their mistress with respect to
that dominion; for everyone knows that giving authority belongs to a lord. If
the church gives power to pastors and doctors, it can render the administration
of the word and sacraments void, for the order instituted by Christ gives
being and efficacy to the things instituted. Therefore, if the power of
governing, feeding, and administering sacred things resides in believers, the
Word and Sacraments, with respect to their dispensation and efficacy, will
depend upon the constitution of the society. If the power of the keys is
derived from the community of believers, then all ministers are immediately
and formally the servants of the church, and they ought to do everything in
the church’s name: rule, feed, bind, loose, forgive and retain sins, preach,
and administer the sacraments. They must also perform their office according
to the church’s directions, whether to a greater or lesser extent, more or
less frequently, more remissly or more diligently. For by whom ought they to
be directed in performing their office, if not by those from whom they have
the office itself, whose work they perform, and from whom a reward is to be
expected? If their office and power are immediately from God, they must
perform it according to his prescription and will render an account to him.
But if their power and function are from the church, the church will render an
account to God and the ministers to the church.” Thus far he. Apollonius also
plainly rejects this in his *Consideration of the Fourth Controversy*, chapter
4, page 71: “We reject the antithesis of those,” he says,

[Volume II; printed p. 164; ONB canvas 172]

“who maintain that the multitude of the church transmits ecclesiastical power
to the church’s governors, and that the church’s governors are servants of the
believers in the proper sense: the church’s mouth, hands, and instruments, by
whose intervention it executes its decrees. On this view the church of the
believers, under Christ the church’s Lord and head and as his bride and wife,
communicates government, dispensation, and the legitimate exercise of
jurisdiction to certain persons whom it selects. As its servants and deputies,
and with authority borrowed from the multitude as the first and immediate
subject of ecclesiastical authority, they govern God’s house and exercise the
principal acts of their function.” In what follows, he refutes this position
with three firm arguments; this is not the place to cite them. I observe only
that it clearly follows from the adversaries’ position not merely that pastors
are servants of the church, but also that, as appears from Apollonius, they
openly defend that very position.

Some nevertheless reply that these arguments bear equally against pastors who
ordain other pastors, because although ministers receive from the church the
application of the office, they do not receive the office itself. And even if
they receive the office from it, they have it only instrumentally under Christ,
not by any dominion. *Reply.* It does not follow, because the adversaries say
that the church received this power first and principally from Christ and that
pastors receive it first and principally from the church; or that the church
possesses the keys *originally*, while ministers possess only their *exercise*,
and that from the church. Ministers are therefore servants of the church, not
in the relation of means to an end, as in 2 Corinthians 4:5 and 1 Corinthians
3:22, just as the angels are called “spirits performing a ministry, who are
sent out for service for the sake of the heirs of salvation,” Hebrews 1, last
verse, but in the relation of inferiors to their superior masters. Thus, just
as ambassadors do everything

[Volume II; printed p. 165; ONB canvas 173]

in the name of their lords, so pastors ought to do everything in the name of
the church. Furthermore, although this power granted to the church is
ministerial with respect to Christ, it is not purely ministerial with respect
to the ministers whom the church sends and delegates, but has the character of
dominion, because they hold that this power is given to the church as its first
and proper subject. The church will thus be mistress and the supreme dispenser
of this power. Moreover, according to the adversaries, ministers as such have
no power except what the church delegates to them. They must therefore be
subordinate to believers as servants and representatives, to be removed from
office whenever the church of the believers sees fit. Indeed, they are subject
and subordinate in every way to the believers, or the church of the believers,
because as such they have no power in themselves. The reason is evident: as
such they are not believers, and the adversaries assert that this power has
been given only to believers as such. Therefore the church would send and
ordain ministers in the same way that a king or the estates of a kingdom send
or create subordinate officials: their own ministers who must render account
to them and do everything in their name.

(5) If the keys have been given to believers, then they have been given to them
either *formally* or only *virtually and radically*. If *formally*, then they
could formally exercise all the power given through those keys. What need
would there then be for ministers to proclaim the gospel, administer the
sacraments, and exercise discipline? All the absurdities already enumerated
would then follow, as would more to be enumerated later. If only *radically and
virtually*, then, 1. they were given to Peter only *radically* and not
*formally*. 2. Let them say to whom they were given *formally*. To pastors? How
will that be proved except by this text and parallel passages? 3. How will it
be proved by this text that the power of the keys was given to ministers
*formally*, but to believers only *rad-

[Volume II; printed p. 166; ONB canvas 174]

ically*? We must not distinguish where the law does not distinguish. 4. Even
if it were conceded that this power was given to believers *radically*, it
would not follow that they can actually exercise it, either alone or jointly
with ministers. For although some say that the power of civil government is
radically given to the people, it does not follow that the people can exercise
all this power, either by themselves or jointly with their governors.

(6) I would like to know whether this power was given to Peter insofar as he
represented individual believers considered separately, or insofar as he
represented believers taken collectively and associated as a corporate body.
If the former, then this power was not given to the church as such, and
individuals can exercise it separately. If the latter, the argument by which
they prove that this power was given to Peter in the name of believers, or
insofar as he represented believers, will not establish this. For the one
argument they present is that Peter had made such a confession of faith; but
this confession of faith can be made by believers considered separately as
well as jointly.

(7) But this sole argument they bring forward proves absolutely nothing, for
the inference on which this whole structure rests is invalid: Whatever was
given to Peter by reason of this confession of faith was given to him simply
insofar as he was a believer and represented believers. This, I say, will
never be proved, not even by a nineteenth inference. For Christ could have
given Peter, as one making such a confession of faith, a power that did not
belong to him at all insofar as he was a believer, but insofar as he was a
minister, and which was to be exercised by him as such. Let the adversaries
show, if they can, that this could not have been done. Then I would like to
know whether this power was given to true believers, who make a true and
unfeigned confession of faith just as Peter did, or also to those who make not
a sin-

[Volume II; printed p. 167; ONB canvas 175]

cere but a hypocritical confession of faith. If the latter is said, then, 1.
the foundation of this authority given to the church is destroyed. For Peter’s
confession was true, sincere, and neither feigned nor hypocritical; and Peter
represented not hypocrites, but true believers. Therefore the keys will be
given to these alone. 2. It would follow that the keys were not given to
believers as such. 3. Nor would they be part of the liberty and privilege of
the redeemed, as the adversaries describe them. If the former is said, then,
1. pastors who lack true faith cannot exercise the power of the keys and will
not be pastors. Consequently, those whom they baptize can no more be called
baptized than those baptized by women; they cannot excommunicate anyone, even
the most wicked person, administer the Lord’s Supper, or preach the word. Yet
Judas was an apostle and was sent to preach, Matthew 10:2.

Finally, if the keys were given to believers because Peter represented them
when he made that confession, it would follow that that ministerial church,
and ministerial power itself, was built upon the rock and therefore could not
fail. For believers, insofar as they are such and insofar as they make a
confession like the one Peter made here, possess the keys and thereby become a
*ministerial church*, that is, a church possessing the power to exercise the
keys. But even such churches can fail, as experience has demonstrated; and
from this the papists also conclude, though falsely, that the church cannot err
in defining dogmas of faith. (8) The text itself distinguishes Peter from the
church. Christ does not say, “and I will give the church,” but “I will give you
the keys,” and so forth.

But enough on this passage, since all our divines, disputing against the
papists, solidly establish that Peter here represented the apostles and their
successors in the pastoral office. They establish it by reason, from the
Scriptures, and from the authority of the Fathers—*Irenaeus, Nazianzen,
Cypria-*

*n, Basil, Ambrose, Theophylact, Cyril, Euthymius, Jerome, Augustine, Bede,
Chrysostom*, and others. Our following arguments also show the emptiness of
