# CHAPTER XVI

## The Decalogue is not the pure and simple covenant of grace

As we have seen, our opponents maintain that the Decalogue contains the pure
covenant of grace—indeed, nothing but the covenant of grace. That this is
utterly false is abundantly clear from the arguments by which, in the
preceding chapter, we proved that the Decalogue is not the formula or
stipulation of the covenant of grace. Nevertheless, we will also make the
point clear here in a few words.

1. If this law of the Decalogue is the pure and simple covenant of grace, it
first came into force after Adam's fall, not before, because before the fall
there was no gospel and no covenant of grace. But it is false that this law
first existed after the fall, for it was inscribed on Adam's heart in the
state of innocence, as the opponents themselves acknowledge. Therefore, it
does not contain the pure and simple covenant of grace.

2. This law of the Decalogue contains the things commanded to man in his
integrity, as the opponents acknowledge. Therefore, it does not contain the
pure and simple covenant of grace and nothing else. The reason for the
inference is evident: the covenant of grace was not commanded to man in the
state of innocence, nor did he then need it.

3. If the Decalogue contains nothing besides the covenant of grace, then it
does not contain those things which the covenant of works required, because
these two covenants differ specifically and are opposed to each other. But
the Decalogue contains the things which constituted the condition of the
covenant of works, as is well known, for *Do this and you will live* belongs
to the law of the Decalogue and is the very condition of the covenant of
works, as is conceded.

4. If the law of the Decalogue contains nothing besides the covenant of
grace, then, supposing that man had not fallen, it would not have existed. If
man had not fallen, he would not have needed the covenant of grace, which was
instituted solely to restore fallen man. But this cannot be said, because man
in the state of innocence neither was nor would have been without law.

5. Moreover, let us suppose that God had never instituted the covenant of
grace. Who will deny that God could have chosen not to restore fallen man but
to leave him lying in misery? Would there therefore have been no moral law or
law of the Decalogue? Who will affirm this? Yet it necessarily follows from
our opponents' assertion, for that which contains nothing besides the pure
and simple covenant of grace would not exist at all if the covenant of grace
had not been instituted.

[Printed p. 168 | IA PDF 176 | IA leaf 176 | IA decausadei02brow]

6. If the Decalogue contains nothing besides the pure and simple covenant of
grace, then it must be said that heathen nations whose ears have not yet
heard even the slightest report of the Mediator are under the covenant of
grace and have the covenant of grace in force among them. Yet this is
manifestly false. The reason for the connection is that they have the
Decalogue, at least materially, and the law which binds them is the same law
contained in the Decalogue. It is no answer to say that among those nations
the law was not promulgated in the same way as among the Israelites, for it
is materially the same law. A different mode of promulgation does not change
the law's nature or cause it to contain the pure and simple covenant of grace
and nothing else when it previously contained nothing of the kind. The nature
of the same law is not changed each time it is promulgated in a different
manner.

7. The opponents themselves acknowledge that this law of the Decalogue is,
at least for the most part, the same as the natural law. But the natural law
does not contain the pure and simple covenant of grace. Therefore, neither
does this law of the Decalogue. The minor premise needs no proof, and the
major premise is conceded.

8. That which makes no mention of Christ and does not present him as Mediator
does not contain the pure and simple covenant of grace and nothing else. But
the law of the Decalogue makes absolutely no mention of Christ and does not
present him as Mediator. Therefore, and so forth. The major premise is most
certain, for the covenant of grace cannot be conceived without Christ the
Mediator. The minor premise is also extremely well known. Romans 4:13–14,
together with Galatians 3.

9. If the law of the Decalogue contains nothing besides the covenant of
grace, then the way of saving sinners through faith in Christ would in some
manner be known among the Gentiles to whom the gospel has not yet been
promulgated. But this is false. Therefore, and so forth. The minor premise is
conceded, because the gospel is called a mystery, and a mystery hidden from
the Gentiles, in Romans 16:25, Acts 14:16, Ephesians 1:9, and elsewhere. The
major premise is entirely true, because the moral law is not wholly unknown
to them. Indeed, the opponents themselves assert that it is known by nature.

10. That which is the power of sin, which is the sting of death, does not
contain only the covenant of grace. But the law is the power of sin, which is
the sting of death, as Paul testifies in 1 Corinthians 15:56. Therefore, and
so forth. The major premise is certain, for the covenant of grace is not the
power of sin but rather the death of sin: it presents the remission of sins
(Jeremiah 31:31 and following; 33:8; Hebrews 8:8).

11. That which does not prescribe the evangelical way of justification does
not contain the covenant of grace alone. But the law does not prescribe the
evangelical way of justification. Therefore, and so forth. The major premise
needs no proof, for the covenant of grace is nothing other than the
evangelical way of justifying. The minor premise is proved by the law's
declaration that the person who does the works of the law will live
(Leviticus 18:5; Ezekiel 20:11; Romans 10:5; Galatians 3:12). This, however,
is not the evangelical but the legal way of justification.

12. [Dr. Burmann says in *Synopsis*, book 2, chapter 12, section 6:] *Now that
the covenant of works has been violated, the law which previously ought to
have procured for man friendship with God and life has become a sentence of
death and condemnation* (Romans 7:10). Therefore that law does not contain
the pure and simple covenant of grace and nothing else.

13. That which is the ministry of death and condemnation does not contain the
pure and simple

[Printed p. 169 | IA PDF 177 | IA leaf 177 | IA decausadei02brow]

covenant of grace, as is plain, for the covenant of grace is the ministry of
life, Spirit, and righteousness (2 Corinthians 3:6, 8–9). But the law is such
a ministry, as Paul testifies in verse 7 of the passage just cited. Therefore,
the law does not contain the pure and simple covenant of grace.

12. If the law contains nothing but the covenant of grace, the Apostle would
not have said in Galatians 3:10 that all who are of the works of the law are
under a curse, as is plain.

13. The covenant of grace is a testament (Matthew 26:28; Hebrews 8:10, 12;
9:15–16). But the law does not contain this testament. For this testament,
according to Burmann's definition in the place cited above, book 2, chapter
13, section 11, is *that eternal purpose of God, or immutable will concerning
the giving of his inheritance, or concerning the appointment of heirs of his
goods—both Christ and the seed given to him.* The Decalogue neither teaches
nor contains this, as is evident. Does the Decalogue contain that twofold will
of God which Burmann says is here in the same place? First, *that will by which
he willed his Son to be heir of all things*—Hebrews 1:2, *whom he appointed
heir of all things*; Isaiah 53:10, *When his soul shall make his “anor,” his
satisfaction, he will see his seed*; and Zechariah 6:13, *And the counsel of
peace was between them both*—the Lord and the man Tsemach, that is, the
Branch. Second, does it contain *that will by which he willed certain people
to be heirs of Christ, to whom by his death he bequeathed this heavenly
inheritance*? Is there, I ask, any mention here in the Decalogue of these two
wills? If not, how will it contain this testament? Is there any mention here
of the Testator, or of the goods of this testament: namely, reconciliation,
adoption, justification, and eternal life?

14. Dr. Burmann himself says in the place cited above, book 4, chapter 5,
section 17, that *with respect to the restored state, the law drives man to
Christ by its threats, terrors, and curses* (Romans 10:4). [He says:] *This
pedagogy of the law is beyond its nature, which is fitted only to produce the
knowledge of sin and despair.* It is consequently evident that the commands
of this law do not contain the pure and simple covenant of grace. For it is
not beyond the nature of the covenant of grace to entice or drive people to
Christ. Indeed, it is a means fitted to lead people gently to him and is
entirely framed for this end. It has no other nature, nor can it be said to
produce despair. On the contrary, it is framed to raise the despairing soul
and comfort it with the hope of obtaining salvation. Indeed, it directly
shows the way to prevent and remove despair and is not by its nature fitted
to produce only the knowledge of sin.

15. That which does not contain the promise does not contain the covenant of
grace. But the law does not contain the promise. Therefore, and so forth. The
reason for the major premise is that the covenant of grace is called the
promise in Galatians 3:17. The minor premise is certain from Galatians 3:18
and Acts 13:39. This well-known saying also expresses the point: *The law as
law only commands; it does not promise. It orders but does not help; requires
but does not give; burdens but does not strengthen. The gospel, however, both
commands and promises, both helps and strengthens, and both requires and
gives what it requires.*

16. The words of Dr. Burmann examined above, which occur in book 4, chapter
25, section 2, sufficiently refute this opinion. [He says: *Just as that
administration of the Old Testament differs from the Old Testament itself,
of which it was merely an adjunct, so the law and the covenant of works
itself differ widely from that legal administration. The latter was nothing
other than a certain administration of the covenant of grace, under which
the law of works was repeated and the law was mixed into the administration
of grace as a pedagogue.*] From this we can infer the following points. 1.
The law and the covenant of works are synonymous and

[Printed p. 170 | IA PDF 178 | IA leaf 178 | IA decausadei02brow]

are taken for the same thing, but the covenant of works does not contain the
covenant of grace. Therefore, neither does the law. 2. In that administration
the law of works, or covenant of works, was repeated in no other way than by
the repetition of the law itself. Therefore, if the covenant of works was
repeated by the repetition of the law, the law did not contain the covenant
of grace. 3. If the law was mixed into the dispensation of grace as a
pedagogue, the law did not contain the covenant of grace. For if it contained
the covenant of grace, it would not have been mixed into the dispensation of
the covenant of grace as a pedagogue; it would have been the very
administration of the covenant of grace itself, not something added or
annexed to the dispensation of the covenant of grace.

17. If the law contained the covenant of grace, we would be justified through
the law, because we are justified through the covenant of grace. But this is
false, as is plain. The connection of the major premise cannot be denied.

18. If the law contained the covenant of grace, it would not be opposed to
Christ in the way we see it opposed in Galatians 2:21 and Romans 10:3–4,
because Christ and the covenant of grace are not opposed.

19. Nor would the law be opposed to faith as we see it opposed in Galatians
3:2, 5, 11–12; 2:16, 24; and Romans 9:32. For the covenant of grace is not
opposed to faith.

20. If the law contained the covenant of grace, Christ could be called the
surety of the law, because he is the surety of the covenant of grace. But
this cannot be said. Therefore, and so forth.

21. If the law contained the pure and simple covenant of grace, and nothing
other than the covenant of grace, the Apostle would never have denied that
the inheritance is from the law, because the inheritance is from the promise
or covenant of grace. Yet he denies this in Galatians 3:18.

22. Nor would he have denied that a law could be given which was able to give
life, for the promise or covenant of grace gives life. Yet he denies this in
Galatians 3:21.

23. If the law contained the covenant of grace and nothing else, how were the
faithful guarded under the protection of the law, shut up unto the faith
which was to be revealed, as the Apostle speaks in Galatians 3:23?

24. If the law contained nothing but the covenant of grace, we would not now,
even after faith has come, have been freed from the law, for we have not been
freed from the covenant of grace. Yet Paul says in Galatians 3:24–25, *And so
the law was our pedagogue*—*but after faith came, we are no longer under a
pedagogue.*

25. No absolute promise stands in the Decalogue. Therefore, it does not
contain the covenant of grace, for the covenant of grace has many absolute
promises, such as the promises of a new heart, faith, repentance, and so
forth. Therefore, and so forth.

26. That which does not propose faith as the condition of the covenant of
grace but says, *Whoever does these things will live by them*, does not
contain the pure and simple covenant of grace. But the law does not propose
faith as the condition of the covenant. Rather, it proposes as the condition,
*Whoever does these things will live by them* (Romans 10:5; Galatians 3:12).
Therefore, and so forth. The major premise cannot be denied.

27. Dr. Burmann himself says in *Synopsis*, book 4, chapter 26, section 5,
that *the Israelites had some things from the fathers and others from Moses*
(John 7:22): *from the fathers, the promise of grace and through it remission
of sins and eternal life; from Moses, the law and, when it had been violated,
the yoke of ceremonies.* In section 6 he says: *And thus a twofold ministry of
Moses can be distinguished: one which he shared with the fathers—namely, to
repeat the promises given to the fathers—and another peculiar to
himself—namely, the ministry of the law*, and so forth. It is consequently
evident that the Decalogue, which belonged to Moses' ministry and is called
the law, is distinguished from the promise

[Printed p. 171 | IA PDF 179 | IA leaf 179 | IA decausadei02brow]

of grace and does not contain remission of sins, and therefore does not
contain the pure and simple covenant of grace. Thus in section 9 he says,
*And since law and grace were thus mixed, and death and life, curse and
blessing, were heard together*, and so forth. From this it also appears that
death and the curse belong to the law, just as life and blessing belong to
grace. Therefore, the Decalogue, from which nothing besides death and the
curse was heard, does not contain the covenant of grace alone.

28. This law is everywhere opposed to the promises of the new covenant
concerning the free remission of sins. Therefore, it does not contain the
covenant of grace.

29. If the Decalogue contains nothing other than the covenant of grace, it
cannot be denied that the fathers under the Old Testament obtained remission
of sins, and so forth, which the opponents nevertheless deny. Nor can it be
said that anything in the Decalogue is contrary to the repose of the New
Testament, which the opponents nevertheless say. Nor can it be said that any
of the precepts of the Decalogue has been abrogated. Therefore, the fourth has
not been abrogated, for the covenant of grace now remains in force. Yet they
assert that it has. This argument is valid *ad hominem*.

30. This law embraces things to be done and omitted, with a promise of
salvation and a threat of a curse, and it enjoins works. It consequently
presents the righteousness which arises from works (Romans 10:4–5), not that
which is from faith (Philippians 3:9). Therefore, it does not contain the pure
and simple covenant of grace. For the law of faith and the law of works are
opposed, as are work and grace (Romans 3:27; 11:6), faith and law (Romans
3:31), and grace and debt (Romans 4:3–4).
