## Part XVII: An Exposition of *Katartizein* in Romans 9:22

An exposition of the verb *katartizein* in Paul, Romans 9:22

But to engage our adversary more closely, I acknowledge an argument rashly seized from Chrysostom because Paul, speaking of vessels of wrath, describes them with the passive word *katērtismena*, that is, fitted together. “They were made and prepared not by God,” says Dr. Andreae, “but by the devil and their own will, and for this reason were destined by God’s just judgment to eternal condemnation.” I certainly admit that sins, which are the true causes of condemnation, arise from no other source than people’s own fault and from Satan inflaming their desires. But what does this contribute to the point at issue?

We do not dispute the intermediate causes subordinated to God’s eternal decree concerning the execution of either election or reprobation, nor their outcome, since these matters are abundantly clear—partly from God’s Word in the case of the elect and partly also from conscience itself in the reprobate. Rather, we inquire about the decree itself, which precedes all those causes and their effects in order. We say its cause lies in God’s own bosom and secret. When the Apostle reached it, as Augustine says in *On the Predestination of the Saints* 8, “when it had been said, ‘Why does he still complain?’ was Paul’s answer, ‘O man, what you said is false’? By no means; rather the answer was, ‘O man, who are you?’” And in *Sermons on the Words of the Apostle* 20: “Paul rested because he found wonder.” But where would wonder be if, as these men think, foreseen unbelief were the cause both of the condemnation of those who perish and of the decree concerning their condemnation? “He calls God’s judgments unsearchable, and have you come to search them? He says his ways cannot be traced, and do you trace them?”

But come, let us also see how futile and empty that argument is. Paul uses the passive word *katērtismena*. As I said before, I do not deny that every evil arises from Satan and man himself, not from God. Yet as regards God, who also orders well things that are evil in themselves, it is good that evils too should exist, and that God should justly use sinful instruments. But if God must therefore be excluded from this *fitting*, why not also from the causes of our—

—salvation? For passive words are also used of us: *prepared beforehand, called, chosen,* and *glorified*. The foundation of that inference is therefore empty.

Nor am I unaware that Augustine often understands by the mass of clay the human race not only as already created but also as corrupted by original sin, from which, by God’s will, are born those upon whom he has mercy and those whom he justly hardens. But if that interpretation holds, the cause of destination to wrath would be as well known as the cause of destination to mercy, which the same relation of contraries also demands. Why, then, would the Apostle cry, “O depth,” if it were so?

Next, the verb *katartizein* is applied to the likeness of a potter, who constructs vessels both for honor and for dishonor, for each end proposed to him. Who does not see that it refers not to intermediate causes subordinated to the decree’s execution, inhering in those condemned rather than in God, but to the decree of God the maker, which precedes all those causes in order, with a manifest allusion to the creation of the human race in Adam? Moreover, the Apostle does not say that these vessels were fitted “for sin,” but fitted together “for wrath and destruction,” that is, created and established. This is certainly not any intermediate cause by which man himself is author of his own destruction, but the final end proposed to the Creator.

What more? In the preceding verse does not the Apostle himself, comparing the maker of the human race to a potter, use the active verb *epoiēsen*, “made,” concerning vessels prepared both for dishonor and for honor? Finally, let Solomon be the best interpreter of this passage for us. He uses the verb *to create* and speaks of “the day of evil,” by which nothing other than that destruction is declared, when he says that God created all things for himself, even the ungodly for the evil day (Proverbs 16:4).

Speaking of this, Augustine says in Letter 109: “Although God did not make sins, who but God CREATED the natures themselves, which in themselves are undoubtedly good, though faults of sins would arise in them by the choice of the will, and in many such faults as would require eternal punishment? Why, except because he willed? But why did he will? O man, who are you to answer back to God?” And in *Against Julian* 5.3: “God finds material for his wrath in everyone except those whom he freely chose. The other mortals who are not of this number, though from the very same mass as they, were MADE VESSELS OF WRATH and are born for the usefulness of the former. For God CREATES none of them rashly or by chance, nor is he ignorant what good he works from them, since he works even this good—that he creates human nature in them and adorns the order of the age from them.”
