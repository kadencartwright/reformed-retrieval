CHAPTER II

On Solitary Worship

Above we distinguished worship into *solitary and social*. We will say some
things about social worship later; some points concerning *solitary worship*
must first be set out here.

We subdivided solitary worship into *ejaculatory and solemn*. Some things must
be said here about each, beginning with *ejaculatory* worship.

Even nature now corrupted teaches every nation that *God exists*, for this is
τὸ γνωστὸν τοῦ Θεοῦ, “what may be known of God” (Romans 1:19). It is manifest
among them—so manifest that the most wicked of human beings cannot be ignorant
that he is a just judge (verse 32). Experience also teaches that every nation
and people, however barbarous and savage, is touched by reverence for some
divine power. No one who believes that God exists will deny that this God must
be worshiped religiously by every rational creature. For every consideration
persuades us that God, who created everything from nothing, should be worshiped
by rational creatures who understand what it is to worship. Even the impious
and abominable idolatrous madness and superstition which rages among the
nations confirms this. But this is so clearly established that it would be
pointless to prove it.

Nor will Christians deny that it is our moral duty at every time, so far as it
can be done, to acknowledge and attest God’s dominion and our subjection to and
dependence upon him. Since this cannot be done in a solemn manner because of
the other business to which we are called, it is just and agreeable to reason
that, so far as it can be done, we declare in an ejaculatory manner God’s
primacy and independence

[1674 logical printed p. 243 | IA leaf 261 | PDF p. 261]

and our dependence, subjection, and homage. This can be done either *in the
mind alone* or also *with the outward voice*, when by sincere, pious, and
spiritual thought or speech we lift our heart to God, devote ourselves to him,
present our supplications, seek light in distress, give thanks for a benefit
received, and adore his wisdom, faithfulness, care, omnipotence, and his other
attributes which appear in the works of creation or providence and are set
before us to behold and observe, and so forth. We can do this in the midst of
our labors and the particular exercises of our calling, especially mechanical
ones in which a person’s mind and understanding are not always equally
seriously occupied, and also during the other labors of scholars, after some
pause or interruption.

Right reason dictates that this is our duty, and those positive precepts which
bind *always*, though not *at every moment*, confirm it. Such are: “Pray
without ceasing” (1 Thessalonians 5:17; Luke 18:1); “Rejoice always” (1
Thessalonians 5:16; Philippians 4:4); “Pray with every prayer” (Ephesians
6:18); “Give thanks in everything” (1 Thessalonians 5:18); and “Trust in him at
all times” (Psalm 62:8). There is also the example of the saints in Psalm 34:1:
“I will bless Jehovah at all times.” See also Psalm 142:1–2. General precepts
such as “You shall love God with your whole heart and with your whole soul,”
and the like, abundantly show the same thing. All these and similar precepts
require at least this: whenever our other most pressing business permits, we
must acknowledge God our supreme Lord, either with the mind alone or with the
mind and mouth, celebrate his virtues, truly and sincerely attest our
dependence upon him in everything, and address him as if by an apostrophe, as
Jacob did in Genesis 49:18 and Nehemiah in chapter 13, verses 13, 14, 21, and
22. This exercise is most pleasing to God, because in this way we attest our
belief that he is Spirit, that he knows our heart’s most secret thoughts,
searches hearts, and knows well what the spirit understands (Romans 8:27); and
that he is omnipresent and omniscient, so that nothing is hidden from him, not
even the most inward thoughts of our heart. In this way the soul breathes
toward God. Here appears the Spirit of God’s Son, whom the Lord sends into the
hearts of his people, crying, *Abba, Father* (Galatians 4:6). In this way we are
made fit to perform public worship, for this sudden and ejaculatory devotion
lifts the heart to God and brings it into a spiritual disposition. It is a
mark of a spiritual soul occupied spiritually, whether in contemplating the
work of creation, as is clear from Psalm 8:3–4; meditating upon God’s immense
benefits conferred upon ourselves or upon God’s church; considering God’s
judgments exercised against the church’s enemies; or the like.

Besides this worship there is another to which every consideration and
religion persuades us, which we call *solemn*, although it too is solitary. We
call it solemn because here a person presents his whole self to God, both body
and soul, and withdraws himself from every other worldly business in order to
devote himself to God and his worship, even if this is done in the greatest
privacy.

That even natural reason requires this duty of us is taught by the practice of
the nations, which invoke their gods in dangers, and by individual persons who
do this in solitude. We have an instance of this in the prophet Jonah, chapter
2, verse 5. Every consideration also requires that God, who is the author of
our life and the creator of body and soul, should be celebrated by both body
and soul. Indeed, right reason will teach us that a human being was created
endowed with reason in order to glorify God, and thus that it is most just

[1674 logical printed p. 244 | IA leaf 262 | PDF p. 262]

that each person solemnly acknowledge God as his creator, preserver,
benefactor, and supreme good, and adore and worship him as solemnly as he can.

God’s supreme, independent, and absolute dominion necessarily requires this.
For if he is the supreme Lord of all, he must be acknowledged as such by all
who are endowed with reason, and everyone is bound to render solemn homage to
him. What earthly lord would not require such a thing from his servants or
subjects?

Our dependence upon him with respect to both body and soul also demands this.
For we possess nothing, whether pertaining to soul or body, which we did not
receive from him. Is it not therefore just that we solemnly attest this with
both body and soul? Gratitude will certainly require at least this from us.

Indeed, this is supremely necessary, for we must cultivate communion with God.
Without this solemn worship and approach to God in both body and soul, we will
have scarcely any communion with him. Ejaculatory prayers, however effective,
pass quickly. We are soon submerged in other earthly business and at once lose
the good impression because of our corruption; very soon we are excessively
drawn away by distractions and return to our former condition.

The precepts named above, whether general or particular, likewise require this.
“But you,” Christ says in Matthew 6:6, “when you pray, enter your chamber, and
having shut your door, pray to your Father in secret,” and so forth. A solemn
duty of individuals is therefore prescribed here. See Aretius on this passage.
I do not say that Christ requires nothing else here than that individuals pray
solemnly to God in their chamber, but it is certain that Christ requires this
duty among others.

The example of the saints and other examples recorded in Scripture demonstrate
this. See Daniel 6:10, 13; chapter 9 throughout; Psalms 5:3; 59:16; 88:9, 13;
116:17, 4; 18:6; 118:5; 55:17; and Genesis 24:63. Indeed, Christ himself taught
us this by his example (Mark 1:35; Luke 5:12–15, compared with verse 16; 6:12–13;
Matthew 14:22–23; Luke 21:27; 22:41, 44–45). And God promised it in Zechariah
13:10–13.

Moreover, no one will deny that human societies are bound to worship God
publicly and solemnly, and that each person in some society is obliged, when
occasion is given, to join in celebrating God’s praises. But suppose there is
no society with which someone can worship God. Will he therefore not be bound
to honor God solemnly? When he is alone does he depend less upon God, the
supreme creator and preserver of all, than when he is joined with others? Why,
then, will nature not teach us this duty?

Nor will anyone deny that each person in this fallen state, even if separated
from others, is bound to seek his happiness in God. It is therefore necessary
that each person, even in a solitary state, seek his salvation and communion
with God through this means, which is necessary in itself and by nature fitted
to attain that end.

Indeed, if this is not conceded, it will follow that at least some people are
exempt from all solemn worship, because it can happen that someone is unable
to venerate God with any society, whether in a household, in a church, or in
any other way—for example, if he happens to live alone among barbarians or

[1674 logical printed p. 245 | IA leaf 263 | PDF p. 263]

idolaters. If, therefore, no solemn worship is moral and natural except that performed by
a public society, the law of nature requires no solemn worship from a solitary
person. This is absurd. We must therefore say that the law of nature requires
solemn solitary worship. Indeed, this worship appears more moral and necessary
than the other because it is more possible and can always be practiced. What,
has God not given soul and body, tongue and heart to a solitary person just as
to one joined with others? Does the law of nature not therefore require each
person to acknowledge his benefactor, recognize his dependence upon him,
celebrate his God with heart and mouth, confess his sins to him, supplicantly
seek his help, humble himself before him, and expect good from him, and thus be
rendered morally more capable of receiving God’s benefits? No sound person
will deny this.

Moreover, no Christian is ignorant that he has particular causes or occasions
for approaching God: particular benefits received which require thanksgiving,
particular sins which must be acknowledged, particular evils which must be
subdued (1 Kings 8:38–39), help which must also be sought, and the like. All
these demonstrate this to be everyone’s duty: namely, to worship God in
solitude and solemnly. And most true Christians, if not all, have experienced
God’s intimate presence and communion even in their chamber. See Genesis
32:23–24, 28, 30, compared with Hosea 12:3–4. This is indeed a particular
manner of walking with God and a special part of our πολίτευμα, our heavenly
citizenship (Ephesians 2:18–19; Philippians 3:20).

Experience also teaches that those who neglect this manner of worship are
overwhelmed by many evils and temptations, are lukewarm, and are for the most
part destitute of the spirit of supplication, and so forth. Conversely, those
who apply themselves to this business are accepted by God, obtain many good
things, and are heard by God. See 2 Chronicles 33:11–13; Psalm 6:6–9; 142:1–2;
Genesis 32:27–28; Daniel 9:20, 22; Jeremiah 33:1–2; Acts 10:9–11; Exodus
32:10–13; Deuteronomy 9:13–14, 18, 25; Job 14:13; Matthew 7:7.

Perhaps no one except atheists, profane people, and worthless persons of that
kind will doubt this solitary worship. Together with those carnal pigs from
the herd of Epicurus, they say to the mighty God in Job 21:14–15, “Depart from
us, for we do not delight in the knowledge of your ways. What is the Almighty,
that we should worship him? Or what will we gain when we intercede with him?”
See also Psalms 53:4; 76:6. But perhaps some will deny that this worship should
be called solemn. Yet for what reason? Is this worship not distinguished from
ejaculatory worship, and is it not more solemn? Is not the whole person
occupied in it? Are those who worship God in this way not withdrawn entirely
from other earthly business and intent upon this for some period? What else
can be required to render worship solemn? When Daniel in chapter 9 set his face
toward the Lord God, seeking him by prayer and supplications with fasting,
sackcloth, and ashes (verse 3), he was undoubtedly worshiping God solemnly.
David did likewise when, evening, morning, and noon, he meditated and cried
aloud until his voice was heard (Psalm 55:18).

It can therefore be gathered that not all solemn worship is public and that
some private worship can be called solemn. And since the adversaries admit
that all solemn worship requires solemn time, it follows that some solemn time
is also owed to this solemn solitary worship.

[1674 logical printed p. 246 | IA leaf 264 | PDF p. 264]

From this one may conclude that the adversaries stray far when they maintain
that nothing in the fourth precept is moral except what pertains to the public
worship of God—that is, ecclesiastical worship, or worship performed in an
ecclesiastical assembly. They thereby exclude not only this solitary worship
but domestic worship as well, and all the time necessary for them. For they
say that the moral content of the fourth precept is the public worship of God,
and consequently some time necessary for celebrating the public worship of
God, because, they say, it is agreeable to the law of nature that some time be
appointed for the public worship of God. And the fourth precept is moral in
this respect: that time be established for celebrating God’s worship. But is
it not equally agreeable to the law of nature that some time be established
for the private worship of God? And will this not likewise be a moral aspect
of the fourth precept? We will say more about this later in its place; let it
suffice to have noted it here in passing.
