CHAPTER SIX

ON ORIGINAL SIN

ROMANS 5:12

“Therefore, just as through one human being sin entered into the world, and
through sin death, so death spread to all human beings, in whom all sinned.”

This passage is illustrious and striking, if any is, for confirming the
doctrine of the Reformed churches concerning original sin. Hence:

It is asked whether there is in the human being a sin properly so called which
they call original, subjecting the human being to eternal death and
condemnation; or whether it is only sin improperly so called and merely an
infirmity,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 311 | PDF p. 362]

or a weakness and disease of nature, subjecting the human being neither to
punishment, nor to guilt properly so called, nor to the wrath of God. The
Remonstrants, together with their Pelagians and Socinians, say that no such sin
properly so called exists. Their *Apology*, chapter 7, folio 64, says: “It is
contrary to the nature of sin that something should be deemed sin, or properly
imputed as sin, which was not committed by one's own will.” Hence in the
*Censure of the Leiden Professors*, folio 87, they say “that no one is
condemned on account of this sin.” And there, together with Jacob Arminius and
Corvinus, they teach “that it conflicts with the divine mercy, goodness, and
justice for tender infants to be sentenced to eternal torments merely on
account of that sin which they never committed in their own person and for
which Adam, its first author, obtained pardon. For thus God would deal much
more severely with innocent infants and human beings than with the apostate
angels, who by no means pay the penalties of another's sin.”

We, on the contrary, maintain—whatever insane reason may say against it—that
all mortals, with the sole exception of the man Christ, sinned in Adam and
therefore are born in sin, and from the womb are subject both to temporal and
to eternal death.

1. For David says in Psalm 51:5, “I was formed in iniquity; in sin my mother
conceived me.” The first Hebrew word is חוֹלָלְתִּי, which means “to be
affected by the pains of childbirth”; the other is יֶחֱמַתְנִי, “my mother was
made warm with me.” Tremellius has, “my mother cherished me.” Chemnitz says
that David “does not subtly inquire here into”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 312 | PDF p. 363]

“the nature of voluntariness.” The Septuagint renders it ἐκίσσησε, “she
conceived.” Add Job 14:4: “Who will bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not
even one.” Job 15:14: “What is a human being, that he should be spotless? And
that one born of a woman should appear righteous?” Genesis 6:5: “Every
formation and thought of the human heart is only evil from childhood.”
Ephesians 2:3: “And we were by nature children of wrath, just like the rest.”
John 3:6: “What is born of the flesh is flesh.” Romans 5:12: “In Adam all
sinned.” Verse 15: “By that one man's offense many died.” Verse 18:
“Therefore, just as through one offense guilt came upon all human beings unto
condemnation,” and so forth. Verse 19: “For just as through the disobedience of
one human being many were constituted sinners,” and so forth.

2. Infants die and undergo death, the wages of sin. Therefore, they are
sinners. Nor is death a chastisement in them, as the Remonstrants maintain,
since none are capable of chastisement properly so called except those who
possess the use of reason.

3. Infants were formerly circumcised and now are baptized for the remission of
sins. Therefore, they were born in sin.

4. The Pelagians formerly taught the same things concerning original sin:

1. “Adam's sin harmed no one except himself, nor is the human being now born
worse than he was created.”

2. “Christ died to expiate sins committed in imitation of Adam.”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 313 | PDF p. 364]

3. “We are sinners and are born such in Adam by imitation, not by nature.” The
Arminians and Socinians teach precisely the same things.

It is asked whether, because original sin was not committed by a personal
will, it is therefore not sin properly so called. The Arminians say that by
this very fact it is not sin properly so called. We maintain the contrary.

1. This sin is voluntary by the will of nature, not of the person; by a
representative will, not one's own.

2. Opposition to God's law is more essential to sin than voluntariness is. Nor
is voluntariness essential to sin in general, because the soul's powers,
propensities, and inclinations—which precede the voluntary—are stained by sin,
although the nature of voluntariness has no place in them.

3. Scripture says that we all sinned in Adam and are constituted sinners,
Romans 5:12, 19. Nevertheless, our personal and physically individual will was
not in Adam.

4. Nor is the nature of voluntariness formally in the arm that kills a human
being, and yet it is punished for the killing. Nevertheless, our will, though
not physically, was legally and morally in Adam by a special covenant and
God's free imputation, so that such sin is imputable according to that
covenant.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 314 | PDF p. 365]

It is asked whether such a covenant can be proved, by which Adam's sin is
imputed to all his posterity. The Remonstrants deny it. We prove it.

1. Scripture says more than once that “we are born in sin; sinned in Adam; and
through the offense of one are constituted sinners.” But this was not done by
nature. Therefore, it was by covenant and God's special law.

2. Where there is a promise and an inflicted threat, there is a covenant and
compact. But these declarations were made to Adam and his posterity: “the one
who does this will live,” and “on the day you eat, you will die.” Therefore,
there was a covenant and compact. For if the same penalty is inflicted upon us
that was inflicted upon Adam, then the same legal covenant which bound him
also binds us.

It is asked whether concupiscence is sin, especially after baptism and
regeneration. The Remonstrants say “that it existed in the human being before
the fall and is not sin in itself.” And after regeneration, in the regenerate,
the Roman Catholics and Arminians say that “it is sin only metonymically,
effectively, and improperly.”

We maintain, on the contrary, that both in itself and after regeneration it is
univocally and properly called sin.

1. For Paul, when deliberately explaining the nature of concupiscence,
designates it by the name of sin fourteen times in Romans chapters 6, 7, and 8.
And one must not depart from the proper sense of words where the nature and
proper attributes of a thing are being explained.

2. The definition of sin which the apostle

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 315 | PDF p. 366]

assigns in 1 John 3:4 applies to it. For it is a transgression forbidden by
God's law. Romans 7:7: “I would not
have known concupiscence unless the law had said, ‘You shall not covet.’”

3. Concupiscence is something God hates, because Paul, according to the inner
human being and his renewed part, hated it, Romans 7.

4. Concupiscence must be mortified and crucified; it must not be obeyed but
vigorously resisted, Romans 6:13–14; Colossians 3:5.

5. Concupiscence is not from God, according to the testimony of the apostle
John, and it is something evil, as the adversaries confess. But if it is only
the evil of punishment, it will be from God as a good work of justice.
Therefore, it will necessarily be the evil of fault.

6. Concupiscence desires against God's Spirit in us, Romans 7; Galatians 5.

7. It is “the surrounding evil which we are commanded to lay aside,” Hebrews
12:1.

8. It makes Paul “carnal and enslaved to sin” and “works every
concupiscence,” Romans 7:11; it deceived and killed him, verse 13. It is “sin
appearing to be sin,” verse 17; “sin dwelling in Paul,” verse 18. It is “not
good,” and that which hinders the performance of the good which God's law
commands and God's Spirit in Paul approves as good. Paul describes this evil
both privatively and positively in verse 14: “I do not find how to accomplish
the good”; verse 20, “sin dwelling in Paul”; verse 21, “present evil”; verse
23, “a law”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 316 | PDF p. 367]

“in the members, rebelling against the law of the mind and leading Paul captive
to sin.” Finally, it is an evil which makes Paul morally wretched, verse 25;
and, when delivered from its tyranny, he gives thanks to God through Jesus
Christ. Its wisdom is hostility against God and cannot be subjected to God's
law, Romans 8:7; and 6:6, 7, 11, 14, 16, 18.

It is asked whether concupiscence is formally prohibited by God's law. The
Remonstrants deny it. The Roman Catholics say that the commandment “You shall
not covet” binds us to the means—so that we do not yield to concupiscences—but
not to the end, that we should root them out entirely, because this is not in
our power. They maintain that they are prohibited so that it might be clear,
not what we must perform from obligation, but what must be desired by wish
and pious effort; and therefore that it is forbidden by way of instruction so
that we may be admonished where we ought to hasten. Thus Bellarmine, *On the
Loss of Grace*, book 5, chapter 10; Stapleton, volume 2, book 3, chapter 5;
Becanus, part 2, tractate 5, section 5, question 3, chapter 9. We teach the
contrary.

1. For it is an axiom consonant with Scripture, reason, councils, and the
fathers, and received by all the learned: “Whatever God commands, we are bound
to perform.” For a command essentially reveals that God wills us to be bound
to perform it.

2. It is false that we are not bound to the end of the law because the end is
impossible. For in that case we would not be bound to love God above all things
and to be

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 317 | PDF p. 368]

perfect and holy as our heavenly Father is, although this is commanded, 1 Peter
1:18; Matthew 5:48.

3. “The law is spiritual and requires people who are altogether spiritual, so
that it may be observed perfectly even with respect to its end,” as Bellarmine
says in the same place. It is false, therefore, that we are not guilty if we do
not attain the end of the law.

4. If not everything the law forbids is an evil which we are bound to flee,
then, by parity of reasoning, not everything the law commands is a good which
we are bound to do.

5. A moral law which does not bind to obedience is both law and non-law, which
is a contradiction.

6. If it is prohibited only for the sake of instruction, God could have
commanded in the Decalogue that we should know the nature of Cassiopeia and of
the polar stars in the same way that he wills us to know concupiscence; yet in
that case this knowledge of the stars would have bound us.

7. If concupiscence is prohibited only as a defect of nature arising from the
condition of matter, the law, with respect to its end, would forbid a living
and animal nature to be in us. That is as absurd as God commanding that a
human being should not be an animal and living.

8. Augustine, *On Marriage and Concupiscence*, book 1, chapter 29: “The one who
does what is written, ‘Do not go after concupiscences,’ does much good; but he
does not complete it, because he does not fulfill what is written.”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 318 | PDF p. 369]

Augustine therefore thinks that concupiscence is prohibited by the tenth
commandment.

It is asked whether concupiscence is sin when the consent of the will does not
accompany it. The adversaries teach that in this case it is not sin. But we
maintain the following. One first-first motion is purely intellectual in the
mind, such as knowing the devil's temptations speculatively; this is not sin,
for it was in Christ. Another is a first-first motion in the appetite, and
another also a second-first motion. Each presupposes some cognition and
semi-consent, or consent that is begun and imperfect, and this is sin.

1. For this motion is contrary to the most intense habit of loving God which
the law requires.

2. It is prohibited by the tenth commandment. For Paul says that it is “sin
and an evil leading him captive and enslaving him to sin,” although he did not
give it consent, Romans 7:15, 16, 19, 20. For Paul says of those motions, “I do
not do what I will.”

3. God's law requires perfect conformity between the will and all the
inclinations of the sensitive and rational appetite which precede the consent
of the will, and between the will and God's will revealed in the law. For the
inclinations toward vengeance, fornications, drunkenness, heresies, and
vainglory, which the saints experience in themselves with holy grief, are
culpable by this very fact: the Holy Spirit causes us to grieve over such
things, even if we

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 319 | PDF p. 370]

never give them consent.

It is asked whether the struggle between flesh and Spirit which is in the
regenerate is wholly natural and, on the part of the resisting flesh, not at
all culpable. The Remonstrants say it is not culpable and is merely the
struggle of the lower appetite against reason. We deny this.

1. For Paul laments such a struggle in Romans 7, on account of which he says
that “he is carnal and sold under sin,” verse 14; that “nothing good dwells in
his flesh,” verse 18; that “he is hindered and held back from performing
good,” verses 19–20; and that “he is led captive to the law of sin,” verse 23.
But Paul would wrongly lament his condition if this struggle were the
Creator's natural work. For he would thus be spewing complaints and
blasphemies against the Creator rather than piously mourning the state of sin.

2. He would complain about a wholly natural and not at all culpable struggle
as foolishly as if someone poured out complaints against nature because heavy
things tend downward and light things upward.

3. In vain would Paul pray to be delivered from that struggle and give God
thanks for deliverance through Christ, verses 24–25, because “there is no
condemnation for those who are in Christ,” Romans 8:1, if this struggle were
wholly natural.

It is asked whether this struggle is always in the regenerate as long as they
live here. In their *Confession*, chapter 11, section 11, the Remonstrants,
together with their Socinus on Romans 7, pages 54–55, teach “that some are so
perfect that”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 320 | PDF p. 371]

“they are wholly free from this struggle; and that such people sometimes sin,
not from culpable innate concupiscence, but perhaps through some error,
ignorance, surprise, and some clouding of the mind.” We teach the contrary.

1. For if any such people existed in this life, it would not be necessary for
them to pray, “Forgive us our sins,” and they could say that they had no sin,
which the apostle John says is a lie, 1 John 1:8.

2. Such people would be more perfect than Paul, the chosen vessel, in whom
this struggle was perpetual, and than David, a man according to God's heart,
who did not merely fall into adultery and murder from a clouding of the mind,
but from this bitter principle, Psalm 51:1: “My mother begot me in sin.”

3. Once the acquired habit of sinning had been cast off, as they invent, there
would be no sinful principle in these perfect human beings other than there
was in Adam before the fall. For take away concupiscence and reveal God's will
to the regenerate—a revelation which the regenerate possess, 2 Corinthians
3:18; 2 Corinthians 4:6; Ephesians 1:17–18—and I ask: what was in Adam before
the fall which is not in a human being so perfectly regenerated? And so there
is no reason for them to pray, “Open the eyes of the mind,” and “Increase our
faith,” Psalm 119:18; Luke 17:5.

4. In Galatians 5:17 Paul says that this struggle remains; and in Romans 8:6,
that “what the flesh savors is death”; and in 1 Peter 2:11, that “fleshly
desires wage war against the soul.”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 321 | PDF p. 372]

5. This doctrine rests on the following hypothesis: “that this struggle
arises, not from innate concupiscence, but from an acquired habit of sinning;
and that this habit can be cast off by the perfectly regenerate so that they
are as perfect and free from every stain of sin as the holy angels.” Thus they
could say that they had cleansed their heart, contrary to Proverbs 20:9; that
they did not need a Mediator, the remission of sins, or an Advocate at the
Father's right hand; that their life was perfectly conformed to God's law in
every respect; and that they did not care even if the Lord entered into
judgment with them. All these things are absurd.

It is asked whether, because every sin is remitted in baptism, concupiscence is
therefore not sin after baptism. The Roman Catholics and Arminians teach that
no sin remains after baptism because God blots out the sins of the elect like
a cloud; casts them into the depth of the sea; buries them; purges them from
every sin; does not remember sins; and in Christ frees from every condemnation.

We teach that the guilt of sin with respect to its legal being is wholly
removed, so that the obligation to punishment and the binding force unto
condemnation are taken away. But sin remains with respect to its real being
and physical inherence. The passages alleged by the adversaries prove the
former part. We prove the latter.

1. For no one in this life is

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 322 | PDF p. 373]

pure from sins, and the one who says that he has no sin is a liar, 1 John 1:8.

2. For sin truly exists and dwells in the regenerate as long as they are in
this life, as Scripture and the experience of all the saints sufficiently
prove.

It is asked whether the remission of sin in baptism does not root out every
being of sin in the baptized. The adversaries affirm it. We say that the act
of remitting does not remove the essence of sin, but only removes sin with
respect to its actual ordering to punishment, or removes the actual obligation
to punishment, which is the second act of sin. But the essence of sin remains,
that is, the power of binding and potential guilt. Therefore Paul says in
Romans 8:1, οὐδὲν κατάκριμα, “no condemnation,” not οὐδὲν κατακριτὸν, “nothing
condemnable,” “is for those who are in Christ.”

It is asked whether actual sins take their origin, not from original sin, but
from an acquired habit of acting badly and from pure free choice. The
Arminians, together with their Socinians, affirm it. We deny it.

1. For David rightly acknowledged that his adultery flowed from the fact that
“he had been born in sins,” Psalm 51.

2. From an unclean heart proceed the things that defile a human being, Matthew
15:18; John 3:6. “What is born of flesh is flesh,” that is, it savors of a
corrupt origin.

3. For all the works of the flesh flow from the flesh itself, Galatians 5:19;
Romans 3:11, 12, 13; and 8:10.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 323 | PDF p. 374]

4. For there cannot be an acquired habit of acting badly in children, nor is
free choice in them created in a state of pure nature and free from sin, since
“every formation of the human heart is only evil from childhood,” Genesis
6:5.

5. For in every human being there is an inclination to sin, Romans 7:14, 18,
21, 23; Galatians 5:16–17; Jeremiah 17:9; Job 9:1, 23. But evil acts flow only
from evil inclinations.

6. For this doctrine supposes that a human being can be free from sin, and
that a habit of acting well can be acquired not by the infusion of grace but
by the exercise of good acts with the aid of free choice, as the Peripatetics
taught concerning the habit of moral virtue; and that human beings become
good or evil, children of God or of the devil, by the exercise and industry of
their own free choice.

7. This belongs to the Socinians, who utterly deny that Adam's first fall,
since it was only one act, could have the power to corrupt Adam's nature, much
less the nature of all his posterity. Thus they maintain that human beings
after Adam's fall are as innocent and upright as Adam himself was before the
fall. So teaches the *Racovian Catechism*, on the prophetic office of Jesus
Christ, chapter 10, folio 250; Socinus, *Theological Lectures*, chapter 4,
folio 23.

It is asked whether, because original sin is the punishment of the first sin,
it therefore cannot be sin. The Remonstrants affirm it. We deny it.

1. For the same thing is both sin and the punishment of sin,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 324 | PDF p. 375 | ends before CAPUT SEPTIMUM]

as we have proved above from the Scriptures.

2. For they concede that this sin is a punishment, which nevertheless is
contrary to their principles. For since they teach that there is no sin in
infants, in the order of justice no punishment for sin could be inflicted upon
them. Therefore, it is not only sin but also a punishment of such a kind that
it is wholly unavoidable for Adam's posterity.

3. According to their principles, punishment is involuntary. But in infants
who lack the use of reason, neither the voluntary nor the involuntary has any
place.
