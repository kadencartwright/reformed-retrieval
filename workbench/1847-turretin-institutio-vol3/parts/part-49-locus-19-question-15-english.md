QUESTION XV.

Is baptism administered by heretics valid? We distinguish.

[Marginalia: The origin of the controversy among the ancients.]

I. The question was disputed long ago in the time of Cyprian and Stephen,
bishop of Rome, with great contention of minds. Some maintained that baptism
administered by a heretical minister was lawful and valid; others denied this
and contended that such persons must be rebaptized. Cyprian and his fellow
African bishops judged that baptism administered by heretics and schismatics
was not true baptism, and therefore that those who came to the catholic faith
must be rebaptized. This is clear from Cyprian’s various epistles to Januarius,
Quintus, Stephen, Julian, and others, and from the acts of the Council of
Carthage held under Cyprian’s presidency. Yet they affirmed these things
without acting contentiously or stubbornly with anyone in their defense, but
fostered peace with dissenting brothers and colleagues. They judged no one or
removed anyone from the right of communion if he thought differently, as they
themselves say at the beginning of the council. In this they differed greatly
from the Donatists, who drew this error from them but defended it with the
greatest contention, abandoning the communion of believers who decided
differently. Thus the error became a heresy and the disagreement became a
schism. On this one should read Augustine’s books *On Baptism against the
Donatists* and *On Heresies*, to Quodvultdeus, heresy 69.

II. Stephen, bishop of Rome, however, affirmed from the ancient custom of the
catholic church that those baptized by heretics were to be received without a
new washing. This is clear from the epistolary dispute between Stephen and
Cyprian, where he explains his opinion, whose substance is: “If anyone comes
to us from any heresy, let nothing be innovated beyond what has been handed
down, namely that a hand be laid upon him for repentance.” In the following
century this question was decided at the First Council of Nicaea, canon 8,
where it is said of those who call themselves Cathars: “If at some time they
come to the catholic church, it has pleased the holy and great council that,
after receiving the laying on of hands, they should thus remain among the
clergy” (ὥστε χειροθετημένους αὐτοὺς μένειν οὕτως ἐν τῷ κλήρῳ). Hence it is
clear that that great synod regarded as valid not only baptism but also
clerical ordination received through the ministry of heretics. Nevertheless,
canon 19 excepts the Paulianists or Samosatenians, whom the synod wills “to be
baptized again altogether” (ἀναβαπτίζεσθαι ἐξ ἅπαντος), because they did not
acknowledge the Trinity. Hence it is clear that Cyprian and Stephen erred
through *immoderate pulling in opposite directions* (ἀμετρίαν τῆς ἀνθολκῆς):
the former in wishing those baptized by any heretics to be rebaptized, and the
latter in judging that none of them should be rebaptized but that all should
be received without a new baptism. Each deflected toward an extreme, but the
middle is safest here when a distinction among heretics is applied.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

III. We therefore think the question can be resolved by distinguishing among
heretics. Some corrupt the substance of baptism and omit or change the form of
institution. Such formerly were the Arians, who denied the Trinity of Persons
in the unity of essence, and such today are the Socinians. Others retain the
substantial elements and defend the true doctrine of the Holy Trinity
contained in the formula of baptism, but “err in other heads of doctrine,” as
the Novatians and Donatists did formerly and the papists and Arminians do
today. Concerning the former, we judge that baptism administered by them is
null, and thus that those who have been baptized by such heretics are not
properly to be rebaptized but

[1847 vol. III printed p. 339 | IA leaf 373 | PDF p. 373]

baptized, because they utterly corrupt the essential form of baptism, to which the
invocation of the Trinity belongs. For this reason, as was said, the baptism
of the Arians was rejected long ago in canon 19 of the Council of Nicaea, for
they baptized “in the Father, the only true God; in Jesus Christ, a savior and
creature; and in the Holy Spirit, the servant of them both,” according to
Jerome, *Against the Luciferians*, volume 3, page 66, and Athanasius, discourse
3, *Against the Arians*. Yet another distinction can be applied here. Either
the minister alone is infected with that heresy, or the whole church with him.
If the latter, we deny that it is true baptism. If the former—that is, if the
church thinks rightly despite the pastor’s error, and he is a secret
heretic—then, provided that Christ’s formula is retained, we believe the
baptism is valid and that those once baptized need not be rebaptized. The
sacrament is the possession of the church and is dispensed in the church’s
name and in its faith. Therefore, the minister’s secret error detracts nothing
from the integrity of baptism, provided that its essentials are observed and
nothing is changed in either the word or the element. Hence it is clear how to
answer the question whether baptism conferred in an orthodox church by a
minister imbued with antitrinitarian or Jewish errors, but not exposed, is
valid. The public faith of the church, into which the one baptized is
initiated, and the promises Christ made to him must be considered. Since these
do not depend upon the minister, he cannot render them null and vain, however
much he labors under a secret heresy.

IV. But if heretics retain the fundamentals of baptism that constitute its
essence and neither change nor corrupt the form, we hold that baptism
administered by such persons is valid, although they err concerning the faith
in various heads and their baptism is defiled in accidental matters by various
foreign rites.

V. The reasons are as follows. (1) The essentials remain there with respect
to both form and matter, namely the word with the element and the formula
prescribed by Christ, so that it is administered in the name of the Trinity.
(2) Neither the prophets, Christ, nor the apostles ever condemned as invalid
the circumcision performed in the Jewish church by idolatrous and heretical
priests, such as the Pharisees. (3) Zipporah’s example teaches that
circumcision defective with respect to human agents is nevertheless valid
before God. (4) We do not read of any baptized by heretics being rebaptized by
the apostles.

VI. Although heretics are not true members of the invisible church, this does
not prevent them from administering true baptism, provided they retain its
substantial elements, because in this action they merely lend God their tongue
and hand. God is the one who baptizes and is efficacious through the ministry.
Just as God can gather a church for himself from adults through a corrupt
ministry, so he can gather it from infants through baptism administered by
heretics. Although they do not belong to the orthodox church, they can
nevertheless belong to an external but impure church, and among them human
unfaithfulness does not nullify the faithfulness of God. It is not human
baptism but God’s baptism, which he sometimes wills to preserve in an impure
church, just as we see that God still preserved a remnant under Ahab in
Elijah’s time (1 Kings 19), however variously the church had been corrupted.

VII. But if it is asked whether baptism of this kind may lawfully be sought or
received in any case, the answer requires a distinction. It must be sought in
no case from heretics of the first order, who overthrow the foundations of
Christianity and baptism, because no true baptism can exist among them. But
concerning the others, who retain the essence of baptism but err ruinously in
other matters, whether doctrine or worship, although we do not judge that
their baptism must be repeated,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 340 | IA leaf 374 | PDF p. 374]

we nevertheless do not think it can be sought or received without sin. The
reception of such baptism displays our communion with them and participation
in their errors, from which Scripture commands us to flee, and we cause the
weak to stumble contrary to the apostle’s command. What has been said
generally about the baptism of heretics must be applied particularly to the
baptism of the papists. Although we judge that baptism should be retained in
those who come from them to us, because the essentials of baptism remain among
them, we nevertheless deny that it can rightly be sought or received from
them, as will be proved more fully in what follows when we treat that subject.
