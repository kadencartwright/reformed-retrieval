## A Most Complete Treatise on the Doctrine and True Use of Predestination

Taken recently by Raphael Eglinus of Zurich, a student of theology in the
Genevan school, from Theodore Beza's lectures on the ninth chapter of the
Epistle to the Romans.

### Preliminary matters

Since we have now arrived at the passage of Paul in which he discusses the
primary cause of our salvation—namely, God's eternal and immutable purpose—a
doctrine which some avoid like a reef and far too many explain incorrectly,
before we expound Paul's own context we shall first discuss the whole subject.
We shall begin by explaining certain terms in the order in which Paul listed
them in the preceding chapter and then mentions them in this chapter. Once
these are rightly understood, it will not be difficult to understand what must
be believed about this entire subject from God's Word. The terms are these:

The providence of God, and its:

Purpose (*prothesis*).

Foreknowledge (*prognōsis*).

Predestination (*proorismos*).

Election.

Reprobation.

Calling.

The fruit of calling.

Hardening.

The fruit of hardening.

The will of God.

Necessity.

Contingency.

The freedom and bondage of the human will.

#### Providence

We therefore call providence in God that most wise and excellent power of God
by which, from eternity within himself, God determined how, when, and to what
end he would create all things, universal and particular, and in what manner
he would govern them once created. From its efficacy all things, both those
already done and those yet to be done, depend. Through the intervention of
intermediate causes, they have been directed most wisely and at the same time
most rightly to their ends from the beginning of the world; they are directed
now; and they will be directed until the consummation of the whole world.

In this providence we consider purpose, foreknowledge, and predestination.
These differ in account rather than in reality, so much so that they are
sometimes taken for one and the same thing: namely, for that eternal counsel
of God which precedes all intermediate causes not only in time—for it is
eternal and therefore above all time—but also in the order of causes. Thus the
Jerusalem church in Acts 4:28 subsists in predestination, defined by “determined
counsel,” as the supreme cause; and Peter, in 1 Peter 1:2, mentions
foreknowledge as the supreme cause of our salvation. Paul, on the other hand,
not only distinctly subordinates these three in Romans 8, but also in Ephesians
1:11 ascends from predestination to purpose.

#### *Prothesis*: purpose

In these three words the prefix *pro-* denotes the eternity of this divine
counsel, which precedes all intermediate causes even in order. In the term
*prothesis*, however, “placing” designates the establishment of that counsel,
that is, its immovable stability. Although Paul applies the term *prothesis*
only to election, nevertheless, since election is not of everyone, all who are
not chosen must be understood to be rejected by the same counsel; and that
eternal counsel of God must be no less firm with respect to rejection than
with respect to election. Since the verbs “to place” and “to be placed” are
also used in both directions, as in Luke 2:34, 1 Thessalonians 5:9, and 2 Peter
2:8, I see no reason why *prothesis* should not be referred as much to those
reprobated as to those elected.

#### *Prognōsis*: foreknowledge

If in *prognōsis* the verb “to know” is understood of God's simple knowledge,
then certainly *prognōsis*, which they call foreknowledge, will have no
character of an efficient cause. For things are not future because God
foreknew them as future; on the contrary, they are future, and he foreknew them
as future, because he decreed that they would be future. Indeed, although,
properly speaking, in the divine counsels as they are in themselves there can
be neither earlier nor later, whether in time or in an ordered sequence,
nevertheless, as these things are proposed for us to consider in God, the
purpose by which future things are decreed precedes in order the foreknowledge
of their existence.

But if “to know” is taken for deciding—that is, for sanctioning and decreeing
after the cause has, as it were, been considered—then the term *prognōsis* will
signify the supreme justice and rectitude of that same counsel which we called
*prothesis*, a counsel established only after, as it were, the wisest
deliberation and a just ordering of intermediate causes. Some likewise apply
*prognōsis* only to the elect, because in a Hebrew idiom “to know” is sometimes
taken for acknowledging and regarding favorably, as when Psalm 1 says that
God knows the way of the righteous, and Matthew 7, by contrast, that he does
not know workers of iniquity. This is refuted by the fact that *prognōsis* is
also transferred to things which, just as they are entirely good with respect
to God's decree, are with respect to human beings the greatest and most
enormous crimes. Thus Acts 2:23 says that Christ was delivered up by “the
foreknowledge of God” and crucified by the hands of the ungodly.

#### *Proorismos*: predestination

Finally, the term *proorismos* signifies that same eternal counsel which we
call *prothesis* and *prognōsis*, but with respect both to the final end and to
the other intermediate ends: that is, insofar as God extends that eternal
counsel and decree, through definite stages determined by himself, toward a
fixed terminus. Hence in Acts 2:23 the word is not simply “counsel,” but
“determined.” From these words it is also clear that the opinion of those who
wish only the elect to be embraced within predestination is not sufficiently
firm. This can also be proved from Augustine's testimony. Thus he writes in
book 15, chapter 1, of *The City of God*: “We mystically call two communities
‘cities,’ that is, two societies of human beings: one predestined to reign
eternally

[Printed page 403; PDF page 3; OCR object 0418]

with God, the other to undergo eternal judgment with the devil.” Finally,
since Paul openly proceeds step by step no less from hardening than from mercy
to the will of God, and since predestination is nothing other than the
appointment of his will to a definite end, whether salvation or destruction,
it cannot rightly be denied that predestination pertains both to that twofold
final end and to the means subordinated to either end.

Let this suffice concerning God's purpose itself. Since it is directed to a
definite end, it remains for us to explain both what its final and highest end
is and what the intermediate ends are. We say, therefore, that this final and
consequently single end is the glory of the craftsman himself. We do not
consider it as it is in God himself, to whom nothing can be added and from whom
nothing can be taken away, but insofar as it is manifested in the things
created by him and in their administration, as the wise man testifies in
Proverbs 16:4. This end indeed pertains to every created thing without
exception and even transcends all the ends of times and ages, but here we
consider it as peculiarly applied to the human race.

We therefore say that from eternity God determined to set forth his glory to
be seen, as though in a mirror, chiefly in two things: supreme mercy and most
severe judgment, as he speaks of himself in the law, Exodus 20:5–6, and
frequently elsewhere. Hence it is said that he loves some and hates others;
that he created some for honor and others for disgrace; and that he elected
some and rejected others. That is, he determined freely to elect, love, and
bestow eternal salvation upon some, but justly to reject, hate, and destroy
others by eternal judgment—and both for the manifestation of his glory. This
is said expressly of those elected in Proverbs 16:4; Isaiah 43:7; Ephesians
1:5–6; Romans 8:28 and 9:21, 23; and 1 Corinthians 2:7; and of the reprobate in
that same passage, Proverbs 16:4, as well as Romans 9:21–22 and Exodus 9:16.

From God's Word, therefore, we establish two ends which are opposed to one
another but converge in that final end: namely, election and reprobation as
considered in God's eternal purpose itself. The former, election, we rightly
define as God's eternal decree, proceeding from his purely gratuitous love,
concerning the election of certain people whom he pleased to choose, in whose
eternal salvation he would declare his supreme goodness and mercy.
Reprobation, likewise—not insofar as he executes it but insofar as he decreed
it from eternity—is God's eternal decree concerning the remaining people, in
whose at once most just and most severe condemnation he would declare, to his
own highest praise, his supreme hatred against all sin.

#### Intermediate causes in the execution of election and reprobation

Having set forth God's supreme purpose and its ends, it remains for us to
explain the causes through whose intervention God executes that purpose for
the glory of his name, both in freely electing and saving and in justly
reprobating and condemning. Some of these causes are considered in God himself
and others in human beings themselves, and both groups have a twofold
difference. Some are common both to those elected and those reprobated. In God,
creation is such a cause. In human beings, the pure understanding of the first
human and his upright, spontaneous will, free toward either alternative, were
indeed effected by God; these two things are understood chiefly by the name
“image of God.” The fall, however, and both the blindness of the understanding
and the bondage of the will under sin proceeded from human beings themselves,
though not apart from God's decree, and inhered in them.

The causes peculiar to adults who are elected and therefore saved are, as
regards God himself, effectual calling through the Word; and, in the elect
adults themselves by God's grace, faith and its effects, from which
glorification finally follows. The fountain of all these is JESUS CHRIST the
mediator, from God's gratuitous mercy. Among those reprobated and consequently
condemned, as regards God, there is righteous abandonment; but within the
reprobate adults themselves there are spontaneous hardening proceeding from
their own selves and its fruits. Hence through God's just condemnation they
are themselves the authors of their eternal death, to the glory of God the
just judge. We set all these intermediate causes, flowing from God's eternal
purpose, before the eyes in the following diagram.

Come, then, let us explain more fully all those intermediate causes which lay
hidden in God's purpose from eternity and were finally produced in their own
time.

#### Creation

First of all, once the ends of which we have spoken had been established in
the craftsman's mind, it undoubtedly followed that he should command his work
to exist. We therefore rightly give the first place to the purpose of creation.
Yet man certainly had to be created such that he would be fit and suited to
attain the ends for which he was created. Those to be elected could not be
saved through mercy unless they had first become wretched—that is, had
deserved God's wrath—from which they would nevertheless be delivered through
grace. Nor could the reprobate be justly condemned unless through sin they
were liable to just condemnation.

#### Corruption

But since God is goodness itself, he could not create a defective work, nor
did he. The heresy of the Manichaeans is abominable, although they did perceive
that the supremely good God cannot be regarded as the author of evil. On the
other hand, if he had created man so good that he could not turn from good to
evil, God the creator could have exercised neither mercy nor judgment among
the human race; that is, he could not have executed the ends proposed to
himself from eternity. Therefore the good God created man—that is, male and
female—good, but mutably good. Yet if God had himself effected this mutability
from good to evil, he would certainly have to be regarded as no less the
author of evil than if he had created evil from the beginning, and much more
so if he had in any way compelled the man whom he had created good to
transform himself into evil.

Man therefore had to be created intelligent—for things without understanding
and reason cannot be said to act either well or badly—and endowed with a will
both spontaneous (that is, moving itself voluntarily and intelligently from
an intrinsic principle) and free in itself either to embrace a recognized good
or a forbidden evil. Thus the change from good to evil necessary for the
execution of God's eternal purpose would arise, certainly not apart from God's
decree, whose outcome cannot fail, but nevertheless from nowhere other than
the intrinsic, spontaneous, and free movement of the human will. In this way,
when this contingent change in man occurred, an entrance would be opened both
for mercy and for God's just judgment.

Accordingly, what Paul said of the entire multitude of the elect to be saved
by gratuitous mercy, gathered together as though into one body—namely, that
God shut them all up under sin so that he might have mercy upon them all—must
also be said of the mass of the reprobate to be justly condemned: that God
shut all of them too under sin so that he might justly destroy them all; and,
finally, that he shut both groups under sin so that he might be glorified in
both.

Now that the human race had thus been created and arranged in God's mind both
for each of those subordinate ends and for the final end, it remains for us to
see how he executed his eternal purpose, both concerning the repair of the
fall in his elect and their salvation through mercy, and concerning the just
condemnation of the reprobate. We shall treat the elect first.

### The intermediate causes God supplied for executing the eternal counsel of election

Just as God had decreed from eternity to create a human race that would fall
of its own accord in Adam, so from eternity he decreed to create, as it were,
a second Adam in whom he would save all those to be elected through ineffable
mercy. He did so in such a manner that in one and the same truly wonderful
work, astonishing even to the angels themselves, he would temper supreme mercy
with the most severe judgment. Therefore this second Adam had to be truly man,
and indeed taken from the substance of the first, because it was fitting that
the sin of the elect and its fruits be pardoned in such a way that the penalty
was exacted from no nature other than the one which sinned. Otherwise God
would not have been just, whether he were so merciful as to forget his justice,
or exacted the penalty of death from a nature other than the one which
deserved death.

What created nature could possibly have been equal to sustaining God's
judgment—and that the most severe? Certainly none above or below, however much
it might be endowed with divine and most excellent strength, for it would have
had to be equal with God. This deliverer, therefore, who was to undergo God's
wrath in such a way that he not only did not remain under it but also liberated
all who were to be given to him, had to be not only most divine in quality but
also truly and actually God. Hence came the incarnation of the eternal,
only-begotten Son, predestined by the eternal Father before all time, 1
Corinthians 2:7. Just as the Father had decreed through this Son to create the
human nature which would fall of its own accord, so in the same Son he would
rescue that nature from destruction in those whom he was to give the Son and
lead it to its appointed end, eternal life, 2 Timothy 1:9 and Hebrews 2:10.

But what kind of man did he have to be in himself? Certainly a created man
endowed with supreme goodness and the most perfect justice. For the eternal
Son of God could neither unite himself to a man contaminated by even the
slightest stain, nor, if he himself needed purity and a deliverer, deliver
others. Yet, as we said before, he had to be taken from that mass of human
nature which had been wholly corrupted in Adam, while a guilty and corrupt man
can beget only the corrupt and guilty. It follows that this mediator had to be
a true man from Adam's posterity in such a way that no corrupt man begot him.
Rather, that power of God which is itself God—the Holy Spirit—would create him
in a woman's womb, and indeed that of a virgin, so that the extraordinary
conception would be established: wholly pure, uncontaminated, and thus most
holy, from the sanctified substance of the virgin.

With these things thus decreed and established by an altogether hidden
wisdom, it remains for us to see how, through that one Savior, God in actual
fact elects and saves, at the time it pleased him, those whom he had decreed
from eternity to elect and save in him through mercy (Ephesians 1:5; 2 Timothy
1:9).

#### Internal and external calling joined together

Just as medicine against every disease would be useless unless the sick
person, having been made certain of it, applied it to himself as is fitting,
so, in order for the wretched to be led from sin and death to eternal life
through that one mediator,

[Printed page 405; PDF page 5; OCR object 0420]

first of all that mystery which is therefore called the Gospel must be
declared (2 Timothy 1:9), so that it may both be perceived by the understanding
and, as it were, applied by a sure assent. Faith is from hearing, Romans 10;
and, as one of the ancients says, just as that virgin, having faith in the
angel's words, conceived Christ according to the flesh in her womb through
her ears, so we, receiving the word of the Gospel inwardly through our ears by
faith, must, as it were, conceive Christ according to the Spirit.

This is what our Apostle calls *klēsis* in Greek, which the Latin writers
translated “calling,” because a speaker's will is ordinarily understood by the
instrument of the voice. But in the subject we are treating, since all human
beings are born not only deaf but also wonderfully pleased with themselves in
their deafness, both the ears of the heart must be opened and God's voice must
resound in the ears of the body. The former is the work of God the creator
alone. The Lord established that the latter be accomplished in his Church by
the ministry of people appointed to this work—unless he sometimes acts in
people outside the ordinary course—lest, just as happened long ago to the
Israelites, who could not bear the majesty of the divine voice at Mount Sinai,
we too be unable to sustain the glory of the Gospel message, however gracious,
when spoken by God himself, much less offer it our ears and assent.

This calling, therefore, is partly purely internal, as the Holy Spirit fits the
hearer's mind for the faculty of understanding and at the same time
effectually disposes his will to assent. It is partly external, as the sound
of the words of God's servant sent to evangelize presses into the ears of the
elect hearer the very thing which the same Holy Spirit engraves upon his mind
and will.

#### True faith

Hence, therefore, knowledge of salvation understood is born, and assent in
turn follows this knowledge. It is not the simple and bare assent by which
someone assents as to certain truth to the Gospel narrative—which apart from
that grace of the Holy Spirit is to some a stumbling block and to others
foolishness—but assent joined with what our Paul frequently calls
*plērophoria*, full assurance: namely, application to the believer's own
conscience of the Gospel promise to which he has assented as most true. This is
a wholly gratuitous gift of God, bestowed peculiarly upon the elect alone,
earlier upon some and later upon others, as seemed good to God, the Best and
Greatest. Since the Gospel offers Christ to be embraced only under the single
condition of faith, we must conclude both that no one is saved without this
faith and that none except the elect are given it. From this a third point
follows: the course of this faith can sometimes be interrupted because of the
human weakness even of the holiest people in this life, but the seed of true
faith once sown is never wholly removed, since God's counsel cannot fail and
those whom the Father has engrafted into the Son through this true and living
faith will never be cast out.

#### The effects of faith

The effects of this true faith in the elect, which are understood under the
name “justice,” are twofold. Some are outside us, namely in Christ, yet become
ours according to the formula of the Gospel promise, through imputation
spiritually applied to us by Christ himself through true faith, as we have just
said. Others inhere within us, implanted by God's grace.

#### Justification: three parts of Christ's justice freely imputed

To the first kind belong, first, the justice of the penalty borne by Christ
for all the sins of the elect, by which all their sins are freely abolished
before God; and likewise the full performance of the law, offered by the same
Christ to the Father for the elect so that they might be freed from the law's
curse, since human beings are bound not only not to sin but also to perform
God's law fully. Although this part of that justice coheres completely with
the preceding one, it nevertheless differs from it in account. It so utterly
abolishes before God's tribunal the guilt which we contracted both through
original sin and through our innumerable subsequent iniquities that we appear
before the Father's tribunal in Christ not only as not guilty but also as just,
as though the law had been performed.

To these two parts of the justice imputed to us in Christ a third is also
added: that full sanctification of our human nature conceived by the Holy
Spirit in Christ's flesh. This too, imputed to us through faith, entirely
covers that native corruption which has indeed begun to be abolished in the
believing elect but has not yet been abolished, and which would therefore be
useless for absolving us before God. Thus absolutely nothing now remains on
account of which the elect are liable to God's condemnation; on the contrary,
the entire righteous requirement of the law is found in us, obtained through
Christ's justice. We heard the Apostle in the preceding chapters explain most
fully, distinctly, and individually these three parts of imputed justice.

The other kind of justice, bestowed by the Holy Spirit upon us who have been
engrafted into Christ through faith, is distinguished in two respects from the
justice imputed to us. First, the latter is ours in such a way that it
nevertheless inheres in another subject, namely Christ, while the former
resides in us and within us by the same grace of God. Second, the latter is in
every way absolutely complete—for otherwise we would have no hope of eternal
life—but the former, so long as we live in this body and the remnants of the
flesh fight against the spirit, is wholly maimed and imperfect, whether we
consider the understanding or the will. For the Holy Spirit so illuminates
our minds that we know only in part, and he so purifies our wills that, as long
as we live this life, we endeavor to will and do good only in part.

Nevertheless, this justice which inheres in us, however imperfect, is not
therefore useless; rather, it benefits us in many ways. From it a sure
testimony of our election and adoption is supplied to us, just as this present
life does not arise from sensation and motion but is recognized by them. Those
and only those are sons of God—that is, elected and adopted in Christ—who are
led by God's Spirit, Romans 8:14. And our Lord God regards the fruits of this
justice—namely, good works—not as they are in themselves but insofar as they
are effected in us by his Spirit. He regards them favorably and

[Printed page 406; PDF page 6; OCR object 0421]

both rewards them, as it were, with blessings pertaining to this life and
crowns them with eternal life—not indeed because they in any way merit such a
thing, but because such is the greatness of his beneficence.

This, then, is the way by which the Lord gradually leads to the eternal life
appointed for them those whom he purposed from eternity freely to elect: he
causes them to be born in their own time, then engrafts them into his Christ
through effectual calling and the instrument of faith, and fully justifies them
in him. In this way he manifests the glory of his supreme mercy.

### The counsel of reprobation and the means by which God executes it

It remains for us to discuss the reprobate, who are condemned by God's just
judgment: namely, the other part of the human race. I am not unaware how
hateful this subject is to some. Indeed, some for this reason completely avoid
the whole doctrine of predestination, something they certainly cannot do
without accusing the Holy Spirit of rashness or error—which is manifest
blasphemy—and overturning the foundation of our faith. Others confess that it
should indeed be handed down to the Church, but mutilated of one part, namely
the decree of reprobation, as though the decree of election could be
established without necessarily also positing the decree of the rejection of
the rest. Surely no one who has decreed to receive everyone can be said to
have decreed to elect.

Next, to reply briefly to those who apply the saying that God wishes all to be
saved, understood of God's supreme and eternal purpose, even to every
individual human being: when asked why, then, every person is not saved, they
answer that God's will is conditional in both directions. It is therefore
ratified in those who believe, because they fulfill the appended condition of
faith, but is nullified in those who do not believe, because they make
themselves liable to the appended condition of unbelief. To reply to them, I
deny that the conclusion of their argument can stand unless they maintain
with the Pelagians that it lies in a person's natural choice, without special
grace, both to believe and not to believe; or at least that without another
grace people can either embrace the grace offered in order to believe or not
embrace and despise it. This too is purely Pelagian. For it would necessarily
follow that our faith and salvation are not gratuitous in every respect,
because a natural faculty rightly disposed to embrace it would concur with
that grace.

Indeed, even if all this were granted to them—which God forbid—the manifest
vanity of that opinion is clear from the fact that the grace of believing
cannot truthfully be said to be offered to every human being unless perhaps we
dream that the grace of faith is infused in some intrinsic and extraordinary
way into so many infants dying throughout the world, and likewise into so many
myriads of adults departing this life before they ever heard anything of
Christ. This delusion needs no refutation. Finally, since God's express Word
openly sets before us the decree of rejection as well as that of election—and
reason itself altogether compels us to do so—no one could justly excise the
other part of this treatment unless he professed that he could be wiser than
the Holy Spirit.

But what, I ask, drives them to this? They fear that if a decree of
reprobation is established in God, whose counsel cannot fail, the necessity
of Stoic fate will be introduced; that, once the contingency of man's
voluntary actions is removed, God himself will be made the author of sins; and
that consciences will be cast into most dangerous temptations concerning
“particular” election, as they call it—although there certainly can be no
universal election of individuals. I shall soon show that they fear this in
vain, partly in treating another question, namely how God's decree and will
move secondary causes, and partly, God willing, most certainly and fully from
the exposition of Paul's context itself. Now, just as we previously set forth
the causes by which God executes his eternal purpose of election in the elect,
come, let us likewise demonstrate the means by which those destined for
condemnation open from themselves an entrance to God's eternal judgment,
without any fault in God.

The supreme and primary cause of their destruction, therefore, is their
original corruption, first introduced into Adam by his spontaneous will and
then propagated by God's just judgment into each and every one of his
descendants. If God, the perpetual enemy and avenger of all evil, left every
human being without exception in this corruption to perish, he would wrong no
one, since he is debtor to none.

Therefore, when he does not dignify with his saving grace the rest whom he has
left in this corruption, excepting those whom he pleased to choose, he is so
far from being unjust or excessively severe on this account that, on the
contrary, if he did not show himself to be the enemy and avenger of evil at
least in some, he could rightly appear to have forgotten himself. Thus this
just abandonment is the second step toward the precipice of destruction. It
does not, however, have the character of an efficient cause, but rather of a
deficient one, which some call a *sine qua non* cause, perhaps without
sufficient consideration. Properly speaking, a *sine qua non* cause is that
which, although it is not efficient, nevertheless concurs in the effect in
such a way that the effect could not follow without it. For example, the
setting of the sun is not the efficient cause of night—the earth's shadow
rather is—yet if the sun did not set, there would be no shadow of the earth.
But in the subject we are treating, the root of sin does not arise from
abandonment as the earth's shadow does from the setting of the sun; it inheres
within the person before abandonment.

We must furthermore observe that “abandonment” does not mean that God so
deserts and disowns those who are corrupt and to be condemned by just judgment
that he ceases to be God with respect to them, like a father who disowns his
son and neither thinks, inquires, nor determines anything about him. For if it
were so, it would follow that man by his obstinacy removed himself from God's
rule and providence. This is utterly false, as is clear from the demons
themselves, who are so subject to God's rule that they could not even enter the
swine without obtaining permission. Those, therefore, are so abandoned by God
whom

[Printed page 407; PDF page 7; OCR object 0422]

he appointed from eternity to just destruction through intermediate causes
that he is by no means inactive, but acts the part of a judge according to his
eternal purpose, and this in more than one way. Some—although the particular
judgments concerning them are hidden from us—he carries off to just
destruction before mature age and the capacity of understanding, since all
are born children of wrath and no one is saved outside God's covenant in
Christ. Others he advances to adulthood and sometimes even to extreme old age.
Although, as the Apostle says, he does not leave himself “without witness”
among them, Acts 14:17, he does not grant them even the grace of hearing the
Gospel, which alone is the saving knowledge of God, John 17:3. He nevertheless
affects them with many other benefits pertaining to this life, so that even in
them he may testify to some extent and for a time to his patience and goodness.

Still others he additionally dignifies with the voice of his Gospel, and is
sometimes so beneficent toward some of them that he produces in them even an
assent and a certain appearance of faith. Indeed, he adorns some of them with
those notable, peculiar spiritual gifts, as appears in Balaam, Saul, Judas,
and those mentioned in Matthew 7:23. When they afterward abuse this goodness
and truth of God against the sense of their own conscience, by their own fault
they voluntarily summon a so much more severe judgment of God upon themselves.

This abandonment, therefore, is followed by hardening, that is, an increase of
inborn corruption opposed to true faith and repentance. This hardening is
indeed inflicted by God as a just judge, but in such a way that he does not
himself effect it, nor is he its cause otherwise than a magistrate can be
called the cause of the punishment and death inflicted upon a criminal. To
execute this penalty of hardening, God uses the very heart of the person whom
he punishes with hardening. Thus God hardens the corrupt and condemned partly
by justly handing them over to Satan and partly by handing them over to their
own lusts. Satan hardens by tempting, stirring up evil thoughts in them—as he
is said to have entered Judas's heart—and dragging them away, ensnared, at his
pleasure. Finally, man hardens himself by his own corrupt and purely
spontaneous will, which is properly the principle of all man's voluntary
actions.

It does not conflict with this that God is the author of every natural motion,
even in the most particular things. The moving faculty itself, which God
created from the beginning and sustains in human nature, and which moves
particular actions insofar as it is natural, is far different from the defect
of that faculty, which flows from the spontaneous motion of a faculty of
willing previously corrupted through itself. In the same way, the faculty of
walking and the act of walking itself differ from the defect of lameness and
the limping which arises from it. Therefore, when God moves an evil will, not
insofar as it is evil but insofar as it is a will, he always moves it well and
justly. But the evil will, thus moved by God so that it too moves itself,
always moves itself badly insofar as it is evil. God can therefore in no way
be considered the author of an evil thought or action.

The case of good actions, however, is different. Whenever the mind thinks well
and the will wills well, God is the author and mover not only of the faculty
of willing insofar as it was implanted in human nature by God the creator, but
also of its being good and of its good motion. A new gift of grace corrects the
depraved faculty and directs it once corrected toward a good end. Hence God is
rightly said to act in the saints and to crown his own good works in them. But
when the nonregenerate and their thoughts and actions are in question, God,
who cannot be the author of evil, by no means infuses a new depravity into
them. Rather, to execute his just judgment of hardening in them, he uses
through their own selves the depravity which inheres in them through the
original corruption of human nature. Thus, when their hardened hearts finally
fill up their measure, the Lord summons them from this life to his tribunal
and consigns them to the just penalties appointed from eternity. In avenging
what rightly displeases him, he manifests his glory, just as he declares his
immeasurable mercy in saving his elect. We have said that this glory was his
final purpose in creating human beings.

## Explanation of the question: How God uses the human will

#### The will of God

This question can indeed be easily understood from what we said shortly
before, but it seems we should discuss it more fully. First, we must know that
we do not consider the will in God in only one way. Although whatever God
ordains to exist is always good, nevertheless he is said to will good but not
to will evil. If from this we infer with some that no will of God intervenes
in things done wickedly, but only his bare foreknowledge, then not only will
everything we said about Adam's fall and the eternal decree both of
condemnation and of the causes from which condemnation follows collapse, but
this most absurd and utterly false conclusion will also follow: that demons
and all human beings, insofar as they are evil, are for the most part removed
from God's rule—namely, insofar as he does not prevent their actions but only
circumscribes them within definite limits.

Hence arises the distinction between the will of decree and permission, which
it is not difficult to show is incorrectly understood by some. We do not
reject it when rightly explained; indeed, we profess that it should be
retained. First, then, I ask: whenever the actions of evil people are directed
against the good, whether the Lord tests or chastises his own, shall we exclude
God's decree and will from this testing or chastisement? Certainly not, unless
we wish both to contradict innumerable passages of Scripture and deprive
ourselves of the chief consolation in adversities, and even to oppose what is
self-evidently necessary about God, at least when the punishments of sinners
inflicted through still worse people are in view. If someone requires the
express words of Scripture, he will find the term “will” in 1 Peter 3:17 and
4:19, where testing is discussed; and on the same subject the term
“predestination”

[Printed page 408; PDF page 8; OCR object 0423]

in Romans 8:29. Likewise, the whole church at Jerusalem expressly mentions
the Lord's determined counsel and hand when speaking of the sufferings
inflicted upon the most innocent Christ by the most abandoned of all men. And
what of Job, that most righteous of men? “God,” he says, “gave; God has taken
away.” The passage immediately adds in Job 1:22, “In all this Job did not sin,
nor did he attribute anything foolish to God.” For Job recognized God's decree
from its consequence (as they say in the Schools), and in that very chapter it
is expressly declared to have preceded. So too David calls his adversaries
God's sword and hand in Psalm 17:13–14. And Paul says in Romans 8:32, “The
Father did not spare his own Son, but delivered him up for us all.” Yet through
what did he deliver him, if not the crime of Judas the betrayer, the shouts of
the raging Jews, and finally the grossly unjust sentence of Pilate sitting in
judgment? Indeed, unless we knew that God wills, ordains, and decrees for our
good that we should be afflicted even by the ungodly, with what confidence
could we take refuge in him?

As for the chastisements of the saints, whatever instruments God may use, if
we do not confess that they are not merely governed by God but also decreed
and ordered by him, who can deny that the principal consolation in accepting
and enduring them is taken from God's children as they look to their
chastening Father?

Now as for that class of crimes in which evil people are set against one
another, insofar as those crimes are God's just judgments, who would exclude
from them God's counsel, and therefore his decree and will, and thus that
power which exacts the penalties of sins, not merely by permitting or
abandoning, but also by justly executing them through evil people? Such was
the error of Julian the Pelagian. Let us therefore hear how soundly and
weightily Augustine refutes him from God's Word in book 3, chapter 5 of his
work against him:

“What do you mean,” he says, “by saying that those who are said to be handed
over to their own desires must be understood as abandoned through divine
patience, not driven into sins through divine power? As though the same
Apostle had not put these two together, patience and power, when he says: `But
if God, willing to show his wrath and demonstrate his power, endured with much
patience vessels of wrath made ready for destruction' [Rom. 9:22]. Which of
the two, then, do you say applies to what is written: `If the prophet goes
astray and speaks, I the Lord have led that prophet astray' [Ezek. 14:9]—is it
patience or power? Whichever you choose, or if you confess both, you
nevertheless see that the sin of prophesying falsely is both sin and the
punishment of sin. Or will you say here too that the Lord's statement, `I have
led that prophet astray,' must be understood as `I abandoned him, so that in
accordance with his deserts he went astray in delusion'? Take it as you wish;
nevertheless, he was punished for sin in such a way that he sinned by
prophesying falsely. But consider what the prophet Micaiah saw: the Lord
sitting upon his throne, with the whole host standing about him on his right
and on his left. And the Lord said, `Who will deceive Ahab king of Israel, so
that he will go up and fall at Ramoth-gilead?' [1 Kings 22:19–22]. One said
this, and another that; then a spirit came forth in the Lord's presence and
said, `I will deceive him.' The Lord said to him, `By what means?' He answered,
`I will go forth and be a lying spirit in the mouth of all his prophets.' And
he said, `You will deceive him and prevail; go forth and do so.' What will you
say to these things? Certainly the king himself sinned by believing false
prophets. Yet these very things were also the punishment of sin, with God
judging and God sending an evil angel, so that we might understand more
clearly how it was said in the Psalm that he sent the fierceness of his wrath
through evil angels. But did he do so by going astray, by judging anything
unjustly or rashly, or by acting so? Far be it from us. It was not said to him
in vain, `Your judgments are like the great deep.' Nor does the Apostle cry in
vain, `O the depth!' Hence Augustine in the same passage continues:

“Who could be so foolish that, on hearing what is sung in the Psalm, `Do not
hand me over, Lord, to my sinful desire' [Ps. 140], he would say that the man
prayed that God might not be patient toward him—if God hands people over so
that evil things occur only by displaying patient goodness while they occur?
What, moreover, do we mean when we say every day, `Do not lead us into
temptation' [Matt. 6:13], unless that we not be handed over to our lusts? For
each person is tempted when he is drawn away and enticed by his own lust
[James 1:14]. Are we perhaps asking God in this prayer that his goodness not
be patient toward us? Then we do not invoke his innocent mercy but rather
provoke his wrath. Who in his right mind would think so—indeed, what madman
would say it? God therefore hands people over to dishonorable passions so that
they do what is unfitting; but he himself hands them over fittingly. The same
acts are both sins and punishments of preceding sins, and they earn
punishments to come: just as he handed Ahab over to the falsehood of the
false prophets, and handed Rehoboam over to false counsel [1 Kings 22; 2 Kings
24]. He does these things in wondrous and ineffable ways, for he knows how to
work his just judgments not only in people's bodies but also in their very
hearts. He does not make evil wills, but uses them as he wills, since he cannot
will anything unjustly.” So far Augustine.

[The term *will* is equivocal in its various uses.]

What follows? First of all, the term *will* must be distinguished, since in a
general sense it sometimes comprehends even *not-willing* (if I may so speak).
For when God does not will something to happen, he certainly does not will it
because he wills not to will it, since he is subject to no compulsion.
Therefore, in this sense God will rightly be said to will all things without
exception, and consequently to decree them, since his decree that something
should or should not occur is nothing other than a determination of his will.
But when the term *divine will* is restricted solely to those things which he
approves simply and in themselves, then it must certainly be said that he
wills only those things which are good in and of themselves. Whatever is
wicked and unjust cannot occur without his hating it, and consequently
forbidding and not willing it.

[God's will is necessity, but it does not impose compulsion on creatures.]

Next, since, as Augustine likewise says, this will of God understood generally
is the necessity of things, and far more things are done badly than well, we
must consider how God cannot be called either the author or the efficient
cause of those things which are done badly, since whatever is done badly
arises both from that will of God and necessarily (always understand *will*
here in the general sense just explained). For otherwise, by what right could
he claim that those things were done with him as their author and efficient
cause? We must therefore know that God, as the first and supreme mover, not
only determined each and every future event according to his supremely wise
and excellent will, but also created the intermediate causes through which he
appointed the things he had determined to occur, and did not make them all of
one kind. For some, by a certain power implanted in them by the Creator, which

[Printed page 409; PDF page 9; OCR object 0424]

we generally call *natural*, are borne toward their merely natural effects in
such a way that they lack every kind of sensation. Others, however, possess
sensation and a certain intrinsic form of preconception, and so perform not
only those merely natural actions common to their whole species but also
particular actions suited to the circumstances of times, places, and persons,
as we observe in beasts. Yet they do this without any exercise of true reason
and without any discrimination between good and evil. Consequently none of
these is properly said to act either well or badly; and it is not our present
purpose to discuss them. There remain those beings to which God has, in
addition, given understanding to judge and distinguish the causes of right
and wrong things, partly from principles innately present within them and
partly from the good pleasure of the Maker disclosed from without. He has
also duly assigned them a spontaneous motion that stirs itself by its own
power toward doing or not doing things. Beings of this kind are angels and
human beings; to these alone, therefore, understanding and will are
attributed, and they alone are consequently said to act either well or badly.
Our present discussion, however, is not about angels. Therefore, insofar as
human actions are human, their principle and true efficient cause must be
placed in the human will itself, since human beings act by their own
spontaneous and intrinsic motion. Yet insofar as they carry out their work by
this inner power in such a way that God does through them what he has
ordained, this work must be judged not human but divine, and its principle is
that general will of God. Thus what seemed to be one work is twofold, and each
work must be evaluated according to the different character of its principle.

Accordingly, if the human will agrees with God's will, whether taken in the
broader or, as we said, the narrower sense, both works will be judged good:
both God's good work, as he uses the human will through the grace of
regeneration, and the human being's work, as he spontaneously directs his
regenerated will toward the good. But if the human will resists God's revealed
will—by which, as Augustine says, God testifies that he judges nothing good
unless it agrees with his goodness and commandments—then, although what is
done against that revealed will does not occur without the intervention of
that general divine will by which he decrees everything that happens, it will
nevertheless be judged an evil work. Yet what God has done even through that
will, sinning by its own proper motion, will still be just and always holy.
Here we must be very careful not immediately to suppose that everyone who
wills what God wills thereby agrees with God, or that everyone who wills what
God does not will, or does not will what God wills, thereby opposes God. To
judge agreement or disagreement rightly, we must consider not only the thing
which someone wills or does not will, but above all the mind and intention of
the one willing or not willing. Augustine speaks beautifully about this in
chapter 101 of the *Enchiridion to Laurence*:

“Sometimes a person with a good will wills something that God does not will,
although God wills with a will that is good to a far higher and far more
certain degree. For example, a good son may will his father to live, while God
with a good will wills him to die. Conversely, it can happen that a person
with an evil will wills what God wills with a good will, as when an evil son
wills his father to die and God also wills it. The former, certainly, wills
what God does not will; the latter wills what God also wills. Yet the piety of
the former, though he wills something different, is more consonant with God's
good will than is the impiety of the latter, who wills the same thing. It
matters greatly what it is fitting for a human being to will, what it is
fitting for God to will, and to what end each directs his will, so that it may
be either approved or condemned. For God fulfills some of his own wills,
which are certainly good, through the evil wills of evil people. Thus through
malevolent Jews, and by the Father's good will, Christ was killed for us—so
great a good that the apostle Peter, when he did not want it to occur, was
called Satan by the very one who willed to be killed. How good appeared the
wills of the devout faithful who did not want the apostle Paul to go to
Jerusalem, lest he suffer there the evils which the prophet Agabus had
foretold! Yet God willed him to suffer these things for the proclamation of
the faith of Christ, training him as a martyr of Christ. God did not fulfill
this good will of his through the good wills of Christians, but through the
evil wills of the Jews. Those who did not will what he willed belonged to him
more than did those through whose willing it was done which they did not
will—because God indeed did that very thing through them with a good will,
whereas they did it with an evil will. But whatever the wills of angels or
human beings may be, whether of the good or of the evil, and whether they will
the same thing as God or something different from God, the will of Almighty
God is always unconquered. It can never be evil, because even when it imposes
evils it is just; and what is just is assuredly not evil.”

The sum of all this is that, even though what is done badly by a human will
does not occur without the intervention of God's decree—lest we be forced to
say that anything happens either against his will or without his concern,
which cannot be said without blasphemy—God must nevertheless not be regarded
as the author or efficient cause of any evil. For he does not approve
everything he decrees with respect to an instrument which moves itself
sometimes well and sometimes badly; an evil will is evil from no source but
itself; there is no agreement between God's will and the will of evil people;
and, finally, God's work, always good, which he performs even through a
sinning will, is one thing, while the work of the evil will that acts, always
evil, is another.

[God's will is always one and good.]

But if someone asks here how God can decree by that will understood generally
something which he nevertheless does not approve by his will revealed in his
Law or by a particular revelation—from which some infer that two wills, and
indeed mutually conflicting wills, are established in God—I say that the
explanation of this difficulty too must be sought from what was said above.
For when we say that God wills many things, even things innumerable to us
human beings, his own will is not thereby multiplied; the things which he
wills are multiplied. In the same way, when he wills things that conflict with
one another, his will does not therefore conflict with itself; rather, the
conflict is found in the things he wills. Accordingly, when God is said to
decree some things which he does not approve (in which respect we
distinguished his general will, which decrees all things, from his revealed
will, which commands nothing except justly), the words NOT APPROVE must be
understood not with respect to God himself, nor with respect to his own work,

[Printed page 410; PDF page 10; OCR object 0425]

(for in this respect he both decrees whatever he approves and approves
whatever he decrees, as also whatever he does). Rather, it must be understood
with respect to the motion of the human will itself, insofar as that will
moves itself. For example, God willed Jerusalem to be overthrown; Satan also
willed it, and the Chaldeans willed it. The Chaldeans are said to have done
God's work, so much so that God therefore calls Nebuchadnezzar his servant.
Thus, insofar as this is the work of God, who decreed that it should be done
and approved it once done, everything here is just. For it is God's just work
to take vengeance upon those who obstinately rebel, to chastise the godly when
they stray, and to test them in various ways. But insofar as this is the work
of a most ambitious and savage tyrant and of utterly criminal plunderers, God
rightly condemned it and avenged it by destroying Babylon, although it was
not perpetrated without the intervention of his decree. “But where,” you will
say, “does this discrepancy come from?” From the fact that the nature of the
whole human race in its two first members was indeed created good but
mutable, and changed itself by its own intrinsic, spontaneous motion. Hence
whatever the will of all human beings wills, it wills badly, and whatever it
does, it does badly. Nevertheless, God executes his just decrees through
these and those people as often, when, and in whatever manner he pleases—that
is, as we said before, partly by setting evil people against one another,
partly by chastising his own, and partly by testing and confirming them in
various contests for the sake of his glory and their good.

Nevertheless, some persist in objecting that two wills, and indeed contrary
wills, are thereby established in God if we say that he calls some whom he
does not will to be saved. For, they say, we must hold either that calling is
a sure testimony of God's will to save those who are called, or that calling,
as it concerns those who are rejected and destined for condemnation, is a
mere mockery by God, who testifies through the mouths of ministers to
something other than what he himself wills to be done. They even cite Christ's
words to perishing Jerusalem: “How often I willed to gather your children, and
you were unwilling!” Far be it from us, however, to attribute any such thing
to God. We answer, therefore, from that other saying of the same Christ,
“Many are called, but few are chosen,” that calling extends more widely than
the decree of election. They thus make a false assumption who think that
every outward calling is necessarily a testimony of divine election; nor do
they notice that, in speaking this way, they rush from Scylla into Charybdis.
For if God has decreed to save everyone whom he outwardly calls, then either
those who resist his call must perish against God's will, or God must finally
condemn them only after changing his will, or else we must maintain with the
Pelagians that the outcome of God's decrees depends not upon his immutable
will but upon human choice, in whose power it lies to render God's decree
either void or effectual. Or, finally, we must come to the view that God has
determined nothing concerning human beings except from his foresight of their
entirely free choice, so that the right to honor or disgrace belongs not to
the potter (as Augustine aptly puts it), but to the clay.

It therefore stands that outward calling must be distinguished in such a way
that the calling which belongs to the elect is that which is joined with a
true inward calling: the Holy Spirit opens the ears of the heart and, through
true faith, inwardly confirms what is heard outwardly, as Scripture testifies
everywhere. “Why, then,” you will say, “does he call outwardly?” So that, by his
just judgment, they may be rendered more and more without excuse and hardened
in their own stubbornness, and so that, by setting forth a most just example
of his terrible severity, he may declare the greatness of his kindness toward
his own elect and thereby demonstrate his glory on both sides all the more
plainly—both in showing mercy and in judging. God is therefore no pretender.
On the contrary, he at last shows in the event itself that he acts in earnest
in what he does, no less with the reprobate, whom—as debtor to none—he calls
either never or ineffectually, than with the elect, whom he freely calls both
inwardly through his Spirit and outwardly through the ministry of the Word.

As for that saying of Christ, we answer that it must be considered not with
respect to the eternal will of the Son himself, which is one and the same as
the Father's, but with respect to him as a man sent to reveal the Gospel, or
insofar as he was a minister of the circumcision. In this sense the statement,
“As the Father has sent me, so I also send you,” must likewise be understood:
certainly not with respect to his office as Mediator, but with respect to the
ministry of the Gospel. It is no wonder that all ministers of the Word
sincerely desire those whom they are sent to call to believe and be saved,
since the Lord has not disclosed to them his secret concerning each
individual person, except perhaps extraordinarily—as he did to Moses when he
was sent to Pharaoh, and to Isaiah when sent to many who would harden
themselves. To make this matter still clearer, we must consider and weigh both
the remarkable case in which Isaiah, by God's own command, announced King
Hezekiah's death as certainly future, although it did not follow, and the case
of Jonah, who foretold that the Ninevites' destruction would occur within
forty days, although as to the outcome he foretold it in vain. From these
examples it is manifestly evident that, although God is altogether immutable,
he does not always will or decree to do what by his outward Word he testifies
that he will do; nor has he always revealed his secret will to the ministers
of his Word. Yet he must not therefore be regarded as double-hearted or a
mocker. Even when the design of what he does in this way is understood
neither by the human beings through whom he speaks nor by those whom he
addresses, he always acts in earnest: whether by presenting threats as though
they were irrevocable decrees to those whom he wills to terrify and bring to
repentance rather than destroy, or by offering some taste of his goodness to
those whom he justly wills to perish, so that by their voluntary contempt of
it they may testify more and more to their own wickedness.

### The fall of the angels and of the first human beings

We must now answer another difficulty, though one which can again readily be
resolved from what was said above: namely, if God's decree imposed necessity
upon either the angelic or the human will,

[Printed page 411; PDF page 11; OCR object 0426]

how is he not himself the author of its corruption or fall? I answer again
that, speaking generally, what God had determined did indeed occur
necessarily; but it does not follow that it occurred necessarily from its
intermediate cause. Consequently, the necessity of the event itself did not
remove the motion of the intermediate cause—that is, of the angelic or human
will—which spontaneously and freely inclined itself toward what God, who
cannot be deceived, had determined. Necessity is one thing, compulsion
another. The former does not remove the contingency of the will (which we
shall discuss presently), whereas the latter destroys even the will itself of
the agent: for the moving principle of compulsion is external, while that of
the will is internal and spontaneous. To make this plainer, I say that the
will both of the angels who did not remain in the truth and of those first two
human beings was created in such a condition that, if one considers it as it
was in itself, one must confess that, relying upon that very capacity implanted
by God the supremely good Creator, it could be borne either toward good or
toward evil. It therefore fell into evil contingently: that is, it moved
itself not under compulsion but entirely spontaneously and of its own accord,
and indeed while their right understanding resisted their will in this
matter—that is, in embracing evil. For the rule of contradiction concerning
contradictories stands: neither alternative is determinately necessary, yet
one of the two must necessarily occur. For example, being broken and
remaining whole are contradictories. Thus one or the other, but not both at
once, could happen to Christ's bones; yet one of them necessarily—that is, in
actual fact—had to happen to Christ's bones. Accordingly, as far as their own
power and will were concerned, the executioners could either break Christ's
bones or leave them whole. They therefore left Christ's bones whole
contingently, their will inclining itself not by compulsion but spontaneously,
and even unknowingly, toward what God had determined and foretold before all
ages. Likewise, before that hour Christ could have been killed, considered
with respect to his mortal nature and the evil will of his adversaries.
Accordingly, at the hour determined by the Father he was killed contingently
with respect to his mortal nature and the evil will of his adversaries,
because at that hour, in that respect, he could either have been left alive or
killed. Yet with respect to the Father's will and decree he could be killed
only at that hour; and therefore in this respect he was necessarily killed at
that hour. We must think the same concerning the fall of those angels and of
the first human beings. God was neither its author nor its supporter; rather,
he so used the intermediate cause—their mutable will—that he did not alter
its free and spontaneous nature by any compulsion. Even now he similarly uses
their altered and corrupted wills in such a way that the entire fault inheres
in them, while through those sinning wills he executes his own most just and
eternal counsels.

“What then?” someone may say. “Can those evil angels, or human beings born
corrupt, even now be borne toward either good or evil by their own intrinsic
motion?” Certainly not. For Satan with his angels lies entirely in evil and is
engulfed in it, awaiting nothing but God's terrible judgment. To say that
human beings possess any disposition, much less a settled capacity, for
thinking or embracing the true good before the adventitious and entirely
gratuitous grace of regeneration would be as false and foolish as to suppose
that a rotting corpse—to which the Apostle compares us before that gratuitous
vivification in Ephesians 2:1—is endowed with any vital sensation or motion.
“By what right, then,” you will say, “is someone condemned for sin when he can
be borne only toward evil?” By the best of rights. First, because he is
unwilling even to acknowledge his own darkness, much less to admit the
physician and the medicine, according to the saying, “If you were blind, you
would certainly see.”

Second, because this enslavement under Sin, in which we are all born children
of wrath, arose not from creation, as the impious Manichaeans believed, but
from a voluntary alteration of an upright nature. Debtors—especially those
who through their own fault have become unable to pay—nevertheless remain
debtors; nor does their creditor wrong them by demanding what is his, unless
perhaps he should see fit, from sheer liberality, to remit the debt to some of
them.

Third, even if God had taken away from the evil will every freedom to
deliberate in either direction concerning anything, that spontaneous evil
motion alone, as opposed to compulsion, would nevertheless suffice to
establish its guilt. But we do not deny that even the evil will possesses a
freedom spontaneously flexible in either direction, or *to autexousion*.
Rather, we say this: the first two human beings, created in the image of God,
possessed a clear understanding for recognizing and approving the true good,
and hence, by the logic of opposites, for discerning and detesting the evil
forbidden by God. Likewise they possessed a will either to follow that good,
as truly right reason dictated, or to prefer evil to that good by obeying
Satan's suggestion rather than that truly right reason. Thus they possessed a
will flexible toward either good or evil. But once the light of understanding
was removed (at least as concerns the saving knowledge of the true God and of
the duty which human beings owe both to God and to their neighbor), and once
the will was corrupted to such a degree that it generally does not obey even
those remnants of right reason which remain in order to strip everyone of all
excuse, but, as the character in the Poet says, “seeing and approving the
better”—that is, the less evil—“nevertheless follows the worse,” we must
conclude that the human will as it has become through sin never deliberates
between what is truly good and what is evil. Instead, it wavers either between
things which indeed have the appearance of good (such as all the virtues of
the philosophers) but are not truly good, although some fall farther and some
nearer from the true end; or between such things and those which are truly and
utterly evil; or finally between this or that thing which is merely evil. No
matter which side they choose, they can only sin. For this freedom of theirs
is truly servile, so that the Apostle rightly says that all human beings are
sold under sin and Christ calls them slaves of sin and corruption.

[Printed page 412; PDF page 12; OCR object 0427]

If someone asks here, “What, then, was that good work of God which he justly
performed through those first human beings, sinful though they were?” I answer
that it was a preparation both for executing his just judgment against those
upon whom, as debtor to none, he would not show mercy, and for displaying in
actual fact, in Christ and in a far more excellent manner than if they had
never fallen in Adam, his goodness toward those whom he decreed from eternity
to save through mercy. If someone proceeds farther and asks what he does
through Adam's other descendants, I answer from what precedes that the Lord's
just works through the evil wills of evil people are these: the punishment of
evil people by evil people; sometimes the chastisement of good people who go
astray by those same people; and, in the case of his own people even when they
walk uprightly, partly their testing and partly his fatherly, *prophylactic*
care (as physicians speak), as the blessed outcome finally proves through
affliction and trial. I say something more: the Lord justly performs the
office of judge not only through evil people but also within them. He does not
infuse new wickedness into them, for he is never the author of evil, nor does
he merely fail to sustain them. Rather, adding the efficacy of Satan, he gives
them over to their own lusts to be blinded and hardened. Castellio's ignorance
was therefore great when he treated as contradictory these two statements:
that God hardened Pharaoh, and that Pharaoh hardened his own heart. For God
also sometimes, by setting his Word before them, sharpens the wickedness of
the ungodly by his just judgment. We see this not only in Pharaoh, to whose
hardening Moses was sent, but also in the greater part of the Israelite
people, to whose dulling Isaiah was sent, and finally today, to our deepest
grief, in many thousands of people.

Failure to notice this badly deceived those who, because they supposed the
salvation of the human race to be the sole end of God's purpose, were
therefore afraid that God would be called a hypocrite (far be it from us)
unless they confessed that he also wills to save everyone to whom he presents
the word of the Gospel. Thus, in manifest error, they substitute a universal
calling for the calling which is indefinite on the part of the ministers of
the Word, but from eternity utterly certain with God and defined for specific
people. For it is evident that far more people must be excluded from this
universality than included in it: namely, all those who, since the creation of
the world, have departed, daily depart, and will hereafter depart from this
life before they could hear anything at all about the Gospel. It does not
follow from this either that the Lord is cruel—for he would certainly be just
even if he saved not one person—or that death proceeds from God's Word itself,
which is deservedly called the word of life. Destruction flows not from the
Word itself, but from the crime of the person who rejects it when offered,
according to the saying, “Your destruction is from yourself, Israel; but your
salvation is from me.”

Thus the Lord performs his own work both in and through evil people, a work
altogether different from theirs, just as he acts in good people—that is, the
regenerate—and through good people in a very different manner. For in his
singular mercy he creates both the willing and the doing. And although they
too are moved in a truly free manner—that is, having been freed from corruption
through the Spirit of the Son—so that they also move themselves, and the work
is consequently twofold, nevertheless, since both God's will as he moves them
and their will as they move themselves concur, though not with equal holiness,
God is rightly said to accept and crown them as his works rather than theirs.
And he does this once again from mercy, not from merit: he overlooks the flaws
even of their most righteous works, and freely regards them in his only-begotten
Son as endowed not with their own righteousness but with the perfect
righteousness of his Christ. The term *synchōrēsis*, that is, permission,
rightly understood, concerns this distinction. Some take it very badly when
they oppose it to God's will—that is, his decree—a view which Augustine, cited
above, refutes most fully. Others use it rightly when they wish thus to
distinguish God's action as he truly acts in and through the good from the same
God's execution of his just judgments through evil people even while they sin:
he does not produce the evil will in them or move it wickedly, but merely uses
their evil will well.

This also answers what some object concerning a condition interposed between
God's decree and its execution. If the condition is necessarily going to
occur from God's decree, they infer that God is the author of evil; but if it
is uncertain, they say that we are wrong to maintain that the event depends
upon God's immutable predestination. I answer from Augustine, book 5, chapter
9 of *The City of God*, where, after refuting the Stoics' fate by which all
contingent things are subjected to necessity, he says:

“It does not follow that, because the order of all causes is certain to God,
nothing therefore lies within the choice of our will. For our wills themselves
belong to the order of causes which is certain to God and contained within his
foreknowledge, since human wills too are causes of human works.”

Likewise in the same place: “Our wills have as much power as God willed and
foreknew them to have. Therefore, whatever power they have, they most
certainly have it; and that they themselves will exist at all, and what they
will have power to do and will do, was foreknown by him whose foreknowledge
cannot be deceived.”

And again: “If necessity is defined as that according to which we say that
something must be or must occur in this way, I do not know why we should fear
that it will take away our freedom of will.”

In sum, then, whatever God decreed would occur upon a condition must occur
when the condition is fulfilled. If that condition depends upon necessary
causes, it too occurs necessarily, both by necessity of the consequence and
by necessity of the consequent. But if it depends upon contingent causes, it
also occurs contingently with respect to those causes; yet necessarily, if
God's immutable decree is considered, not by reason of the consequent but
only by reason of the consequence. So much for these matters. Let us now turn
to Paul's text itself.

Paul has most splendidly expounded the mystery of all the parts of our
justification revealed to us in the Gospel, whose sum is that we are freely
saved in the one Lord Jesus Christ apprehended by faith. But this whole
treatment would have been defective and useless unless he had laid beneath it
a foundation upon which our faith itself might rest, or opened the fountain
from which it flows, and likewise explained the means by which, as though by
an outflow,

[Printed page 413; PDF page 13; OCR object 0428]

it is conveyed to us by the Holy Spirit. For how could any of us, however
greatly endowed with faith, stand even in the lightest temptations with that
security and utterly certain confidence which he described at the end of the
preceding chapter, if faith rested upon itself, or upon any strength, however
great, situated within us? Foreseeing this, the Apostle in the preceding
chapter ascended from the Love by which we love God, as a necessary effect of
Faith perceptible even to our senses, to Faith; from Faith to Calling; from
Calling to Predestination; and finally from Predestination he raised us to
God's eternal and immutable Purpose. Only when the anchor of our faith and
hope is fastened there can we easily overcome every storm. Having presented
this mystery in a few words and as in an outline at the end of the preceding
chapter, he now undertakes at last to expound it expressly, part by part. He
divides the treatment itself into two principal parts. The first, beginning at
verse 6 and ending at verse 23, declares the doctrine of Predestination itself
in a most divine manner. The second, extending through chapter 12, shows how
that doctrine is to be applied and what its use is. These two things must be
carefully distinguished; those who have confused them have introduced the
greatest and most destructive errors into this doctrine. Yet if the doctrine
itself is removed, Faith cannot even be defined, much less can our consciences
be pacified through it.

The Apostle employs an altogether divine method in explaining this otherwise
most obscure subject clearly and plainly. He uses not abstract propositions
but hypotheses, or examples drawn from altogether indisputable cases, and
everywhere employs them to teach and prove the doctrine itself, so that the
matter seems almost to be placed before our eyes. Among these hypotheses he
gives the first place to the rejection of the greater part of the Jews, in
which the purpose of reprobation is set forth for contemplation as in a
mirror, and the second to the calling of the Gentiles, in which the purpose of
election is similarly displayed. He does so chiefly for two reasons. First,
no surer or more illustrious example could be selected on either side. Once
the purpose of reprobation is proved not in some very few but in an almost
innumerable multitude, who will be surprised that some are rejected? And once
this is shown not in some foreigners but in the greater part of that very
nation with which the covenant of salvation had been especially established,
who can deny that the decree of reprobation proceeds from God's free will?
Conversely, in the other part, who—having observed the example of the Gentiles'
effectual calling—will not grant that whomever God pleased were destined for
election, with no other sufficient cause preceding his will except his own
single, utterly free will? Second, even if the Apostle had not undertaken a
treatment of predestination, yet unless he had addressed the stumbling block
that arose from the obstinacy of the Jews—all but a very few of whom resisted
Christ as a false Messiah—everything he had taught about faith in Christ and
its effects would have seemed ready to collapse. For how would anyone have
thought it credible, unless convinced by sure reasons and sacred testimonies,
that one rejected by his own people but received by strangers should be
regarded as the Messiah? Paul therefore rightly seized from this hypothesis
the occasion to treat this mystery, not in order to stop with it, as some have
wrongly supposed, but to draw from this hypothesis the general theses of the
doctrine. It follows that this passage must in a certain sense receive a
twofold explanation: one referring to the hypothesis of the Jews' rejection,
and the other to the thesis of the doctrine of predestination itself. I shall
take pains to do this carefully.

## On Romans, Chapter 9

[Verses 1–5]

**I speak the truth.**) Since in the preceding treatment the Apostle had
proved that eternal life is situated in the one Jesus Christ apprehended by
faith, it followed that the Jewish nation, considered in its greater part,
stood outside the hope of salvation, for the Jews—with very few exceptions—
professed themselves enemies of Christ and the Gospel. Yet this could appear
not merely false but joined with blasphemy, since it would follow that God's
word could fail: that is, the covenant of eternal life specially established
through the Messiah with Abraham and his offspring, and confirmed with almost
countless seals. Nor does the reply answer this difficulty that blame for this
rejection should be attributed not to God but to the covenant-breaking Jews
themselves. For although this rejection occurred through the Jews' fault and
unbelief, not God's—as Paul will later show—this did not seem sufficiently to
vindicate God from mutability, because the efficacy of that covenant depended
not upon the Jews themselves but upon the truthfulness of God, who had freely
bound himself to this nation. It therefore seemed that he ought to provide
them not only salvation but also a teachable heart, and so actually to fulfill
the covenant. On the contrary, the Apostle now labors to show both that God
retains his truth and that the Jewish nation, considered from its present
obstinacy and in its greater part, has deservedly been cut off and excluded
from Christ's benefit. He uses an insinuation of the greatest weight and
altogether divine: he grants the Jews every prerogative by which God had
distinguished this nation from all others, and affirms by a most sacred oath
that he is so far from testifying such horrible things about his own nation
under the impulse of some wicked passion that, on the contrary, if it were
possible, he would willingly redeem its rejection by his own destruction. I
do not intend now to touch upon every part of this insinuation; instead, let
us approach the subject itself.

[Verse 6]

**Yet it cannot be that the word of God has failed.**) Since it was well known
what this word of God was—namely, that the covenant of eternal life established
with Abraham and his offspring was so called—Paul had no need to explain it.
This proposition is universally true, because God can neither change nor lie
in any

[Printed page 414; PDF page 14; OCR object 0429]

way. What he has decreed within himself will occur must therefore necessarily
come to pass, especially whenever he has testified to his decree in express
words. Truth itself persuaded even pagan people of this; hence Homer's saying:

> For no word of mine can be recalled, nor is it deceptive,
> nor unfulfilled, whatever I have confirmed with a nod of my head.

What Castellio and impious people of the same sort bring forward on the
opposite side from Jeremiah 18:7 and following, from the account of Hezekiah's
illness and the Ninevites' repentance, and even from Luke 7:30, where he
interprets the verb *athetein* as “to rescind,” is a childish fallacy. For the
designation “divine Word” extends far more widely than God's decree or counsel
by which he announced that he would or would not do something. Nor is there
only one kind of such divine announcement. God therefore speaks many things
by commanding, warning, and exhorting which nevertheless do not attain their
event; yet God's word—that is, his decree—does not therefore fail. In speaking
this way the Lord shows not what he has decreed to do or not to do, but what
human beings owe him. So too, whenever he has pleased, the Lord simply
announces many things which he will do, as to Hezekiah and the Ninevites, which
he nevertheless decreed not to do; nor must he therefore be judged a hypocrite
or liar. Announcements of this kind belong to one threatening, not decreeing,
although they are uttered in the form of a simple decree, so that people who
need such goads may be driven, even against their will, to consider the
greatness of their sin. But the counsel of God which the Pharisees are said in
Luke 7:30 to have *athetēsai* against themselves—that is, to have rendered void
and useless for themselves by their obstinacy—does not concern that decree of
God about future or non-future events. Rather, it means generally that some of
them, by believing, made fruitful for themselves God's benefit offered
indefinitely to the Jews through John, while others, by rejecting it, made it
not merely fruitless but even harmful to themselves. Likewise the Gospel,
which the Apostle calls the counsel of God in Acts 20:27 and Zechariah calls
the knowledge of salvation in Luke 1:77, is to some an aroma of life unto life
and to others an aroma of death unto death. Yet God's decree concerning those
foreordained to life in Acts 13:48 cannot therefore be rescinded, nor can his
decree concerning the others who were written down for judgment in Jude 4 and
appointed to this very thing in 1 Peter 3:20.

I therefore say that the doctrine which claims that God's word can be
rescinded—that is, what God has decreed he will do and especially what he has
testified by his Word, such as the covenant with Abraham and his offspring
which the Apostle discusses here—is as impious as it is blasphemous to say
either that God can be changed or that the truth or falsity of the divine Word
lies within the human will. We explained a little earlier how these things
nevertheless do not remove the contingency of voluntary human actions. The
Jews' argument was as follows: God's word concerning the salvation of
Abraham's offspring cannot fail; therefore Abraham's offspring—that is, the
Jewish nation—cannot fall from salvation. If this were conceded, it would
follow that the Christ whom Paul proclaimed was not the true Messiah to whom
that covenant of eternal life referred, since he was not received by the Jews.
As for the antecedent of this enthymeme, Paul wholly concedes its first part:
namely, that God's covenant cannot fail. But in the latter part he
distinguishes the term *offspring* as follows.

**For not all who are from Israel are Israel.**) That is: not everyone born
from Jacob, the common father of the whole Jewish nation, is therefore among
those Israelites, or that offspring with which the covenant that cannot fail
was established. The following inference, I say, is invalid: “If Israel has
fallen away, God's word has failed,” since Israel is twofold—one Israel is
included in the covenant, while the other is not. This was precisely the
fault in that enthymeme from which some still suffer today: they establish a
Universal Calling in place of an Indefinite Calling. Calling in this argument
is what is called the word of God, that is, the declaration of the divine
promise made in words. Yet these two differ greatly. A universal statement is
false if even one contrary instance is found: if there could be even one
human being who is not an animal, the universal proposition “every human being
is an animal” would be false. But for an indefinite statement to be true, it
is enough that what it signifies be so in some instance. Consequently, if
even one person from Abraham's posterity is saved, that word of God, “I will
be God to you and to your offspring,” has not failed.

[Verse 7]

**Nor because they are Abraham's offspring are they all children.**) Because
he had mentioned Jacob as the common father of the twelve patriarchs, lest
anyone wonder why he began with him when the covenant under discussion was
originally established with Abraham himself, Paul now ascends as far as
Abraham. He presses the same point: not every child of Abraham should be
regarded as the offspring to be blessed. He proves this by bringing forward
an example from Abraham's own household.

**But in Isaac your offspring shall be called.**) Paul uses the verb
*klēthēsetai* partly according to a Hebrew idiom, in which a thing is said to
be called not without purpose, but in such a way that its name indicates both
that it exists and what it is. Thus things that do not exist are said to be
called as though they did, by an allusion to the creation of things: God
summons by name things not yet existing as though they already existed, and
by this voice, as it were, causes them to exist—Nothing itself heeding the
command of God, who creates all things from nothing. Thus the name Isaac was
given to this son of Abraham and true offspring, which the Apostle after Moses
designates with the verb *klēthēsetai*. But a further and far more hidden
mystery lies in the term *offspring*. It is claimed as proper to Isaac, so that
by this designation he may be distinguished from Ishmael, even though the
latter too was named Ishmael by God's command. By this testimony Paul
therefore proves his answer to the Jews' preceding objection: the name
*offspring* does not apply to everyone descended from Abraham, and hence God's
word had not failed even if the greater part of the Jews fell away by failing
to acknowledge Christ.

**That is, it is not the children of the flesh who are children of God, but
the children of the promise are counted as offspring.**) Having proved that
not everyone descended from Abraham is that offspring with which the covenant
of eternal life was established,

[Printed page 415; PDF page 15; OCR object 0430]

it remained for Paul to produce the mark by which the true children included
in the covenant might be distinguished from the others, although descended
from the same Abraham. If someone who attacks the immutable decree of eternal
predestination—some on both sides, that is, in both Election and Reprobation,
and others (certainly much more foolishly) in Reprobation alone—were asked
this question, what would he answer? Surely that the true children of Abraham
are distinguished from the spurious by Faith and the fruits of Faith, and,
conversely, the spurious from the true by Unbelief and the fruits of Unbelief.
This is indeed true with respect to the actual execution of God's eternal
decree and the recognition of it among human beings themselves. But it has
nothing whatever to do with the point. The question is not how the Elect or
Reprobate are manifested. The question is whether, when the Lord covenanted
eternal life with Abraham and his offspring, he willed to include every person
who would be born from Abraham, or only certain persons whom by his good will
he had destined to receive this beneficence. If the Apostle had wished to
defend the former part of this question, as some attempt to do today, he would
have had to confess one of two things: either that God afterwards changed his
will, or that the efficacy of the divine will was suspended upon human choice,
in whose power it lay to believe or not believe. Neither is Christian: the
former is manifestly impious, the latter altogether Pelagian. Paul therefore
does not touch either of these alternatives here even in the slightest, for
he would have strayed entirely from the proposed question. He reserves for
its proper place the discussion of how the actual execution of both Election
and Reprobation is recognized from their proximate causes. But teaching that
the twofold offspring must be distinguished according to the divine decree,
as the Jews' proposed objection and the question arising from it required, he
calls one the offspring of the flesh, exemplified in Ishmael, and the other
the offspring of promise, which he wishes us to behold in Isaac. To explain
what it is to be a child of promise, he adds:

**For the word of promise is this: At this very time I will come, and Sarah
will have a son.**) From these words it is utterly evident that by *promise*
Paul does not mean the Faith and fruits of Faith foreseen in Isaac, as those
would have it who put Isaac's Faith and its fruits—which God supposedly
foresaw when distinguishing Isaac from Ishmael—in place of the Promise. On the
contrary, Paul locates the distinction between Ishmael and Isaac—that is,
between an offspring not included in the covenant of eternal life and the true
offspring included in the covenant—in God's own will, which he customarily
calls *prothesis*, purpose. He brings forward a promise which does not look to
Isaac as though he were already born and believing, but by whose power Isaac
was to be born and become an heir—that is, a participant in the covenant of
eternal salvation. Nor can God be presented to us here by Paul as though he
then determined for the first time that Isaac would be born and become an
heir. Rather, what he had determined from eternity he at last revealed to
Abraham in the words of that promise. It cannot be said without impiety that
God forms a new counsel, for mutability would then befall him. Whenever
anything of this kind is said in Scripture, it must be understood not with
respect to God himself, who is always one and the same, but with respect to us
and to diverse, and sometimes even contrary, effects. Castellio, failing to
consider this and therefore teaching that human beings can rescind God's
counsel and that God can be frustrated in his hope, falls into manifest
blasphemies.

In sum, Paul signifies that only those are Abraham's children included in the
Covenant whom God himself gives to Abraham from his eternal and gratuitous
goodness—a goodness proceeding from no source but his own good pleasure—which
he declares by promise. This is the proper and genuine meaning of the word
*epangelia*. From this Paul leaves us to understand—since the doctrine of
contraries is necessarily the same—who are called children of the flesh:
namely, those who indeed have the same Abraham as father, but only by the
power and efficacy naturally implanted in human beings for generation,
according to that universal word, “Increase and multiply.” Or, to speak still
more plainly, they are those whose father Abraham is not considered insofar
as God covenanted eternal life with him and his offspring and thus separated
him from other people by this quality, but insofar as he is a man like other
human fathers. Let us now see how this applies first to Ishmael and then to
Isaac, and after that how Paul ascends from the hypothesis of Ishmael and
Isaac to the thesis of Predestination.

“For,” someone will say, “both Ishmael and Isaac were begotten through the
union of male and female, and consequently both are Abraham's offspring in
this common respect.” They certainly are, but not in the same manner. Ishmael
was born of two vigorous parents and by the merely human counsel of Sarah,
who distrusted and despaired of offspring—a counsel whose outcome was also
unhappy—and thus he was merely a child of the Flesh. Isaac, however, was born
of a hundred-year-old father and a ninety-year-old mother naturally barren,
by an extraordinary power declared through God's express promise. He was not
so much born from them as divinely given to them, and is therefore called not
a child of the Flesh but of the Promise. Yet there is another reason for this
designation which must be considered especially in this argument. God can
also grant an extraordinary power of procreation to ungodly people for
begetting reprobates. Thus Isaac is called a child of Promise not only for the
former reason, but especially because he was so given to Abraham that God
decreed to fulfill the covenant of eternal life in him, but not in Ishmael.
Hence Paul sets before us, in the person of Ishmael, the eternal decree
concerning those justly to be reprobated in their time, and, in Isaac, the
decree concerning those freely to be elected in their time. This is evident
from verse 16, where the Apostle expressly recalls these hypotheses of Ishmael
and Isaac, and of Esau and Jacob, to the thesis. But before we proceed further,
I see that we must answer some people's objections. Some deny that what is
said here of Ishmael and Isaac concerns the covenant of eternal life. What,
then, does it concern? “The land of Canaan,” they say, “outside which Ishmael
was cast.” As though the land of Canaan were a sym-

[Printed page 416; PDF page 16; OCR object 0431]

bol to be specified merely as some earthly soil in which God wished, after
excluding the Ishmaelites, to feed only the Israelites and even fatten them
with the goods of this life! Rather, it was the visible pledge and symbol of
that eternal covenant and, as the author of the Epistle to the Hebrews says,
of that heavenly city for which those fathers longed. Thus, at that time, to
be expelled from it at God's command was a testimony of the rejection decreed
from eternity which is discussed here. For if their opinion were true, I ask,
what could have been more inept than for the Apostle to oppose these examples
to Jews who boasted that they were God's people even though they fought
against Jesus Christ? If the examples are explained as these people wish, they
belong far less to the proposed question than if someone asked about onions
were answered about garlic. “Do we therefore,” they say, “regard Ishmael as
utterly rejected and consequently condemned?” I answer that secret judgments
concerning individual persons must indeed be left to God himself. Yet Paul
does not rashly present in Ishmael's person an example of those who, although
born from Abraham and circumcised with outward circumcision, were nevertheless
not included before God in the covenant of eternal life. Moreover, the
antithesis between these two persons would not stand unless in Ishmael we
consider the contrary of what everyone recognizes in Isaac without dispute.
Nor do I find in what the Angel of the Lord foretells about Ishmael when
addressing his proud mother, or in what Moses himself recounts, anything that
savors of the character of a person elected by God. Indeed, since it is
certain that the utterly depraved Antichrist Muhammad arose from his race,
God still seems to exercise in this progeny—excepting those from this people
whom the Lord has judged and will hereafter judge worthy of his mercy—the
force of that saying: “The son of the slave woman shall not be heir with the
son of the mistress.”

It now remains to proceed to the second point we proposed: to explain how this
hypothesis applies to the general thesis of the doctrine of Predestination.
First, in this hypothesis we see both Ishmael and Isaac born in Abraham's
household—that is, in the Church—and sharing all external things in common;
yet both are not counted in that covenant which belongs to the true Church,
but only one, and indeed the younger. If Ishmael, although born in the Church
and circumcised, nevertheless did not belong to the covenant of eternal life,
what shall we judge about so many peoples disowned outside Abraham's household?
I except those particular persons among whom the doctrine of the Patriarchs
had not yet altogether died out, as is evident from the examples of
Melchizedek and even of Abimelech and Pharaoh in those times. In this case,
therefore, as in a mirror, we must contemplate each of the following points.

First, the human race considered in God's eternal decree has a threefold
condition. Some are born outside the Church and remain such. God dignifies
them with no calling necessary for salvation—that is, with no revelation of
his gratuitous Covenant—and therefore they are necessarily placed outside the
hope of salvation, since faith comes by hearing and without faith it is
impossible for anyone to please God. Yet with respect to the execution of the
divine decree they are without excuse: partly because all are born children
of God's wrath, not of promise (Eph. 2:3), and partly because all adults are
found outside Christ in both ungodliness and unrighteousness, suppressing the
truth of God in unrighteousness (Acts 14:17; Rom. 1:18; 2:14). Others, whose
mirror is Ishmael, are born in the Church and adorned with every external mark
of Christianity, but nevertheless are not among the true sheep before God,
who is debtor to none. Their condemnation, with respect to the divine decree,
also occurs necessarily; but their obstinacy and unbelief are entirely
voluntary and all the less excusable because they have willingly and
gratuitously defiled more of God's gifts. Finally, others, from the mere good
pleasure of the supremely merciful God, are effectually called from the rest
of humankind to salvation, or even before calling—that is, before they reach
maturity—are freely saved as included in God's gratuitous covenant. Some are
born within the Church itself and given the covenant's outward token from
their birth, as Isaac was; others are born outside the Church but in their
time truly admitted into it, as our Apostle will demonstrate in the calling
of the Gentiles.

Second, from these things we understand that more are called than elected.
Consequently, although charity commands us to hope well concerning everyone
to whom the outward Word is presented and the sacraments are administered,
these outward things are not simply necessary signs but only probable
evidences of predestination to salvation. God's decree is therefore not
rescinded, nor is God mutable, even if he does not will everyone outwardly
called to be saved.

Third, the ministers of the Word and Sacraments must not inquire into the
secret of that eternal decree. They must simply take care to expend their
labor with the highest faithfulness and diligence upon everyone to whom they
are commanded to announce God's Word and administer the Sacraments, leaving
God's secret counsels to God. Thus Abraham, at God's command, circumcised
Ishmael no less than Isaac and taught him the Lord's way. Yet the Lord did not
therefore make sport of Ishmael; rather, he set that same Covenant before him
unto severe judgment, while through the same Abraham's ministry he called
Isaac to it for an entirely different end. As debtor to none, God would have
wronged neither even if he had called neither to the saving covenant. Nor was
Abraham a liar or deceiver when he earnestly sought the salvation of both
Ishmael and Isaac until he understood that Ishmael must be cast out.

Fourth, the supreme cause of the divine purpose is God's will alone. Since it
is the sole rule of justice, it cannot be unjust, however hidden it may be in
God himself. Thus when we ascend to that supreme cause, above which there is
nothing, we must by no means say that God destines this person rather than
that one either to salvation or destruction because he finds something
already existing in the one or foresees something future in him by which he is
impelled to destine this person rather than that one to salvation or
destruction. This is so absurd that it is also impious, since either God's will
necessarily precedes the very existence

[Printed page 417; PDF page 17; OCR object 0432]

and qualities of things, or things exist from themselves both in their being
and in their qualities. On the contrary, we must say that it is just for this
person rather than that one to be destined either to salvation or destruction
because God so wills, and he wills nothing except what is just, even if we
cannot penetrate his secrets. But when the execution of this eternal decree
is under discussion, because intermediate causes appointed by God intervene
between the execution and the decree itself, then at last we must say that
those condemned are condemned because their corruption and sins deserve it,
according to the sayings, “Your destruction is from yourself, Israel,” and
“The wages of sin is death.” Those saved, however, are saved because it is
just that God should show himself both supremely severe as judge in the
penalties which he exacted from our surety, and supremely merciful in this:
having freely given the Son, he also effectually calls freely; and after they
have apprehended him through the gratuitous gift of Faith, he freely justifies
and likewise freely glorifies those whom he saw fit from eternity to destine
both for creation and for this end. Our Apostle will show this more clearly in
the immediately following example of Esau and Jacob.

[Verse 10]

**And not this alone, but Rebecca, when she had conceived by one man, namely
by Isaac our father, experienced this.**

**11. For while the children were not yet born and had done nothing good or
evil, so that God's purpose according to his election might remain firm, not
from works but from him who calls,**

**12. it was said to Rebecca, The elder shall serve the younger.**

**13. As it is written, Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated.**

The Jews still had something to object against the preceding example: namely,
that Ishmael was not included in that Covenant because he was a slave woman's
son, whereas they were all born from Sarah, Abraham's freeborn and true wife,
and indeed from Isaac himself, who was included in the Covenant. The Apostle
therefore now opposes to them another, far more explicit example, whether we
consider the persons themselves—the very same father, Isaac; the same mother,
Rebecca; and finally twins conceived and born together—or God's own clear and
indeed repeated testimony. All these circumstances tend to prove that the
supreme and sufficient cause of the divine, eternal, and immutable decree by
which God covenants eternal life with some but not with others—which is the
same as destining some to salvation and others to his judgment—is God's will.
This will precedes in order, not merely in time, which no one can dispute, all
the intermediate causes through which God executes this decree, as the
Apostle will presently teach. The Apostle therefore says, “While the children
were not yet born,” to remove consideration of the existence of the persons
themselves: both Esau, destined to servitude and hatred outside God's covenant,
and Jacob, to be admitted to dominion and love by the covenant formula. He
adds further:

**And before they had done anything good or evil.**) This excludes from God's
decree every foresight both of Esau's unworthiness and of Jacob's worthiness
by which God could have been impelled to the decree either of hatred or love.
“It was said to Rebecca”—that is, the Lord revealed his eternal decree to the
mother in these words. God did not then determine for the first time what he
had decreed from eternity; he then at last signified in express words what he
had decreed from eternity. “The elder”—according to the flesh, namely, since he
was the firstborn and far more excellent in outward gifts—“shall serve the
younger.” He will therefore not be the heir, but along with the birthright he
will also fall from the paternal inheritance and be reduced to a servile
condition equal to Ishmael's. The two differ from one another in this single
respect: Ishmael was born a slave, namely from a slave woman, since even by
the jurists' rule the child follows the womb; Esau, however, was born from a
freeborn mother but was destined by God to the condition of a slave with
respect to the right of the covenant inheritance. And lest anyone interpret
this slavery simply as the subjection to which the Edomites were later
subject—a subjection neither perpetual nor pertaining to the covenant of
eternal life, in which there is neither slave nor free—the Lord's testimony is
added through Malachi: “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated.” By these words the
Lord himself, the best interpreter of his own will, signified that the dominion
to which he destined Jacob cohered entirely with the love by which he
determined from eternity to love and save all those included in his covenant;
and that the slavery to which he destined Esau cohered with the hatred opposed
to that love.

**So that God's purpose according to election might remain firm, not from
works but from him who calls:** that is, so that it may be clearly established
that God's will to include someone in the covenant of salvation flows not from
any worthiness in the person whom the Lord separates from the rest and calls
to salvation, but from his own mere and sole good pleasure; and therefore that
God's decree concerning the salvation of his elect is most firm and altogether
immutable. This is why we said earlier that the Apostle necessarily had to
ascend, just as he moved from sanctification to faith, so also from faith to
the eternal and immutable Purpose of eternal Predestination. Otherwise
everything he had discussed concerning Faith and its effects would have
remained defective and mutilated. But if the decree of election to occur in
its time is firm and depends upon God's will alone, the same must certainly be
maintained concerning the opposite side—the decree of future Rejection—not
only because the doctrine of contraries is the same, whether we speak
physically or theologically, but also because no one can destine one or some
from among two or more people to Election without, by the very fact that he
destines some to Election and his love, testifying that he destines the others
to Rejection and hatred. Likewise, if in the decree of choosing or destining
to salvation, as the Apostle sets before us in this example, no foreknowledge
even of the existence of the very persons whom God decreed to choose supplies
the cause of his decree, the same must necessarily be maintained in the
opposite decree of Rejection. Just as it is true that a person's future birth
and possession of a true and by no means idle faith did not provide God with
the cause for destining him to love and salvation—although God will not
actually love and save him unless he is born and believes—so it is equally true

[Printed page 418; PDF page 18; OCR object 0433]

that a person's future birth and continuance in unbelief did not provide God
with the cause for destining him to hatred and condemnation, although God will
condemn him only once he has been born and is an unbeliever.

The Apostle will presently show how it does not follow from this that God is
the author of the destruction of evil people just as he is of the salvation
of the good—an objection that seems to arise from this doctrine. But before I
approach that objection and its explanation, we must refute the opinion of
those who interpret the Apostle's preceding words differently. Some of them
altogether deny that this passage concerns Election or Reprobation as they
relate to eternal life. Instead, they say, it concerns possession of the land
of Canaan, to be granted to one, Jacob, but not to the other, Esau. It is as
though the Apostle reasoned: “Just as God admitted one of Isaac's two sons to
the earthly inheritance and excluded the other from it, so it is nothing new,
nor is the covenant of eternal life rendered void, if he now includes only
some in the covenant's promise.” But who does not see that, if this
interpretation holds, wholly unequal things are compared—an earthly and
temporary inheritance with a heavenly one—and that the argument proceeds no
better than if someone said: “A father of two children can without injustice
appoint one his heir to the entire estate, while commanding the other, as
already sufficiently wealthy—as Esau was when Jacob died, being far richer
and more powerful than his brother in livestock—freely to yield his portion to
his brother. Therefore he can also without injustice bless the one with every
kind of happiness and sentence the other to death.” Or: “A father can
disinherit one of his sons; therefore he can also kill him.” The dialecticians
certainly do not teach us to argue from comparisons in this way.

Next, who does not see that the Apostle intends to argue from the sign to the
thing signified—that is, from the land of Canaan as a sacrament, or rather a
type, to the heavenly inheritance which is the truth of that type? It follows
that those persons must be understood not insofar as they did or did not
possess the land of Canaan as an earthly inheritance, but insofar as, by
possessing or being excluded from it as the type of the eternal covenant, they
were simultaneously included or not included in the covenant. Moreover, why
would Paul rise all the way to God and recall his hatred and love rather than
stop with Jacob himself? Finally, does not Paul, in the preceding chapter 8,
ascend from the effects of Faith to Faith and from Faith to God's eternal
Purpose, thereby manifestly show that his aim is to proceed from these
hypotheses to the thesis—that is, to an unqualified treatment of
Predestination? So much for them.

I now come to Castellio and his followers. In those dialogues of his, full of
blasphemies, he is not ashamed openly not only to say but repeatedly to insist
that God changes his purpose: that although he desires each and every person
to be saved, he decreed nothing concerning their salvation or destruction
except upon a condition placed in their own human power and free choice. Thus,
when disappointed in his hope, he sometimes destroys the obstinate or
defectors whom he had hoped would be saved; and at other times he saves the
repentant whom, while they remained rebellious, he had destined to destroy.
But what is this other than recalling Pelagius from hell, suspending God's
Providence upon the human will, and finally subjecting the craftsman to his
own work? More than enough concerning this openly impious and blasphemous
dogma. Let us now see how he distorts these words of Paul.

First he denies that these testimonies—whether the saying of Moses, “The elder
shall serve the younger,” or that of Malachi, “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated”—
include those persons. Rather, he says, they refer to two peoples, the Edomites
and Israelites, who sprang from those two men. But to what end is this? First
of all, if he wishes to exempt Esau from servitude and hatred, why should I
not by the same reasoning say that Jacob must be excluded from dominion and
love? If the latter is utterly false, why is the former not false too? Indeed,
who does not see that Paul's foundation—or rather the Lord's own word—collapses
if these things are said of the two peoples in such a way that the heads of
the peoples themselves are not included in the servitude joined with hatred
and in the dominion cohering with God's love? For it was certainly two
children, not two nations except insofar as they were comprehended in those
children, who struggled with one another in their mother's womb; and the first
Edom himself, from whom the Edomites arose, was settled on Mount Seir. Nor do
we read anything concerning Esau that does not savor of a profane man—which I
would by no means judge of every individual among his descendants, among whom
Job and his friends may not rashly be counted. Finally, even if we grant them
that the persons of Esau and Jacob are not regarded here, but rather the whole
and entire nations, what will follow from this against our explanation? It
will always stand that one entire nation was destined to Election within the
Covenant and the other entire nation to Rejection outside the Covenant, which
certainly happened to the Edomites and Israelites.

To evade this, Castellio retreats to another invention. “For,” he says, “the
Lord's statement in Malachi, `Esau I hated,' is not predicted of a future
thing, as though Esau had been hateful to God before doing anything evil;
rather, it is narrated as a past event. The word `I hated' means nothing other
than `I loved him less than Jacob,' as in Christ's words, `Unless someone
hates father and mother, and so forth, he is not worthy of me' (Luke 14:26),
and Deuteronomy 21:15, where the woman called hated is the one less loved.” I
am especially astonished that this man either failed to notice or concealed
the fact that it is Scripture's constant practice, when discussing God's Acts
not as he executes them in their times but as decreed by him, to use the past
tense to designate the eternity of his decree. Thus it is said of Jeremiah,
“Before you came forth from the womb I knew you; before I formed you in the
belly I knew you; before you came forth from the womb I sanctified you, and I
gave you as a prophet to the nations.” And Paul says of himself in Galatians
1, “God separated me from my mother's womb and called me through his grace.”
And in 2 Timothy 1:9 we are said to have been saved and called according to
the grace given us in Christ Jesus before the times of the age. This refuge is
therefore sought in vain, since the matter itself cries out that Malachi's
testimony is cited

[Printed page 419; PDF page 19; OCR object 0434]

as an exposition of the earlier statement, in which God uses the future-tense
verb “shall serve,” with the time when the Lord spoke thus expressly noted.
Who does not see that this signifies that a Destination to hatred was joined
with the servitude to which Esau was destined—a destination which preceded
Esau's birth, and consequently also his wickedness, not only in time, for that
is sufficiently clear in itself, but especially in the order of causes? It
does not follow, as this man supposes, constantly running upon this rock, that
Esau was hateful before he was born or evil, since God hates nothing except
evil. To be destined to hatred from eternity because it so pleased God is one
thing; according to the execution of that eternal Predestination, to be
hateful to God because of corruption and its fruits is another. God finds the
cause of his hatred wholly inhering in human beings themselves. But the sole
cause of Destination to hatred is his always-just will, whose glory is
concerned to fashion vessels both for the shame justly inflicted upon them and
for the honor freely bestowed.

I acknowledge that in some places the verb *to hate* is said not absolutely
but relatively, that is, in comparison with someone else whom one loves less;
likewise people are sometimes called poor not because want oppresses them but
in comparison with others who are very wealthy. Yet it does not follow that
this interpretation applies everywhere. Indeed, unless something particular
prevents it—which must be recognized either from the nature of the subject
itself or from some circumstance—we must retain the proper and native meaning
of the words. What, then, does the Lord discuss here? Certainly, as we showed
before, not some inheritance fertile or barren in itself, but the covenant of
eternal salvation. Possession of the most fertile land of Canaan, received
from God's hand for this use, was the type of that covenant's blessing, richest
of all. It follows that Esau's exclusion from that region and rejection into
the barren wilderness was a testimony to exclusion from the Covenant made
with the fathers. Therefore they must either demonstrate that someone outside
the covenant—that is, outside Christ—is loved by God, or confess that Esau
presents an example of those whom God hates outside the covenant of salvation,
that is, whom he has destined to his destructive hatred.

It remains to answer those who confess that the decree of Election, or
Predestination to salvation, is gratuitous—although they do not all hold one
opinion, as we shall presently say—but suspend the decree of Reprobation upon
foreseen unbelief and its fruits. Yet throughout this entire treatment the
Apostle expressly establishes this rule, opposing Isaac to Ishmael, Esau to
Jacob, and hatred to love on precisely the same basis. He excludes the
foresight of both Esau's and Jacob's birth and of their goodness and wickedness
as a contributing cause. They offer the following reasons. “This argument,”
they say, “is invalid: `If vessels of mercy are loved before they have done
anything good, then vessels of wrath are also hated before they have done
anything evil.'” But none of us ever argued in that way. We argue as follows,
and this certainly cannot be refuted: If God destined to love and salvation
whomever he pleased before they were born—that is, from eternity—and before
they did anything good—that is, without consideration of any goodness of
theirs which might impel him to that decree—then by precisely the same
reasoning we must necessarily maintain that he destined to hatred and
destruction whomever he pleased, with no consideration of any wickedness of
theirs as it concerns making that decree. For according to the Apostle's
doctrine, how can any regard either for goodness or wickedness be found in the
destination to future love or hatred, when the Lord in decreeing does not
consider those persons even as born or existing in the nature of things? But
those who disagree with us here constantly confuse God's decree with its
execution. The execution itself and the intermediate causes through whose
intervention God executes his eternal counsels in their time follow God's
decree in the order of causes and effects. They, on the contrary, wish the
intermediate causes to be prior in God's mind, so that God forms his counsel
from foreseeing them. Nothing can be asserted more contrary to God's own
nature and the divine Word by this doctrine, indeed less consonant with
reason itself, and consequently nothing more Pelagian—that is, more false.

Moreover, neither part of their argument is universally true, nor will it ever
be found in our writings. For not everyone who is loved is loved before doing
anything good, if you consider God's actual love, so to speak, toward his
elect. He so loved them first that he also loves them afterward as they bear
the fruits of his Spirit. Nor did he hate everyone whom he hates before they
had done anything evil; for he hates them now as sinners. What more?
“Election,” Castellio says, “can exist without Rejection, just as love without
hatred.” I grant that love can be given without hatred, if, for example, all
are loved. But no one who is not altogether insane has ever supposed that
Election can be spoken of when there is only one being in existence, or that
someone who receives all can be said to choose. To choose is nothing other
than to receive one, or destine him for acceptance, and to reject another, or
destine him for rejection. Hence the saying, “Many are called, but few are
chosen.” What could be said more foolishly than these words of Castellio?
“Adam,” he says, “when he was alone, was Elect”—namely, with respect to those
who would be born from him and destined to destruction—“and if he had not
sinned, all would have been Elect.” Rather, none would have been Elect, because
none would have been Rejected, since the Elect is one *from among many*; all
would have been saved, though with respect to God's decree this could not and
ought not to have occurred. “And Christ,” he says, “is Elect by the Father,
although he is nevertheless first and last, that is, alone.” But how could he
be first who had no successor, or last who had no predecessor? God is called
first and last, or Alpha and Omega, not because he is alone, as this most
foolish man dreamed, but because he precedes the beginning of every existing
thing and, even if all created things should perish, he who is nevertheless
free of every end would survive them all, always the same and like himself.
Christ, however, is called Elect not insofar as he is God the only-begotten,
but insofar as he is the first-

[Printed page 420; PDF page 20; OCR object 0435]

born among many brothers and the only one among all human beings anointed so
as to be both God and man, and the fountain and foundation of every actual
Election to salvation. It therefore stands that intermediate causes depend
upon the supreme cause, not conversely the supreme cause upon intermediate
ones except as concerns effect and execution. Since God's decree is the
supreme cause of all existing things, and Election and Reprobation considered
in God's eternal Purpose belong among relative things, it follows necessarily
that the basis of both is the same and entirely equal in God's will. It does
not follow that the basis of both is also equal in the execution of his
decree, because, as we shall presently understand from the Apostle, God
ordained the intermediate causes of actual Election differently from those of
actual Rejection. Castellio's bulwark therefore falls: “It is permissible to
Choose and Love freely, but it is not permissible to Hate freely, that is,
from mere and naked will.” This is indeed true if one considers actual
Election and Love, and likewise actual Rejection and Hatred—that is, in the
human beings actually loved or hated. But Paul will teach, by setting forth
the analogy of the potter, that God may from mere will destine people both to
gratuitous Election and Love and to Rejection and just future Hatred. This
will is never tyrannical but is the perpetual rule of his Justice.

I now come to those whom we said do not share the same opinion. Some of them,
as concerns Election, grant that it proceeds from God's gratuitous goodness
alone, excluding all foresight both of faith and of works as something that
impelled God to the Purpose of Choosing; in this we agree with them. But the
same people are unwilling to exclude from the decree of Reprobation the
foresight of corruption and its fruits, which they wish to be the antecedent
cause of the decree of Reprobation. This is certainly incoherent, since, as we
said before, the doctrine of contraries is the same. Nor can Paul's words
“before they had done anything good” be explained differently from “before
they had done anything evil” without manifestly distorting Paul's context by
this diversity of interpretation. They nevertheless bring forward something
with some appearance of reason. They wish God, as he formed from eternity his
counsel to choose some and reprobate others, to have set before himself the
human race as Corrupt and therefore worthy of the curse, from which he decreed
to exempt some freely through mercy, while destining the rest to destruction
because of that foreseen corruption. But this opinion presupposes what cannot
befall God: namely, that he formed his counsel concerning humanity's end and
outcome only when he considered them as already corrupt, since they wish the
foresight of corruption to precede that decree in the order of causes within
God's mind. Yet the End to which a work is directed is first in the intention
of every wise craftsman. How, then, could foreknowledge of corruption—which
certainly presupposes the integrity of nature as prior, since a privation
presupposes a state—precede in God's mind his deliberation concerning
humanity's outcome and supply God with the cause or occasion for determining
the end of the human race? Corruption must therefore have followed God's
ordination, not the reverse, although, as we shall say in its place, God is
not the author of that Fall.

Therefore, just as the state of integrity preceded the state of corruption,
and creation preceded the state of integrity, in the order of causes; and
since the whole order of intermediate causes proceeds from God's ordination,
which is not uncertain but directed to a definite end, we must also confess
that God's eternal decree to reveal his glory both in some whom he saw fit to
save through mercy and in some whom he would destroy by just judgment precedes
in the order of causes not only corruption but also integrity and even the
Purpose of creation itself. No other cause of this Purpose can be given than
the always supremely wise, good, and just will of the craftsman himself.
Moreover, if this doctrine were true, there would certainly be no place for
the objections to which the Apostle thought he must presently reply. The
question would not be why God destines some to salvation and others to
destruction, but why he destines these rather than those—a matter we shall
discuss in its place. Let this answer suffice for them.

Others, although they likewise seem to agree with us that election is entirely
gratuitous, fear that if they agree with us fully they will make God not so
much the judge as the author of sin. They therefore deny, with respect to
God's decree itself, that particular Election stands above all causes. Rather,
they contend that all are universally called to salvation. This calling, they
say, is not feigned on God's part, for he wills all to be saved and no one to
perish; but it is ineffective solely through the fault of those human beings
by whom it is rejected. Yet I seem to detect here many things which I cannot
concede. First, I deny that the grace of the Gospel—which alone saves—was ever
offered universally to all nations, much less to every individual, or is now
offered so. I need no arguments to prove this, since the unbroken history of
all ages demonstrates it.

Next, if this view holds, God must in his own order have considered the human
race as to be corrupted before considering it as to be created, than which
nothing more absurd can be said. Again, when God was to create the human being
good, either he decreed nothing concerning his future—which again is most
absurd to think—or he determined one of two things concerning him: that the
human being would either remain good or corrupt himself. If God decreed, and
by his decree created, the human being to remain in goodness, corruption must
have occurred while God was ignorant, unconcerned, or even unwilling—none of
which can be attributed to God without blasphemy. The reply some make about
Permission has no weight. We must hold absolutely that God permits what he
permits not unwillingly but willingly. This will of God is either the same as
the decree itself or certainly altogether consonant with it. From this follows
the very thing I concluded above: the corruption of the human race, subordinated
to the decree of Creation, occurred only as God decreed, willed, and at last
also permitted it. But if someone prefers to suppose that whatever decree of
God there was here was nevertheless not definite or simply determined,

[Printed page 421; PDF page 21; OCR object 0436]

but was suspended upon the freedom of the human will, flexible in either
direction, I answer again that this is no less absurd than if someone said God
decreed nothing about creating the human being. When God is under discussion,
to decree nothing and to decree uncertainly are equivalent, if the matter lies
so entirely in the will of the one about whom the deliberation is made that,
as far as the one decreeing is concerned, it can either happen or not happen.
As for human deliberations, I admit that people are often compelled to adopt
counsels of this kind, since they often deliberate about things which are not
in their power. Yet it does not therefore follow either that God is the author
of Adam's fall or that Adam's human will lost its freedom flexible in either
direction, as we explained more fully in the Preface.

Moreover, if God decreed to save everyone to whom the grace of the Gospel is
offered, as those whose doctrine I now oppose maintain, then, since he saves
no one except in Christ through Faith, we must also say either that God finds
the capacity to believe naturally implanted in every human being or that he
grants it to everyone whom he dignifies with the preaching of the Gospel. The
former is purely Pelagian. If the latter were true, the seed of God's Word
would be preached to no one unto judgment, since Faith would be God's common
gift without exception to everyone to whom the Gospel is announced. This is
refuted by many Gospel parables, by the most lamentable destruction of the
Jewish nation which Paul now treats particularly, and by the daily experience
of every age. If they reply that everyone to whom the Gospel is preached is
indeed given the *dynamis* to believe but not the *energeia*—that is, the
capacity to will to believe, but not Faith itself—it will again follow that
Faith is partly God's gift and partly proceeds from the choice of our will,
which is a semi-Pelagian doctrine. I do not say this as though people given
that first renewing grace were stumps and logs, but because the ability and
the activity are things wholly joined and proceed from that one good pleasure
of God. Since this belongs only to those whom God destined to salvation, it
likewise follows that only they are inwardly drawn. The Word of the Gospel is
indeed set before some others, but without the bestowal of renewing grace,
which is peculiar to those destined to Election.

“Then,” they say, “either God is a pretender, or the person whose ministry he
uses among those others is.” On the contrary, both the God who sends and the
minister sent act in earnest in what they do. As for the minister, he plants
and waters in good faith as he was commanded. Ignorant of God's secret counsel
—although God has sometimes revealed it to his servants, as to Aaron and
Moses sent to harden Pharaoh and to Isaiah when he would blind most of the
Jews—he desires all his hearers to believe and be saved; but after discharging
his own duty, he commits the fruit of his labor to the Lord. God himself, by
his just judgment, renders more and more without excuse whomever he was not
pleased to exempt from the corrupt mass. As they are reproved more by the Word
of God set before them, they oppose the truth all the more stubbornly according
to their innate wickedness, either closing their ears so that they do not hear
even the faintest sound of the Gospel, or resisting their own conscience after
it has been enlightened to some degree.

“But,” they again reply, “by what right will God complain that they do not
believe if by nature they are not capable of believing?” This objection makes
a false assumption. Human beings do not suffer from this inability by nature;
the entire fault arose from the voluntary corruption of that first man, in
whose loins all human beings propagated from him sinned. If, in that most
wretched condition subject to God's eternal wrath, the Lord pleases to destine
many or few to be abandoned to destruction, how can he seem unjust, when he
would not have been unjust even if he had destined no one to salvation? If
even that propagated corruption alone, intervening between the decree to
destroy and its execution, absolves God as just judge from every suspicion of
injustice—as we shall presently understand from the Apostle—who will think
that those who produce those fruits of corruption are treated unjustly?

It remains to answer someone who offers a new explanation of this passage,
one certainly unheard of by me until now. He explains Paul's passage thus:
“Just as Paul, when affirming that Jacob was loved while he had not yet done
anything good, does not exclude Faith in Christ, without which no one can be
either loved by or pleasing to God, but excludes works alone, in the sense
that Jacob was not elected because of works; so conversely, when he writes
that Esau was hated before he had done anything evil, he does not intimate
that unbelief, which is opposed to Faith, was not the cause of his
Reprobation, but that it was the simple will of God without regard to
unbelief, as some teach. For Christ, as was just said, refers the cause of the
world's Reprobation in the clearest words to human Unbelief. We must not
contradict Christ's assertion with glosses devised by human beings. Paul wishes
only to show that the other foreseen sins would not have obstructed Esau's
salvation if he had repented and believed in the Messiah. Hence in Reprobation
God principally regards not other sins, for which he shows a remedy in his
Word, but unbelief. Unbelief neither should nor can be removed from the
consideration of reprobation unless we openly contradict Christ's assertion
that this is the world's condemnation: not that human beings were absolutely
written down or destined for destruction without regard to their unbelief,
but that they do not believe in the name of God's only-begotten Son. Therefore
those who maintain that reprobation must be defined from God's simple will in
such a way as to remove regard for unbelief not only have no support from this
passage but contradict the whole prophetic and apostolic Scripture.”

But, I ask, what method of teaching is this? It confuses God's counsel or
decree—by which he determined from eternity, through intermediate causes, to
save some by grace, to destroy others by his just judgment, and to reveal his
glory in both—with the execution of that decree: that is, with the salvation
of those destined to salvation and the damnation of those destined to
destruction. And it wishes the foresight of causes subordinated to execution
to precede the decree itself in order, as though a craftsman did not first in
order determine within himself the work to be made before

[Printed page 422; PDF page 22; OCR object 0437]

determining the means for bringing the intended work to completion, whenever
the one under discussion has it in his power both to establish whatever means
he wishes and to decree the work itself to be made? The whole basis of that
man's criticism is therefore not merely weak but so absurd that it needs no
refutation among sufficiently intelligent people. Moreover, what kind of
thing is this? On his opinion, foresight of faith is the antecedent cause of
the decree of Election, while unbelief is that of the decree of Reprobation.
Then explain, if God regarded everyone as they were born, why he foresaw
unbelief in some but faith in others. All are born in one and the same nature;
whoever says it is sound will be not merely a Pelagian but a heretic. If you
ask from where this distinction takes its origin, is it from free choice
flexible in either direction? If that is his view, he will fall back into
Pelagianism. If it is from grace, I have gained against him the very point for
which I contend: faith is foreseen in some only because God decreed to grant
it only to particular people. But why did he grant it except because he so
decreed? Why did he decree it except because it so pleased him? Why not to the
others? Because he decreed to give it only to these. The decree therefore did
not arise from foreknowledge of faith or unbelief, even if we were to take
foreseen corruption as the beginning of the decree. On the contrary, those
who believe do so because God decreed from eternity to grant them faith, as he
grants it to them in its time. And these others are unbelievers because, not
being included in that decree of God, they follow what their corruption
persuades them to follow.

Furthermore, what new Theology is this—and we should remember that this
exposition, in a great error, treats the decree of Reprobation and Reprobation
itself as the same—that God in Reprobation takes account of Unbelief but not
of other sins, that is, evil works, for which he himself shows a remedy in his
Word? For if no remedy against Unbelief is shown in God's Word, I ask, who
would ever believe? Unless perhaps we say that we are born capable of
believing, although we are born dead in sins, cannot even think from ourselves
the things that belong to God, and were loved by him while still enemies.
Finally, if the author of this explanation calls only those who do not embrace
the offered Gospel Unbelievers and establishes foresight of this one Unbelief
as the cause of Reprobation, he should know that he asserts what is utterly
false. For if this is true, no one will be reprobated except those to whom the
Gospel has been proclaimed, since they cannot be called unbelieving toward a
doctrine about which they have never heard anything. What, then, will become
of by far the greatest multitude of those who departed, depart, and will
depart this life before hearing anything about Christ? According to
Castellio's Theology, the verdict concerning them must surely be: NOT PROVEN.
We cannot say they are loved, since they do not possess the faith that comes
by hearing, because they never heard; nor, again, that they are hated, because
they were not unbelieving toward a word they never heard.

Our Paul teaches far otherwise, bringing forward very different general
causes of human condemnation: namely, corruption, either alone, as in those
who did not sin in the likeness of Adam, or joined with the fruits of
corruption, as in those who suppress in unrighteousness the truth of God that
exists in nature however corrupted (Rom. 1:18), and whom the law of conscience
itself condemns. Such are all people except those whom God, having destined
them from eternity to salvation and given them to his Christ, saves after
bestowing faith upon them.

[Verse 14]

**What then shall we say? Is there injustice with God? Far be it!**) The
Apostle had said, from the hypotheses of Isaac and Ishmael, and likewise of
Jacob and Esau, that although God's covenant remains secure, not all the Jews
are included in it. The reason is that God covenants or does not covenant
concerning salvation with those whom he saw fit from eternity, without regard
either to the persons themselves or to their good or evil qualities. It is
evident that Paul wished to lead us from the hypothesis of the Jews to the
general thesis of Predestination, both because—as we have repeatedly said—
everything he had earlier taught about the firmness of Justification through
Faith would otherwise have been defective, and because it would be too absurd
and foolish to confine within the boundaries and barriers of the Jewish nation
alone a doctrine which concerns the Church gathered from every age and all
peoples. Yet this seems most unjust to human reason, which maintains that the
causes of destination either to hatred or love must be situated in the
persons themselves, lest either those worthy of hatred be loved or those
worthy of love be hated. It therefore wishes not merely the execution of the
divine decree, which the Apostle grants, but even its antecedent causes to be
considered in human merits, on the basis of which these or those persons would
finally be destined to hatred or love. This, then, is the objection to which
the Apostle next responds.

Those who wish this objection to arise from elsewhere, because they remove the
foundation we posit for it, do not all hold the same view. Some wish Paul to
oppose these words only to what he had said about one member, the decree of
Election, which they infer from the answer contained in verses 15 and 16. But
they are plainly mistaken. In verse 17 the Apostle manifestly answers
concerning the other member, and in verse 18 he provides a conclusion
pertaining to both members—the decrees of Election and Reprobation—in such a
way that on both sides, by entirely equal and similar reasoning, he ascends to
God's will as the supreme cause and stops there altogether. From this the
other and final objection again arises. Furthermore, if, as they think, God's
Mercy is the antecedent cause of the decree of Election, while the analogous
antecedent cause of the decree of Reprobation is foreknowledge of corruption,
as some wish, unbelief, as others contend, or other sins, as still others
prefer—all certainly wandering from the aim of truth by confusing the cause
of the decree, namely God's always-just will, which does not arise from
consideration of intermediate causes but wisely

[Printed page 423; PDF page 23; OCR object 0438]

and justly orders them, with the intermediate causes established for the
execution of his decree—what even the slightest suspicion of injustice could
fall upon God because he saves some, especially through mercy? And why would
Paul, in answering this objection, mention mercy? I confess that I cannot
sufficiently marvel how this opinion found any followers.

Others think the objection arises not because God destined some to love and
others to hatred, but because he destined these rather than those to love or
hatred. Since he determined such unequal things for two equals—as Jacob and
Esau were in every respect—he could seem to be a respecter of persons. But it
is perfectly clear that the objection does not arise simply from this. The
Apostle said not merely that of these two twins, born from the same father,
the same mother, and finally the same birth, one was destined to love and the
other to hatred—for we have shown that “I loved” and “I hated” must be so
explained—but also added in express words, “Before they were born and had done
anything good or evil.” By these words he showed that the fact one later
became upright and the other wicked was not the cause that impelled God to
destine the former to love and the latter to hatred. Yet in executing that
decree God loved Jacob as faithful and upright, and this from grace, while he
hated Esau as a wicked despiser of the covenant.

Next, I deny that everyone who distributes unequal things to equals is a
respecter of persons, unless he does so for unjust reasons. Suppose a creditor,
from two joint debtors—such as Esau and Jacob were born, to use the jurists'
term—freely remits the debt to one because he pleases, while demanding it from
the other by his right. This is how these people wish the foreseen corruption
of the human race to have preceded God's decree in the order of his mind. Will
the creditor therefore be judged unjust toward either? Indeed, if there is
any occasion here for partiality, it will be found not in the one from whom he
exacted the debt but in the one to whom he remitted it. Likewise, if a king
grants mercy to one of two equally wicked people and sends the other to the
cross, will there be any appearance of injustice in his judgment because he
punishes the latter? If there were any injustice, it would lie not in punishing
him, but rather in failing to punish the other who was equally wicked. Thus if
this had been the Apostle's proposed aim—granting what they wish, namely, that
God's decree, at least concerning reprobation and destruction, follows the
foresight of corruption in the order of causes—Paul would not have needed to
vindicate God's justice in the destruction of the reprobate, which is the one
thing that offends the people whom I now address, but only in the salvation of
the elect. Yet we see the Apostle answer distinctly concerning both members
and reach precisely the same conclusion. This is therefore not the genuine
exposition, since the objection Paul refutes cannot arise from it.

Finally, someone has contrived still another new interpretation here. He
thinks this objection arises because the Jews, zealous for their outward Legal
righteousness, complained that God would appear unjust if he loved some in
Christ but rejected others who, although laboring, running, working, and
serving the Law, nevertheless remained unbelieving toward the Gospel. He must
see for himself whence he can infer this, since not even a syllable about it is
found in the preceding words, while the particle “therefore” demonstrates even
to the blind that this objection arises from the immediately preceding things
Paul said about Ishmael and Isaac, Esau and Jacob. No one can apply those
examples to the Jews whom Paul discusses on account of their Legal
righteousness, only a very small portion of which had been constituted in the
times of those examples; and let this man see how Ishmael or Esau could have
pleaded its pretext. Further, how could the Jews whose Rejection the Apostle
discusses here by producing the examples of Ishmael and Esau have advanced
this objection without confessing that Christ was the true Messiah and that
they themselves were unbelievers? This opinion is therefore too foolish to
need a longer refutation. Paul's later statement that those Jews were rejected
because they pursued righteousness not from Faith but from the Law has nothing
to do with this passage, since here he treats the destination of Reprobation,
whose sole cause is God's will, whereas there he treats the causes of the
decree's execution. Let us now hear Paul refute that objection in these words.

[Verse 15]

**For he says to Moses, I will have mercy upon whom I have had mercy, and I
will show compassion to whom I have shown compassion.**) Some of the ancients—
Origen, that most impure writer, and others who followed him—dream that this
and indeed everything down to the words of verse 20, “But indeed, O man,” are
spoken not in the person of Paul answering but in that of an adversary
confirming his objection by these reasons. This is so futile and frivolous
that I do not think it needs refutation among people endowed even with common
sense. I explain it as follows. The objection born from what the Apostle had
taught especially by the example of Jacob and Esau had a twofold foundation:
God's decree concerning both those to be elected and those to be reprobated
rests upon nothing whatever inhering or foreknown as future in those elected
or reprobated. Paul answers this distinctly, first concerning the Elect in
verses 15 and 16, then concerning the reprobate in verse 17, and finally joins
a conclusion about both together in verse 18. As concerns the former, he
answers that although God, without any regard for qualities here but looking
to himself alone, destines to love and therefore to salvation whomever he
pleases, this is nevertheless not unjust. For between his decree—which is
eternal and precedes every cause, including the intermediate ones—and the
execution of that decree, that is, between the purpose of Choosing and actual
Election, gratuitous mercy intervenes. It not only absolves him of all
injustice but also wins for him the praise of supreme clemency. The Apostle
therefore adds:

[Verse 16]

**Therefore Election is not of the one who wills, nor of the one who runs, but
of God who shows mercy.**) To understand the force of this answer, we must in
a very few words

[Printed page 424; PDF page 24; OCR object 0439]

recognize that, although it is proposed in few words, it embraces a most exact
answer. Misery—which no one can deny—precedes mercy. Since this misery arose
not from Creation but from the human fall and its effect, original Corruption,
the fountain of all sins, while an upright state of creation necessarily
preceded that fall, it follows that all these intermediate causes are
subordinated to the decree but precede the mercy from and through which God
executes in time the decree of his eternal Election. It is therefore as if the
Apostle said: “Although God destines some to his love without regard for any
qualities, he is nevertheless not unjust. When actual Election occurs he finds
everyone shut up under the misery of sin, and would not be unjust even if he
destroyed all. He is therefore supremely merciful both in saving some and in
saving them entirely freely.” If someone asks why Paul does not expressly name
those intermediate causes which precede Mercy, I answer that he does so partly
because, once Mercy is posited, the things upon which it necessarily depends,
as I said before, are sufficiently understood; next, because he discussed
them most fully above, especially in chapter 5; and finally, because by
proposing the analogy of the potter he will presently rise in its proper place
to the supreme cause. In the same way, content to name Mercy, he mentions
neither Faith nor Christ—that is, the means of that mercy—because he did not
intend to treat them precisely here.

By *willing* he means the thought and endeavor of an upright mind, and by
*running*, good works. The Apostle testifies that neither impelled God to
destine anyone to love from eternity, nor impels him effectually to call in
time anyone destined to love from eternity. For in the order of causes God
first freely decreed to choose; next, by freely calling those destined to
Election, he actually chooses them; and finally, in the Elect he likewise
freely works both the Willing and the Doing. Those who supply the word “only”
here go very far astray. They suppose that Paul does not exclude our will and
works from both the decree and actual Election, but merely denies that they
suffice unless joined with God's mercy. Augustine, who went astray in this
respect when he had not yet been freed from Pelagius's philosophical errors,
later corrected himself and expressly refuted it. So much for the Elect. The
same Apostle answers concerning the Reprobate as follows:

[Verse 17]

**For Scripture says to Pharaoh, For this very purpose I raised you up, that I
might show my power in you, and that my name might be announced in all the
earth.**) Just as in Galatians 3:22 Scripture is said to have shut up all
things under sin, though God himself does this by his just judgment, as below
in 11:32, so here Scripture is said to address Pharaoh, in which God himself
is introduced addressing Pharaoh thus through Moses. The Apostle now comes
to the other member, the decree of Reprobation, in which he similarly shows
that there is no injustice, although it rests upon God's will alone. First he
shows that the matter is so by adducing God's own testimony about Pharaoh.
Next, from the excellent end of the divine counsel he gathers that a counsel
of this kind cannot but be judged excellent, much less unjust. Third, he
concludes that God cannot seem unjust when condemning those whom he destined
from eternity to hatred and destruction, because he condemns only those
worthy of destruction: between the eternal decree of Reprobation and its
execution—that is, the destruction of the reprobate—their hardening intervenes.

As we said concerning the preceding member, to perceive the weight of this
answer we must know that the verb *to harden* here means not to make something
hard from soft, but to increase its hardness by confirming it therein. This is
apparent from the opposed term Mercy, which means freely to remove hardness
and its effects. According to Scripture's common usage, this hardness denotes
both the stupidity and the obstinate wickedness of all the native faculties
of the human being for knowing and loving God. Since the human being was not
created in this utterly corrupt condition, precisely the same must be held
about this member as about the other: just as hardness precedes hardening, so
softness—the upright state of creation—precedes this hardness and fall.

Furthermore, careful attention must be paid to the phrase *exēgeira se*, “I
raised you up,” that is, “I caused you to exist,” which the Hebrew expression
*he'emadtikha* likewise declares by the usage of that language. We should seek
no better interpreter than the Holy Spirit himself speaking through the mouth
both of Moses and of the Apostle, so this must refer altogether to Pharaoh's
very arising, as is also apparent from the verb *poiēsai*, which the Apostle
presently uses in verse 21. Next, it must be observed that Hardening is
included in the verb “raising up.” The Apostle looks to the whole history of
Pharaoh, in which the verb *to harden* is very frequently repeated; but there
was no need to recount in full this history known to everyone. If this were
not observed, Paul would seem in the immediately following verse to use a
faulty inference—that is, to conclude more than the premise warrants.

[Verse 18]

**Therefore he has mercy upon whom he wills, but he hardens whom he wills.**)
These words contain the conclusion of the entire preceding answer in both its
members, although it is stated incompletely. We must add: “And consequently
there is no injustice in him.” It was unnecessary to express the missing
words, since they are readily understood from the proposed objection. Three
things are distinctly signified here. First, God takes the cause of neither
decree from anywhere but himself. This statement again refutes the Pelagian
opinion which makes foreseen faith or good works the cause of eternal
Election. Second, just as the supreme and sole sufficient cause of this decree
is God's mere will, so the fact that he includes these rather than those in
the decree either of love or hatred proceeds from the same will of his, not
because he finds the cause of this distinction in the persons themselves who
are to be elected or reprobated. Third, mercy intervenes between the decree of
Election and its execution, while hardening intervenes between the decree of
Reprobation and its execution, and these absolve God of all injustice. That
this is indeed the genuine

[Printed page 425; PDF page 25; OCR object 0440]

explanation of this passage is openly demonstrated, in my judgment, by the
sequence both of its words and arguments; this will appear even better from a
comparison with the contrary expositions.

First, then, are those who do not wish verse 18 to be spoken by Paul, but by
some adversary who attacks Paul's preceding response by a reductio ad
absurdum. This perversion of the passage is most plainly refuted by certain
reasons. First, since the doctrine of contraries is the same—otherwise they
would not be compared with one another in this response—if the latter member,
which says God hardens whom he wills, were absurd and false, then the first,
which says God has mercy upon whom he wills, would also be false and absurd.
Next, in the following verse the change to the person of the adversary speaking
is clearly marked. There would be no place at all for this unless Paul is
presented as speaking here and the adversary as replying to him afterward.
Finally, if this conclusion were impious and absurd, whether on both sides or
only one, the Apostle would have refuted it. So far is he from refuting it
that, on the contrary, the next objection arises from its affirmation and is
confirmed by the Apostle with manifest arguments in verse 19 and following.

But what of Castellio? “It is permissible,” he says, “to Choose and Love
freely, but it is not permissible freely to reject and hate, that is, from
mere will.” I concede this, but just as God may freely destine people to love
and salvation, or gratuitous Election, so I say that from the same mere,
gratuitous will God may destine those whom he will justly hate and destroy in
their time.

Others tie knots in the words themselves. Some interpret “I raised you up” as
“I raised you to the kingdom.” But since nothing of the kind is added here,
and the Apostle ascends to the supreme cause of the existence of things—that
is, God's creative will—while by no means treating Pharaoh either as a private
person or as King, why is this gloss fetched in? Others translate it, “I made
you firm,” because hardening is mentioned in the conclusion. But the verb
*exēgeira* does not admit this interpretation except metaphorically: it is the
bodily gesture of those unwilling to yield ground, who customarily stand
stubbornly upon their feet with body erect and stiffened. But I ask for an
example of this meaning, which even if admitted favors our interpretation. The
Greek translator in Moses renders it *dietērēthēs*, “you were preserved.” The
Chaldean paraphrase and the opinion of the Hebrew interpreters favor this
interpretation, from which some Latin interpreters also rendered it, “I made
you remain”—as though we should seek a better interpreter than God's own Spirit
speaking through Paul.

Finally, others stick at the word *Hardening* as though at a reef. It certainly
corresponds to the Hebrew word *hazaq* and means to render strong and
obstinate, opposite to the verb *raphah*. Origen explains it most foolishly,
as though God were said to harden those whose wickedness he wishes to use for
the correction of others, by not immediately punishing them but by penalties
which gradually grow heavier. If this interpretation were true, it would
follow that the Apostle says nothing about the actual question. The objection
does not ask about the end—although the Apostle does not omit it in his
answer—but about the cause of the decree of Reprobation. Indeed, the Apostle
does not even touch lightly upon the end which Origen dreams of here, but from
Moses recalls an entirely different and ultimate end of these divine
counsels: God's own glory. Solomon likewise says that God made all things for
himself, even the wicked person for the evil day.

Far more foolish is the invention of Pighius, the most impure sophist in our
memory. He interprets these words as though a lenient master, seeing his
servants made worse by his leniency, said, “I corrupted you,” meaning only that
he did not chastise them and that, by abusing their master's leniency, they
became worse. If this were so, not only would the Apostle fail to answer the
question, but what occasion would there be for the following objection? On
this reasoning God would not be said willingly to have hardened Pharaoh so
that he was too weak to resist God's will. On the contrary, Pharaoh, resisting
and prevailing over God's will, would have hardened himself.

Others therefore explain, “I hardened,” that is, “I did not soften.” I concede
that one who is hardened is not softened, and that if God softened them they
would not be hardened. But I deny that everyone who is not softened is
necessarily hardened, since their hardness might not increase but remain the
same as before. Yet *to harden*, opposed to Mercy, which means to make soft
from hard, signifies not merely leaving someone in his former hardness but
making him harder from hard, as is also demonstrated in the whole history of
Pharaoh. Further, if hardening follows because someone is not softened, it
must be confessed that to harden is different from not softening, just as the
consequent differs from the antecedent. Moreover, even if I conceded this—for
I admit that the reprobate are hardened while they are not softened, but not
that God infuses a worse mind into them—the Apostle under this interpretation
would not have solved the proposed knot. The question would always remain why
God decreed from eternity not to soften some before they were born or had done
anything evil, that is, without regard either for their future native
corruption or for the wickedness that would follow. We proved in its place
that this is the state of the question.

Finally, others flee to Permission, which the Greek theologians call
*synchōrēsis*, interpreting “I hardened” as “I allowed you to become hardened,”
or “I permitted you to be hard and not converted.” I certainly confess that
neither innate obstinacy nor its confirmation arises from God as efficient
cause, but from the human will, spontaneously wicked. Yet I first deny that
Permission is opposed to will. For what could be more absurd than that God
permits hard-

[Printed page 426; PDF page 26; OCR object 0441]

ening either because he is indifferent toward by far the greater part of
humanity or because he is driven to it against his will? If he permits neither
with indulgent indifference nor under compulsion, he certainly permits
willingly. For, as Augustine rightly insists in many places, it is good from
the standpoint of God who permits that even evil things should exist; otherwise
he would not allow them to exist. And he allows them not unwillingly but
willingly, so that God may reveal his glory both in their forgiveness in
Christ and in their just punishment. Unless this were so, the Apostle would be
impious for ascending equally from hardening no less than from mercy to God's
will, a will which cannot be resisted.

Next, since we must confess that in hardening God performs the part not merely
of a spectator but also of a judge, rightly angry with human corruption, who
could place this Permission in Abandonment alone without stripping God of his
office as Judge? A judge cannot be said to perform his office if he should
pronounce the following sentence upon some desperately wicked person whom he
has in his power: “Since you refuse to be corrected, go and rob as long and as
much as you wish, and attack whomever you please, until I seize you again and
inflict just penalties.” Far be it from us to attribute anything of the kind
to God. Those whom God destined from eternity to just destruction he finds in
their time corrupt and spontaneously obstinate. He does indeed abandon them
with respect to the means and grace of eternal salvation, which are reserved
for those alone who are destined to salvation, but not with respect to his
rule and office as Judge, as Augustine splendidly demonstrates against Julian
the Pelagian in the passage transcribed in full above. God therefore judges
them not merely by abandoning them as concerns mercy, but also by handing them
over to Satan and to others like them to sharpen their wickedness. Indeed,
because he knows they strive after what is forbidden, he even employs the
labor of his faithful servants to provoke their desires. Thus he used Moses
and Aaron against Pharaoh and Egypt; Isaiah and the other Prophets, and
finally Christ himself and his Apostles, against the greater part of Israel.
So even now the truth of the Gospel is announced to almost the whole world
unto destruction not only in its outcome but also by God's just judgment.
Finally, he hands them over to their lusts and to a mind devoid of all
judgment, yet in such a way that through those sinning instruments he does
nothing in them except justly—for, with respect to God who thus hardens,
hardening is the punishment of preceding obstinacy—and through their wickedness
he executes nothing except justly as concerns himself who orders it. Thus he
either tests his own by employing the work of those abandoned people, or sets
those destined to Reprobation against one another so that they may fill up
their measure, as is evident from innumerable passages of sacred Scripture.

But what has exercised the minds of so many that they sought these evasions?
As for the good and those truly zealous for God's glory—I take no account of
the others, who, as their writings plainly show, were driven by various
passions to attack the truth—this one thing drove them into these straits. By
two arguments they think the following necessarily results: if God's mere
will is established as the decree of Reprobation, which precedes in the order
of causes all the intermediate and even foreseen causes of the resulting
destruction, and if the outcome of this decree is said to be altogether
necessary, then the blame both for all the evils which are causes of
condemnation and finally for condemnation itself is transferred to God as the
efficient cause of them all. We freely grant them that this cannot be said
without horrible blasphemy. Yet this is the very objection which the Apostle
refutes here. He refutes the first ground of this inference by showing that,
although God's decree both to save and to destroy arises from God's mere will,
God nevertheless found a way by which the decree of salvation is executed
only through mercy, and the decree of destruction only when the cause of
destruction is found in those who perish.

The cause of their error, then, is that by confusing decree with execution
they wish the causes of both to be the same: foreseen in the decree, actually
existing in the execution. If this were true, not even these questions would
need to be asked. Who would accuse God of injustice if foreseen corruption and
its effects impelled him to the decree of Reprobation? Yet the Apostle,
prudently—or rather divinely—instructed, saw that even this would not satisfy
the flesh. For who does not see that, if we grant and concede that God decreed
nothing about the human race except after foreseeing its corruption, which
impelled him to this, it follows either that the human being was initially
created rashly by God and likewise fell rashly, or that God's prior counsel
failed, or finally that, after foreseeing corruption, God changed the counsel
he had formed before creation concerning the first human beings and their
offspring? If that counsel was one and the same—as we affirm it was, since God
is immutable—it follows that what we teach is most true: the decree both of
gratuitous love for some and of just hatred for others preceded corruption
itself, and consequently creation too, in the order of causes. Conversely, God
so subordinated both creation and corruption to the execution of this decree
that he is indeed the author of creation but not of corruption; the latter's
author is the human being's own spontaneous will at Satan's suggestion, as we
explained more fully in the preface.

Moreover, I marvel that those who hold this opinion do not notice that even
thus they fail to untie the knot which they tie for themselves—indeed, they
seek a knot in a bulrush. Since corruption is common to each and every human
being, the Lord foresaw it no more in some than in others. Therefore something
must have been added to this foreknowledge, which made all equally worthy of
hatred before God, to explain why he decreed freely to save some and justly to
destroy others. I am not now asking why he chose these rather than those, but
why he decreed to destroy some and save others from among people equally
corrupt. What else shall we establish this to be than his mere will, by which
he pleased to destine some to mercy and salvation, others to hardening and
just

[Printed page 427; PDF page 27; OCR object 0442]

condemnation.

“Then,” you will say, “just as God himself produces softness in those whom he
destined to love—for Faith with all its effects is God's gift—does he similarly
create hardness, that is, unbelief, obstinacy, and other such things in the
hearts of those whom he destined to hatred?” Certainly not. The first human
being brought this hardness upon himself and his descendants by not
cooperating with God but falling away from him and voluntarily stripping
himself of his native softness. The correction of this evil proceeds from the
mere grace of the Creator, who refashions him through grace. But the obstinacy
by which someone is confirmed in that same hardness comes from that very will
which at the beginning voluntarily hurled itself headlong into that Charybdis,
curable by God the Creator alone. The chief organ and instrument of the
hardening justly inflicted by God, and of the sins arising from it, is the
human will itself, voluntarily confirming itself in wickedness. It is so
enslaved to Sin that, just as according to the Apostle in Romans 8:7 it cannot
submit itself to God's Law, so it also does not wish to, and therefore sins
both necessarily and voluntarily. We explained above how and to what extent
this necessity does not remove Contingency. Castellio's ignorance was
therefore extraordinary when he opposed these two as though they were
contradictory: “God hardened Pharaoh's heart,” and “Pharaoh hardened his own
heart.” This is no less false and absurd than if someone inferred: “Judas
hanged himself; therefore God did not punish his crime by hanging,” or “The
Chaldeans, driven by their own wickedness, raged against the Israelites;
therefore God in his just anger did not hand his people over to the Chaldeans.”

[Verse 19]

**You will therefore say to me, Why does he still find fault? For who has
resisted his will?**) The sum of the preceding answer to the second objection
is this: God, as he pleased and impelled by no reason but his own will, decreed
from eternity to reveal his glory both by saving some and condemning others.
Yet he can in no way appear unjust on this account, because he executes this
decree through intermediate causes ordained by himself in such a way that he
saves none except through mercy and condemns none except when the cause of
condemnation is found in the guilty themselves. From this answer arises this
other and supreme objection on behalf of those condemned—for against the other
member concerning those saved through mercy nothing is replied. If what God
willed must occur—for it could never happen that anyone would break God's
will, that is, what God decreed would occur—and if the same God ordained as he
saw fit not only the destruction of those whom he destined to condemnation but
also the causes through which he executes this decree, by what right will he
punish those who necessarily cannot oppose his decree?

From these words we must especially observe what we said above in the Preface:
the term *will of God* sometimes means everything he decreed would occur—a
will which, if anyone could break it, would require God either to be mutable,
not to be simply omnipotent, or to be bound by intermediate causes—and
sometimes what he commands to be done in his Law. Satan and all the
unregenerate, and even what remains of the flesh in the regenerate, continually
resist the latter will, preventing life according to its prescription. Those
who do not observe this distinction fall into great errors and manifestly
slander us when they accuse us of attributing to God two wills which conflict
with one another. But let us now hear the Apostle's answer:

[Verse 20]

**But indeed, O man, who are you to answer God in return?**) The Apostle
concedes both parts of the antecedent: that this is God's will and that this
will cannot be resisted. He certainly would not have conceded either if it
were false or, as some even maintain, blasphemous. Otherwise, whence would
this objection arise, or why would the Apostle not answer with a simple
denial? Yet some say this is not God's will. They turn the indefinite
proposition “God wills all people to be saved” into the universal, meaning each
person without exception; or, as Thomas, though a Scholastic, nevertheless
explained it with a convenient expression, “kinds of individuals, not
individuals of every kind.” Likewise, some simply and necessarily infer from
outward Calling God's will to save everyone he calls: that is, as they say in
the schools, they do not distinguish the will of sign from the will of good
pleasure. Unless we concede this confusion to them, they contend that we make
God a hypocrite—far be blasphemy from the expression. Others wish foreknowledge
of corruption and its fruits to be the cause of the decree of Reprobation.
Still others contend that the outcome of the decree of Election is not only
uncertain but even mutable and suspended upon the human will itself, which was
Castellio's plainly blasphemous opinion.

What, then, does Paul do? In these words he reveals the true and proper cause
of all these replies: human pride. Because they are so devoted to their reason,
or rather their folly, that they wish to subject God's Justice to their own
judgment, they therefore think that God cannot escape the charge of tyrannical
cruelty if he is said to have destined some to destruction by an immutable
will which human beings cannot resist. The Apostle therefore recalls such
people to acknowledge their weakness, or rather their unworthiness, by setting
before them the distinction between the human being—a vessel fashioned from
clay—and God, the always most just and wise craftsman who takes the rule and
standard of justice from no source but himself. He uses the verb
*antapokrinesthai*, which is said not of a simple question but of a stubborn
and proud reply, as those customarily make who do not acquiesce when someone's
reasons, however just, are offered. If this is always regarded as hateful
among human beings, especially when an inferior is taught by a superior—even
though it often happens that one superior in dignity is not superior in
reason, and someone's will is not always to be regarded as reason—how
intolerably arrogant is it not immediately to acquiesce in God's will when it
has been made known to us? For nothing is more certain than that this alone is
right and

[Printed page 428; PDF page 28; OCR object 0443]

just which God wills. Properly speaking, his will is not governed by reason;
rather, it governs reason itself, so that it can never be tyrannical but is
instead the rule of everything just and equitable. To it alone truly belongs
the saying of this verse: “Thus I will, thus I command; let my will stand in
place of reason.” Paul adds:

**Will the thing formed say to its maker, Why did you form me this way?**)
With these words Paul illustrates the preceding answer by an argument from
comparison. It signifies the exceedingly great difference between God the
Creator and the human being created, while also alluding to the first
formation of the first human being from the clay of the earth. For before God
the craftsman fashioned a body of flesh from it and infused a soul into it,
what else was the human being but clay in God's hand, as though in that of a
potter, as is said in many passages of Scripture and especially Job 10:9? The
phrase “you made me this way” means, “Why did you create me flexible and
mutable, though I would be changed by myself?” This is clear because Moses
openly says that the first human being was created in God's image and was
therefore excellently good, not such as human beings are now.

From this it is also clear that the Apostle leads us to that level of the
divine will beyond which, at least as concerns us, there is nothing higher,
and upon which the very creation of the human being depends. Far from the
Apostle's meaning is the idea that when God destined some to salvation and
others to destruction, he had the fall of the human race in mind, or set the
human race before himself in his foreknowledge as already fallen, and was
thereby impelled to make such a decree. On the contrary, if the apostolic
answer is to have any weight, we must maintain that the decree discussed here
precedes creation itself, and therefore the corruption of the work, in the
order of the causes tending toward the execution of the decree. So a wise
potter necessarily deliberates before putting his hand to the work what he
will make and to what end. The words “you made me this way” thus openly declare
what I just said: either the first-formed human being or his descendants in his
person are introduced here, seizing the occasion for this Objection not from
that upright state in which the first human being was created, but from his
mutability and from the change certainly to follow from it. For why would a
human being complain that he had been created good?

It follows altogether that what we said above is true: even if we interpret
“you made” as “you permitted me to become,” that universal corruption of the
noble human workmanship nevertheless did not occur without the intervention
of the divine decree, although the potter here bears no blame whatever for it.
The analogy must not be stretched so far that a human being is compared to a
clay vessel possessing in itself no sensation or motion, much less
understanding. Likewise, when the Assyrian king is compared in Isaiah 10:15 to
a saw, rod, and staff, this does not signify that he was merely an insensible
instrument. It declares only that the Assyrian undertook and waged that war
solely because God so willed and ordained. God, as Augustine rightly describes
in the passage cited above, orders fittingly and works fittingly not only in
the bodies but in the very hearts of evil people the things which they, by
their own wickedness, do unfittingly. Thus in this argument too God must be
said to have rightly used both Satan's wickedness and the free, spontaneous
mutability of the human will for the execution of his eternal decree because
he so willed. The event must therefore be maintained to have been most just
with respect to God who ordered it. The Apostle then adds:

[Verse 21]

**Does the potter not have power over the clay, to make from the same lump one
vessel for honor and another for dishonor?**) By a comparison from the lesser
to the greater, the Apostle inferred that our God and Creator has much more
right over us than any potter has over his clay. Proceeding farther, he shows
that the analogy must also be considered anagogically: the term *lump* denotes
the very thing he earlier called clay, namely the human race itself. I approve
this interpretation as necessary. But I cannot agree with the many who
understand that, in the decree discussed here, God set the human race before
himself in his eternal foreknowledge while decreeing, either as already
created, as some wish, or even as already corrupted, as others contend. As I
showed before, creation cannot be established as the antecedent cause of this
decree without simultaneously maintaining that, in the order of causes, God
first decreed to create the human being and only afterward deliberated what he
would do with the creature. Nothing more absurd can be said, since in the mind
of every wise craftsman the end precedes undertaking the work. It is therefore
most certain that God's decree concerning the human race preceded Creation
and all the ends of Creation. Creation, I say, as well as Corruption does not
precede that decree in the order of causes, but only precedes the execution of
the decree.

Otherwise Paul should not have ascended so high, nor led us to God's supreme
will as the just and necessary cause of every event even through contingent
intermediate causes. He should simply have answered that Corruption and any
fruits of that Corruption were the cause of the will by which God destined
some to Reprobation and destruction, and therefore they had not even the
slightest occasion to accuse God of injustice.

Next, the clay in a potter's hand from which he fashions vessels is not yet a
vessel except materially, but only the lump from which he makes the vessels.
By the designation *lump*, therefore, we must understand the human race as to
be created, not as already created, much less as already corrupted.

Moreover, if matters stood as they claim—that is, if by *lump* the entire
human race in general were understood as corrupt—the potter would certainly
not be said to make some vessels for honor and others for dishonor, but from
vessels already full of dishonor to renew some

[Printed page 429; PDF page 29; OCR object 0444]

for honor and leave others in their dishonor.

Finally, the verb *to make*, and the designation of clay and of a still
unformed lump, seem manifestly to declare that in this analogy God is set
before us determining to create the entire human race in that first human
being—who is therefore called the first-formed—from whom every person would be
propagated until the end of the ages. Even then he formed this counsel: in
some, whom he was already inscribing in the book of life as to be born in
their times, chosen, called, believing, justified, and glorified, he would
reveal his glory by raising them through grace in Christ to the honor of
salvation; in others, by justly casting them down through contrary stages into
the dishonor of eternal destruction. But here again we must refute other
contrary explanations.

[Castellio's impiety concerning the divine books.]

First we encounter the blasphemous Castellio, who in those *Dialogues*
published four years ago by Socinus of Siena—the full brother of the most
abandoned Laelius, Samosatenian and Arian—under the name of Felix Turpio of
Orvieto labored greatly and indeed vainly to corrupt this passage. He was not
ashamed to write that it was to be wished that Paul had explained his mind
more clearly and openly both here and in certain other places, lest he seem to
disagree with himself—as though Castellio were a teacher more learned than the
Holy Spirit himself. What more? “If the one disagreed with the other,” he says,
“I would rather believe Jeremiah than Paul in this passage, for two reasons.
First, Jeremiah writes both more openly and more suitably to God's nature, if
you insist stubbornly upon some words of Paul rather than the perpetual tenor
of his doctrine. Second, God himself speaks in Jeremiah, whereas Paul speaks
in his own person. How much difference there is between whether he speaks in
his own person or by God's command is apparent from Paul himself, who writes
in one place, `To married people I command—not I, but the Lord'; and a little
later, `Concerning virgins I have no command from the Lord, but I give counsel.'
You see that he does not wish the same authority to be attributed to his words
as to God's.”

Good God! Can anyone be tolerated who sets God's servants, beyond all
exception, against one another, or finds some disparity between the doctrine
delivered through the mouths of the Apostles and that of Christ himself? At
least he should have weighed the conclusion which the Apostle soon adds there,
by which he closes the whole discussion: “I think that I too have the Spirit
of God.” But to what end are these things?

“The analogy in this passage,” he says, “is not between God and a potter, but
between God and the potter's material.” Of course this is not madness! Paul
manifestly—as both the objection itself and its answer require—first sets the
human being arguing against God, and then in the attached analogy opposes the
clay to the potter, so that God sustains the person of the potter and the human
being the person of the pottery. And I ask: when that first-formed human being
was created so that from him both those freely to be elected and those justly
to be reprobated would be propagated, what else was he but clay—or, as Abraham
says, earth and dust—in the Lord's hand as that of a potter? But let us hear
something more cleverly devised:

“We must understand,” he says, “that two analogies are intertwined here. One
concerns vessels for dishonorable things, which Paul does not treat expressly
here; the other concerns vessels that are broken. For being broken and being
used for a dishonorable thing are not the same. The first analogy therefore
runs as follows: just as a potter makes from the same lump some vessels for
honorable things and others for dishonorable things, so God creates from the
same lump of the human race some people to be in Christ's body like eyes,
ears, or hands—such as Prophets, Apostles, Evangelists, and Teachers—and others
to be like feet or unclean parts, such as the rest, to whom God has not granted
such great gifts. Here no one has cause to complain against God the potter.
There is no part of the body so lowly that it is not good and useful, nor any
member so low that it serves no purpose. The other analogy is this: just as a
good and wise potter makes no vessel to be broken, but breaks any vessel that
happens to be defective, so God created no person to be destroyed; but if
someone happens to become evil, he destroys him.”

But what kind of thing is this, when there is not even the slightest mention
here of vessels to be broken, but of vessels prepared for dishonor? He
expressly testifies that being broken and being used for a dishonorable thing
are not the same—although, had he not been a crafty sophist, he should have
said that to break vessels is one thing and to fashion them for uses unseemly
for the vessel itself another. Why erase what Paul treats expressly so that
something of which he did not even think here may be substituted? Again, those
compared here are prepared not for unequal honors among themselves but in
general for eternal dishonor, which Paul soon calls *apōleia*, destruction.
Why then, I ask, does this man recall vessels made for various honorable uses
in the Church? This whole account is therefore mere trifling. Nor did Paul, as
he most absurdly supposes, allude to Jeremiah 18:6, which treats not this
decree of God but its execution against an obstinate people forgetful of
their condition. By adding that Symbol through Jeremiah, the Lord testifies
that he could and still can crush them if they do not repent as easily as a
potter crushes an earthen vessel, as in Psalm 2:9 and Isaiah 30:14. If there is
an allusion here, Paul looked rather to Isaiah 45:9, which ascends higher,
though to a different end.

Next, because the Apostle speaks not absolutely but comparatively, comparing
these vessels with one another, if to fashion for dishonor signified the same
as to fashion for breaking, what else would follow but that only the vessels
fashioned for honor remain, while the rest lose the name of vessels? A broken
vessel ceases to be a vessel. If it is not a vessel, how will it be a vessel
serving dishonor? Both kinds of vessels must therefore remain in this analogy
and be equal to one another in being vessels. Their distinction lies in honor
and dishonor, not in some remaining vessels while others cease to be vessels.
See where this man's subtleties end.

Someone else writes as follows on this passage: “The analogy of the potter and
clay is not introduced by Paul with the design that all its parts should be
ap-

plied. It is enough for analogies to correspond to the present matter in some
respect.” I concede this. “But what is the chief aim here?” “Having shown the
difference between a potter and God,” he says, “it is to vindicate God's
Justice all the more from the wicked murmuring of human beings.” I concede
this too. But by what argument does he vindicate it? “If,” he says, “no one
reproves a potter even when he forms different vessels from one and the same
lump, some for honorable uses and others for less seemly uses, how much less
should or can God be reproved if he dignifies some but permits others to be
hardened, who rise up against him through unbelief and impenitence as Pharaoh
did? Resistance is present in the human being who ought to have obeyed God;
but resistance has no place in the potter's vessel. For the potter himself
prepares both vessels, one for honor and the other for dishonor, by an
effectual will. God, however, does not make vessels of wrath. Finding us all
children of wrath by nature, he permits those who do not obey the Gospel to be
hardened by God, as their hardness deserves, and in this manner is most justly
said to prepare them for destruction.”

So he says. In these words, I maintain, many things are said neither truly nor
appropriately. First, the objection to which the Apostle responds arises not
from the verb *to Harden* but from the verb *to Will*, as appears from the
words themselves. The question in this objection is not why God hardens; it is
how God has just occasion to complain about the hardened if hardening depends
upon God's decree and will, whose outcome is necessary. To this objection it
would be a most foolish answer for someone asked about the force of the divine
will to reply about corruption inhering in the human being. The Apostle would
thus answer foolishly not this objection but the previous one, passing over
the thing about which he was asked.

Next, if the Apostle wished to demonstrate the ground of God's will as this
man claims—constantly confusing God's will or decree, beyond which nothing
higher can be given, with its execution—namely, corruption and resistance, on
account of which God permits some to be hardened, as he prefers to speak, then
the Apostle would not have shut the adversary's mouth but opened it wider. For
he will continue to ask: since God finds everyone equally corrupt—to deny this
is manifest Pelagianism—that is, not disposed even to think the things of God,
much less to believe them, how does it happen that from among the equally
corrupt some obey while others resist? This distinction—I do not now ask why
those who resist God's will do so, but how, from among those equally incapable
of obeying, some nevertheless obey and others resist—must arise either from
human beings themselves or from God's giving the grace to believe to some but
not to others. No one will say it arises from human beings unless he is wholly
or partly Pelagian. It remains, therefore, that it comes from God's mere will:
although he could destroy all, he saves only some through mercy, and indeed
whomever he pleases. But all this has nothing to do with this latter
objection. Its force always remains: the outcome of that supreme divine will
by which he destined some to dishonor is altogether immutable and necessary,
even through contingent intermediate causes.

Moreover, if that interpretation holds, this passage will contain a simple
argument from comparison, as when Psalm 2 says the Lord will break the wicked
like a potter's vessel, and when Jeremiah says that the Jews, however proud
and swollen, will be broken like a flask. But the matter itself here shows
that the Apostle so argues from comparison that there is also an allegorical
analogy: the potter represents God, the creator of human beings; the clay
vessels, the human race; destination to honor, destination to gratuitous
salvation; and destination to dishonor, destination to destruction.

Furthermore, by what plausible reasoning will the words “to fashion vessels
for dishonor from a lump of clay” be taken to mean “to permit them to be
hardened”? Hardening, which belongs to a vessel already corrupted, necessarily
presupposes in the order of causes—for we do not ask about temporal order—not
only corruption but also creation, since what does not exist cannot be
corrupted. Indeed, the term *lump* does not even apply to a corrupted vessel,
because it is not yet a vessel, but a lump of clay.

Finally, since the Apostle expressly says that both kinds of vessels were
fashioned from the same lump, and since the term *lump*, as we just said,
applies not to vessels already fashioned but to those to be fashioned, who
does not see that God's supreme, eternal, and immutable decree is set before
us here, which precedes creation itself in the order of causes? Before creating
the human being, God had to determine within himself both to create him and
the end for which he would create all the human beings thereafter propagated
from him; the foreknowledge of corruption was not the antecedent cause of his
decree. From all this I think everyone sees how poorly this interpretation of
the passage fits the proposed objection and the Apostle's response.

[Verses 22–23]

**But what if God, willing to show his wrath and make his power known, endured
with great patience vessels of wrath prepared for destruction, so that he
might make known the riches of his glory toward vessels of mercy, which he
prepared for glory?**) This is the Apostle's third answer to the preceding and
final objection. Its sum is that we must not stop with subordinate ends—the
honor of some and the dishonor of others—when determining the equity or
inequity of the divine decree, which in refuting the preceding objection the
Apostle vindicated from all injustice on both sides. We must proceed all the
way to the ultimate end. If it is established that this divine decree has not
only a powerful, wise, and just author, but also an excellent end and outcome,
no place remains for reproving such a decree as unjust. The Apostle declares
this end to be threefold. One is that, by justly sentencing those fashioned
for dishonor—only, of course, as corrupt and wicked—to the severest eternal
penalties, God may demonstrate his supreme power, which never strays from
Justice. Another is that in the very display of his severity he may also reveal
his leniency to some extent, since he very often endures for a long time even
the vessels prepared for dishonor and allows them in this life to enjoy the
the vessels prepared for dishonor and allows them in this life to enjoy the
the vessels prepared for dishonor and allows them in this life to enjoy the
greatest

[Printed page 431; PDF page 31; OCR object 0446]

benefits, although they abuse them unworthily. The third is that, from the
comparison of contraries—the comparison of his most severe judgment, which the
Apostle treats more fully below in 11:22, with the supreme kindness he employs
toward vessels prepared for honor—the magnitude of his mercy may appear.

Thus the Apostle completes the entire discussion of the foundation of Faith's
most solid firmness—that is, the eternal Predestination of those whom God saw
fit freely to attain salvation, while the others were destined by just
judgment. He proposed it by examples in both the calling of the Gentiles and
the rejection of the Jews and completed it by application of the thesis. He
did so with care: having established the chief doctrine from clear testimonies
of Scripture, he reduced whatever human wisdom can bring forward against it
to two objections, as though to two chief heads. The first is directed against
the intermediate causes through whose intervention God executes his eternal
decree; the latter is aimed against God's eternal and immutable decree itself.

But here again we must refute what some bring against this certain and clear
interpretation of the passage.

“Forbearance,” they say, “does not apply to things which we ourselves knowingly
and willingly make and approve. Therefore, since God endures the vessels of
wrath with great gentleness of mind, it follows that he does not make them,
but, as the Apostle says, bears or endures them.” This objection contains many
errors. First, there is a manifest equivocation in the words “vessels come to
be.” They can be taken of the creation of the first human being in whom his
whole posterity was created. They can also be understood of the first human
being's corruption, in whom all people are born children of wrath, not indeed
by destination but by the corruption of their nature and insofar as all sinned
in him (Rom. 5:12; Eph. 2:3). Third, it can be understood of the birth of each
person destined to Reprobation, and finally it can also refer to the actual
rebellion of each reprobate. All these must be distinguished so that we may
understand in what sense the conclusion is true or false.

I therefore say that not I, but the Apostle—or rather God's own Spirit—clearly
says that God is the creator of both kinds of vessels, and indeed from the
same lump. Thus if *vessels of wrath* are taken as vessels destined to wrath,
and the verb *to come to be* refers to that first creation of the human race in
one human being, it would be no less blasphemous to deny that the vessels of
wrath were made by the very one who fashioned the vessels of mercy than to
attribute to the reprobate themselves the quality of being unbegotten, or
Deity, since one God alone is from himself, unbegotten and without beginning.

Lest someone reply that they were indeed made vessels in Adam but not vessels
of wrath, since wrath presupposes sin, which does not come from Creation, I
answer what I have said both before and now. God, the supremely wise craftsman,
did not begin to deliberate about the end of that first human being and the
outcome of each one to be propagated from him after creating him, nor did he
consider him as already created and fallen. Rather, in the order of causes,
before creating him God determined with a constant and immutable purpose the
end for which he would create him and then the intermediate causes through
which he would bring them to the appointed end. It follows that all the
reprobate are rightly and truly said to have been made in Adam simultaneously
and at the same moment, as God determined from eternity, not only as vessels,
that is human beings, but also as vessels of wrath: human beings destined to a
just condemnation which would follow contingently through intermediate causes
inhering in the reprobate themselves, but would occur altogether necessarily
from God's decree.

But if the phrase “vessels of wrath come to be” is referred either to the
first-formed man's voluntary fall, in whom the whole human race propagated
from him is comprehended, or to each person's native corruption, or to the
actual rebellion of individual reprobates—since the author of this
interpretation uses “come to be,” not “were made”—I say that this objection has
nothing to do with either the Apostle's aim or our interpretation. The Apostle
does not treat the execution of the divine decree or the intermediate causes
of its execution, whose efficient principle, source, and cause, as we have now
so often repeated, is the spontaneous motion of the evil human will. He treats
the divine decree itself; it is sheer madness to seek its antecedent cause
anywhere but in God himself, and within God to scrutinize rather than adore
it.

By the same reasoning that stale calumny about Stoic necessity, so often cast
at us, vanishes. The Apostle himself would be guilty of it if it were truly
directed against us, since in refuting this objection taken from the necessary
outcome of the divine will discussed here he concedes both that it is God's
will and that this will cannot be resisted. The Stoics bound not only human
wills, or the principle of human actions, but everything done and occurring
with the chains of their Necessity. They did so in such a way that they did
not subject intermediate causes to God but rather bound God himself to
intermediate causes. Instructed by God's Word, we indeed confess that God's
will cannot be deceived or changed and that absolutely nothing occurs rashly.
But we subject intermediate causes to God's will, which moves them with free
will according to their own nature and orders, varies, and changes both their
order and effects when and how he pleases and as he decreed from eternity,
rather than being himself subject to some prescribed order. And since by
God's ordination the human will is the intrinsic and spontaneous principle of
human actions, we teach that their contingent effect, insofar as it concerns
the actions themselves, does not at all conflict with the necessary outcome
of the divine decree, to which the will voluntarily and spontaneously, not
under compulsion, accommodates itself.

Let Corruption with its fruits, therefore, arise from the spontaneous
defection of the human will from rectitude; let just judgment against sinners
come from God as avenger of sins; and let the just destination to this just
judgment come from God, necessary as concerns its outcome but compelling no
one. It both shuts the mouths of the reprobate themselves, none of whom God
con-

[Printed page 432; PDF page 32; OCR object 0447]

demns except those deserving condemnation; and it supplies to the freely
Elect, Called, Justified, and Glorified a singular occasion for considering
and acknowledging the greatness of his mercy.

Yet the chief knot of that objection still seems not to have been untied:
namely, that tolerance does not concern things which we ourselves knowingly
and willingly do and approve; from which it would follow that vessels of wrath
are not made by God but tolerated. I recognize the argument drawn from
Castellio's cesspools, but it is altogether leaden. First, we have often
already said that vessels of wrath are said to be made by God, not insofar as
they deserve God's wrath in themselves—partly on account of native corruption,
which by itself makes everyone a child of wrath, and partly on account of the
fruits of corruption, namely sins—but insofar as they are destined for wrath.
Yet is not this too a signal instance of God's mildness and patience toward
those destined for wrath: that he does not destroy them immediately from their
very birth, though destined for perdition and already lost in themselves, but
permits them to enjoy both the use of this life and many other benefits, making
his sun rise upon good and evil? Yet God must not be thought to do this for the
reason imagined by Castellio, as though God hoped for or awaited their
repentance. Repentance is God's own purely gratuitous gift (2 Tim. 2:25), and
is not even offered to everyone. Rather, he does it in order both to reveal his
mildness, which proceeds slowly toward the severity of judgment (whence it is
called *longsuffering*, μακροθυμία), and so that, after filling up their
measure and being more and more convicted by the testimony of their own
conscience, they may at last, as Peter says in Acts 1:25, depart to their own
place—that is, to the perdition destined for them from eternity and finally
assigned to them in fact by God's just judgment. For just as they cannot
repent, so too they do not will to repent. God himself is not the author of
this “not-willing,” so to speak, nor of a propensity toward every evil that is
contrary even to the law of nature; its author is the spontaneous motion of
the human will, corrupted from within itself.

Secondly, the Apostle does not say that vessels of wrath *are made*, that is,
as we have said, are destined for wrath, through God's tolerance—for who could
be tolerated who does not yet exist? Rather, though destined from eternity to
just destruction and deserving just condemnation from their very birth, they
are tolerated with singular mildness. This is so much the case that, as the
Wise Man testifies, it is impossible to judge from the prosperous or adverse
circumstances of this life whom God hates and whom he loves (Eccles. 9).
Failure to observe this drove the Epicureans to deny God's providence and
greatly exercised even the holiest men, as is evident from Psalm 73, other
passages in the same work, and the prophet Habakkuk.

Finally, what could be more absurd than to oppose tolerance to what someone
knowingly and deliberately does and approves, when, on the contrary, no one
who endures something against his will is properly said to tolerate it?

But let us hear another objection. God, they say, is said by the Apostle
*actively* to “prepare beforehand” (προετοιμάσαι) vessels for glory, whereas
vessels for dishonor are said with a *passive* verb to be “fitted out”
(κατηρτισμένα) for destruction—namely, not by God but by Satan and themselves.
I recognize Chrysostom's observation on this passage. Although it produces a
true meaning when rightly understood, it nevertheless rests on a very weak
foundation. Passive words are also everywhere applied to the Elect, as when
they are called “called,” “beloved,” and “shown mercy” (κλητοί, ἠγαπημένοι,
ἠλεημένοι); are they these things, I ask, from themselves? Conversely, when
those destined for Destruction are said by Jude to have been “long ago written
down beforehand” (πάλαι προγεγραμμένοι), that is, long ago enrolled for
judgment, are they said to have been enrolled by themselves? The Syriac
interpreter therefore rightly rendered each of those expressions passively in
this passage.

And why, I ask, should we go any farther, when nothing more absurd can be said
than that some vessel is “fitted out” (κατηρτισμένον), that is, constructed
and joined together, not by a potter but by itself?

Moreover, in treating both vessels for honor and vessels for dishonor, the
Apostle compares God with a potter and the vessels with human beings, not as
already fashioned but as to be fashioned for contrary uses—not as already
created, much less corrupted, but as some to be created for destruction and
others for glory. He uses a single active verb, “to make” (ποιεῖν), not
repeated but common to both clauses. It must therefore be understood in
exactly the same way in each clause as regards the potter's destination of
them. Then, you will say, just as he is the author of the causes of salvation,
that is, faith and its fruits in his Elect, will he also be considered the
author of the causes of destruction, that is, corruption and sins in the
reprobate? Far be this blasphemy from us. As we said earlier from Augustine,
unless it had been good that Evils should also exist, God would not have
permitted them to exist; he certainly permitted them, not unwillingly but
willingly. And sins, insofar as they are punishments of preceding sins, are
inflicted by God as a just judge. Yet he must not be considered either the
efficient or assisting cause of any Evil or any iniquity, since it takes its
beginning from the motion of the human will, which spontaneously and without
compulsion moves itself toward Evils—for where there is compulsion, there is
no will. But how often have we now said that it is one thing to inquire about
the eternal and immovable decree of God, which justly orders all inferior
causes, and quite another to treat the intermediate causes through which he
executes that eternal decree at their appointed times?

In his reply to this second objection, therefore, the Apostle declares that no
cause of this decree can be assigned other than pure will—a will that is never
tyrannical but always best and wisest in and from itself. But in dissolving
the former objection, the same Apostle gave us the causes of the decree's
execution, disclosed in God's Word, experience itself, and even the perception
of our own conscience. For those whom he has destined for honor and glory, the
cause is gratuitous mercy in Christ; for those whom he has likewise destined
for destruction, it is hardening, and consequently the corruption and sins in
which they are justly hardened. Thus, after this whole discussion has been
explained both positively and hypothetically, Paul rightly exclaims, “O depth
of the riches!” That exclamation would certainly have no place unless the
supreme and ruling cause of the divine decree concerning vessels destined for
dishonor no less than vessels destined for honor were God's will alone. This
will infinitely surpasses our comprehension, since it is nothing other than
God himself, yet it is always in and from itself

[Printed page 433; PDF page 33; OCR object 0448]

the wisest and best, taking the norm of justice from nowhere else but assigning
it to all secondary and intermediate causes insofar as they are ordered by it.

Finally, we say that the Apostle's following, very clear, judgment requires
this conclusion: just as God's glory is declared in gratuitous mercy toward
the elect, so also in that most just severity toward the reprobate, whereby the
supreme God reveals himself in fact as merciful toward some and as the stern
avenger of Evil toward others. Therefore, in the decree of Election as well as
in that of Reprobation, we must necessarily consider that supreme will of God,
not dependent on intermediate causes but ordering them and leading them to
their appointed ends. A certain new interpreter of this passage objects that
God had no need of this manifestation of his severity in vessels of wrath, and
that it was therefore unnecessary, for the declaration of his power in
judging, that some should be destined as vessels for dishonor. For, he says,
God gave the fullest proof and example of his power and severity both in the
fallen angels and in the death of his Son, which the Son endured most bitterly
for us, so that there might be no reprobates among humankind but all might be
saved in Christ. I reply that this objection is absurd in itself and impious
in its consequence. For what, after all, has God ever done because he needed
it in order to reveal his glory? Far be it from us to think or speak in this
way. Let the author of that objection therefore see against whom he directs
it. Instructed by God's express Word, we teach that God made everything for
himself, even the wicked for the evil day (Prov. 16:4), not because he needs
any created thing, good or evil, or because the salvation or destruction of
anyone makes him more glorious in himself, but because it seemed good to him
to reveal his glory by declaring both his mercy and his severity even among
human beings. Let that man dispute as much as he likes; I, by contrast, will
rest in this one conclusion.

As for the assertion that Christ underwent the punishments of our sins by
God's counsel so that there might be no reprobates, but all—that is, every
human being who would ever be born—might believe in him and be saved in him, I
say that its evident falsehood is proved both by Christ's own words and by the
many impious consequences that necessarily follow from it. “I know,” says
Christ, “whom I have chosen” (John 13:18), and, “I do not pray for the world,
but for those whom you have given me, and for those who will believe in me
through their word” (John 17:9). Nor is Christ said to have been sent so that
every individual should believe in him, but so that whoever believes in him
should not perish but have eternal life. Tell me, then: does faith belong to
all, that is, to every individual? The Apostle denies it in 2 Thessalonians
3:2. Indeed, Faith has always belonged to very few in comparison with the
rest. But is not a disposition or aptitude for Faith offered to all? By no
means. Faith comes from hearing, and hearing from the voice of one preaching;
but that voice is by no means sent to every individual. God is known in Judea,
and he did not act thus with every nation (Ps. 76:2). Nor does Christ say that
all are called, but many. Indeed, Christ does not even say that as many are
elect as are called, but that many are called and few chosen.

What more? Even if God's Word did not here shed light for us, does not the
reality itself teach by countless daily examples in every age that people have
died, die every day, and in all probability will continue to die to whom the
Gospel was never offered in any way?

In saying this I neither accuse God—whose immense mercy I rather admire even
in this, that he offers the grace of the Gospel to even one human being—nor do
I excuse human beings, for whose condemnation native corruption alone is
sufficient even without contempt of the Gospel. I rebuke that utterly false
dogma from which so many false and absurd consequences follow and by which,
above all, Pelagianism is necessarily established. Swept into this precipice,
the wretched Castellio wrote not only that everyone is born with a free power
to embrace the Gospel, but also asserted at one time that there will be no
eternal punishment for those to whom the light of the Holy Spirit in the
Gospel was not offered, and at another that he did not know. From this it
necessarily follows either that eternal life exists outside Christ—and hence
that what Christ himself says in John 17:3 is false—or that none are farther
from the danger of eternal death than those who have never heard anything of
Christ. See, then, where those men are finally carried who nevertheless rebuke
us as investigators of God's secrets.

On the proofs of predestination as certainly known, to be applied to oneself by
each person.

“Those whom he also called”—namely, “us, not only from the Jews but also from
the Gentiles.” Verse 24.

Nothing has obscured the doctrine of God's eternal Predestination as much as a
confused and disordered treatment in which the doctrine itself and its use or
application are discussed together and at the same time, even though these
two questions differ greatly from one another and should therefore be taught
distinctly in the Church. Paul, the best and wisest master of theological
method, especially in this epistle, observed this carefully. For until now,
having treated the entire topic of Predestination from verse 6 of this chapter
through verse 23, he now at last demonstrates how it is to be applied to
individuals, or from where and how the firmness of our salvation is to be known
by us—which is the true use of the preceding doctrine. And he preserves
exactly the same method of teaching which we showed him to have followed in
treating the doctrine itself: first positively in the words, “those whom he
also called,” and then hypothetically. In the case of the Elect he begins from
the words “us, not only from the Jews but also from the Gentiles,” and
continues through verse 27. In the case of the reprobate, from verse 27 he sets
forth the Rejection of the greater part of the Jewish nation and continues
through the concluding verse 32 of chapter 11. There he at last concludes the
whole preceding disputation, begun at verse 16 of the first chapter, with a
most divine epiphonema extending to the beginning of chapter 12; from there he
proceeds to explain the practice and use of the whole Christian doctrine with
no less exact a method.

The sum of this Apostolic teaching concerning assurance of our Election and,
consequently, our future glorification is this: we must seek it not in that
most profound abyss of the eternal divine decree, which surpasses the
comprehension even of all

[Printed page 434; PDF page 34; OCR object 0449]

the angels, but from the means through which God himself most certainly reveals
to our minds through our ears that decree which has been hidden in itself from
eternity.

This revelation is most meaningfully declared by the name *Calling*. For this
Calling is, as it were, a spoken testimony proceeding from God's own mouth,
than which nothing can be more certain or explicit, since God is truth itself.
Now the Son of God, who alone must be heard by us as interpreter of the divine
will for salvation, no longer speaks with us himself as he did when he spoke on
earth, but by the power and efficacy of his Holy Spirit. And again, the Holy
Spirit does not speak into our ear essentially or in his own person
(αὐτοπροσώπως), as the fanatics and enthusiasts dream, but through the Pastors
and Teachers sent by the Son for this very purpose (John 20:21; Eph. 4:11) and
appointed by the Holy Spirit, that is, by a legitimate calling (Acts 20:28).
Thus, by the designation “Voice” or “Calling of God,” we are to understand the
ordinary ministry of the Gospel: the voice of the Gospel sounding in God's
Church through human ministry and the administration of the Sacraments,
sanctioned by God's ordinance.

This must not, however, be understood as though God's power were simply and
without exception bound to human ministry. God can also teach people
immediately (ἀμέσως) and endow them with a most abundant knowledge of his
mysteries; nor is it as though no one could become a true Christian through
private reading and meditation upon the divine writings. Rather, God has
instituted this order of sacred ministry for calling and sealing his Elect,
and we ought to rest in it, neither seeking miracles nor readily believing
them.

Nor, again, must this be understood as though a human voice could change
anyone's stony heart into flesh, implant in it Faith that embraces the promises
of salvation, and insert delight in God's Law. For this entire work of the
second creation—which is signified in John 6:44 by the term *Drawing*, and from
which we are called new men and New creatures—belongs to the divine power alone
which created the human being when he did not yet exist. Rather, the Holy
Spirit, by his own power, which cannot be communicated even to the angels,
engraves on our minds what the words uttered by the minister of the Word
signify to our ears and what, in addition, the Sacraments announce to our other
senses. He simultaneously renders those minds teachable and effectually
instructs both the understanding and the will, so that what Isaiah says may be
truly fulfilled, that all are taught by God. God opens our hearts so that we
not only attend but also apply to ourselves with certain Faith what we have
heard through our ears—a Faith which, by the same power of God, we testify to
as far as we are able by hatred of Evil and zeal for what is Right.

We must therefore take the greatest care here against a twofold precipice:
first, lest with superstitious magicians and idolaters we transfer to a human
being what is the work of God alone; and second, lest with the enthusiasts we
distinguish God's ordinary work in his Elect from the human work so far as to
separate them too, since Distinction and Separation differ greatly from one
another. I speak of the Elect, and at that only at the time when it has seemed
good to God to reveal his Election in them and to cure their former ignorance,
whether simple or deliberately cultivated, and consequently their obstinacy
and rebellion, as the unbroken experience of every age makes clear.

As for the reprobate, it must be fully admitted that the inward ministry of the
Holy Spirit is sometimes not at all joined with the outward ministry of human
beings, as happens to those for whom the preaching of the Gospel is nothing but
foolishness or a stumbling block. At other times they are indeed joined, but
not to the point that the Spirit, by that true Faith which distinguishes the
Elect from the reprobate as their proper mark, grafts those hearers into that
Vine of ours as branches who will embrace for themselves in particular the
doctrine of salvation offered to them, adhere inseparably to the vine, and be
fruitful. The cutting off of such branches is treated in Matthew 12:45, John
15:2, 1 Corinthians 10:5, Hebrews 6:4 and 10:26, 2 Peter 2:13 and 22, and often
elsewhere. Nor does it follow from this that God mocks people or is a
dissembler, unless he inwardly offers through the Holy Spirit to every person
the grace which he outwardly offers through external ministry. We refuted that
objection earlier in its place.

There is therefore a twofold calling from God. One is the inward calling of the
Spirit through the outward ministry of human beings, effectually to draw the
hearers. The other is an outward calling made only through human beings, so
that those devoted to just destruction may be rendered more and more “without
excuse” (ἀναπολόγητοι).

Consequently, not every outward Calling is that to which the Apostle recalls
us as the most certain assurance of our eternal Election, but only that which
is joined with the inward efficacy of the Spirit, as the Lord says, “Many are
called, few are chosen.” The difference between these callings is shown by the
very word *Spirit*. For by “Spirit” here is meant a twofold effect of the Holy
Spirit, proper and peculiar to the Elect alone. The first is that true Faith,
that excellent gift of God by which the individual Elect particularly embrace
the promises of eternal life offered both in the Word and in the Sacraments
through the gratuitous propitiation in Christ—that is, the testimony of the
Holy Spirit which is testified to our spirit through both the Word and the
Sacraments. The second is our Renewal, by which we begin not only to think but
also to will and do the things of God, as the new man gradually grows and the
old man slowly dies.

From these two effects, which are most closely conjoined and never to be
separated, the efficacy of calling is both perceived inwardly by ourselves and
revealed outwardly by external effects. Thus we can no more doubt that eternal
decree concerning our particular Election than if we had seen God himself from
eternity with our own eyes, consciously decreeing this concerning our
salvation. Nor can we doubt his aid in every temptation any more than if we saw
him present and coming to our help; nor, finally, can we doubt our glorification
which will follow in its own time any more than if we already sat together with
him in Heaven.

[Printed page 435; PDF page 35; OCR object 0450]

Hence come the things taught in Romans 5:2, 5; 8:9, 16, 25, 38; 1 Corinthians
2:10–11; 2 Corinthians 1:22; Hebrews 4:16; 1 John 2:5; 3:10, 19, 24; 4:13;
5:10; and everywhere else. Hence too Paul's exhortation in 2 Corinthians 13:5:
“Test yourselves, whether you are in the faith; prove yourselves. Do you not
recognize yourselves”—namely, that Jesus Christ is in you? And hence Peter's
admonition in 2 Peter 1:10: “Therefore, brothers, be diligent to make your
Calling and Election firm”—that is, firm in our minds.

In sum, just as from truly good works we ascend to the gift of Sanctification,
and from Sanctification to Faith, which coheres with it by an unbroken bond and
shines no less surely than fire, so from those certain effects we infer not
just any Calling but the effectual one; from this Calling, Election; and from
Election, finally, Predestination in Christ, as firm as God's throne is
immovable. We do so by the most certain connection of effects and causes. Once
our anchor is fixed there, borne up by the hope which never puts us to shame,
and victorious through God's Spirit after overcoming the storms of temptation,
we press on until we escape into the harbor of glorification. This doctrine of
Predestination is therefore the sole foundation of our Faith and Hope. It must
be applied to us in precisely this manner: by the same steps by which the
Apostle said in Romans 8:30 that God descends to claim us, we in turn ascend to
him.

Those therefore sin gravely who omit even a single step of this ladder, whether
ascending or descending. Such are those who, although they ought to begin from
the lowest step—that is, from the fruits of Regeneration—in order to understand
the declaration of eternal election, mount by a single leap to God's eternal
purpose. Such too are those who, omitting the fruits of Faith, dream that they
possess true faith; those who, omitting both faith and its fruits, rise to the
outward Calling as though being outwardly called were sufficient testimony of
Election; those who pass from their own works, which they dream to be good, to
God's purpose as though Election flowed from their foreseen works—that is, who
place at the bottom what is highest and at the summit what belongs below; and,
finally, those who make foreseen Faith the cause of God's decree concerning
those to be Chosen.

The error is likewise plain of those who slander this doctrine and reason as
follows: if those destined for salvation will necessarily be saved, and those
destined for destruction will necessarily perish, there is no reason for us to
care about the Lord's Word, Faith, or good works. On the contrary, salvation,
which is the completion of this divine decree, is reached only by Faith,
repentance, and the fruits of repentance as the means subordinated to the
execution of Election. And, I ask, why do these people not also conclude that
they should neither eat nor drink, since the Lord has appointed the days of
everyone, which no mortal can pass beyond?

Not unlike this is the insanity of those who think that this doctrine imbues
people with a certain security and renders them sluggish in good works because
we teach that those who have once truly believed can never fall away. We
certainly teach from God's Word that the gift of true Faith is as irrevocable
(ἀμεταμέλητον) as God's eternal decree is neither reversible, unfulfilled, nor
deceptive (οὐ παλινάγρετον, οὐδὲ ἀτελεύτητον, οὐδὲ ἀπατηλόν), as even otherwise
profane poets proclaim. But at the same time we add that, just as those endowed
with true faith never fall away, so they are accustomed, through serious
hearing and meditation upon God's Word, through an ardent zeal for both piety
and love, and with prayers added, to nurture the Faith and Repentance begun in
themselves; or—as very often happens in grievous temptations—to recover them
when interrupted for a time and “rekindle” (ἀναζωπυρεῖν) them when, as it were,
put to sleep, as our Apostle says. To this end belong Paul's exhortations that
we work out our own salvation with fear and trembling (Phil. 2:12), and his
other exhortation that we not grow weary in running until we reach the finish
in the world to come; and Peter's admonition that, having once escaped the
corruption in the world, we be diligent to make our Calling and Election firm,
that is, by the testimony of the fruits of Faith to confirm ourselves more and
more in the full assurance of our Election.

Those who do not do this should consider by what right they claim true Faith
for themselves. For how, after all, could Faith pacify our hearts unless, by
looking to Christ, the head of all the Elect, and being truly recognized in us
from the effects we have described, it carried us up to the very citadel of
that immovable and eternal divine decree concerning our salvation in Christ
and at last rested there? And in the gravest temptations or even falls, what
could restore our spirits and stir up in us through the Holy Spirit those
unutterable groans, except that assurance of a particular—particular, I
say—Election, since a particular Faith is necessary to each person for
salvation?

It is no objection that at times—whether because God thus tests us or because
of our negligence—the effects of Sanctification once begun in us are so feeble
and slight that they seem almost nonexistent, and the testimony of the Holy
Spirit sounds so faintly within us that it is scarcely heard. Indeed, sometimes
even the recollection of God himself terrifies rather than comforts us, as we
see happened to David himself in Psalm 77:4. For that same Psalm supplies us
with the surest remedy against this temptation, bidding us remember former
times and rely on the immovable firmness of God's promises. We shall experience
that, however gravely his Elect have fallen through their own fault, God, just
as he casts them down to hell for a time, also brings them back from hell at his
appointed time, endowing them with the Spirit of repentance. This always
happens, and always will happen, to all the Elect. Nor is this contradicted by
the saying, “Let him who stands take heed lest he fall”; by the parable of
those whose latter state is worse than the former (Matt. 12:45); by what Peter
writes of those who return to their vomit (2 Pet. 2:22); or by what Paul writes
of those who fall away from the faith (1 Tim. 4:1), as though the pledge of
true Faith were not certain and irrevocable (ἀμεταμέλητον), as some wrongly
infer. For if this were so, it would follow that

[Printed page 436; PDF page 36; OCR object 0451]

God's counsel is changeable or dependent upon us; that Faith is not the proper
gift of the Elect; and that our consciences cannot be pacified by Faith. All
these propositions are so false and absurd that I consider them to require no
refutation. We therefore acknowledge that true Faith can often be interrupted,
put to sleep, as it were, and seemingly lost as regards its effects.
Nevertheless, after even the smallest spark of it has once been given to one
of the elect, we believe that even then it remains asleep, as it were, beneath
the ashes, and is again stirred and kindled by the Holy Spirit as by a kind of
fan. In just the same way, those drunk with wine or overtaken by lethargy have
not the mind itself restored to them, since it was never taken away, but the
use of the mind. Just as God gives perseverance, however often interrupted, to
all to whom he gives true Faith—for he never abandons his work (Ps. 138:8) nor
ever permits his own to be tempted beyond their powers (1 Cor. 10:13)—so too he
appoints the means through which they persevere and, however grievously fallen,
are restored. Those exhortations look to this end; they do not warrant the
inference that the Elect can fall away as regards God's certain purpose. As for
the others, endowed not with true but with temporary (προσκαίρῳ) Faith, it is
no wonder that they fall from the degree they had reached and procure for
themselves a correspondingly graver judgment, since they never belonged to
that true Election and engrafting into Christ. For 1 John 2:19 stands firm: “If
they had been of us, they would surely have remained with us.” So much, then,
for the Elect.

As for the Reprobate, great caution is needed here. Although faith and unbelief
(ἀπιστία) are opposed, it is not permissible simply to take unbelief as a proof
of Reprobation in the same way that true faith is to each person a certain sign
(τεκμήριον) of his Election. If by unbelief (ἀπιστία) we mean the absence of
Faith, it is not proper to the reprobate alone but common to every individual
human being from the very beginning of this life. For of ourselves we cannot
even think the things of God (2 Cor. 3:5), nor does flesh and blood reveal
Christ to us (Matt. 16:17). But if by unbelief we mean that obstinacy which is
added to native corruption and causes some people, though hearing, not to hear,
and voluntarily to close their eyes against the truth presenting itself to
them—as the greater part of humankind is accustomed to do—then this too is at
first common to called reprobates and called elect alike. Yet it remains so
only for a time in the elect, until it is healed by God's grace when, sooner or
later as he pleases, he opens the hearts of his elect.

The following conclusion through the effects of true Faith, with the Holy
Spirit as its author, is therefore most certain: “I believe; therefore I am
Elect in Christ from eternity, and, justified in him, I shall be saved.” But
far be it from the following conclusion to be true: “I am ignorant of Christ,
or do not believe the Gospel, and I perceive no effects of Faith in myself;
therefore I am reprobate and shall be condemned.” For which even of the Elect
was born with knowledge of Christ, and how many believed the Gospel
immediately? This argument is no more necessary than if someone at midnight
reasoned: “The sun has not risen; therefore it will not rise.” Had this answer
occurred to those who met the wretched Spiera while he labored in that despair,
they surely would have restored him—if only he had been capable of healing.

What then? Since the Elect themselves, who were unbelievers, become endowed
with faith by God's grace, sometimes even at the final breath and instant of
life, we would wrongly conclude from anyone's present unbelief and lack of any
perception of the effects of Faith that he is Reprobate, since no one is
Reprobate except the one who dies an unbeliever. Rather, let the person whom
this temptation troubles take courage. Let him persuade himself that he must
hear the Word of Faith set before him, even against his inclination; let him
pray even against his inclination; and let him not think it random that he is
called while so many thousands of other people are passed over. Once he has
attained the point of perceiving his unbelief and beginning to be displeased
with himself on its account, let him recognize that he has placed one foot on
the way of salvation. And let him not cease until he obtains what he seeks,
namely true repentance and Faith. If he has once resolved on this, his labor
will certainly never be in vain, since scarcely anyone who is not of the number
of the Elect has ever resolved to do it. As for what some cite in support of
the contrary position from Hebrews 12:17 concerning Esau, I say they understand
it wrongly. That passage does not concern obtaining repentance from God;
rather, Esau is said to have pleaded with his father in vain to revoke the
blessing which the father had bestowed on Jacob through Jacob's fraud. In this
matter he was repulsed by his father, although he had sought it from him with
tears.

On the use of the doctrine of Predestination as it concerns ministers of God's
Word and others judging concerning one another.

Since, as the Apostle says, no one truly knows what belongs to a person except
the person's spirit which is in him (1 Cor. 2:11), and since Faith alone,
through the Holy Spirit, apprehends the mind of Christ—that is, the counsel and
decree of our salvation in Christ—it necessarily follows that every Elect
person is properly conscious to himself of his own Election. Nevertheless, the
Lord has entrusted to his ministers called to this work the tasks of
instructing, correcting, comforting, and reproving, which cannot be done
without some knowledge of the inner person. Moreover, we are bound to help one
another when walking rightly and to recall wanderers to the way, as both the
common Calling of all Christians and the particular Calling of individuals
requires. It is therefore rightly asked whether, and to what extent, this
doctrine concerning the application of Predestination can have a place in
discerning others. I answer that this too is a great use of the doctrine, but
it must be employed with great caution. Since a tree is to be evaluated by its
fruits, we must help and diligently exhort—and indeed confirm more and more in
the testimony of their Election—those whom from their words and deeds we have
recognized as good trees, planted as it were by God's own hand and watered by
the Spirit. Yet we must do so in such a way that we always ex-

[Printed page 437; PDF page 37; OCR object 0452]

hort them to perseverance and constancy in repentance and diligently admonish
them that what is within should correspond to what is outward, lest perhaps we
be deceived here or, rather, they deceive themselves. For we can make only a
probable judgment concerning the particular Election of others. Those wholly
ignorant of the Gospel mystery must be instructed kindly and patiently from
God's Word; the Apostle also instructs us to bear gently with those weak in
Faith. Those who stubbornly resist must be pressed more sharply. Finally, those
who abuse their knowledge of the Gospel must be severely rebuked by setting
before them both the threats of the law and those written in the sacred
Scriptures against despisers of the Gospel, together with God's dreadful
judgments. They must even be recalled, if possible, to the right way by the
application of the gravest ecclesiastical censures, as though by a kind of
force; this is the final remedy. Thus, finally, we must deal even with every
most obstinate sinner in such a way that we do not utterly despair even of
those in whom the marks of Reprobation rather than Election appear, since the
mercy of the Lord our God is immense even at the final breath of life. Here too
many things often happen beyond all human hope. Yet John did not rashly warn
that there is a certain sin unto death, for which one ought not to pray because
it is never forgiven (1 John 5:16). We would have no need of this teaching if
that sin were never manifest to us in anyone; Julian the Apostate furnished a
clear testimony of it.

But because judgments of this kind are most dangerous, it is safer to set these
things forth generally to terrify even the most wicked than to apply them
individually to anyone. We see that Christ did this when he spoke indefinitely
of that horrible sin against the Holy Spirit. Yet without doubt Christ knew
perfectly well to whom it applied, not only insofar as he was God but even
insofar as, as man, he was endowed with a singular light of wisdom; he could
even have marked them by name as persons set before his eyes. Paul likewise
speaks indefinitely: “I wish,” he says, “that those who trouble you would be
cut off” (Gal. 5:12). So too, in this very passage, the Apostle discusses the
rejection of the greater part of the Jews and sets before us in that most
sorrowful example the cause of their actual Rejection, namely incurable
contempt for the Gospel, yet he identifies no one by name. And lest anyone
think that what we have said about God's immovable eternal decree is overturned
when Paul says there that the Jews, having been cut off, will eventually be
restored, while the Gentiles, now grafted in, will be cut off again if they are
proud, one must remember that the passage does not concern particular Jewish or
Gentile individuals but the peoples themselves considered universally. Their
condition is evaluated by what any given nation professes to embrace in
general. It is indeed true that at one time a nation formerly rejected for its
obstinacy is affected by God's mercy, and that, conversely, those who have
abused God's grace are repudiated by God's just judgment, while God's decree
concerning particular persons—whether to be chosen and saved or reprobated and
destroyed—always remains immovable. I acknowledge, however, that the same
Apostle so curses Alexander the smith in 2 Timothy 4:14 that he sufficiently
shows he is to be regarded as once for all Reprobate and condemned. Similar
examples occur in David and elsewhere. But these must not be turned into
examples for imitation, since it is more probable that those holy men of God,
endowed with a measure of the Spirit far different from ours, spoke thus from
either a particular revelation or an impulse of the Holy Spirit, than that they
intended to furnish us with an example to imitate.

Now, in order that the whole method of the dogmatic part of this Epistle, from
verse 16 of chapter 1 through the end of chapter 11, embracing the sum of the
Christian religion delivered both most divinely and most learnedly, may be set
before our eyes, we have thought it good to arrange this entire treatment in
the following theses in their order.

ON JUSTIFICATION

First thesis.

I.

Righteousness and salvation, or eternal life, necessarily cohere.

II.

Every individual human being is guilty before God both of impiety toward God
and of injustice toward other human beings.

III.

Therefore, either every individual human being must perish or they must borrow
this righteousness from someone else.

IIII.

Christ, true God and man (Rom. 1:3–4), is the one in whom this righteousness
inheres. It is therefore to be sought in him alone, formerly promised and now
manifested.

V.

The efficient and principal cause (κύριον αἴτιον) of obtaining this
Righteousness by gratuitous imputation is the Holy Spirit. Its outward
instrumental cause is the preaching of God's Word (Rom. 1:21), and its inward
instrumental cause is Faith. Its formal cause is Imputation, corresponding in
the opposite direction to that imputation by which all our sins, all our guilt,
and, finally, all the corruption of our nature were imputed to Christ. Its
material cause is Christ himself; its final cause is the glory of God, at once
supremely just and merciful.

VI.

The first part of this righteousness of Christ, by the gratuitous imputation of
which alone we are justified and saved, consists in the punishments by which
Christ most fully expiated all our sins, having received them upon himself, so
that in him alone we might obtain their absolute remission.

VII.

The second part of this righteousness consists in Christ's obedience, or the
perfect fulfillment of the whole law on our behalf, which is opposed to all
our native guilt and to our other innumerable sins,

[Printed page 438; PDF page 38; OCR object 0453]

since we are bound not only not to sin but also to obey God fully.

VIII.

The third part of this same righteousness is the most perfect restoration of
our very nature, assumed by Christ in our flesh. It will shortly be discussed
in the eleventh thesis.

IX.

This righteousness, inhering in Christ as its subject, is followed by an
unbroken bond by sanctification in us. It is begun by the same causes which we
recounted above in thesis five, and it is likewise opposed to our corruption,
which is also called the old man.

X.

The parts of this sanctification begun in us are the mortification of our
corruption; its burial, that is, the progress of the abolition already begun;
and, finally, the beginning of the new man, which is called vivification and
Spiritual resurrection. These three parts correspond to three objects—namely,
Christ's death, burial, and resurrection—represented to us by the visible
Symbols of baptism.

XI.

Because this sanctification begun in us heals the native corruption of our
nature only to some extent, we therefore have an absolute need of a very
different restoration of that same nature, so that we may stand without
condemnation before God's tribunal, where perfect integrity, to the extent a
created thing can possess it, is necessarily required. This restoration, like
the preceding parts of true righteousness, exists in the one and same Christ,
who was made man for us so that, after utterly abolishing sin, he might restore
human nature most completely. It is imputed to us in that same Christ, into
whom we are grafted by Faith, so that by gratuitous imputation we obtain most
fully in Christ all that righteousness which God thus requires from us.

XII.

This sanctification begun in us therefore does not absolve us before God in any
respect—so far is it from meriting the crown of life in any way. It is only the
most certain witness of our engrafting into Christ and, consequently, of our
gratuitous absolution in him, however harshly we may be afflicted in this life.

XIII.

Since this righteousness is bestowed only on the Elect through firm Faith, it
necessarily has the gift of perseverance conjoined with it. Thus all who have
obtained this righteousness in Christ through Faith must finally be glorified.
For the salvation of believers rests, strictly speaking, not upon their faith,
but upon God's eternal and immutable purpose to save the Elect apprehended by
Faith in Christ; from that purpose proceed both Faith itself and all its
effects.

ON PREDESTINATION

Thesis I.

God's covenant to grant eternal life in his Christ is indeed most certain and
most firm. Yet it is not destined even for all those born in the Church from
holy parents, much less does it extend to those who are complete strangers and
will remain forever outside the Church.

II.

From eternity and by his will alone, which precedes everything even as regards
the order of causes, and consequently without regard to any foreseen worthiness
or unworthiness, God destined only those whom it seemed good to him—whether
individual human beings or even whole peoples—some to love and salvation, and
others to hatred and destruction.

III.

Yet God cannot on this account be judged unjust toward either group. Although
by his will alone and without regard to any worthiness in them he destined
those whom it seemed good to him to love and salvation, nevertheless by that
eternal decree he in fact loves and saves them only through the righteousness
gratuitously imputed in Christ, by the steps we have already described.
Conversely, insofar as by his will alone and without regard to any unworthiness
in them God destined from eternity those whom he pleased to hatred and
destruction, he nevertheless does not in fact hate or destroy them except as
they deserve hatred and destruction through their own corruption or even their
untamed obstinacy. For it is one thing to destine to love, and another in fact
to love and save; it is likewise one thing to destine to hatred, and another in
fact to hate and destroy. Between destination and its execution intervene the
intermediate causes ordained by God, which acquit him of every appearance of
injustice.

IIII.

This decree of God is altogether immutable and therefore cannot be broken by
any human efforts. Yet God is not on that account angry with the Reprobate
without their deserving it, nor is his eternal and immovable decree to be
regarded as unjust.

V.

First, God as Creator has supreme right over all things to be created by him.
He cannot even will to abuse that right, since his will is the sole and most
certain rule of everything just and right. Human beings who answer back to God
in this matter ought therefore to be ashamed, just as if clay arrogated to
itself the role of judge.

VI.

Secondly, only by this course, which he follows both in destroying the wicked
and saving those justified in Christ, does he reveal himself, as he truly is,
in the best and wisest way: supremely merciful—especially from the comparison
of severity toward some with mercy toward others—and supremely just. Thus he
also exercises his just judg-

[Printed page 439; PDF page 39; OCR object 0454]

ment against the wicked while not permitting even them to be entirely without
his beneficence.

ON THE PARTICULAR APPLICATION OF PREDESTINATION TO THE ELECT

Theses drawn not only from the context of Paul but also confirmed methodically
by several certain arguments.

Thesis I.

Whatever counsels and decrees of God he wills human beings to understand are
known either *a posteriori*, or insofar as their effects lie hidden in natural
causes, or from his own revelation. They are known *a posteriori* when
something happens and has now come to pass, and we then understand that God had
previously decreed that it should happen. They are known from effects latent in
natural causes when, seeing smoke somewhere, we infer that God has decreed a
fire. These natural causes are either necessary by hypothesis, that is, by
God's ordinance—as when the times of eclipses of the Sun and Moon are known—or
they are uncertain and wholly contingent, in which case they cannot produce
certain knowledge. Revelation may be simple and universal, as Noah knew that
the flood would come and we know that the last day will come; or universal as
regards an entire people, city, or family, as the Lord foretold many such
things through the Prophets; or indefinitely universal, as salvation is
promised to every believer, from which we know that all who believe will be
saved; or definitely particular, as Abraham knew that the Messiah would be
born from him, Moses that he was destined to be the people's deliverer, and
Saul and David that they would be Kings. God has revealed to human beings many
things of this kind, whether pertaining to the course of this life or to the
heavenly kingdom.

II.

The eternal decree concerning the Election of individuals to eternal life will
in fact be known *a posteriori* only when the Elect have been glorified. Nor
can it be understood from natural causes, because it does not depend on natural
causes but upon God's eternal, supernatural will. It must therefore be known
from revelation; yet revelation considered only universally and indefinitely
would not pacify the consciences of individuals.

III.

If the mode of this revelation were particular and personal—that is, if the
eternal decree of Election had to be revealed separately by God himself to
every individual—God in turn would have to appear to as many people as there
are Elect people. That this has not pleased him is clear from the fact that he
has instead willed a certain common method and mode of revealing his eternal,
salvific counsel to exist in the Church, common to the whole Church: namely,
the preaching of the divine Word and the administration of the sacraments.

IIII.

Yet that God does not will by this method, insofar as it is common to the whole
Church, to reveal universally the decree of his Election to every individual
is clear from the fact that not all who hear are Elect, but only those who hear
and believe.

V.

The method of knowing this decree must therefore be general to some extent,
insofar as all the Elect are called to their salvation through hearing God's
Word and the administration of the Sacraments; and, again, it must to some
extent be particular, insofar as it is effectually directed to individual
persons.

VI.

Hence we make that Revelation which is called *calling* twofold. One is
outward, performed by human ministry and common to all hearers. The other is
particular and personal: besides being outward it is also inward and coheres
with the outward calling—lest anyone invent private enthusiasms for himself
outside God's preached Word. It is the work not of human beings but of the Holy
Spirit, wholly proper and peculiar to the Elect alone, whom he draws in this
way.

VII.

The outward Calling is therefore perceived by the bodily senses; the inward is
engraved on the mind by the Holy Spirit and is recognized from two effects
which follow perpetually and necessarily. The first is the audible testimony
of the Holy Spirit, as though speaking audibly in our hearts, that we are
children of God, so that we boldly cry, “Abba, Father.” The Apostle furnishes
the fullest testimony of this assurance in Romans 8:17 and following. The
second consists in this: our understanding is enlightened by knowledge of the
truth, and our will is changed so that it begins to will and do what is good.

VIII.

Thus we have the testimony of our Election sealed in our hearts. Just as we do
not investigate one another's thoughts in another person's mind, since we
cannot see into it, but discern them from speech and words, so too we would
scrutinize in vain God's eternal decree concerning our salvation hidden, as it
were, in God's own breast. It must be understood only from his own voice
sounding in the Church and made effectual in our minds, as we have said.
Therefore, just as he descends from on high all the way to us through the
intermediate causes which Paul explains in Romans 8:29–30, so we must be borne
from effects to causes and to the supreme grace, all the way to God himself,
and fix our anchor there: from the effects of Regeneration to Justification;
from Justification to true Faith; from true Faith to effectual Calling; and,
finally, from effectual Calling, rising to that eternal and immovable decree of
God concerning our salvation. From it we may conceive that certain hope of our
victory and glorification in Christ against every temptation.

ON THE APPLICATION OF THE DOCTRINE OF PREDESTINATION TO THE REPROBATE

Thesis I.

The doctrine concerning opposites is indeed the same,

[Printed page 440; PDF page 40; OCR object 0455]

which is why we said that the account of the eternal divine decree is exactly
the same and equal in the destination both of Election and of reprobation. But
when we come to the intermediate causes through which God actually executes
that decree, their account differs, even though they are opposed to one
another. Consequently, the same conclusion must not be established concerning
them.

II.

Faith as known from its effects, just like effectual Calling from which we
ascend to the eternal decree of Election, comes wholly not from nature but from
God's grace alone. It is an irrevocable (ἀμεταμέλητον) gift of God peculiar to
the Elect alone. Corruption, by contrast, the mother of unbelief (ἀπιστία) and
all sins, comes from nature—not, however, from nature as it was created, but as
it voluntarily and spontaneously made itself, through no fault of the Creator,
although not apart from his decree. Moreover, it is common both to the Elect
and the Reprobate until the Elect are regenerated, and consequently it is not
perpetual in everyone.

III.

This conclusion is therefore most firm and as immovable as God's own truth: “I
truly believe; therefore I am Elect.” But far be it that the following should
be absolutely true: “I do not believe, or I perceive no effects of Faith;
therefore I am Reprobate.” That is inferred no less foolishly than if you said,
“The sun has not risen; therefore it will never rise,” or, “This drunkard does
not reason; therefore he will never reason.”

IIII.

Reprobation is therefore not necessarily inferred from unbelief, from the
gravity or multitude of sins, or from any obstinacy, however stubborn. It is
inferred only from that unbelief (ἀπιστία) and stubbornness which perseveres
until the final breath, for the Lord sometimes delays the gift of Faith and
Repentance until then.

V.

Nevertheless, those who neglect to enter the way of Faith and salvation must
be diligently admonished, both by themselves and by others, especially those
upon whom God has imposed this burden; otherwise they will perish. For even
though Reprobation is not inferred necessarily and absolutely from unbelief
(ἀπιστία) and its fruits, on account of God's immense mercy, nevertheless, on
account of the wholly uncertain end of this life and God's just judgment, it
is probably and almost necessarily inferred that the workers of iniquity will
perish and find the doors shut (Matt. 7:23; Ps. 5:21; 95:7–8).

VI.

The exception is that sin against the Holy Spirit which is unto death and
therefore the most certain sign (τεκμήριον) of Reprobation and condemnation.
But it is discerned by God rather than by human beings, so it is plainly safer
to leave it to God than to apply it individually to anyone without exception.

END.

The text of Moses therefore proves that the figures of speech in this passage

chastisement of the God who shows mercy. Then God will choose no one, and no
place is left for election; only the freedom of choice remains to accept or
reject mildness and wrath. But when God is stripped of the power and wisdom of
choosing, what will he be but an Idol of fortune, by whose divinity everything
happens at random? At last it comes to this: people are saved and condemned
without God's knowing it, for he has not distinguished by certain election
those to be saved and condemned. Rather, after offering to all both general
mildness that tolerates and hardens and Mercy that rebukes and punishes, he has
left it to human beings whether they wish to be saved or condemned. Meanwhile,
perhaps, he has gone to the Ethiopians' banquet, as Homer says. Aristotle too
depicts such a God for us, one who sleeps and permits anyone to use and abuse
his goodness and corruption. Nor can reason judge otherwise about him. Just as
reason itself scorns and despises divine things, so it judges God as if he too
scorned them and, abandoning the wisdom, will, and presence of choosing,
distinguishing, and inspiring, had entrusted to human beings the busy and
troublesome work of accepting and rejecting his mildness and wrath. This is
where we arrive when we wish to measure and excuse God by human reason, when we
do not revere the Majesty's secrets but penetrate them by scrutiny, so that,
with his glory crushed, we vomit out a thousand blasphemies in exchange for one
excuse, etc.

And page 494a.

Thus it happens that, while we do not permit God's will alone the will and
power to harden, show mercy, and do all things,

we attribute to free choice itself the ability to do everything without grace,

The cause of the false interpretation of these words, “I will harden,” etc.
The same page.

Let us examine the reasons for inventing a figure of speech in this passage.
“It seems absurd,” says the *Diatribe*, “that God, who is not only just but
also good, should be said to have hardened a human heart in order to illustrate
his power through that person's wickedness. And Origen acknowledges that God
gave the occasion for the hardening, though he casts the blame upon Pharaoh.”
The answer shortly follows.

Absurdity, then, is one of the chief reasons why the words of Moses and Paul
are not to be understood simply. But against which article of faith does that
absurdity sin? And shortly thereafter: By the same argument you will deny every
article of faith because it is by far most absurd that God should be a man, the
son of a virgin, crucified, and seated at the Father's right hand. It is absurd
to believe such things. Let us therefore invent figures here too, with the
Arians and Manichaeans, lest Christ be simply God or true man; in this fine
manner we shall handle the Scriptures.

But the figures do not even help to avoid the absurdities. For in the judgment
of reason it remains absurd that a just and good God should demand
impossibilities from free choice and impute to it the fact that it cannot will
the good. Nor does he act any more gently or mildly by not conferring the
Spirit than if he hardens or permits hardening. Reason will dictate that these
things do not belong to a good and just God, because it wishes to touch and see
how he is good and not cruel. It would indeed understand if it were said of
God, “He hardens no one and condemns no one, but has mercy upon all and saves
all”—that is, if hell were destroyed and the fear of death removed so that no
future punishment need be feared. Therefore reason fumes and contends in this
way in order to excuse God and defend him as just and good. But faith and the
Spirit judge otherwise, believing God good even if he were to destroy every
human being.

And page 494b.

What difference does it make whether free choice is hardened or deserves to be
hardened, when hardening necessarily inheres so long as that impotence remains
by which it cannot will the good without grace?

And shortly thereafter: How God works evils in us.

It is asked how God is said to work evils in us, such as hardening, handing us
over to desires, leading astray, etc. We should surely have been content with
God's words and believed simply what they say, since God's works are utterly
indescribable. Yet in deference to reason—that is, to human folly—we choose to
speak foolishly, become foolish, and try by stammering to see whether we can
move it in some way.

And page 495a.

First, even reason grants that God works all things in all and that without him
nothing happens or is effectual. For he is almighty, and this belongs to his
omnipotence, as Paul says to the Ephesians. Now Satan and fallen humanity,
abandoned by God, cannot will the good—that is, the things which please God and
which God wills—but, turned toward their own desires, continually seek what
belongs to themselves. Their will and nature, thus turned away from God, are
not nothing; will and nature remain in both. And these are no less subject—as
creatures and works of God—to omnipotence and divine action than are the other
creatures and works of God. Here you see that when God works in and through the
evil, evil things indeed occur, yet God does not do evil. Because he is good,
he cannot do evil, although he uses evil instruments which cannot escape the
force and motion of his power. The defect is therefore in the instruments,
which he does not allow to remain idle. Evils occur when God moves them no
differently than if a craftsman were to cut badly with a jagged and toothed ax.
For the wicked person cannot but err and sin when, moved by the force of divine
power, he is not permitted to remain idle but wills, desires, and acts in a
manner corresponding to what he himself is. These things are fixed and certain
if we believe that God is almighty and that the Wicked person is God's
creature, yet, turned away and left to himself, cannot will or do the good
without God's Spirit. God's omnipotence causes the wicked person to be unable
to escape God's motion and action, but necessarily to obey as subject to him.
His corruption, or aversion from God, causes him to be incapable of being moved
or driven well. God cannot cease his omnipotence on account of that person's
aversion, while the wicked person cannot change his own aversion. Thus he
necessarily sins and errs until he is corrected by God's Spirit, etc.

And shortly thereafter: Whence hardening comes.

The matter of hardening is as follows. The wicked person, like Satan his
prince, is wholly turned toward himself and his own things. He does not seek
God or care about the things of God, but seeks entirely his own resources and
glory, his own works and kingdom, and wishes to enjoy them in peace. If anyone
resists him or wishes to diminish any of these things, the same aversion by
which he seeks them moves him too: he is indignant and rages against his
adversary. He can no more refrain from raging than he can refrain from seeking
and desiring; and he can no more refrain from desiring than from being God's
creature, albeit a corrupted one. This is that rage of the world against the
Gospel.

And shortly thereafter, on the same page, column b.

This very provocation of the wicked, when God says and does what is contrary to
them, is their hardening and aggravation. For they become worse when their
aversion is resisted or something is taken from it. Here God, about to wrest
his tyranny from the wicked Pharaoh, provoked him and hardened him further and
aggravated his heart when he assailed him through the word of Moses and did not
give him the Spirit inwardly, but permitted his wicked corruption, with Satan
reigning, to become inflamed, rage, and proceed with security and contempt.

And shortly thereafter.

Therefore, when God is said to harden or work evil in us—for to harden is to do
evil—let no one think this means that he creates evil in us anew, etc. Rather,
whoever wishes to understand these things at all should think that God works
evils in us, that is, through us, not through God's fault but through our
defect. For since we are evil by nature and God is good, when he drives us by
his action according to the nature of his omnipotence, nothing else can happen
than that he, being good, should do evil with an evil instrument, although in
his wisdom he makes good use of this evil for

[Printed page 444; PDF page 44; OCR object 0459]

his own glory and our salvation. Thus, finding Satan's will evil, though not
creating it—for it became evil when God abandoned and Satan sinned—God seizes
it by his operation and moves it wherever he wills, although that will does not
cease to be evil through this very motion of God. In this sense David says in
2 Samuel 16 that the Lord had commanded Shimei to curse him. Yet there was no
outward command by which God ordered so virulent and evil a work as cursing.
David therefore looks to the fact that the almighty God spoke and things were
made—that is, that he does everything by the eternal Word.

And shortly thereafter.

Thus he hardens Pharaoh when he presents to Pharaoh's wicked and evil will a
word and work which it hates through its innate defect and natural corruption.
And because God does not inwardly change that will by his Spirit but continues
to present and thrust these things upon it, while Pharaoh, by contrast,
considers and trusts in his own strength and power, Pharaoh becomes puffed up
and proud, is hardened, and grows more and more provoked the more Moses presses
and threatens. Yet his evil will would not move or harden itself by itself; but
because the almighty actor drives it with an inevitable motion, as he does the
other creatures, it must will something. And because at the same time God
outwardly presents what naturally provokes and offends it, Pharaoh cannot avoid
his hardening, just as he cannot avoid either the action of divine omnipotence
or the aversion—that is, the wickedness—of his own will. Thus Pharaoh's
hardening is accomplished by God, etc.

And shortly thereafter.

Having therefore rejected human figures and glosses, we understand God's words
simply, without any need either to excuse God or accuse him of injustice. For
he who says, “I will harden Pharaoh's heart,” speaks simply, as if he said, “I
will cause Pharaoh's heart to be hardened, or to be hardened through my working
and acting.” Inwardly, by a general motion, I shall move the evil will itself
so that it proceeds according to its own impulse and course of willing.
Outwardly, I shall present a word and work against which that evil impulse will
strike, since it can do nothing but will evilly, while I by the power of
omnipotence move that evil itself, etc.

And page 497a.

Hence Moses repeats after almost every plague, “And Pharaoh's heart was
hardened, so that he did not release the people, as the Lord had spoken.” It is
as though he said that the Lord, who had foretold Pharaoh's hardening, might
appear truthful. If there had been any changeability or freedom in Pharaoh
which could have gone in either direction, the Lord could not have predicted
his hardening with such certainty. But since the one who can neither be
deceived nor lie promises it, his hardening necessarily had to occur. This
would not happen unless his hardening lay wholly beyond human powers and in
God's power alone, in the manner stated above: namely, God was certain that he
would not omit the general operation of omnipotence in Pharaoh, since he could
not omit it.

Why God does not cease the motion of omnipotence; why he does not change wicked
wills; why he permitted Adam to fall; and why he created us impure. Page 496a.

Someone may ask why God does not cease that very motion of omnipotence by which
the will of the wicked is moved so that it continues to be evil and grows
worse. The answer is that this is to wish that God would cease to be God on
account of the wicked, since you desire his power and action to cease—that is,
that he should cease being good lest anyone become worse.

But, they will ask again, why does he not simultaneously change the evil wills
which he moves? Answer: this belongs to the Majesty's secrets, where his
judgments are incomprehensible. It is not ours to ask this but to adore these
mysteries. If flesh and blood, offended here, murmurs, let it murmur; it will
accomplish nothing, and God will not therefore be changed. Although the wicked,
scandalized, depart, the Elect will remain. The same answer will be given to
those who ask why he permitted Adam to fall and why he created us all infected
with that same sin, when he could have preserved Adam and created us from
elsewhere or from seed first purified. The reason is that he is God: there is
no cause of his will, nor is there any reason prescribed to it as a measure and
rule, since nothing is equal or superior to him. Rather, his will itself is the
rule of all things. For if there were some rule, measure, cause, or reason for
it, it could no longer be God's will. What he wills is not right because he
ought or had to will it in this way; on the contrary, because he so wills,
whatever occurs must be right. Cause and reason are prescribed to a creature's
will, but not to the Creator's will, unless you set another creator over him.

Another objection and its solution. Page 498b.

What offends that common sense or natural reason is, of course, that God by his
will alone abandons, hardens, and condemns human beings, as though he delighted
in the sins and such great and eternal torments of the wretched, although he is
preached as possessing such great mercy and goodness, etc. It has seemed
unjust, cruel, and intolerable to think this of God, etc.

Yet natural reason itself, which is offended by that necessity, is forced by
its own judgment to concede it, convicted even without any Scripture. For all
people have this judgment written in their hearts, recognize it, and approve
it, though unwillingly. First, God is omnipotent not only in power but also in
action; otherwise he would be a ridiculous God. Second, he knows and foreknows
all things and can neither err nor be deceived. Once these are conceded, they
are forced by an inevitable consequence to admit that we come to be not by our
own will but by necessity; likewise, we do not act as we please by the right of
free choice, but as God foreknew and acts by infallible and immutable counsel
and power, etc.

On Moses's statement in Genesis 25, “The elder will serve the younger.” Page

The *Diatribe* evades this passage by saying that it does not properly concern
human salvation. For God can will that a person should be a slave and poor yet
not be rejected from eternal salvation.

Answer: Let it be so. Even if that passage has nothing to do with human
salvation but is understood of temporal ser-

vitude—which is itself not true—it is nevertheless cited rightly and
effectually by Paul to prove that it was said to Sarah, “The elder will serve
the younger,” not through the merits of Jacob or Esau but through the one who
calls. For Paul disputes whether they attained through the power or merits of
free choice what is said of them, and by the invincible words of Scripture
proves that they did not, but that it came by the grace alone of the one who
calls, since they had not yet been born and had done nothing good or evil, etc.

And shortly thereafter, page 501b.

How did free choice help Jacob? How did it harm Esau, when by God's
foreknowledge and destination each, not yet born and having done nothing, had
already been assigned the things he was to receive: namely, that the former
should serve and the latter rule? Rewards are determined before the workers
are born and work.

And shortly thereafter.

The question now is not whether that servitude pertains to salvation, but by
what merit it is imposed upon the one who had not deserved it.

Furthermore, that Moses does not treat servitude alone, and that Paul rightly
understands this passage of eternal salvation, is proved from the text itself.
For the oracle in Moses says: “Two peoples will be divided from your womb, one
people will overcome the other, and the elder will serve the younger.” Here two
peoples are plainly distinguished. The one, though younger, is received into
God's grace so that it may conquer the elder, not by its strength but by God's
favor; otherwise, how could the younger conquer the elder unless God were with
it? Since, therefore, the younger will be God's people, the passage treats not
only outward rule or servitude but everything pertaining to God's people:
blessing, the Word, the Spirit, the promise of Christ, and the eternal kingdom.
Scripture later confirms this more fully when it writes that Jacob is blessed
and receives the promises and the kingdom. Paul briefly indicates all these
things when, sending us back to Moses's fuller treatment, he says that the
elder will serve the younger.

On the passage of Malachi, “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated.” Page 500b.

The *Diatribe* twists this passage by three devices. The first is: “If you
press the letter,” it says, “God does not love as we love, nor does he hate
anyone, since affections do not befall God.”

And page 501a.

Answer: What do I hear? Is the question how God loves and hates, rather than
why he loves and hates? We know perfectly well that God does not love or hate
as we do, since we both love and hate changeably; he loves and hates by an
eternal and immutable nature. Thus accidents and affections do not befall him.
And it is precisely this that forces free choice to be nothing: that love is
eternal and immutable; that hatred toward human beings existed eternally,
before the world was made and not merely before any merit and work of free
choice; and that everything occurs necessarily in us according as he either
loves or does not love from eternity. Thus not only God's love but also his
manner of loving imposes necessity upon us, etc.

But grant that God's loving is an effect of love and his hating an effect of
hatred: do those effects occur apart from and beyond God's will? If not, they
occur only by God's willing, so that what God wills he either loves or hates.
By what merit, therefore, is Jacob loved and Esau hated before they are born
and act? Paul therefore stands firm, most aptly introducing Malachi in support
of Moses's judgment: namely, God called Jacob before he was born because God
loved him, not because Jacob first loved God or moved him by any merit, etc.

The second device is that the passage does not appear to speak of the hatred by
which we are condemned eternally but of temporal affliction.

Answer: What has this to do with the matter, since Paul proves that this
affliction was inflicted upon Esau without merit and by God's hatred alone? For
what cause did God have to love Jacob and hate Esau when they did not yet
exist? Moreover, it is false that Malachi speaks only of temporal affliction or
the destruction of Edom. He reproaches the Israelites for their ingratitude:
although God loved them, they in turn neither love him as Father nor fear him
as Lord. He proves that God loved them both from Scripture and from his work.
Although Jacob and Esau were brothers (Gen. 25), God nevertheless loved and
chose Jacob before he was born, but hated Esau so as to reduce his region to a
wilderness. Likewise he brought Jacob back from captivity and restored him,
but did not permit the Edomites to be restored and threatened them with
destruction, etc.

And shortly thereafter, page 501b.

How can the claim now stand that the Prophet speaks of temporal affliction,
when in evident words he testifies that he speaks of two peoples born from two
Patriarchs: the former received as a people and preserved, the latter abandoned
and finally destroyed? To receive as a people, or not to receive as a people,
does not pertain only to temporal goods or evils, but to all things. For our God
is not only God of temporal things but of all things, etc.

The third device is that God is said to hate the not-yet-born because he
foreknows that they will do things worthy of hatred; hence the Jews were cut
off by the merit of unbelief and the Gentiles grafted in by the merit of faith,
etc.

Answer: We are not disputing cutting off or grafting in, but whether people
can believe or disbelieve by the power of free choice. We are not asking who
believe and who do not, nor what follows believers and unbelievers, but by what
merit and work they attain the faith by which they are grafted in or the
unbelief by which they are cut off. Here Paul teaches that this comes to pass
through no work of ours, but only through God's love and hatred. Once it has
come to pass he exhorts them to persevere lest they be cut off. But an
exhortation proves not what we can do but what we ought to do.

On Paul's passage in 2 Timothy 2, “In a great house there are gold and wooden
vessels,” etc. Page 502.

Then follows the similitude: there are different vessels, some for honor and
others for dishonor. This completes the teaching that vessels do not prepare
themselves, but the master does. This is also the meaning of Romans 9, that the
potter has power, etc. And the words which follow, “If anyone cleanses himself
from these,” are understood not of filth but

[Printed page 446; PDF page 46; OCR object 0461]

of vessels of dishonor, so that the meaning is: if anyone remains separate and
does not mix with wicked teachers, he will be a vessel of honor, etc. Page 503.

Page 502a.

For it does not follow that anyone can therefore cleanse himself by the power
of free choice. In sum, if this evasion is valid, Paul's entire disputation has
no force. He would introduce in vain those who murmur against God the potter if
the fault appeared to belong to the vessel and not the potter. For who would
murmur if he heard that someone worthy of condemnation was condemned? But this
is the source of the murmuring of human reason: namely, if a vessel which has
earned nothing except that it does not belong to its own right is cast into
eternal fire, the blame must be imputed not to the vessel but to the potter who
creates and tempers the clay. This is what Paul makes the wicked say: “Why does
he still find fault? Who resists his will?” This is what human reason can
neither comprehend nor bear. It wants God to act by human law, to do what seems
right to it, or to render an account of why he is God. God must thus be reduced
to order and laws prescribed to him, so that he may condemn no one except the
person who, in our judgment, has deserved it, and so that the potter may make a
vessel for dishonor from preceding merits, etc.

But if God works in this way, looking to merits, why do they murmur and
complain? Why does Paul need to restrain them? Furthermore, where does the
potter's power to do what he wills remain if, subject to merits and laws, he is
not permitted to do what he wills but is required to do what he ought? For
regard for merits conflicts with the power and freedom to do what one wills,
as that master of a household proves when he opposed the freedom of his will
over his own goods to the workers who murmured and demanded a right (Matt. 20).

But let us suppose that God must be such as to regard merits in condemning.
Shall we not likewise contend and concede that he should regard merits in
saving too? If we wish to follow reason, it is equally unjust to crown the
unworthy and to punish the undeserving. We shall therefore declare God unjust,
as one who delights in the evil and wicked and invites and crowns their
wickedness with rewards. But woe to us, so wretched, before such a God. For who
will be saved?

See the wickedness of the human heart. When God saves the unworthy without
merits—indeed, when he saves the wicked despite many demerits—it does not
accuse him of injustice or complain why he wills this, although even by its own
judgment it is most unjust. Rather, because this is pleasing to it, it judges
it equitable and good. But when he condemns the undeserving, because this is
displeasing to it, it declares it unjust and intolerable; here it complains,
murmurs, and blasphemes.

Reason therefore judges this matter not according to equity but according to
affection. For if it regarded equity, it would complain against God equally
when he crowns the unworthy and when he condemns the undeserving. It would
equally praise and proclaim God when he condemns the undeserving as it does
when he saves the unworthy. For in either case there is equal injustice if you
look to our perception—unless it were not equally unjust to praise Cain for
murder and make him king as it would be to cast innocent Abel into prison and
kill him.

Since reason praises God for saving the unworthy but accuses him for condemning
the undeserving, it is proved that it does not praise God as God, but as one
who serves its own advantage. That is, it seeks and praises itself and its own
things in God, not God and the things of God. But if God pleases when he crowns
the unworthy, he should not displease when he condemns the undeserving. If he
is just there, why will he not be just here? There he pours grace and mercy on
the unworthy; here he pours wrath and severity on the undeserving. In both
cases he is excessive and unjust before human beings, but just and true before
himself. For how it is just for him to crown the unworthy is incomprehensible
now; but we shall see when we arrive there, where it will no longer be believed
but seen with unveiled face. Likewise, how it is just for him to condemn the
undeserving is incomprehensible now, yet it is believed until the Son of Man is
revealed.

Conclusion of the whole disputation concerning the condemnation of the wicked
and God's justice.

But if what troubles you is the difficulty of defending God's clemency and
equity when he condemns the undeserving—that is, wicked persons of this kind,
who, born in wickedness, can by no means provide for themselves so as not to
be, remain, and be condemned as wicked, and who are forced by natural necessity
to sin and perish, as Paul says, “We were all children of wrath, like the
rest,” since they are created such by God himself from seed corrupted through
the sin of the one Adam—then the answer is this. Here the most merciful God
must be honored and revered in those most unworthy persons whom he justifies
and saves, and something must be granted to his Divine wisdom, so that he is
believed to be just where he seems unjust to us. For if his Justice were of
such a kind that it could not be judged just by human comprehension, it plainly
would not be divine and would differ in no way from human justice. But since he
is the true and one God, and moreover wholly incomprehensible and inaccessible
to human reason, it is fitting—indeed, necessary—that his justice also be
incomprehensible, as Paul too exclaims, “O depth of the riches of the wisdom
and knowledge of God! How incomprehensible are his judgments, and unsearchable
his ways!” (Rom. 11). They would not truly be incomprehensible if we could
fully grasp why they are Just. What is a human being compared with God? etc.

And page 525a.

If, with nature herself as teacher, we confess that human power, strength,
wisdom, knowledge, and all our things are nothing when compared with divinity,
why, I ask, do we vex only God's justice and judgment? Why do we arrogate so
much to our judgment that we wish to comprehend, judge, and evaluate the divine
judgment? Why do we not likewise say here, “Our judgment is nothing when
compared with the divine judgment”? Consult reason itself: once convicted,
will it not be compelled to confess itself foolish and rash because it does
not permit God's Judgment to be incomprehensible, although it admits that every
other divine thing is incomprehensible? Evidently, in all other things we grant
God divine majesty; only in judgment are we prepared to deny it. We cannot for
even a little while believe him just, although he has promised that, when he
reveals his glory, we shall all then see and palpably perceive that he was and
is Just. I shall give

[Printed page 447; PDF page 47; OCR object 0462]

an example to confirm this faith and console that evil eye which suspects God
of injustice. See how God governs this corporeal world in external affairs: if
you regard and follow the judgment of human reason, you are forced to say
either that there is no God or that he is unjust—“I am often troubled,” as the
poet says, “to think there are no gods”—because the wicked fare most
prosperously and the good, by contrast, most unhappily, as proverbs and
experience testify (Job 21; Ps. 73). Is it not most unjust in everyone's
judgment that the wicked prosper and the good are afflicted? Yet so runs the
course of the world. Indeed, not only have the greatest intellects fallen so
far here as to deny that God exists and to imagine that fortune turns all
things at random, but even the Prophets Jeremiah, Job, David, and other godly
people who believed God existed were still more tempted concerning God's
injustice. Yet this temptation and apparent injustice of God is most easily
removed by the light of the Gospel and the knowledge of grace, through which
we are taught that the wicked indeed flourish bodily but perish in soul, and
that there is a life after this life in which whatever is not punished and
rewarded here will be punished and rewarded there, etc.

Thus the light of the Gospel, which operates through the Word and faith alone,
accomplishes so much that this question, treated in every age and never solved,
is so easily resolved. What do you think will happen when, the light of the
Word and faith having ceased, the divine Majesty is revealed through himself?
Do you not think that the light of glory will then very easily solve this
question which is insoluble in the light of the Word or grace, since the light
of grace so easily solved the question insoluble in the light of nature? Set
before me three lights: the light of nature, the light of grace, and the light
of glory, etc.

Page 525b.

In the light of nature it is insoluble how it is just that the good person is
afflicted and the wicked fares well. But the light of grace dissolves this. In
the light of grace it is insoluble how God condemns one who by all his own
powers can do nothing but sin and be guilty. Here both the light of nature and
the light of grace dictate that the blame belongs not to the wretched human
being but to the unjust God, who gratuitously and without merits crowns one
wicked person but does not crown another, perhaps less wicked or at least no
more wicked, but condemns him. Yet the light of glory dictates otherwise and
will then show that God, whose judgment is now of incomprehensible justice,
possesses only the most just and manifest justice. Meanwhile, let us believe,
warned and confirmed by the example of the light of grace, which accomplishes
a similar miracle in the light of nature.

Here I think more than enough has been done for the godly person who will yield
to truth without obstinacy. If we believe it true that God foreknows and
preordains all things, then his foreknowledge and predestination can neither be
deceived nor hindered. Further, if nothing happens unless he wills it—which
