Fifth, the fact that Paul denies justification to works, but calls them works
of the law, may seem to support this view. Calvin thinks this moved Chrysostom,
Origen, and Jerome to accept it.

The answer is ready. Paul denies justification not only to works of the law
but absolutely to works: “not from works” (Ephesians 2:9; Romans 4:2, 4–5).
That Abraham was not justified by works is proved from the nature and merit of
a work as a work, not as ceremonial. If justification is not from works
because a wage is owed to a work by merit, ceremonial works are excluded not
because they are ceremonial but because they are works. Since this applies
equally, indeed more, to moral works, they too are excluded.

Calvin adds a fitting answer: Paul called them works of the law because works
are righteous before the Lord insofar as we seek through them to render him
worship and obedience. To strip all works more explicitly of power to justify,
he named those most capable of justifying, if any could: works of the law,
whose promises alone give works value before God. Thus Paul does not exclude
only ceremonial works, but all works, especially moral ones. This argument
therefore does not prove their cause but destroys it.

Objection 6. Finally, throughout this argument Paul deals with Jews or
Judaizers and disputes against justification from the Mosaic law which they
wished to retain. Hence immediately before concluding in Romans 3:28 that a
man is justified by faith without works, he asks in verse 27, “Where is your
boasting?”, addressing the Jew who boasts and rests in his law (2:17).
Therefore he seems to treat only their Mosaic law and deny justification only
to its works.

I answer. Paul had proved not only that Jews violated the Mosaic law but that
Gentiles violated natural law,

[1679 printed p. 112 | IA leaf 120]

and concluded that neither—indeed no one—is justified by works of any law,
but by faith in Christ alone.

Even if he treated only the Mosaic law, it was moral as well as ceremonial,
and the Jews boasted intensely in both. Denying justification to the whole
Mosaic law therefore does not affect ceremonial works alone.

As for “your” in “Where is your boasting?”, that pronoun is added by the Latin
Vulgate and retained by Ambrose and Jerome, who take Paul as addressing a Jew.
Greek fathers do so as well. Yet Origen plainly did not understand ceremonial
law alone, for he enumerates among works of the law righteousness, chastity,
wisdom, and the other moral virtues. Moreover, “your” does not appear in the
Greek text, even in Chrysostom or Theophylact, though their Latin translators
inserted it.

Grant nevertheless that Paul addresses a Jew and teaches that every occasion
for boasting in his law and works has been taken away. This cannot prevent
Paul's conclusion—justification by faith alone without works—from being
understood generally of works of ceremonial, moral, or any other law. The
whole sequence proves it. Chapter 1 makes Gentiles guilty of violating their
natural law; chapters 2–3 make Jews guilty of violating their given law. Thus
both are under sin: “Have we any advantage? Not at all; for we have already
charged both Jews and Greeks that all are under sin” (Romans 3:9). Paul then
burdens the Jews with scriptural accusations in verses 10–18, applies them in
verse 19, and concludes in verses 20 and 28 that no flesh is justified—neither
Gentiles by works of natural law nor Jews by works of their Mosaic law,
ceremonial or moral. Even if “your” is added, the question about boasting
serves to prove the syllogism's minor premise, that Jews are guilty, not to
limit its general conclusion: no human being is justified by works of any
law, not merely the Mosaic ceremonial law.

Since the Mosaic law has arisen, I add one final point to finish this
controversy and prepare the next. Even if Roman Catholics understood “works
of the law” as works of the entire Mosaic law insofar as Mosaic, they would
not prove what they most desire: that although Jews could not be justified by
works of the Mosaic law, Christians are justified by the same works insofar
as they flow from faith and grace. This will appear later. Their former view,
that ceremonial works alone are excluded, has now been sufficiently refuted
and rightly condemned because it is wholly alien to Paul's intention and
argument; because, as Peter Martyr notes, it led some to dismiss Romans 1–11
as irrelevant ceremonial controversy while valuing only the moral teaching
from chapter 12 onward; and because Aquinas, Bellarmine, and many better
instructed Roman Catholics reject it. Even Alfonso de Castro, though giving
this opinion first place, distrusts it and adds a second, alternative answer.
