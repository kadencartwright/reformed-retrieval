QUESTION XXIII.

ON CONSECRATION.

Is consecration in the Eucharist effected by pronouncing the words, “for this
is my body”? And ought they to be pronounced secretly? We deny against the
papists.

[Marginalia: State of the question. On consecration.]

I. The first question concerning the eucharistic symbols and the rites
employed about them regards their consecration. Although the Council of Trent
touched upon this only in passing because of disagreement among the schools,
when it said in chapter 3 of session 13, “This faith has always existed in the
church: immediately after the consecration, the true body of our Lord and his
true blood exist under the species of bread and wine, together with his soul
and divinity; his body indeed under the species of bread, and his blood under
the species of wine, by the force of the words, etc.” Nevertheless, the
opinions of their doctors are exceedingly diverse. Some maintain that Christ
consecrated with no words, but by absolute power and will, by touch, or by some
external sign, as Innocent III, Biel, Catharinus, and certain others. Others
maintain that he did so by words other than those in the Gospel. Others hold
that he did indeed use the same words, but that they were spoken twice: first
openly for the institution, and afterward secretly for the consecration. The
more received and common opinion among them today is that consecration is
effected solely by pronouncing those words that are called formal or
sacramental, and that to this extent those words are operative and converting,
“true instruments of consecration, so that they possess force as

[1847 vol. III printed p. 373 | IA leaf 407 | PDF p. 407]

instruments of God.” So Bellarmine speaks in book 4, chapter 2, *On the Mass*.
Suarez, Vasquez, de Valencia, and others hold the same.

II. We, however, deny that consecration is to be attached to the pronunciation
of the sacramental words. Rather, it is to be located in Christ’s entire
action, and especially in the blessing by which the elements are transferred
and raised from common and natural use to sacred and mystical use, when Christ
is said by the evangelists to have blessed and given thanks after taking the
bread.

III. But so that this entire mystery may be perceived more clearly, it must be
observed that blessing is threefold: the Creator blesses the creature, the
creature blesses the Creator, or the creature blesses the creature. 1. The
Creator blesses the creature when he endows it with some good and affects it
with some benefit. Thus God’s blessing is operative and intimates his
beneficence; when he blesses, he does what he says, as Augustine aptly states.
2. The creature blesses the Creator when it piously celebrates him and his
benefits because of them. Hence those formulas encountered everywhere in the
Psalms: “Bless the Lord, O my soul” (Ps. 103:1; and Ps. 104:1, 35). Thus the
divine blessing always anticipates the human blessing, and God is blessed
because he has blessed us (Eph. 1:3, 3 [sic]). 3. A creature blesses a
creature when it wishes good to it and prays well for it. This blessing is set
forth now in the words of one asking, where the discourse is directed to God
(as in Deut. 33:11); now of one wishing, where it is directed to the creature
(as in Num. 6:24); now of one promising, which is customary in prophecies (Gen.
49:8, 16); and now of one commanding by power granted by God (as in Gen.
27:27). Again, this blessing regards either persons, by a wish, when we pray
for their good; or things, by sanctification. The latter is either for common
use—for example, with respect to ordinary food, so that the power and efficacy
to nourish us may be conferred on it by God, which is treated in 1 Timothy
4:4—or for sacred and religious use, such as the blessing of the unleavened
bread and the Passover cup customary among the Jews.

IV. In this sacramental blessing a twofold relation (*schesis*, σχέσις) must
be considered: the first to God, the second to the symbols themselves. It is
directed to God so that thanks may be given to him for the marvelous benefit
of the entire work of redemption: that he sent Christ into the world and
delivered him to death, and that he gives him to us as life-giving food. It is
directed to the symbols so that a blessing may be obtained upon the creatures
set forth and now to be presented to believers. By the power of that blessing,
although otherwise unfit in themselves for this purpose, they are rendered
effective for the use and spiritual benefit of those receiving them. Thus this
blessing contains both thanksgiving for benefits already received and
supplication for the consecration of the symbols. The latter is rightly
referred to the symbols, not immediately, but because faith, stirred up toward
God, the author of sanctification, derives from him the power of using them for
the celebration of the sacrament. Thus Christ is rightly said to have blessed
the symbols, not by directing his speech to them, but by invoking God himself
to obtain upon them the blessing they need in order to be an effective
sacrament of Christ’s body and blood.

V. Moreover, Christ the God-man (*theanthrōpos*, θεάνθρωπος) blessed the bread
and cup at the Last Supper in a twofold manner: asking for the blessing as man
and presenting it as God. Therefore it was not merely vocal but also real, by
which efficacy for sacred uses and effects was imparted to those elements and
is imparted even now. For he who blessed then also blesses now in response to
the prayer of the minister who blesses in his name, imparting efficacy for the
use of the faithful participants, so that it may result in a blessing to them
and, by the benefit conferred, render them blessed. To this pertain these words
of Chrysostom in *On the Betrayal of Judas*, volume 5: “Christ is present even
now, furnishing this table; the very same one who furnished that table
furnishes this one now. For no man makes the elements set forth become the
body and blood of Christ, but Christ himself, who was fastened to the cross
for us.” From

[1847 vol. III printed p. 374 | IA leaf 408 | PDF p. 408]

these things we conclude that the true consecration of the symbols is effected
through a blessing distinct from the sacramental words.

[Marginalia: It is proved that consecration is effected through blessing.]

VI. The reasons are as follows. First, a blessing is the sanctification of the
things upon which it is poured forth, and is made for no other end than that
the things blessed may be destined and set apart for sacred use. Since Christ,
when blessing the bread and cup, was undoubtedly heard, it is necessary that
by this blessing the bread and cup had been blessed and sanctified before such
words were pronounced. This is confirmed by what Scripture relates: before
uttering those words, Christ had already blessed and given thanks. For he is
said to have spoken the other words *eulogēsas* (εὐλογήσας), “having blessed,”
and *eucharistēsas* (εὐχαριστήσας), “having given thanks”—that is, according
to Erasmus, Catharinus, Sanctesius, and other papists, “after he had blessed
and given thanks.” Hence Justin, in *Apology* 2, calls the bread
*eucharistēthenta* (εὐχαριστηθέντα), that is, bread over which thanks have
been given.

VII. Second, consecration cannot be effected without prayer; indeed, it
consists in prayer, through which God’s grace is invoked upon the symbols for
their sanctification. But the words “this is my body” have no form of prayer,
ask for nothing, and are directed to the apostles, not to God. Nor are they
the words of one asking or wishing, or of one commanding that something not
yet existing should come to be, or of one promising what will be. Rather, they
are the words of one indicating and narrating the very thing at issue.

VIII. Third, the blessing of which Paul treats (1 Cor. 10:16) is the same as
the blessing of which the evangelists speak. But in the apostle it is the
blessing of consecration, because by the force of that blessing the apostle
infers that the cup “is the communion of Christ’s blood” and the bread “the
communion of Christ’s body.”

IX. Fourth, the consecration of the symbols for sacred use ought to be
effected no differently from the consecration of food each day for common use,
since in each case things destined for human uses are sanctified, although in
unequal degree. But according to Paul in 1 Timothy 4:4, this consecration and
sanctification are effected only by prayers and the word. Common sense itself
also dictates this. Thus the pagans consecrated the statues of their deities,
the Jews consecrated the vessels of the temple with prayers, and Solomon
consecrated the temple with the excellent prayer recorded in 1 Kings 8. Thus
Christ consecrated the loaves by a blessing in the miracle of the loaves.
Therefore, when Christ is said to have taken the bread and blessed it, no one
can doubt that by his prayers he wished to call down God’s blessing upon the
symbols. This blessing was necessary so that they would be fit for the sacred
use to which they were destined, namely, to be the sacrament of Christ’s body
and blood, since it is indispensably necessary to seek this blessing from God,
who alone can give it.

X. Fifth, it cannot be supposed that the conversion and consecration by which
Christ’s body is rendered present under the species of bread and wine are
effected by those words, because they are a historical narration and
declaration of a thing that already existed. Therefore the particle “for” is
inserted in them in the canon of the Mass, so that it may be evident that they
give the reason why that bread is to be taken and eaten—namely, because it is
Christ’s body—and do not practically effect a thing that did not yet exist.
Hence it is not uttered in the imperative, as in the creation of light, “Let
there be light,” but in the indicative, as when it is said, “In the beginning
was the Word.” Nor is God ever read to have said of things that do not exist
that they do exist in order to make them exist. To Lazarus lying in the tomb
he said, “Lazarus, come forth,” in the imperative, because by commanding he
gave him life and strength to come forth. But he does not say in the
indicative, “You live, Lazarus, and are coming forth.”

XI. Sixth, if consecration were contained precisely in those words, there
ought to be no variation in the words among the sacred writers, because where
it is necessary to count

[1847 vol. III printed p. 375 | IA leaf 409 | PDF p. 409]

words, syllables, and parts of syllables, as the papists maintain, there ought
to be no variation. Yet variation occurs in their commemoration of these
words. Compare the words of Matthew, Luke, and Paul. The former has, “This is
my blood.” The latter two, however, have, “This cup is the New Testament in my
blood.”

XII. Seventh, the ancient church held no other opinion, as can be established
from the ancient liturgies—those of Clement in the *Constitutions*, Basil,
Cyril of Jerusalem, Chrysostom, James, and Mark—and then from the testimonies
of Justin, Origen, Irenæus, Basil, Ambrose, Augustine, and others, as the
celebrated Dallæus demonstrated fully and solidly in book 3, chapters 13, 14,
and 15, *On Latin Worship*. In chapter 17 he also proves from Nicholas Cabasilas
and Mark, archbishop of Ephesus, that this was the practice of the most ancient
Christians, of the Armenians, and of all the Greeks, because even today they
make this sacrament in this manner and contend against the papists that it
ought to be so made.

XIII. Eighth, various papists acknowledge this. Christopher de Capite Fontium,
archbishop of Cæsarea, in his book *On the Correction of Scholastic Theology*,
asserts that Scripture, the councils, and all antiquity support him in holding
that the words “this is my body” are not consecratory and indeed would not even
be true unless Christ’s body already existed there through the blessing.
Durandus, in book 4, chapter 41, of the *Rationale*, holds that Christ effected
it when he blessed. Odo Camera says in his *Exposition of the Canon*,
distinction 3: “It is evident that the bread, having received the blessing,
was made Christ’s body, for after the blessing he would not have said, ‘This
is my body,’ unless it became Christ’s body in the blessing.” Cajetan,
Cassander, Calarinnus, Titelmanus, and others hold the same.

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions.]

XIV. Prayers to which a promise has been made are always acceptable to God.
Such are the prayers and blessing by which the symbols are consecrated, since
Christ’s words by which he commanded that this be done—and consequently that
the blessing also be given—include a tacit promise of the effect that would
occur thereafter. Hence it cannot be doubted that consecration effected by
blessing and prayers is certain from God’s ordinance and promise. It is one
thing for consecration to depend upon human prayers; it is another for it to be
effected through prayers and the word of institution. We acknowledge the
latter, but not the former, just as we rightly say that God’s benefits are
obtained through prayers but nevertheless do not depend upon prayers. If
consecration cannot be certain because it depends upon human prayers, much
less can there be any certainty about the effect of words pronounced by a
priest, since we know nothing for certain about his intention.

XV. Although prayers and blessing are not the principal cause effecting the
sacrament, but the divine power alone, or the invisible operation of the Holy
Spirit, prayers and blessing are nevertheless rightly said to effect the
consecration because it is accomplished through their intervention. In the
same way, food is rightly said to be sanctified by prayers, although this is
done primarily and principally by God’s word and Spirit.

[Marginalia: Consecration ought to be performed aloud.]

XVI. Christ does what he says, namely, when he says and commands that
something be done, not when he simply declares and relates how a thing stands.
Nor can it be said that these words are not declarative of what existed
before, but practically effective of what did not exist. For if the words are
practically effective, how could the pronoun “this” signify the body that will
only be effected once these words are uttered?

XVII. To this pertains the other question concerning the pronunciation of the
words they call consecratory. The papists teach that these words are to be
pronounced in a subdued voice so that the people do not hear them. The Council
of Trent, session 22, canon 9, strikes with an anathema “those who say that the
rite of the Roman church, by which a part of the canon and the words of
consecration are uttered in a subdued voice, is to be condemned.” And under
the penalty of a dread anathema it enjoins the rite prescribed by the Roman
Missal, “that the priest, with his elbows placed upon the altar, standing with
his head

[1847 vol. III printed p. 376 | IA leaf 410 | PDF p. 410]

bowed, distinctly, reverently, and secretly pronounce the words of
consecration over the host, etc.” We, however, say that these words and the
entire institution of the sacrament ought to be pronounced in a loud and
intelligible voice so that those present may be taught and instructed.

[Marginalia: Proved 1. By Christ’s example.]

XVIII. The reasons are as follows. 1. Because Christ, whose actions ought to
be our guiding star, celebrated the Eucharist in such a way that all things
were heard by everyone present, including these very words that they call
consecratory. For he commanded them to take, eat, and drink, certainly with
such pronunciation that he could be understood. For no one commands another
to do something while wishing him not to hear and understand the very thing
he commands. But if those commanding words were pronounced clearly, heard,
and understood, the following words, “This is my body; this is my blood, etc.,”
which were placed in the context of the same discourse, must also have been
heard and understood. Otherwise, the apostles could not have written them
down unless they had perceived them with their ears. Add that the apostles
were bound to discern the Lord’s body, but there is no means of discerning if
no admonition preceded, and no admonition that is not heard.

[Marginalia: 2. From the end of the sacrament.]

XIX. 2. Christ did not institute the sacrament in such a way that the action
alone was visible and public while the word, which is the chief part of the
sacraments, was hidden and buried in silence. Bellarmine distinguishes in
vain: “The Eucharist may be considered either as a sacrament or as a
sacrifice. As a sacrifice, the words by which it is effected need not be
capable of being heard, because the sacrifice does not consist in words but in
the oblation, and it is taken in that sense here. Further, even in the
sacraments it is not necessary that the words be heard by the recipients, as
when infants are baptized they do not hear the words of baptism.” For, as will
be proved in its own place, he gratuitously assumes that the Supper is a
sacrifice properly so called. As for baptism, if an infant does not hear, this
does not occur because the words are not pronounced aloud, but because of the
infant’s weakness. From what happens accidentally, however, it cannot be
concluded that something ought to be done in itself. Add that those who
approach the Supper are bound to examine themselves and discern the Lord’s
body, which is not required of infants who are to be baptized.

[Marginalia: 3. From the certainty of consecration.]

XX. Third, according to our adversaries, the certainty of the change of the
symbols depends upon the certainty of the consecration and the pronunciation
of the words, “this is my body.” But when these words are murmured secretly,
how few can be certain and assured either that they were pronounced or that
they were pronounced correctly? Those who communicate are therefore bound to
doubt Christ’s presence, and consequently cannot communicate in faith.

[Marginalia: 4. From the practice of the ancient church.]

XXI. Fourth, many things prove that this secret murmuring was unknown in the
ancient church and that everything was pronounced clearly. Bellarmine himself
acknowledges that the Greeks have constantly used, even to this day, a clear
and intelligible voice in pronouncing the words. If the Latins had departed
from them in this respect, the ancients who noted differences between the two
peoples even in other, lesser matters would not have been silent. Indeed, it
is very well known from Dionysius of Alexandria, Justin, Tertullian, and others
that in former times the people responded “Amen” to the priest reciting the
words. But how could it happen that anyone sincere and truthful would say Amen
to things that he not only did not understand but did not even hear? Moreover,
Emperor Justinian commands in *Novel* 123 “that the prayer said in the holy
oblation and the other prayers be pronounced not secretly, but *meta phōnēs
tō pistotatō laō exakouomenēs* (μετὰ φωνῆς τῷ πιστοτάτῳ λαῷ ἐξακουομένης),
with a voice that can be heard by the most faithful people.” Cassander, in
chapter 28 of his *Liturgy*, confirms by various arguments “that the ancients
read the canonical prayer, and especially the consecration of the Lord’s body
and Christ’s blood, in such a way that it could be understood by the people
and they could acclaim Amen.”

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions.]

XXII. It is falsely boasted that the secret pronunciation of the words of
consecration serves to procure greater reverence for

[1847 vol. III printed p. 377 | IA leaf 411 | PDF p. 411]

the sacrament. For things do not become cheap merely because they are made
public; otherwise the
gospel, which Christ commanded to be preached to all nations, would become
cheap, and baptism, whose words are customarily pronounced aloud, would
become cheap. It belonged to the Gentiles to conceal their mysteries so that
their baseness might not be recognized, but Christ wishes to become known to
all because he has nothing but what is glorious and divine. Christ and the
apostles, who were most reverent toward sacred things, did not for that reason
refrain from clear pronunciation.

XXIII. Since the minister of the Eucharist ought to take account not merely of
himself, but of the people for whose sake especially the sacrament is publicly
celebrated, in praying he ought to regard not himself alone but the people for
whose sake he prays. This he intimates by these words: “We humbly ask and
petition you, our Lord.” But how are the people to hope that they will be
heard, when the priest begrudges them the prayers?

XXIV. There is no danger in the clear pronunciation of the words, but there
is great danger in secretly muttering them. For thus the people, hearing
nothing, will not be able to attend with their minds. Why then is “Lift up
your hearts” cried aloud? Why in former times did the Greeks cry *ekteinate*
(ἐκτείνατε), “Attend,” in the liturgy of Mark? For how will the people be able
to attend if they hear nothing?

XXV. Whoever desires more on this argument should consult Dallæus, book 3,
chapter 5, *On Latin Worship*, where that excellent man solidly refutes
whatever is adduced to confirm this error. In particular, he refutes the
little tale about shepherds pronouncing the words of consecration over bread
and wine, which were suddenly changed into flesh and blood, while the
shepherds themselves were immediately consumed by heavenly fire and perished
for the deed. Pseudo-Alcuin, later than the tenth century, is the first to
recount it in *On the Divine Offices*, under the title *On the Celebration of
Masses*, adding, “From this arose the custom in the church that henceforth
this consecration should be chanted silently by priests.” See also Chamier,
volume 4, book 6, chapters 8 and 9, *On the Eucharist*, who refutes whatever
reasons or pretexts the papists introduce here to confirm this error.
