## Part XXVII: Adam’s Fall as an Example

[Part II | PDF page 187 | logical source scan 186 | printed page 185 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

Although I hope these things will satisfy most who earnestly seek the truth, nevertheless, so that anyone may perceive from these very examples how a remarkable difference in both the counsel and action of two agents, and also opposition in their ends, must be considered in one and the same work, let us examine this also part by part. First let us speak of the first parents. What did God intend in Adam’s voluntary fall? We showed above that Adam could not have fallen with God either unwilling, ignorant, or unconcerned for that distinguished work of his, although God certainly instilled no evil will in him. God intended to open the way both to save his people through mercy, to condemn justly the malice of the reprobate, and finally to reveal his own nature as he describes it in the law. Otherwise no misery would have existed in the elect to be expiated through the appointed mediator, nor any sin in the reprobate to be condemned.

“But where do you get this?” Andreae says. If I answer from the scholastics, especially Aquinas, that the order of the world required it, he will laugh in his customary fashion. I therefore reply—let him laugh if he will—that it is nevertheless as true as truth itself once this foundation has been laid, which no one but the impious or an outright Epicurean can deny, and which I have often pressed: there is one God, from himself and through himself. It follows that nothing has existed, exists, or will exist except what has existed, exists, and will exist by God’s power and will. Whenever past events are in question, therefore, no inference is firmer than this *a posteriori* inference, as the schools call it: “This occurred; therefore God willed it to occur. This followed from that event; therefore God willed that event to occur so that this result would follow.” What I said cannot be denied to have been an effect of Adam’s fall. I therefore concluded it rightly and necessarily, not from human invention.

But what did Satan intend, although unknowingly serving God’s counsel? Because he hates God and wholly seethes with envy, he willed to sow hostility between God and humanity. He certainly sowed it, but to the highest good of those to be elected and to the just destruction of himself and the reprobate, and in both respects to God’s everlasting glory. What did Adam and Eve intend as soon as they made themselves teachable by Satan? They intended to place themselves on God’s throne. Therefore Satan, Adam, and Eve are deservedly punished. They were instruments of God producing that distinguished work, yet did not obey God; rather, insofar as they undertook their own work by their own voluntary motion, they conspired against him.

The difference in the very manner of acting is no less plain. Because God is good, he created Adam and Eve good and endowed with a good will. He told them what he required and added threats to the command to deter them from transgression by fear of danger. He cannot be blamed for not supporting them with new grace, since he is debtor to none and required nothing from them which they could not perform with the powers they had received, since they were not yet enslaved to sin. What, however, does Satan do? He shamelessly slanders God and urges those miserable people to apostasy. What do Adam and Eve do? With a will not only uncoerced and voluntary but still altogether free, though mutable, they freely desert God their creator. Who can complain about God here? Certainly not Satan, Adam, or Eve.

Can we, Adam’s descendants, complain? As for the elect, since they receive far more in Christ than they lost in Adam, what has happened to them about which they should complain? As for the reprobate, since they were conceived only in sins and, if they grow up, are buried under the mass of their own sins, finally convicted by their own conscience, and sentenced to deserved punishments, by what right could they dispute with God? “But why does he not save everyone?” someone will say. Shameless mouth! Wonder instead why he deigns to save anyone. Finally, by what means would God—as the apostle testifies in Romans 11—have shut everyone up under disobedience and, as Solomon speaks, made even the wicked for his glory for the day of destruction, except through Adam’s voluntary fall, appointed by him from eternity partly as merciful and partly as the future just judge? For the supremely good God could neither create evil nor drive a created good toward evil. Let this suffice concerning Adam’s own fall.
