### Chapter III

#### On Efficacious Grace

The question is wherein the efficacy of grace consists, or from what it is
derived. Although this question is of the greatest importance and the hinge
upon which all the disputes about grace and free choice turn, Bellarmine
nevertheless, with his customary cunning, was unwilling to descend into it,
lest he offend certain papists too greatly. He therefore does not dispute
directly against our theologians, nor against anyone by name. Instead, he
brushes over two opinions with a light touch and intimates his own third
opinion more by muttering than by setting it forth distinctly or proving it
solidly. We shall briefly call what he says that bears upon the matter back to
examination.

##### Thesis I

1. Bellarmine. *The first opinion is that of those who place efficacious grace
in human assent and cooperation, so that grace is called efficacious from the
outcome, because, namely, it obtains its effect; and it obtains its effect
because the human will cooperates with it. This opinion is altogether foreign
to the judgment of Augustine and also to the divine Scriptures. It overturns
the foundation of divine predestination and abuses the term “efficacious
grace.”*

Protestant reply. Although not with sufficient courage—as was fitting against
the Pelagian heresy—Bellarmine nevertheless says all these things truly. But
it was appropriate for him to treat this heresy gently and familiarly, because
it has many great supporters among the Jesuits. Molina, in *On Concord*,
disputation 3, says: “We assert that whether the helps of prevenient and
assisting grace which are conferred upon wayfarers according to the ordinary
law are efficacious or inefficacious depends upon the free consent and
cooperation of our choice with them; and consequently it lies within our free
power to render them either efficacious or inefficacious.” These words are
cited and refuted by Matthew de Respolis, book 2, question 1, conclusion 4.
Becanus, in *On Helps*, part 2, chapter 3, has this conclusion: “If, therefore,
the first root is sought why this grace is efficacious and that one
inefficacious, recourse must be had to the free cooperation of the will.” The
opinion of Suárez, Vásquez, and even Bellarmine himself is the same, though
painted over with other words, as will appear shortly.

2. Bellarmine. *There is another opinion, that of others who teach that
efficacious grace is a physical action of God which determines the will to
will and choose the good. This opinion seems to me either to be altogether the
same as the error of Calvin and the Lutherans, or to differ little from it.*

[Printed p. 265 | Google Books PA265 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine was unwilling to say who those “others” are
who defend this opinion, lest he inflict grave prejudice upon his own opinion.
Many others do say who they are, however, among them Álvarez in disputation
38: “Through the efficacious help of grace, physically, or after the manner of
a physical cause, God predetermines created choice so that it
freely and infallibly consents and cooperates with God as he awakens and calls.
All the Thomists defend this conclusion, which I judge to have been handed
down by St. Thomas, St. Augustine, and other Fathers in equivalent words.”

I. As this view is explained there, “the help of grace, efficacious of itself
inasmuch as it comes from God prior to the free cooperation of our choice,”
and “physically predetermining help” signify the same thing; we must not get
stuck upon the word. “To predetermine physically is nothing other than truly
and efficiently—or really—causing choice itself to cooperate freely and
infallibly with God as he awakens and calls. To predetermine is to determine
first in the order of reason, or to cause something to determine itself.”
This opinion, I say, when thus explained, is plainly our own and is taught
quite openly in sacred Scripture.

3. Bellarmine. *This opinion destroys sufficient grace.*

Protestant reply. I. Álvarez denies this, along with the other Dominicans,
because they deny that actual predetermination and efficacious help are
necessary in order that a man may be able to act absolutely and simply; they
maintain that these are required only in order that he may actually act.
Álvarez, disputations 18, 20, 23, and 29. II. If it destroys that grace
sufficient for spiritual conversion which the papists imagine is given to
all and in many is separated from efficacious grace, it destroys an error
built upon no stable foundation.

4. Bellarmine. *This opinion removes free choice from the scene.*

Protestant reply. I. This too is rightly denied by the Thomists. “Through the
efficacy of the help of preoperating grace,” Álvarez says and demonstrates on
page 18, “the freedom of our choice is not destroyed but rather strengthened
and perfected. It predetermines the will toward one alternative, not from
necessity, but according to the proper nature of the will itself. It causes
the will freely but infallibly to determine itself toward one alternative.”

2. Bellarmine is among those patrons of free choice about whom the Jesuit
Azor truly writes, book 4, chapter 22, question 2: “A few more recent
theologians, perhaps in order to make our will free, make it sacrilegious,
when they deny that God inspires our free good works.”

5. Bellarmine. *The third opinion, which is Augustine's, places efficacious
grace in an inward persuasion which can be rejected through free choice and
yet has an infallible effect, because God applies the persuasion which he
knows to be suited to the disposition of those whom he has decreed to draw
efficaciously and infallibly, and which he knows with certainty will not be
despi-*

[Printed p. 266 | Google Books PA266 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*sed by them.*

Protestant reply. This Jesuit opinion, falsely attributed to Augustine,
consists in three things which create a threefold deception for the
unskilled. For it maintains that the following concur in the efficacy of
grace: I. Persuasion or moral motion. II. The suitability of the persuasion
and grace. III. God's foreknowledge of the suitable effect or outcome of the
persuasion and grace in the conversion of the person who is called. All these
things are indeed affirmed by Augustine and are readily granted by us,
because they can easily be proved from Scripture and the nature of the things
themselves. Yet concealed in all of them are snares against which one must
carefully guard. For I. We say that with moral persuasion there is also joined
a real efficiency of God, by which a new principle of spiritual life is
engendered in the heart of the person efficaciously called, and the man is at
the same time awakened to elicit the acts of this life. Bellarmine, however,
wills the inward efficacy of grace to consist only in moral persuasion. II.
We believe that God's grace is of itself, and by its own intrinsic nature,
suitable and efficacious for effecting conversion. But Bellarmine makes this
suitability depend upon extrinsic things, namely, a man's constitution and
disposition, and the circumstances of place, time, and manner in which that
persuasion is applied. III. We understand that God has certain knowledge of a
future conversion because he has decreed it. But the Jesuits have invented a
knowledge which they call *middle knowledge*, by which they say that before
the decree of his will God knew with certainty what Peter, from his innate
freedom, would do if conversion were urged upon him in such a manner, place,
and time. By handling this cause so coldly and carelessly, with a deliberate
and cunning design, Bellarmine has deprived us of every occasion to treat
this opinion here as it deserves. Nevertheless, the objections which he
brings against himself from other papists can profitably be rescued from his
evasions.

6. Bellarmine's objection. *Efficacious grace should not be placed in
illumination and inspiration, or in calling and persuasion, because these
belong to law and letter, not to grace and spirit. First, it must be answered:
There is a great difference between the grace of inward persuasion and an
outward sermon: it is brought to the ears of the heart, pours in light, and
breathes upon it the beginning of a good will. 2. Although
presenting an object belongs of itself not to spirit but to letter, it
nevertheless belongs to grace that a reading or sermon should occur which is
suited to a man's disposition, especially if the inward Teacher is added, who
opens the heart to attend to the things heard or read. 3. The inward grace by
which he cooperates also cooperates physically toward eliciting an act of the
deliberate will.*

Protestant reply. I. Whatever difference there is between outward and inward
persuasion, they agree in this: they only reveal and demonstrate what we ought
to do; they do not grant that we do it. They do not

[Printed p. 267 | Google Books PA267 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

supply spiritual power or bestow the effective principle of the supernatural
operation which they urge. Therefore, although the Pelagians granted both,
Augustine nevertheless rightly declared that they acknowledged letter, law,
and doctrine, but denied the grace which is given to us through the
supply of the Spirit. Álvarez proves this in disputation 1, numbers 6 and 19.
II. Bellarmine's assertion that “the beginning of a good will is breathed upon
us through persuasion” is false, because persuasion is present to many who
have no beginning of a truly good will; and it implies a contradiction for
persuasion to breathe forth the beginning of the very thing which it urges in
its entirety. III. The efficacy of grace cannot properly consist in this
persuasion, because it is present to many in whom grace is not efficacious.
IV. The occurrence of a reading or sermon suited to a man's disposition does
indeed belong generally to grace, but it is not the grace by which the Spirit
and spiritual life are given to us through his supply; consequently, when it
is compared with that grace, it is said to belong to the letter. Nor is there
greater efficacy in the occurrence of a reading suited to urging conversion
than in the occurrence of a temptation suited to urging rebellion. 5. What
Bellarmine adds about *cooperating* grace has no bearing on the matter. For in
chapter 4 he himself makes the first act of the deliberate will, such as
conversion, depend upon operating grace alone, not cooperating grace; but we
are treating conversion.

7. Bellarmine's objection. *If efficacious grace is an inward persuasion by
which someone is called in such a way that it is fitting for him not to
despise the one calling, sufficient grace, insofar as it is distinguished from
efficacious grace, will be nothing other than an inward persuasion by which
someone is called in such a way that it is not fitting for him not to despise
the one calling. The consequent is absurd; therefore so is the antecedent.*

Bellarmine. 1. *It is certain that many are called by God in such a way that it
is not fitting for them not to despise the one calling, and at a time when God
sees that they will not follow; and that they are not called at the time when
they are foreseen to be about to follow, if they were called.*

Protestant reply. The former part of this compound statement is most certainly
false if it is understood of a calling simply sufficient for conversion. The
latter part is equally false if it is understood, as Bellarmine understands
it, of foreseeing an outcome which depends solely upon the extrinsic
unsuitability of the calling.

Bellarmine. 2. *What God does ought not to seem absurd even if we do not know
the cause. Nevertheless, God does not call the reprobate when he sees that
they will follow, because he is not bound to call them in that manner and may
justly forsake them. He does call when he sees that they will not follow, both
so that the power of free choice may be displayed and for the sake of the
elect, that in the destruction of others they may understand how great a
grace they have received from God. Nor can the reprobate refer to God any
blame for their own disobedience.*

[Printed p. 268 | Google Books PA268 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. I. If what God does ought not to seem absurd to us even if
we do not know the cause—which is the one thing said by Bellarmine for himself
here—then Bellarmine certainly ought not to have indulged his own insolence by
snarling at God as he does in chapter 11: “If those at whose heart the Lord
knocks do not have sufficient help to open, why, I ask, does the Lord knock?
Does he not know that they cannot open? Would not a man be foolish who knocked
at his neighbor's door if he knew for certain that there was no one inside who
could open?” II. That God is not bound to call suitably is equally true of all
sinners. It therefore cannot be the reason why God would not call certain
persons when he sees that they will follow, any more than it is the reason for
not calling all of them. III. From the reasons Bellarmine offers why God calls
those whom he sees will not follow, he could answer himself why God calls
those whom he knows not to have sufficient grace. IV. Since it is most certain
that no one is converted unless he is suitably called, the blame for
disobedience or nonconversion can just as readily be referred by the
impenitent to God as not calling suitably as to God as not communicating
sufficient help—indeed, with greater plausibility if he often does not call
those whom he sees would have followed if they had had a suitable persuasion.
Let the Jesuits therefore restrain their insane outcries that those who deny
that sufficient grace is given to all, and that by God's decree, refer to God
the blame for sin and death.

Bellarmine's objection. *That explanation of grace which Bellarmine has given
differs little or not at all from the opinion which places the efficacy of
grace in the human will. Bellarmine nevertheless previously confessed that
opinion to be foreign to Scripture, to overturn the foundation of divine
predestination, and to be an abuse of the term “efficacious grace.”*

Bellarmine. 1. *These opinions differ in that the first opinion wills the
efficacy of grace to depend upon the human will, whereas our opinion wills it
to depend upon the will of God.*

Protestant reply. This difference consists in nothing but a varnish of words.
For I. The third opinion posits nothing that is not contained in the first,
except the suitability of a persuasion singularly suited to the disposition
of those by whom grace is received. This suitability, however, is placed in
the circumstances of place, time, and the like, together with the constitution
and natural disposition of the man to be called. According to that opinion,
therefore, the efficacy of grace depends immediately and proximately either
upon those circumstances or upon the human will. But to refer this efficacy
to those circumstances is more unworthy than to refer it to the choice of the
human will. “For it follows,” as Álvarez rightly observes in disputation 83,
number 9, “that the ultimate differentiating reason why the help of grace is
efficacious must be traced back to something natural—namely, to the partic-*

[Printed p. 269 | Google Books PA269 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*ular constitution of this individual, or to some celestial constellation,
or indeed to some taking of food or drink”—add also, to pleasant sleep—“from
which arises a disposition that is more gentle and cheerful and less averse
to admitting a good persuasion.” 2. The Jesuit Becanus not only acknowledges
frankly but also proves forcefully what Bellarmine denies by dissembling. For
he says in *On Helps*, part 2, chapter 3: “If the first root is sought why this
grace is efficacious and that one inefficacious, recourse must be had to the
free cooperation of the will, not to the foreknowledge of God by which he
foresaw that this or that grace would be suitable. For God's foreknowledge
does not cause a man to cooperate but supposes that he will cooperate.
Therefore the first root from which grace is called efficacious in second act
must be derived from the outcome, or the man's free cooperation.”

Bellarmine. 2. *Suppose the question is asked whether, if two men have
altogether the same awakening and assisting grace—at least in first act—and
hear the same preacher and see the same signs, it can come about that one
believes and the other does not. The first opinion will answer that it can
come about, an answer which contradicts the apostle. But our opinion will
answer that it cannot come about that if two men receive altogether the same
awakening grace, one believes and the other does not. For the one who believes
receives the motion in the manner, place, and time which God foresaw would be
suited to his disposition; the other does not receive it in this way. And by
this reasoning, the one who believes has had a far greater grace of God than
the one who does not believe.*

Protestant reply. This escape is plainly deceptive. If the inward and outward
motion or awakening of grace is the same, and the place and time are also the
same, with like circumstances, but they suit the disposition or character of
the will of one person and not of the other, then the sole cause of the
difference lies in their different dispositions and wills. When the same
spectacles suit the eyes of one person and not another, the cause lies not in
the spectacles but in the eyes to which they are applied. When one and the
same temptation of the devil is suited to seduce one person but not another,
the cause lies not in the temptation but in those tempted. Álvarez is
therefore correct in disputation 108, number 4: “According to this solution,
the difference is finally traced back to the innate freedom of man and not to
anything else received from grace.” The sum of this entire dispute is this:
If God draws us to the good only by persuading, then the efficacy of God's
grace in the faithful for acting well is no different from the efficacy of
the devil's temptation in sinners for acting wickedly. Consequently, just as
the efficacy of temptation depends more upon the will of the one sinning than
upon the devil tempting, so the efficacy of grace depends more upon the will
of the one acting well than upon God awakening him to the good.
