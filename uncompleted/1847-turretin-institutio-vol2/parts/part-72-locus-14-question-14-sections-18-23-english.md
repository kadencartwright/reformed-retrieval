[Marginal heading: 2. From the restriction of his death to those given by the
Father.]

XVIII. Second, Christ was given and died for none other than those who were
given to him by the Father. But each and every person was not given to Christ,
only certain people. Therefore—The truth of the major premise cannot be called
into doubt. In the Father’s counsel according to which Christ had to die and
actually did die, not only was Christ designated as Redeemer, but also those
for whose redemption and salvation he was to die. It is therefore clear that
he had to die for none but those given to him in this sense. Here a twofold
giving may be observed: Christ to people, and people to Christ. Christ was
given to people in order to save them, and people were given to Christ to be
saved. The former giving is indicated in Isaiah 9:6; 49:6; and all the texts
saying Christ was sent and given to us. The latter appears where those given
to Christ are mentioned, as in John 17:2, 6, 12; and 6:37. Since this twofold
giving is reciprocal, it must have the same extent: Christ was given for none
but those who were given to him, and all those for whom Christ was given were
given to Christ. Scripture asserts in more than one place the minor premise,
that not each and every person but only certain people were given to Christ.
It distinguishes them from other people in John 17:2: “As you gave him
authority over all flesh, that he might give eternal life to all those you
gave him,” and verse 6: “I manifested your name to the people whom you gave me
out of the world. They were yours, and you gave them to me.” It designates
them as the people whom he foreknew (Rom. 11:2); the heirs and children of the
promise (Rom. 9:8); and Abraham’s seed, not carnal but

spiritual, from Jews as well as Gentiles (Rom. 4:13; Gal. 3:8, 16; Heb. 2:16);
as Christ’s people and body, or church (Matt. 1:21; Eph. 5:23); vessels of
mercy prepared for glory (Rom. 9:23–24); those chosen in him and predestined
to adoption and conformity with him (Rom. 8:30; Eph. 1:4–5); and the posterity
of the second Adam, all of whom are made alive in him, in contrast to the
posterity of the first Adam, in whom all die (1 Cor. 15:22–23). From all this
it is evident that the object of Christ’s giving is so restricted that it
cannot extend to each and every person, but only to certain people.

XIX. It would be pointless to reply: “This giving of Christ was conditional,
not absolute, namely, under the provision and condition that people receive by
faith this benefit offered to them. Since the greater part do not do so, it is
no wonder if the giving remains ineffectual with respect to them.” This
gratuitously assumes that the giving is conditional, though Scripture nowhere
mentions this condition. Faith is indeed set forth as the necessary means and
condition for receiving and using the benefit. Yet it cannot therefore be
regarded as a condition presupposed by the giving of Christ, since faith is
itself a gift of grace and a fruit of this giving. Moreover, if this giving of
Christ rested on any condition, it would depend either on the person or on
God. Only a Pelagian can say the former. If the latter is asserted, it amounts
to saying that God gave Christ to us as Savior on condition that he himself
give him to us through producing faith—or on condition of a faith which he
alone can give but nevertheless does not will to give. Everyone sees the
absurdity of this.

XX. This is further confirmed from the connection of the twofold σχέσεως under
which he was given to us, namely, as Surety and Head. As Surety, he was to
acquire salvation for us by satisfying for us; as Head, he was to apply it to
us by working in us through his Spirit. Therefore, just as he was not given as
Head to each and every person, but only to his members—the elect who actually
share in salvation—so also he was to be Surety and Sponsor for no others. The
character of both σχέσεως is the same, and the extent of one should not be
greater than that of the other. The same may be proved from the *union of
Christ’s death and resurrection*, in which this twofold relation appeared: he
had to die as Surety and rise as Head. The rationale of the death and the
resurrection is the same, and no reason can be given why the former should
extend more widely than the latter. Thus Paul places these two as
ἰσοδυναμοῦντα and of equal extent: Christ “died because of our sins and was
raised because of our justification” (Rom. 4:25). He also testifies that
“Christ died for all, that those who henceforth live should no longer live for
themselves, but for him who died and was raised for them” (2 Cor. 5:14–15). He
cannot be said to have died for any but those for whom he rose, because no one
shares in the fruits of Christ’s death except through the resurrection. But
the fact itself proclaims that he did not rise as Head for everyone in order
to confer upon them the salvation obtained by his death.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the connection of satisfaction and intercession.]

XXI. Third, the same truth is established *from the connection between
Christ’s satisfaction and intercession*. Since they are parts of the same
priestly office, they must also have the same extent: he satisfied for those
for whom he intercedes, and he satisfied for none but those for whom he
intercedes. The object of the ἱλασμοῦ and the ἐμφανισμοῦ must be the same, for
Paul and John connect them by an indissoluble bond (1 John 2:1–2; Rom. 8:34).
Christ himself expressly testifies that he does not intercede for each and
every person, but only for those given by the Father: “I do not pray for the
world,” he says,

“but for those whom you gave me” (John 17:9). Since offering prayers for
someone is far less than giving blood and life, who would say that he died for
those to whom he denies his prayers? Or that, at the very moment when he was
about to die, he would deny prayers to those for whose sake he willed to pour
out his own blood?

XXII. Nor should it be said with the Remonstrants: “There is a twofold
intercession of Christ: one universal, with respect to the whole world, spoken
of in Isaiah 53:12, according to which he is read to have prayed for his
persecutors in Luke 23:34; the other particular, with respect to believers,
which is treated in John 17 and Romans 8.” This assumes without reason that
there is a universal intercession. Since the Father always hears him (John
11:42), if he interceded for all, all would actually be saved. Isaiah 53:12
does not prove this. It indeed says that he will intercede for transgressors,
but not for all—only for the many mentioned there whom he is said to justify
in verse 11. Nor does Luke 23:34 prove it. He does not pray for all who
crucified him, but for those who sinned in ignorance, as Christ adds there and
who also obtained the fruit of his prayer (Acts 2–3). Even if Christ, from
human affection and the impulse of love, had prayed also for those who perish,
it would not follow that the intercessory prayers he offered as Mediator and
by that special office had to extend to any but the elect and those given by
the Father, to whom Christ himself restricts them.

XXIII. Nor does one escape any better by saying that Christ’s prayers exclude
only *the world of unbelievers*, which is guilty of rejecting the gospel and
hates believers (v. 14), but not the world loved by God, to which he gave his
Son (John 3:16); and that these prayers aim only to obtain the gift of
perseverance for believers. Since this world is contrasted with those given by
the Father through election, it must necessarily extend to all the reprobate
who were not given to Christ, antecedently to their unbelief—whether they were
to be unbelieving toward the gospel offered to them or were guilty only of
violating the law of nature. Since that reprobation occurred from eternity,
they could never have been regarded otherwise by God. Nor does the world which
God is said to have loved so as to give his Son prevent this exclusion,
because, as seen in its own place, it does not extend to each and every person,
but without distinction to the elect. Nor, because Christ prays for the
apostles who already believe by seeking for them the gift of perseverance,
does it follow that he prays only for believers as such and consequently to
their faith. On the contrary, since he prays for all who will believe in verses
19 and 21 “that they may be sanctified in the truth and perfected into one,”
and this cannot occur without the gift of faith, he must pray that faith be
given them. Add that since he says he sanctifies himself for them so that they
also may be sanctified in truth, and this sanctification is attained by none
but the elect, he must be regarded as having conceived these prayers for them
alone, for the bestowal and perseverance of faith and the other gifts
necessary for salvation.
