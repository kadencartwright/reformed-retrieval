V. 5. A witness of falsehoods will not be innocent; he who forges lies will not deliver himself.

In ינקה (*will be immune*) there is a metonymy of the cause. In כזבים (*of falsehoods*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct.

The sum is that one given to lies, whether he does it publicly or privately, will not go unpunished.

It is to be wished that men would abstain from evil out of hatred for wrongdoing, so that no penalty for transgression had to be established; and, for this reason, that each person, moved by zeal for truth toward his neighbour, would speak the truth, since we are members one of another, Eph. 4. 25. But since this cannot be obtained from men, it seemed good to God to keep them in their duty by the threat of punishment. And although men give themselves free rein in every kind of vice, in contriving and fashioning lies they assume every liberty—or rather licence—for themselves. If the cause of this is asked, it is this: almost no civil or ecclesiastical penalty is imposed for telling lies. In the law of Moses, that is, in the law of God’s commonwealth, it is provided that whoever accuses another in a trial, if he does not prove the charge, is to be punished with the same penalty with which the accused would have been punished if he had been held convicted of the alleged crime, even to the penalty of death, Deut. 19. But this most holy law has almost everywhere grown obsolete, especially in this corrupt age, in which Machiavellian discipline holds its place. With impunity set before them, it opens everyone’s mouth to accusations and denunciations, as though it were beneficial to the commonwealth that accusers induced to accuse by any suspicion, even the slightest, should exist in great number. But as for the lies which are spread privately, scarcely any society of men will be found which establishes a penalty for transgressors. God, foreseeing this as though from the watchtower of his foreknowledge, wished through the Prophet to make a remedy for this evil. For it is just as if he said: although false testimonies and lies of every kind are restrained by almost no penalties among men, I nevertheless, the judge of the world, will not permit this wickedness to be unpunished. Therefore, if in speaking falsely we do not fear men for ourselves, we nevertheless ought not for that reason to be bolder in perpetrating evil deeds, since God exists as avenger and punisher, and he will not let the offender go unpunished. Thus it would have been preferable for punishment to have been exacted by men rather than to fall into the hands of the living God, since this punishment is both surer and graver than if it were inflicted by men.

In this kind, however, sin is committed in more than one way. For besides the fact that the greatest part of men, having cast away the fear of God, devote themselves to lies, there are also found

[1632 witness | PDF p. 401 | printed columns 776-777]

others who, although they are kept in their duty to some degree by the fear of God, nevertheless do not abstain from certain kinds of lies as though they were lawful and permitted. Of this kind are jocular lies, and likewise those which are called officious lies. They do not suppose that they are held guilty by the former, while by the latter they even hunt after praise as though it were the reward of virtue. Their opinion must be diminished, since every lie, of whatever kind it may be, when it is borne against the truth, must necessarily also be borne against God, who is the author and patron of truth.

It must also be noted that the false witness and the man who lets loose his tongue in telling lies are placed in an equal degree of wickedness. For although someone who would shrink from false testimony when placed in court may now and then tell a lie, nevertheless the man who becomes accustomed to telling falsehoods privately will not be ashamed to pursue the same custom publicly in court.

V. 6. Many entreat the face of a prince; and everyone is a friend to a man of gift, [or a generous man.]

In פני (*face*) there is a synecdoche of the part. The reason is that those who are about to make supplication keep their eyes fixed upon the face of the man whom they entreat. In מתן (*of gift*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct.

The purpose is that generosity has more power to conciliate the goodwill of men than nobility and princely rank do.

That this is to be observed and considered in the Proverb is gathered from the comparison of the words רבים כל (*many and everyone*). For very many indeed approach Nobles and Princes, but not everyone. Some men are often found so constituted in disposition that, delighted with themselves and their own affairs, they do not hunt after the favour of Magnates—to such a degree that they allow themselves to be heavily pressed and oppressed rather than flee to the favour and patronage of Princes. But everyone runs toward the generous and liberal, if not with their feet as toward Princes, nevertheless with mind and affection. And indeed only those seek the face of Princes who desire to use their agency either to obtain a good or to avoid an evil. But not only those who seek to enjoy the bounty of generous men draw near to them in their affections, as was said; so also do those who, content with their own affairs, would reject even gifts that were offered. Moreover, petitioners approach men of free birth and Princes who, though displaying the most ample honour outwardly, inwardly embrace them with very slight zeal and love, whereas all men vie to kiss generous men with goodwill.

Hence Nobles and Princes are first admonished that, when many petitioners offer them written pleas and make frequent requests, they should obtain wisdom and judgment, by whose assistance they may understand what is to be granted or refused to each person.

Next, generous men ought to take care that, when many wish to enjoy their bounty and munificence, they employ such moderation in giving that they do not exhaust the fountain of generosity. Then they should at the same time employ the prudence by which they may know how to open or shut the fountain of generosity. Finally, they should use judgment toward those on whom they bestow benefits, so that they may knowingly discern what is suitable and fitting for each person.

By this Proverb our inertia is remarkably reproved. First, we do not entreat the most noble God, the author of all nobility, the Prince of Princes and King of Kings, nor do we lay all our petitions and vows in his bosom. We have been especially invited and fortified by promises that we shall carry away from him whatever we have lawfully asked in the name of Christ, above all since he is the one without whom we not only cannot fare well, but cannot even exist.

Next, although God infinitely surpasses all mortals in bounty and munificence, so that he confers excellent gifts even upon his enemies, nevertheless few are found who embrace him with love and goodwill and desire to cleave to his love.

V. 7. All the brothers of a poor man hate him; how much more is every one of his companions far from him: pursuing them with words, they are not there.

Supply: neither brothers nor companions.

In אחי (*brothers*) there is a synecdoche of the part, *brothers* standing for all of us who are joined by blood or affinity. Likewise in רש (*of a poor man*) there is a synecdoche for one afflicted and calamitous. In רחק (*far*) there is a metaphor drawn from those who are separated by a great interval of places, so that they cannot bring aid, and transferred to those who are far removed in zeal and will.

The sum is that a person should obtain better and firmer defences for himself than those which are sought from mortals.

Great is the perversity of men, and twisted and insolent their disposition: to hate those whom God loves for the greatest part, and to turn away from those whom God generally embraces. For God has chosen the poor to be rich in faith, James 2. 5. And plainly, if we are willing to confess the truth, it is no obscure indication that men pursue God himself with hatred when they are so unjust toward those whom he loves. This is perceived even better from the fact that they do not cast away or desert a brother and kinsman, although he has fallen shamefully and is designing an atrocious crime.

Indeed, what is graver, they are ready to defend him in the very matter in which he fell, although they do not approve the crime committed. We experience this in the Benjaminites, who did not hesitate, at the highest peril of their own lives, to deliver from the punishment due to them their brothers, wicked men who with monstrous lust had assaulted the Levite’s concubine even to death, Judg. 19.

Therefore it is worthwhile often to remember that warning in which God has provided that we should not place our hope in men or in the sons of men, but should always possess God as our refuge, asylum, and bulwark, to whom we may run back in a time of calamity.

Next it must be observed that the Holy Spirit accounts it hatred if anyone does not bring aid to a man in distress. For we cannot deny that brothers and companions wish their brother and companion in distress well, and generally desire success for them; otherwise it would be a monstrous thing. But by this manner of speaking the Holy Spirit charges with hatred those who do not come to the aid of a brother or companion in distress. Hence also the wife less loved by a husband, when there are two, is called hated. Therefore we see that hatred, which is a species of homicide, extends more widely and spreads itself to more people than most suppose. They absolve themselves of hatred unless they devise something hostile against another, although they perform no duty of assistance for him.

Then he aggravates the stain of an ungrateful spirit: those who come as close as possible to him in blood, affinity, or the fellowship of life are nevertheless so far absent in a time of need, refusing the aid which they ought to render—near by blood and remote in will; joined by affinity and separated in their pursuits; bound by fellowship and dissociated in duty. Thus the poverty and calamity of a man surrounded by many cousins betrays his cousins’ ἀστοργίαν, lack of natural affection, which lies hidden within.

It is apparent, however, that the bond of brothers is greater and closer than that of companions. Hence those joined by fellowship suppose themselves somehow absolved from the fault of ingratitude because they observe that brothers are deserters. Therefore brothers and kindred are guilty not only of their own desertion, but also because they give companions an occasion for desertion and defection.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 403 | printed columns 780-781]

See Rev. 18. 10, how Kings, at verse 15 Merchants, and at verse 17 Sailors, will stand far off at the time of Rome’s overthrow.

He pursues them, that is, with supplicating and submissive words, as in 18. 23; yet nevertheless they do not show themselves to be either brothers or companions. Poverty itself, or calamity itself, speaks by itself even though the man himself is silent, and calls them loudly. And if anything human were in them, they would run to the cry of poverty and would not wait until they were solicited with words and petitions—especially since most people, and particularly those who once lived in abundance and honour, are prevented by a certain rustic shame from providing for themselves through petitions and entreaties. Thus they more often desire to die of hunger or starvation than to ask for assistance. But the ingratitude of those who, though asked with words and petitions, still are not moved is yet more atrocious. Therefore they must consider what Solomon declared in another Proverb: the Lord’s ear will be stopped against the man who closes his own ear against the cry of the poor. See Job 6. 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, which are referred to the inconstancy of love.

V. 8. He who acquires a heart loves his own soul; he who keeps understanding is on the way to finding good.

In לב (*heart*) there is a metonymy of the subject for wisdom, which resides in the mind. In נפשו (*his own soul*) there is a synecdoche of the part.

The purpose is to teach that the fear of God is the safest and firmest possession; imbued with it, a man will enjoy good when other things fail him.

Since we are admonished to obtain a heart, it is apparent what sort of people we are at birth: namely, heartless, or devoid of heart. Hence it is said often above, and indeed twice and three times in chapter 10, that the ungodly man is destitute of heart. Hence also the natural man is called a brute, as elsewhere in Psal. 82. 7 and 73. 22. So also Prov. 30. 2 and 12. 1. More particularly still, in Job 11. 12 he is compared to a wild ass, and in Psal. 32 those whose sins have not been remitted are compared to the mule and horse. Let us therefore weigh what value the Holy Spirit sets upon a man of the sharpest intellect, furnished with riches, honour, and the fullest learning, who is at the same time devoid of piety, so that we may apply our mind to acquiring true wisdom. It would be grievous to us to be regarded by men as brutes and asses. How much more should it burn us to remain such in this judgment before God, who cannot be deceived? Yet it would go well with the ungodly if, just as they have lived the life of beasts, so they might die the death of an ass or some other brute.

When he says that the man who obtains wisdom and the fear of God loves himself, he indicates clearly enough that everyone who does not do this hates himself. And certainly, since without zeal for piety whatever road a man enters tends straight toward death, is he not openly proved a hater of himself and his own soul, even if he enters the shortest road to honours, riches, and glory? Therefore all the ungodly are Αὐτοκτόνοι, self-killers, by this very fact that they are not zealous for true wisdom—to say nothing of the fact that they devote themselves to perpetrating evil deeds and, as it were, feed upon wrongdoing. He also censures obliquely the man who devotes himself to acquiring riches, honours, and so forth while passing over heavenly wisdom.

But what appears in Matt. 18 seems to conflict with these things: “he who loves his own soul will lose it.” I answer that *soul* is taken in that place for life. Next, he censures that very small love by which God is placed after ourselves.

Next he teaches that it is not sufficient if someone obtains piety unless he holds it firmly and tenaciously. For only “he who has persevered to the end will be saved,” Mark 13. 13, just as

[1632 witness | PDF p. 404 | printed columns 782-783]

by contrast it would have been better never to have entered the course and path of piety than to cast away the pursuit of it through weariness, fear, or another disturbance of mind, 2 Pet. 2. 2.

When he says that a man of this kind will find good, he seems to set him against the man who, deserted by his own people, is destitute of every good because, surrounded by the dependants and goodwill of many, he rested in them without any concern for piety. The reason why this good is obtained is readily apparent. For when heavenly wisdom has been obtained and preserved, a man has God propitious to himself, and God will not permit him to be destitute of any good, Psal. 34. 10.
