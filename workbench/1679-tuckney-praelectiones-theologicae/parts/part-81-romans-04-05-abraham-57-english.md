Bellarmine himself fights to overthrow Osiander's position with several
arguments, but in some of them rather unsuccessfully.

For example, he argues: “Our righteousness by which we are justified is
faith, but faith is not God's essential righteousness. Therefore.” Yet even
according to Bellarmine himself, faith is not our formal righteousness in
justification. It is called our righteousness insofar as it apprehends and
applies Christ's mediatorial righteousness to us. Our theologians rightly
say this. Osiander does not mean this righteousness apprehended by faith,
but God's essential righteousness itself. Although his claim is false, it
nevertheless evades Bellarmine's argument.

Bellarmine's other argument in the same place is not very different: “A
person cannot be designated from a form that dwells extrinsically, but from
one that inheres. Yet if God's essential righteousness were in us, it would
be a form dwelling extrinsically. Therefore we cannot be designated
righteous from it.” It can be answered—without dwelling on the phrase
*dwelling extrinsically*, which at first glance, even if it does not contain
a contradiction by saying that something extrinsic dwells within, is at
least unclear and ill-suited—by letting it pass and understanding a form
dwelling extrinsically to mean one which comes from outside and then dwells
in something. God's essential righteousness transfused into a person in
justification would thus be an indwelling form, but one that comes from
outside, *thurathen*, as Aristotle says of the soul or mind. What difficulty
would there be, then, if a thing were designated from such a form arriving
from outside? Are not some people designated in precisely this way as
demon-possessed, God-seized, or God-bearing? Can the same form which comes
from without with respect to its point of origin not inhere and dwell with
respect to its point of arrival, as the soul inhabits the body and a person
is therefore called animate? Thus in sanctification one is rightly
designated righteous from righteousness infused extrinsically by God but,
through that infusion, inherent in the person. This is not so in
justification, where one is designated from another's righteousness, namely
Christ's imputed to us, as we showed earlier. Bellarmine used this argument
there against imputed righteousness and here against God's essential
righteousness, but in vain in both places.

There is also his third argument, which elsewhere he brings against Christ's
righteousness imputed to us, just as here against the essential righteousness
Osiander posits. It runs as follows:

[1679 printed p. 312 | IA leaf 320]

“If we were justified by God's essential righteousness, we would be as
righteous as Christ or God himself, for he is righteous by his essential
righteousness alone. But we are not as righteous as God and Christ, because
we receive from his fullness and according to measure.” To this I say:

1. Bellarmine's argument has more force against Osiander's position on
Christ's essential righteousness transfused into us than against our
position on his mediatorial righteousness imputed to us for justification.
Yet Bellarmine directs it equally against both: against Osiander in book 2,
chapter 5 of *On Justification*, and against us in chapter 7. He certainly
does not do so with equal right. God's essential righteousness differs
greatly from Christ's mediatorial righteousness. So too does the transfusion
of essential righteousness into us and its intimate union and coalescence
with our essence and substance without any created medium differ from the
moral and forensic imputation of mediatorial righteousness. That substantial
union of essence with us implies unity, identity, and thus equality, whereas
this imputation of mediatorial righteousness says nothing of the kind but
only that we are graciously accepted because of it.

2. Bellarmine's argument gives us occasion to wish for his candor. In
confirming it, he proves that we are not as righteous as Christ because,
though we partake of his righteousness, we draw from his fullness not the
whole of it but a measure suited to us. This is entirely true. But did he
not know well enough that we hold the same thing and use it to answer his
argument directed against the imputation of Christ's righteousness which we
affirm? We are not as righteous as Christ because we receive this
righteousness to our account according to our measure, capacity, and need,
not according to the fullness it has in him. His argument against us
therefore has no consequence.

This is indeed how matters stand: when Bellarmine uses this proof against
Osiander, it has its force; when we object it against Bellarmine, it has
none. It is customary for Bellarmine to treat things as orthodox when he
advances them against Anabaptists and other heterodox people, but as heresy
when we say them against him. We are indeed more than sufficiently unfair
to others whom we favor less. We have inserted among our earlier arguments
the rest of Bellarmine's arguments against Osiander which possess more
force.

Among other arguments, Calvin adduces that Christ is said to be “our
righteousness” and to have been “made” so “by God.” He contends that this
cannot be said of God's essential righteousness, because what existed
naturally from eternity cannot properly be said to have been made for us,
much less made by God, which would mean that God was made for us by God. I
leave you to weigh how much force this has. It seems possible to reply that
the one who existed in himself by his nature from eternity was made
righteousness for us from eternity with respect to purpose and in time with
respect to the actual exercise of this office. He was not made so by the
production of substance or essence, but by designation to the office and
its actual exercise. In this sense God was made by God the Father, just as
“the Lord is said to have rained sulfur and fire upon Sodom from the Lord,”
Genesis 19:24.

But why should we beg support for this truth from weak and soluble arguments
when, as we have already done, we can establish it by insoluble and
invincible ones? Indeed, why expend further unprofitable labor on an
unnecessary matter by using any more arguments to demonstrate something so
evident in itself?

It would be better to overturn the supports with which Osiander attempts to
prop up his position. Yet not even this is necessary, since they are so weak
that they fall of themselves.
