QUESTION XXXIII.

ON THE ORIGIN AND AUTHORITY OF COUNCILS.

Does it belong peculiarly to the Roman pontiff to appoint and convene councils,
to preside over them, and to confer upon them infallible authority in the
doctrines of faith and religion? And ought the Council of Trent to be received?
We deny against the papists.

I. That threefold power of the church which is occupied with its government
and which we have discussed thus far is exercised not only by pastors and
presbyteries, or the ordinary assemblies of particular churches, but also in
councils. These are extraordinary assemblies of several churches of the same
province or of the same nation and kingdom joined together, which have been
called provincial and national councils, or even assemblies of several nations
together, such as those once called ecumenical or universal. They were so
called not because they were convened from absolutely the whole world, but
because they were convened from the whole Roman Empire, which was called “the
whole inhabited world” (Luke 2:1), and consisted of bishops both eastern and
western, the two parts into which that empire was divided. Otherwise, truly
ecumenical councils have neither been held nor can be held.

II. Although there are various controversies concerning councils, three are
nevertheless regarded as the principal ones contained in this question:
convening, presidency, and authority. Before we speak of these, our doctrine
concerning councils and their necessity and use must be set forth briefly. It
will bring no small light to a fuller understanding and resolution of the
question.

[Marginalia: Our doctrine concerning councils.]

III. A council, so called from calling together or summoning—that is, from
convening—is a public assembly appointed either by the civil magistrate or by
the common consent of the churches. In it pious and learned men, duly called
and delegated, take cognizance of ecclesiastical causes and, after comparing
their judgments, determine them from the Word of God according to the power
granted to the church by God, so that the evils which commonly produce heresies
or schisms may either be guarded against or remedied.

IV. Although councils are not simply and absolutely necessary to the existence
of the church, since without them the faith and the church can remain—and for a
long time did remain—unharmed, nevertheless their use and necessity for the
church’s well-being are rightly urged against the disorderly and the
Independents on account of the manifold benefit they can confer. (1) They serve
for the preservation of truth against heresies, so that the saving doctrine of
faith may be asserted against heretics and confirmed by the agreement and
testimony of a public assembly. (2) They serve for the confirmation of peace
and good order against schisms and anarchy, so that ecclesiastical discipline
may be rightly constituted according to the circumstances of times, places,
and persons and restored wherever it has collapsed. (3) They serve for the
defense of Christian liberty against the ambitious endeavors of those who
advance toward tyranny. For just as one cannot hope that the church militant on
earth will be free from diseases of this kind, so this remedy of councils has
become not only useful but also necessary, especially when the church, with the
cross and persecution removed, enjoys outward peace. In the idleness afforded
by such peace, evils of this kind commonly arise more frequently and their
contagion spreads more widely. Then a common fire must be extinguished by
common assistance and effort; evils which seize the whole body must be remedied
and forestalled by the whole body; and what touches everyone ought to be cared
for by everyone.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 261 | IA leaf 295 | PDF p. 295]

V. Here the apostles lighted the way for us by their example. When a dispute
arose about whether the legal ceremonies were to be observed, they immediately
appointed a council, and “the apostles and presbyters gathered to consider this
matter” (Acts 15:6). For although the apostles were infallible and therefore
could have settled this dispute by themselves, they nevertheless willed by
their example, under the guidance of the Holy Spirit, to prescribe an order
which would remain in force perpetually in the church after their death.
Indeed, if the apostles, who had received from God a peculiar prerogative of
inerrancy in the faith, nevertheless would not give judgment concerning the
controversy over the legal ceremonies until they had first consulted together,
how much more ought pastors of a later age, who are liable to error, to join
their labors and seek mutual assistance? For God does not bestow everything
upon individuals, and often many accomplish what a few cannot. Add that
ecclesiastical laws possess greater authority among the people when they have
been framed by the unanimous consent of many pastors.

VI. Although assemblies of this kind do not always have a happy outcome, owing
incidentally to human corruption—for the corruption of the best thing is
commonly the worst, as occurred through the arts of Rome when the Council of
Trent, from which a reformation in head and members was expected, produced
nothing but the confirmation of errors and of Antichristian tyranny—this was
the reason which once moved Gregory Nazianzen to say to Procopius in Letter 42
that “he fled all assemblies of bishops because he had never seen a good and
happy outcome of any synod,” namely, because of the vice, contentiousness, and
love of rule of human beings. Yet this ought not to prejudice a thing lawful
and highly useful in itself. Thus Augustine truly says in Letter 118 that
“councils have the most salutary authority in the church of God,” since Christ
declares in Matthew 18:20 that “where two or three are gathered together in his
name, he will be in their midst.”

[Marginalia: The doctrine of the papists.]

VII. Concerning these councils, it is asked to whom the right of convening,
presidency, and authority belongs. The papists refer all these things to their
pontiff. They assert that it belongs peculiarly to him (1) to appoint and
convene councils, (2) to preside over them and govern the whole assembly with a
kind of praetorian, or rather dictatorial, power, and (3) to give them
infallible authority so that they cannot err in canons and decrees and must be
acquiesced in absolutely, without further examination, as Bellarmine states in
book 1, *On Councils*, chapters 12, 13, 14, 19, and 20, and book 2, chapters 2,
3, and following.

[Marginalia: And that of the orthodox.]

VIII. The orthodox respond in different ways to these several questions. (1)
With respect to convening, they maintain that its right belongs to the church.
For just as Christ entrusted the power of government to the church, so he
deposited with her the authority to procure all things which pertain to the
prosperity of that government, among which the convening of councils is rightly
placed. Yet since this convening cannot occur except by authority, it must more
properly be judged to belong to those who are principal members of the church
and whom God gives his church as nursing fathers and guardians of both tables,
namely kings and magistrates. Jurisdiction and command, as well as coercive
power, which is often needed here, reside with them. But if the magistrate is
alien to the faith, or if the church is in manifest danger, the church ought
not to fail herself. She may use the right and authority of convening which God
entrusted to her so that present and urgent evils may be met as quickly as
possible, if this can be done and in whatever way it can be done. We maintain,
however, that the pontiff possesses no right to appoint councils. (2) We say the
same concerning presidency, and judge that two offices must be distinguished
here, the civil and the ecclesiastical, corresponding to the matters in
councils which pertain to the regulation of external order and those which
pertain to the investigation and decision of spiritual matters. We assign the
former to the civil magistrate, but the latter

[1847 vol. III printed p. 262 | IA leaf 296 | PDF p. 296]

to one or more bishops or pastors elected out of the ordinary order by common
consent. The duties of the civil president are to convene the council, protect
it once convened, prohibit both external violence and internal disorder and
unseemliness, and also sanction by his own authority decrees lawfully made and
confirmed by the signatures of the delegates, so that “a synodical judgment
may be sanctioned by royal ratification.” The duties of the ecclesiastical
president, however, are to submit questions to the council, govern the
discussion and debate, ask for opinions, have the decrees recorded in the acts
by notaries, and the like. (3) Finally, with respect to authority, we
acknowledge in councils no infallibility by reason of the subject matter except
insofar as their canons and decrees agree with the written Word of God. For the
rest, we maintain that by reason of their human efficient cause they are
fallible and can possess no inerrancy from the Roman pontiff.

[Marginalia: It is proved that councils do not depend upon the pontiff with
respect to their convening: 1. because such power was not given to him.]

IX. The foundations of our doctrine are these. (1) The pontiff received this
power—to convene councils, to preside over them, and to bestow infallible
authority upon them—neither from Christ, because no trace of it appears in
Scripture, nor from the emperor, from whom he was accustomed to seek the
appointment of a council, as Leo I did in Letters 9 and 24 to Theodosius and 26
to Pulcheria. Indeed, in ancient times the bishops were not even permitted by
civil laws to assemble without the emperor’s consent. Nor could the pontiff
have it from himself, because no one takes this honor for himself (Heb. 5:4).

[Marginalia: 2. Because convening is an act of civil power.]

X. (2) Because it is an act of civil and coercive power, which does not fall
within the ecclesiastical order nor belong to it by right. Such acts include
defining and prescribing the place, time, and manner of assemblies, furnishing
security to those who attend, supplying expenses, commanding bishops to
assemble, establishing penalties against the defiant, and similar things which
are occupied with external and civil matters and cannot be accomplished
without command.

[Marginalia: 3. Because this is clear from examples in the Old and New
Testaments.]

XI. (3) Because examples from the Old and New Testaments establish that the
appointment of sacred assemblies belonged to faithful kings and magistrates.
This is clear from the example of Joshua (23:24), David (2 Sam. 6; 1 Chron.
28), Solomon (1 Kings 8), Jehoshaphat (2 Chron. 20), Hezekiah (2 Chron. 29),
and Josiah (2 Kings 33). Under the New Testament it is clear from the example
of Constantine the Great at the Council of Nicaea, Theodosius I at the Council
of Constantinople, Theodosius II at Ephesus, Marcian at Chalcedon, Justinian
at the Second Council of Constantinople, and others at other councils. Those
who wish the emperor’s authority and the consent or request of the Roman bishop
to be regarded as two partial causes cavil here in vain. For all the other
bishops also could and ought to have consented and, when there was need, to have
requested a synod.

[Marginalia: Nor with respect to presidency.]

XII. Nor do councils depend upon the pontiff with respect to presidency,
because the rationale of presidency is the same as that of convening. For
establishing the order of proceeding, restraining insolence, and governing the
whole assembly by one’s authority are no less acts of civil and coercive power
than those which pertain to appointing a council. Hence those who are read in
the Old and New Testaments to have appointed and convened such assemblies also
presided over them, whether immediately and in person or mediately through
legates, as long as the majesty of the emperors in the empire and the liberty
of the church remained. Indeed, an ordinary and perpetual right in
ecclesiastical affairs rests upon divine or ecclesiastical law, but neither law
gives the Roman pope the right to preside. Divine law does not, because no proof
of it can be offered. Ecclesiastical law does not, because the members of the
universal church have never consented to confer such a right upon the pope. To
this is added the perpetual practice of the purer councils.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 263 | IA leaf 297 | PDF p. 297]

[Marginalia: Nor with respect to authority.]

XIII. With respect to infallible authority, it cannot be said to be conferred
upon councils by the pontiff. (1) He cannot give what he does not possess, as
was shown elsewhere in Question XI. Hence even the adversaries themselves do
not agree about his infallibility. (2) Councils possess no authority except
when and insofar as their decrees agree with the Word of God and are found to
agree with it upon serious and religious examination, according to
Deuteronomy 4:2, Isaiah 8:20, Galatians 1:8–9, and 1 Thessalonians 5:21. For
since councils represent in epitome the church militant on earth, they are no
more placed beyond every risk of error, whether in doctrine or in morals, than
the church herself. Since no member of the church here is inerrant, it is
absurd to attribute inerrancy to the whole body.

XIV. The authority of councils therefore stands far below the authority of
Scripture, nor do their decisions bind the conscience any further than they
are established as conforming to the Word of God. Hence there belongs to them
in matters of faith a judgment which is not authoritative, infallible, and
supreme, but subordinate and ministerial. It binds with respect to the external
order of ecclesiastical communion and ought to possess great weight among all
the pious, but it does not bind with respect to faith and conscience except
insofar as councils agree with Scripture, which alone can bind the conscience.

XV. Councils can be occupied with three things which occur together in the
church: (1) doctrines of faith, (2) canons or constitutions of government and
good order, and (3) the exercise of discipline upon those who err ruinously in
either faith or morals. Accordingly, their authority can be considered in
different ways. With respect to the first, their decrees have the character of
the opinions of wise and prudent men who can deliberate maturely concerning
each matter. With respect to the second, they are directors who, by the power
granted to them, have the right to sanction what is conducive to good order.
With respect to the third, they are judges constituted by the church to inflict
ecclesiastical penalties upon sinners.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XVI. Although councils ought to be assembled in Christ’s name, it does not
follow that they must be appointed only by the sacred authority of Peter and
his successor. For since acting in Christ’s name is nothing other than doing
something for his glory in obedience to his command and confidence in his
grace, princes and magistrates act in Christ’s name no less than ministers do.

XVII. Even if the Levitical high priest had possessed the right to appoint
councils and to impart infallible authority to them, it would not follow that
the same right belongs to the Roman pontiff, because no one except Christ
succeeded the ancient high priest (Hebrews 7–9). Add that the right of
extraordinary assemblies belonged to the supreme magistrate, as was said, and
that whatever authority they possessed flowed entirely from the Word, not from
the high priest.

XVIII. Councils possess the promise of Christ’s perpetual presence, not
absolutely but relatively—namely, insofar as they are gathered in Christ’s
name and act according to the rule prescribed to them. Otherwise they are
subject to anathema (Gal. 1:8).

XIX. Bellarmine vainly uses the testimonies of more recent pontiffs to confirm
his pontiff’s supreme power over the church and councils. We can answer them
just as properly with the words which the Pharisees impiously directed against
Christ in John 8:13: “You testify concerning yourself; your testimony is not
true.”

XX. Although we are unwilling to give councils infallible authority, we do not
maintain that their authority is nothing. Rather, it possesses great weight in
the church, insofar as their judgment, though not self-authenticating, is
nevertheless public and proceeds from authority granted by Christ. We intend
this to be said only of councils which

[1847 vol. III printed p. 264 | IA leaf 298 | PDF p. 298]

are legitimate and free assemblies gathered in Christ’s name, not of false and
spurious ones which cast aside Christ’s command forbidding anything to be
added to or taken away from his Word, establish whatever they please by their
own judgment, and, not content with the oracles of Scripture, which is the
single rule of perfect wisdom, invent something new from their own heads.

XXI. The external forum is one thing and the internal forum of conscience
another. A judgment defined in a lawful council is judicial and praetorian with
respect to the external forum, so that in external society one must stand or
fall by it, and from it the supreme magistrate derives the right to impose
silence upon dissenters or to banish them from the state. Otherwise no measure
or end could be imposed upon disputes and contentions in external society. With
respect to the internal forum of conscience, a council for us is not mere
consultation and inquiry, as Bellarmine wrongly imputes to us. Rather, together
with inquiry into what is true and just, it is the pronouncement of a judgment,
not praetorian, as the papists would have it, but ministerial; it persuades and
does not compel; it is the pronouncement of an interpreter who declares and
explains the law, not of a judge who makes law.

XXII. The judgment of bishops in a council cannot be judicial, decisive, and
binding in itself, because individuals can take sides and indeed can err; nor
can it be such because of the agreement of many, because they too, as the
adversaries admit, can err; nor because of the pope’s confirmation, because
that is external to the council and subsequent to the judgments of the bishops
and therefore cannot change their nature. The pope himself does not possess an
autocratic judgment concerning the faith, and the papists do not agree whether
the faith of Christians rests upon the judgment of the council or of the pope.

XXIII. It is not necessary that there be in the church some external
infallible judgment by which controversies of faith are decided. For the peace
and salvation of their conscience, the internal or divine definitive judgment
of Christ and the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scriptures is sufficient for
believers. An external, human, infallible, and definitive judgment in the
church is neither necessary nor possible. For controversies do not pertain to
the external order but to the judgment of the mind, for which a judge is needed
who illuminates the mind and sanctifies the will. An external and human judge
cannot do this because he possesses no power over the conscience.

[Marginalia: What must be held concerning the Council of Trent and why it is
rejected by us.]

XXIV. From what has been said thus far, it is not obscurely evident what must
be held concerning the authority of the Council of Trent, which the papists
repeatedly thrust upon us: is it such that it can or ought to be received by
believers? Since it is established by many most weighty reasons that it was
anything but a lawful and free council gathered in Christ’s name, it follows
incontrovertibly that it must be held in no esteem by us but rejected as
spurious and Antichristian. (1) It was convened by the pope, who possesses no
right of convening or presidency, as was said before. (2) The judges of that
council—the bishops and others upon whom the pontiff bestowed this
privilege—could not be competent judges: because they never proved from the
Word of God that this power belonged to them; because they arrogated to
themselves a praetorian and decisive power in the forum of conscience, such as
belongs to no mortal; because in the council they themselves were especially
to be examined, so that the power they claimed, their faith and morals, and
thus their reformation would be called into controversy, and therefore they
could not be judges in their own cause; because they were the pope’s sworn
slaves who did not dare depart even a finger’s breadth from his prescription;
and because they were the opposing party and implacable enemies of the
Protestants, whose cause was being debated, whom they had already struck with
anathema more than once and who therefore, according to

[1847 vol. III printed p. 265 | IA leaf 299 | PDF p. 299]

the canon of Pope Nicholas, *C. quod suspect.* 3, q. 5, could not be judges
because they were suspect and hostile. (3) Its sole standard was not sacred
Scripture and the voice of the Holy Spirit, the supreme judge speaking in
Scripture, but also unwritten tradition together with Scripture. The pontiff
acted as its infallible interpreter, whom they impiously called the soul of
Scripture. (4) It was not free, both with respect to the place, which was
suspect and treacherous, and with respect to the persons, because the
Protestants, whom it most concerned, were never lawfully invited and heard. If
safe conducts were granted to them, this was not done so that they could
participate in the council and freely state their opinion with the others, but
so that they might plead their cause as defendants and receive laws from the
others as dictators and masters. Furthermore, the synod’s assurance, extorted
by the emperor’s entreaties, was ambiguous and deceitful, for the qualifying
clause “as far as concerns the synod itself” was inserted. It left an open door
for the pontiff’s arts, allowing him to determine against them at will. Again,
the council could not be free because the pontiff, at once defendant and
prosecutor, presided through legates. Relying upon a crafty and ambiguous
decree, they claimed for themselves alone the right to propose matters through
the clause “the legates proposing,” so that it was permissible for no one to
offer any subject for deliberation to the council. The Spaniards and the
imperial party rightly complained that this notable fraud “struck at the
vitals of the council’s liberty.” Thus it was permissible for the legates, as
their inclination dictated, to remove controversies displeasing to the pope
concerning the pope’s power, the council’s superiority, and similar matters,
and to forge and formulate articles so that they would be decided according to
their wish. Indeed, almost everything of any importance was not only examined
but also determined at Rome before it was defined in the council. Hence the
well-known saying repeated by the papists themselves: “That Holy Spirit who
governs the counsels of Trent is repeatedly sent from Rome enclosed in a
saddlebag.” And the Cardinal of Lorraine complains gravely in Paolo Sarpi,
book 7, that “the council is by no means free, since nothing may be considered
or decreed except what pleases the legates, and the legates do nothing except
according to the pope’s will.” I pass over the crafty arts by which the
pontiff’s agents endeavored to draw the fathers of the council over to their
side, not only berating but even expelling those who resisted, and applying
themselves to removing freedom of speech in any way possible. I leave
unmentioned innumerable other things which prove their thoroughly corrupt
manner of proceeding. From these things it is clearer than the noonday light
that the council was anything but free.

XXV. (5) So far was any reformation of doctrine or discipline, which had been
desired and expected for so long in both head and members, from being
accomplished in this council that instead a greater confirmation of errors,
corruptions, and papal tyranny resulted from it. Whatever reformation was
undertaken was entirely trifling and hypocritical, placed at the discretion of
the pope, who was the author of the corruption and abuses. Thus the fathers did
not dare touch, much less remove, the root of nearly all the evils, which was
fixed in the pope and the Roman curia and in the tyranny, avarice, luxury, and
ambition of both. Hence, if it seemed that any decrees were framed which struck
at the pontiff and Roman curia in any way, the subsequent decree of Session 25,
chapter 21, immediately cut all their sinews. It provided “that in all decrees
concerning the reformation of morals and ecclesiastical discipline, the
authority of the apostolic see both is and is always to be understood to be
preserved.” Add the right to interpret every decree of the council, which the
pope reserved for himself with the synod’s indulgence. Once this was
established, everything in any way injurious to the pope and curia fell to
nothing and everything was restored to its former state.

XXVI. (6) Many decrees of the synod are ambiguous and pliable in expression so
as somehow to reconcile the opinions of dissenters, and they are the kind that
suit Apollo the Ambiguous better than a Christian council. Hence those
Tridentine

[1847 vol. III printed p. 266 | IA leaf 300 | PDF p. 300]

decrees were rightly called the buskin of Hercules, because they could easily
be received by either contending party as though favorable to itself. This was
done by Domingo de Soto and Andrés de Vega, who, although diametrically opposed
to one another on the topic of nature and grace, nevertheless each cited the
Tridentine canons on his own behalf in books which both published on this
subject while the council was still in session. The same can easily be shown
on other topics, so that it was not said without reason that “the fathers of
Trent pursued unity of words, not agreement in things.” Hence so broad a field
for litigation was left to present-day sophists, and crafty reconcilers—the
Walenburch brothers, the bishop of Condom in his *Exposition of the Catholic
Faith*, and others—relying upon the generality and ambiguity of the Tridentine
decrees endeavor to persuade the simple that we agree on most things. Yet it
is easy to show that this is an attempt to join light with darkness.

XXVII. (7) Neither the emperor nor kings or princes subscribed to this council.
And all France was never willing to receive it so that it would possess the
force of law. The Cardinal of Lorraine was gravely reproached because he had
assented to many articles injurious to France. The fathers of the Parlement of
Paris also charged that many things in the decrees of reformation derogated
from the right and liberties of France. Such were that the bishops are given
power to punish laypeople with monetary penalties and to seize their goods;
that in the article concerning duels they assume the power to proceed against
kings, the emperor, and other superiors, to strike them with anathema, to
divest them of their dominions, to adjudge their fiefs to other lords and the
goods of private persons to the treasury; and that, by confirming
ecclesiastical immunities, the council grants the power to judge cases of
accused bishops to the pope alone and the judges of the church, as though
princes lose their right over their subjects as soon as the latter become
clergy. On account of these and many other things of this kind they constantly
repudiated this council as by no means proceeding from the Holy Spirit, as was
observed by de Thou, Bouchel, Pasquier, and others, and especially by Charles
Du Moulin. In a published consultation he demonstrated that “the Council of
Trent was defectively appointed, null, conducted and concluded contrary to the
ancient decrees of the fathers.” Indeed, Pierre de Marca, archbishop of Paris,
in *On the Concord of Priesthood and Empire*, book 2, testifies that the
assembly of the Gallican clergy frequently asked the kings to promulgate the
Council of Trent upon the condition that by their authority they except those
articles which opposed the customs of the kingdom and the liberties of the
Gallican Church. “The princes,” he says, “were not able to accommodate
themselves to their desires, although they frequently referred this whole
matter to the counsel of the most prudent men”; and he adds as the reason “that
the liberty of the Gallican Church is trampled underfoot in very many decrees
of this council, etc.” See also de Thou, *Continuation*, book 7. And lest
anyone say that any errors there concern discipline alone and not matters of
faith, it is easy to show that such things regard not only discipline but also
doctrine itself. Examples are the article concerning the pope’s superiority
over a council, which was confirmed in this council; the article concerning
the church’s power over the temporal authority of kings, which was defined in
chapter 19, *On Reformation*, in the final session; the article concerning the
immunities of the clergy; and the like. Add that one cannot understand why the
church should be infallible in doctrine but not in discipline, since being
governed according to Christ’s prescription seems no less to belong to the
essence of the church than being led into all truth. This is all the more so
because the connection between discipline and doctrine is so great that
articles of discipline also pass into articles of doctrine, as was just said.

XXVIII. Furthermore, if anyone wishes to know this council and its manner of
proceeding more deeply, let him consult the most upright and accomplished
writer Paolo Sarpi, who related its truly incomparable history to us, as
Conring calls it, with such accuracy and fidelity that nothing further could be

[1847 vol. III printed p. 267 | IA leaf 301 | PDF p. 301]

desired. Cardinal Pallavicino, intending to play the parasite to the Roman
curia, undertook in laborious volumes to refute that golden work and endeavored
with a malignant tooth to gnaw the reputation and fidelity of so great a man.
Yet he achieved nothing but the contempt of pious and learned men, since he was
convicted by the most weighty papist writers themselves—especially Count
Giulio Clemente Scotti and Cesare Aquilino—not only of vanity and arrogance
but also of ignorance, dishonesty, and putrid slanders against a most faithful
historian. Hence it happened that after Pallavicino’s history was published,
among sensible men even within the papacy who compared the two histories,
Sarpi began to be held in greater esteem than ever before. Cesare Aquilino, in
*On the Three Historians*, page 49, warns that “the whole force and weight of
the response to Paolo Sarpi’s history found in Pallavicino’s volumes consists
in answering arguments and in those empty digressions. The rest which he
writes is superfluous, useless, and sometimes harmful, and those two large
volumes can rightly be compared to owls, which appear to be great birds not
because of the size of their limbs and flesh, but because of the profusion of
useless feathers.” And Giulio Clemente, although devoted even to the papist
fathers, published various moral, critical, and historical notes upon the
letter and the thirteen chapters of the introduction to Pallavicino’s history.
In them he proves that Pallavicino deprives himself of credibility in many of
the things which he wrote against Sarpi from sheer envy and malice; that he is
a slanderer and acts in bad faith in various accusations contrary to publicly
known truth; and that his history is fabulous, inept, scandalous, and harmful
to the papacy itself. But who would marvel that the true and sincere history of
that council, which candidly discloses the Roman curia’s worst arts for
oppressing truth and liberty, is less pleasing to the palate of this man? As
the curia’s sworn slave, he applies himself to the single purpose of
establishing its errors and tyranny. Therefore he does not blush to forge a new
gospel for us and fashions an idea of the church, not such as Christ and the
apostles handed down to us, but such as Plato and Aristotle and other wise men
and rulers of the age would fashion a commonwealth. Until now we had learned
from Christ’s gospel that the church is an assembly of people bearing the
cross after Christ, bidding farewell to the world, its vanities, and worldly
politics, regarding the wealth, glory, and pleasures of the age as nothing,
glorying in sufferings, poverty, and persecutions, and diligently applying
themselves to the practice of piety, humility, charity, and other good works as
the safest way to attain salvation. But our purple-clad prelate has discovered
a different and far more convenient Christianity, one pleasing to the flesh
and capable of being reconciled with the world. He maintains that the
government of the church ought to be instituted after the example of the
kingdoms of the world, by a worldly and carnal polity, and that this is by
Christ’s institution (book 1, chapter 23). He admits that the church ought to
set before itself the increase of riches and glory as its end and direct
itself to possessing the perfect human happiness which Christ formed, to such
a degree that if Plato and Aristotle were alive, they would admit that
according to the rules of their worldly wisdom no commonwealth more excellent
and noble than the Christian commonwealth could be formed. And because,
according to the idea of the wise men of the age, a commonwealth cannot be
happy unless it is powerful in wealth and glory and abounds in those wise
according to the flesh, he wills the same rationale to obtain in the church.
He admits that the Roman church is instituted according to this idea and uses
every means to accumulate wealth which, although accused of simony, may be
rightly employed. He mocks those who would reform the church according to the
idea of the gospel, which he calls an ideal reformation sought only by foolish
people driven by misguided zeal without knowledge (*Introduction*, chapter
10). According to him, the church ought to dread nothing more than poverty and
to apply herself to fleeing no evil more earnestly than this (book 17,

chapter 14). Therefore he says that all who claim ecclesiastical goods ought
for the most part to be distributed to the poor are enemies of the church and
that this practice directly opposes the church’s human happiness and the
institution of God and nature. He therefore judges that these goods are
appropriately spent to sustain the splendor of the Roman curia, through which
unbelievers and Muslims can be converted to the faith (book 8, chapter 17).
Thus he says that ambition and desire for glory are two spurs which drive
people to the church’s ministry (book 1, chapter 25). Therefore riches and
benefices must be accumulated to entice people by these bonds of human
interest (book 12, chapter 6). Human happiness and temporal prosperity ought
to be found at Rome as though at their fountain, and riches must be transported
there from all sides to uphold the majesty of that curia. The pope is monarch
of the universe; his power is unlimited and independent; all kings ought to be
his tributaries; and he can dispose of everyone’s goods at will (book 8,
chapter 17, and book 6, chapter 3). I pass over innumerable other things of
this kind which can neither be read without horror nor reported without
indignation. But this is not how we learned Christ.
