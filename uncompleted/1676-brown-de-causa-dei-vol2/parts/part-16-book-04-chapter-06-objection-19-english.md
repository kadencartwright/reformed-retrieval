# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 19.* Galatians 3:23, 24, 25: *But before faith came, we were kept
under the law's protection, confined unto the faith which was to be revealed.
And so the law was our tutor looking toward Christ, that we might be justified
by faith. But after faith came, we are no longer under a tutor.*

*Answer.* There are some who understand *law* here as the ceremonial law.
[*“He had taught before,”* Beza says on verse 23, *“that the ceremonial law was
abrogated, both insofar as it convicted us of sin and death through
transgressions and insofar as it had been the external bond of that very
condemnation. But now, considering that same ceremonial law as the shadow and
figure of the gospel promises which were to be exhibited in their own time,
he teaches that it too has ceased in this respect, the time prescribed for
the exhibition of the mediator and of the things upon which the fathers'
faith depended,*

[Printed p. 77 | IA PDF 85 | IA leaf 85 | IA decausadei02brow]

*having come to an end. He therefore compares it with a tutor,
and afterward with a guardian; corresponding on the other side to this
comparison is the church's childhood under the law, for the church has always
been one.”*] Hence nothing can be concluded for the abrogation of the moral
law. Piscator teaches the same on the passage. [*“He points out,”* Piscator
says, *“what the use of the legal economy was, and from this infers that it was
to be abolished at the coming of the gospel. He teaches that the legal
economy had a twofold use: one, that the Jews might be kept as though by a
certain protection, in hope of Christ's coming; the other, that they might be
instructed as though by a certain tutor concerning righteousness and
salvation to be offered through Christ. The Jews' hope was sustained because,
while the legal economy continued, God repeatedly sent prophets who promised
them Christ's coming. They were also instructed through ceremonies such as
circumcision, and so forth. Through circumcision they were taught that Christ
would be born from Abraham's seed and would circumcise their hearts by taking
away sin through his death and renewing them by his Spirit to the pursuit of
righteousness. Through the Passover lamb they were admonished that Christ, as
an innocent lamb, would be slain on the cross to expiate their sins and thus
to free them from the devil's tyranny. The same was signified through the
other sacrifices. Furthermore, since all these things were shadowed and
prefigured through the legal economy as things which would occur in their own
time, it follows that when the realities themselves were exhibited, those
shadows and figures came to an end.”*] And this indeed is the law which was
given 430 years after the covenant, according to verse 17. For this cannot be
said of the moral law alone, which was in force even in Abraham's time, before
that period, and from the beginning.

2. Even if the apostle is also speaking here about the moral law, which was
ordained through angels by the hand of a mediator and which confines all
under sin, he nevertheless speaks of it as it was given at that time and had
the appendix of ceremonies attached. For the Jews alone were under that law
in this manner. And we are now also freed from that law so considered. They
were kept confined under that law as though under a protection; that is, as
Pareus teaches on the passage, [*“just as people are separated from enemies
outside by defensive walls, ramparts, and ditches, so the Jewish people were
separated from all the Gentiles outside by the economy of the law. They were
shut in so that the nation from which Christ was to be born would not be
intermingled with the other Gentiles. Thus God's giving this nation many
peculiar things—the written moral law, priesthood, rites, sacrifices, and a
form of civil government—was a kind of enclosure and fortification by which
that people, surrounded, conspicuous, impregnable, and unmixed, was preserved
until Christ and remained separated from other peoples.”*] In this discussion,
then, the apostle speaks both about the moral and the ceremonial law, indeed
about that whole economy which the false apostles misused in order to seek
righteousness, either entirely or in part. For this reason he applies certain
things proper to the one law and other things proper to the other. It is
therefore an error and a fallacy to apply everything the apostle says here
about the law either to the moral law alone or to the ceremonial law alone,
excluding the other. Consequently, although the apostle sometimes speaks here
about the moral law, it does not follow that he is speaking about that law
alone when he says it was a tutor under whom we now no longer are. This is
further clear from chapter 4:3, where he calls the law under which the infant
church was placed as under guardians and stewards *the elements of the
world*. Without doubt he means the

[Printed p. 78 | IA PDF 86 | IA leaf 86 | IA decausadei02brow]

ceremonies and legal shadows, which were like rudiments with which children are
customarily imbued in schools, and elsewhere are called *beggarly and
powerless elements*.

3. Even if it were true that the apostle is speaking here about the moral
law, it still does not follow that he speaks about the moral law insofar as it
is the rule of life and conduct. That conclusion cannot follow unless it is
first proved that the law could not come under consideration in some other
aspect, which will never be done, for at that time it could have other uses
which have now been abolished. On these see the commentators, especially
Calvin, who says [*“that the law is considered here as clothed with its own
qualities: namely, that it establishes a reward for works and a punishment;
that is, it promises life to its observers and curses all transgressors, while
it requires supreme perfection and exact obedience. It remits nothing and
pardons nothing, but calls even the smallest errors to account. It does not
display Christ and his grace openly, but points to him from afar, and that
enclosed under ceremonies as though under coverings. And he says that these
qualities have been abolished, not the law itself insofar as it is the rule
of living well, the bridle by which we are kept in the fear of the Lord, and
the spur for correcting the sluggishness of our flesh; finally, insofar as it
is useful for teaching, correcting, and reproving, so that believers may be
equipped for every good work.”*] Furthermore, it is evident that the moral law
as the rule of life and conduct is not meant here, because:

1. He speaks of that use of the law in which the Jews were confined, as though
shut up in a prison. But this use was not the law's directive use, by which it
showed the right way and was a light to their feet, according to Psalm 119:105
and Proverbs 6:23. For insofar as they were directed by this light, they were
free.

2. The law, insofar as it establishes and prescribes the duty we owe, does not
properly lead to Christ so that we may be justified by faith. Instead, it
declares the will of God the lawgiver concerning our natural duty and binds
us to perform it. This law was in force before the fall and had this same use.
Therefore this use does not properly look toward Christ, nor can the law, with
respect to this use, be called a tutor leading to Christ.

3. All people, both Jews and Gentiles, have been freed from this use of the
law, whatever precisely it was, after faith came—that is, after Christ came,
whom the Jews expected and awaited by faith. All are freed from that tutor,
not only Jews but also Gentiles, not only the godly and believers but also
those who have not yet believed. For it is certain that all have been freed
from the yoke of the ceremonial law. They have therefore also been freed from
the yoke of the moral law, if that law was a yoke and tutor with respect to
this direction. Consequently the law will have been entirely abolished, with
respect to everyone and not only believers.

4. But grant that being freed from this tutor is a peculiar privilege of
believers. It is clear that this freedom by no means consists in their no
longer having the moral law as a rule. For through this liberation they become
Christ's and Abraham's seed, according to verse 24, and therefore possess the
same privileges Abraham possessed. Yet Abraham did not enjoy this privilege,
if indeed it is to be called a privilege, for he was under the direction and
obligation of the moral law.

5. Furthermore, that law, or that use of the law by reason of which the law is
said to have been a tutor, did not exist in Abraham's time but finally came
into existence 430 years later, according to verse 17. Yet in Abraham's time,
even when the covenant was ratified by God, this use of the moral law did
exist.

6. Paul himself testifies clearly enough in this same epistle, chapter
5:13–14, that this freedom must not be extended so far

[Printed p. 79 | IA PDF 87 | IA leaf 87 | IA decausadei02brow]

as that: *For you were called to liberty, brothers; only do not seize liberty
as an occasion given to the flesh, but through love serve one another. For
the whole law is fulfilled in one saying, namely, You shall love your neighbor
as yourself.* Who will now doubt that Paul, descending here to ethical
precepts and about to speak of Christian conduct, takes God's law—namely, the
moral law, whose second table is summed up in this, as Christ himself
testifies, *You shall love your neighbor*, and so forth—as the rule of life?
And from this passage Piscator observes that Christian liberty is not license
to do whatever pleases the flesh, but freedom from ceremonies and from the
curse of the law, not from obedience to the law. [*“Rather,”* he says, *“the
more anyone understands Christian liberty and knows that it has been given to
him, the more earnestly he serves God his liberator and obeys his
commandments.”*]
