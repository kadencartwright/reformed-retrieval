# A Letter Written in the Name of a Son Who Had Withdrawn from His Father for Religion's Sake

This is a fresh English translation of the complete Latin presentation at CO
X/1 179–181. CO reports that Theodore Beza translated the work from French.
The unlocated French wording has not been reconstructed: the translation below
is accountable to Beza's printed Latin witness. The first-person voice is the
unnamed son in whose name Calvin composed the letter, not an autobiographical
claim by Calvin. No wording from Beaty and Farley's modern translation supplied
the English.

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 179]

Because I am presenting an account of my action in writing and while absent
rather than in person, I ask you, revered father, to hold me excused. There are
two reasons. The first is that I feared I would hardly find you sufficiently
ready to hear me in a matter that is distasteful and outwardly strange. The
second is that the profound reverence with which I regard you has until now
not permitted me to bring any of these things before you.

Now, however, I have judged that I must take this course. By it I might either
appease you, insofar as you continued to harbor the offense you had conceived
against me, or at least soften it, whatever it may be, until a better
opportunity offered itself in which I could satisfy you fully.

It grieves you that I do not follow you in the form of divine worship which
you hold to be true. If I did this out of contempt, as many frivolous people
do—people for whom all religion is a matter of indifference and who are devoid
of every devotion to the observance of God's worship—I would be utterly
unworthy of any pardon. But if the fear of God and the testimony of conscience
have impelled me to it, I persuade myself that on both those grounds you will
judge my action more tolerable.

Now, so that I do not weary you with many words, this cannot be denied: many
gross abuses and corruptions have a place in the church and are covered with
God's name, while “good intention” is believed to wash all of them clean. In
the meantime, those who live in such a way that, without the authority of
God's will, they decide they will be approved before God are, as the common
saying has it, settling their account without the innkeeper. This must instead
remain fixed: God is to be worshiped according to his judgment, not ours.

I admit that I ought not to be tolerated if I dare pronounce on these matters
from my own perception, as though I were superior to others in judgment. But
the question here is not which person understands better than another. For
that rule is both established and well known, and

### [CO X/1, p. 180]

approved by the common judgment of all: God prefers obedience to every
sacrifice. Thus, even without weighing the matter more subtly, it is
sufficiently established that God rejects whatever does not answer to his
will. The testimony of the Lord's will must be sought from Scripture alone, in
which it stands recorded. It follows that I must guard against whatever
presents itself in opposition to Scripture, unless I wish knowingly to sin
against God.

It may seem absurd to you that I place in this category things commonly
believed to be so holy. Yet I ask only this of you, my father. Although I am at
an age commonly judged more prone to admit anything whatever, do not attribute
my judgment on matters of such importance to youthful fickleness. Rather, I
began to think this way because I was constrained and thoroughly convinced by
an adequate testimony to the truth, one beyond every objection. I could no
longer resist what, by God's grace, I had been permitted to attain through
both reading and hearing.

Therefore, my father, when from that sure knowledge which ought to govern my
conscience it is established for me that something is evil, I judge it
unlawful to take part in it. Nevertheless, it grieves me considerably that I
cannot abstain from those things without offending you. Nothing is more
desirable to me, or more in accord with my wish, than to render and prove my
obedience to you. One thing prevents me—and I humbly beg you not to charge me
for it: if I undertake anything against the resistance of my conscience, I am
compelled to carry within myself a judge against myself.

May every stain of arrogance and of esteeming myself above anyone else be far
from my conduct. I mean only this: because I have been taught by God what is
right and what is otherwise, I can no longer disregard it. Since every person
must one day render an account of himself before God, even granting that
others' ignorance might not count against them in God's judgment, I do not see
what I could offer as an excuse if I were once to turn away from the knowledge
of God and his true worship after it had been made certain to me.

What force the arguments concerning the matters themselves actually have, I
neither attempt to treat here before you with my limited understanding, nor do
I now wish thereby to

### [CO X/1, p. 181]

increase your trouble. But if you do not deny me permission at least to touch
on something upon which I see almost the chief issues turn, understand it as
follows.

When I turn my eyes and mind away from that free remission of sins obtained
for us by the death of Christ the Lord, and from the sealing of that same
grace, which Christ himself instituted to be presented and applied to us in
the most sacred mystery of the Supper, and turn them toward that sacrifice
which operates by its own power and applies Christ's death by itself, and
toward that adoration of the element—what this means I sufficiently indicate
by keeping silent—I cannot think otherwise than that, while the former things
are from heaven, as is entirely certain, the latter appear to have proceeded
from human beings, to say nothing more severe. Consequently, I cannot think
the latter have any weight where the worship of God and the preservation of
his truth without leaven are at issue.

Perhaps even these things have been spoken of too freely here, if you consider
only the limits of my understanding and my slight ability. Finally, however,
I will ask this of you, my father: if you will deign to incline far enough to
hear me, may it not be burdensome to admit my uncle into the discussion with
you. I am not rashly persuaded that this will turn out to your disadvantage.

Farewell, revered father, and acknowledge that this one of yours, withdrawn
for God's sake, is not unworthy of your goodwill.

## Translator's notes

1. The title says Calvin wrote in someone else's name. Accordingly, “my
   action,” “my father,” the youth of the speaker, and the paternal uncle
   belong to the represented son's situation. They must not be used as
   biographical evidence about Calvin.
2. Beza's notice makes the printed Latin a translation witness derived from
   French. This English follows that Latin closely while declining to infer
   the wording or precise stylistic texture of the unlocated French text.
3. CO prints *in cultus Dei ratione quem pro vera habes*. The masculine *quem*
   sits awkwardly after feminine *ratione*; the English gives the evident
   sense, “the form of divine worship which you hold to be true,” without
   silently changing the Latin transcription to *quam*.
4. “Settling their account without the innkeeper” translates *rationem suam
   sine hospite subducunt*, a proverbial picture of reckoning a bill without
   the person entitled to determine it. The application is to inventing the
   terms on which God must accept worship.
5. In *ut aliis sua ignorantia ... fraudi etiam non sit*, *ut* may grant the
   possibility that ignorance will not harm others rather than assert that it
   excuses them. “Even granting” preserves that argumentative function.
6. “Sacrifice which operates by its own power” and “applies Christ's death by
   itself” render the deliberately technical *sua vi operatorium* and *mortis
   Christi per sese applicativum*. The following “adoration of the element”
   refers indirectly to worship of the consecrated eucharistic element.
7. The closing *tuum ... subductitium* is deliberately unusual: it names the
   son as one belonging to the father who has nevertheless been withdrawn.
   The English retains that tension. The request to admit the *patruus* most
   naturally concerns the son's paternal uncle, but the Latin does not explain
   what role he was to play in the proposed hearing.
