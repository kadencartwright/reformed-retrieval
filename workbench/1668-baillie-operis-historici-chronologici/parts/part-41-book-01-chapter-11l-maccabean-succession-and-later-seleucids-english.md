Alcimus came to Demetrius with many accusations against Judas Maccabaeus.
Demetrius confirmed Alcimus in the high priesthood and assigned Nicanor with a
large army to destroy Maccabaeus. Maccabaeus utterly destroyed Nicanor with
his whole army of thirty-five thousand men. Renowned for so many marvelous
victories, he sent envoys to Rome to enter into an alliance with the Roman
people in his name and to obtain instructions to Demetrius that he should no
longer continue to trouble the Jews. This alliance turned out badly for Judas,
for it seems to have driven away divine aid. When Demetrius sent Bacchides to
Jerusalem with an army of twenty-two thousand, Maccabaeus met them in his
usual manner with only a few men—namely three thousand. His soldiers were
seized by an unaccustomed fear and all deserted him except eight hundred. With
these he fought bravely all day until evening against the whole enemy army and
routed the right wing, in which Bacchides himself was stationed. But while he
pursued Bacchides, the left wing attacked him from behind and killed him in
the sixth year of his rule.

Judas’s brothers buried him magnificently in their fathers’ tomb at Modin,
but they themselves were forced to flee with their friends into the
wilderness. Bacchides had occupied all Judas’s strongholds, and the people had
again fallen into the harshest servitude. In order to be freed from it,
several brave men met in the wilderness and by common vote chose Jonathan as
their leader. To make himself better equipped for war, Jonathan sent his
brother John to the Nabataean Arabs, friends of the Jews, to deposit their
baggage with them. But on the way John was robbed by the brigand Jambri of
everything he carried and of his life as well. A few days later Jonathan
exacted severe vengeance upon those brigands. Yet he could not gather forces
with which to free his country from the Greek yoke until, some years later,
other affairs drew Demetrius away from concern with Judaea.

> Marginalia, printed p. 227: *Jonathan assembles an army with difficulty*.

Demetrius, free everywhere from enemies, cast himself into the abyss of pride,
luxury, and sloth. He thereby incurred the hatred of neighboring princes and
of his subjects and was attacked by various conspiracies. He suppressed them
several times, but at last this destroyed him. Heraclides, one of Demetrius’s
chief commanders, had been treated insultingly by him. He therefore put
forward a Rhodian youth who was to claim to be the son of Antiochus Epiphanes
and the brother of Eupator. He took the name Alexander, to which the surname
Bala was added from the true name of either his father or his mother.
Heraclides took him to Rome and told his story in the Senate with such skill
that, although more discerning men saw through the fabrication, most senators
gave it credence

and a decree was obtained that Alexander should be restored to his ancestral
throne with Roman aid. Armed with this senatorial decree, Alexander hastened
to Asia. A large army immediately gathered around him, in part men sent by
Ptolemy and Attalus, whom Demetrius had provoked. Both Demetrius and Alexander
sought by every service the friendship of Jonathan, the Jewish high priest.
Demetrius placed both the city of Jerusalem and its citadel, together with the
hostages in it, into Jonathan’s hands. Jonathan, however, placed greater
confidence in Alexander and therefore embraced his side against Demetrius.
Before the battle Demetrius rather prudently sent his two sons and an enormous
sum of money to his host at Cnidus, beyond the hazards of war. For he was
defeated and killed in battle and left his throne to the pretender Alexander.
This hypocrite, now king of Asia, received Ptolemy’s daughter Cleopatra in
marriage. Both kings came to Ptolemais to celebrate the wedding, and there
each held Jonathan in the highest honor. The Jews were given complete freedom
and great tranquility, but a new face of affairs soon appeared.

Once established on the throne, Alexander indulged his appetites and at once
aroused the resentment of many against himself. Demetrius, the elder of
Demetrius’s sons, gathered forces with the help of Lasthenes the Cretan and
sent them into Palestine under Apollonius. For Alexander’s sake Jonathan
opposed Apollonius and broke him in several battles.

Ptolemy Philometor, Alexander’s father-in-law, entered Syria with a large army
as though to aid his son-in-law. Cities everywhere opened their gates to him
as a friend, but he stationed his own garrisons everywhere. When he came to
Antioch, he openly declared himself Alexander’s enemy and placed the diadems
of both Syria and Egypt upon his own head. He took his daughter away from
Alexander because Alexander had treated her badly and gave her to Demetrius.
But lest too great an increase in his power bring him under Roman suspicion,
he left his ancestral kingdom to Demetrius.

> Marginalia, printed p. 228: *And he is ruined by his father-in-law
> Philometor*.

Unexpectedly deprived of his kingdom by his father-in-law’s treachery,
Alexander joined battle with both Ptolemy and Demetrius with the poor army he
then had. Defeated there, he fled into Arabia with a few men. He had not
remained hidden there long when his head was cut off and sent to Ptolemy at
Antioch.

> Marginalia, printed p. 228: *Demetrius, son of Demetrius, occupies Asia*.

Ptolemy had been thrown from his horse in battle and pierced by many wounds,
and could scarcely breathe. Nevertheless, he greatly rejoiced when he saw his
son-in-law’s head, but his joy was very brief: three days later he died of his
wounds in the thirty-fifth year of his reign.

His brother Physcon succeeded him in Egypt, so called from his short body and
paunch. When this most wicked prince came from Cyrene to Alexandria, he
married his sister and glory, Cleopatra. He cruelly killed in his mother’s
arms her son, his brother’s heir. Nevertheless, he wished to be called
Euergetes, “Benefactor,” but the Alexandrians more correctly called him
*Kakergetes*, “Malefactor.”

> Marginalia, printed p. 228: *And Physcon occupies Egypt*.

Meanwhile Demetrius possessed Syria without a rival, but his imprudence soon
threw everything into confusion. When Jonathan besieged the citadel of
Jerusalem, which was extremely troublesome to the city, Demetrius was incited
by Jewish apostates and led an army against Jonathan. Yet he led it back
without doing harm after peace was established on equitable terms. But see
the king’s recklessness! To save expense, he dismissed all the veterans whom
his father had supported with generous pay even in peacetime, retaining only
foreign mercenaries. Hence came murmuring and spirits most ready for revolt.

While the Antiochenes were rioting, Demetrius suffered such a shortage of
soldiers that he was compelled to beg assistance from Jonathan. Jonathan sent
three thousand Jews to Antioch. While they surrounded the palace, one hundred
and twenty thousand Antiochenes attacked; the Jews fought so bravely on behalf
of a king deserted by his own men that, after burning and plundering the city,
they killed one hundred thousand. Yet despite this incomparable service,
Demetrius kept no faith with Jonathan and prepared to harass the Jews through
the garrisons of the Jerusalem citadel and other places. He was immediately
called away from this endeavor by another matter.

> Marginalia, printed p. 228: *Demetrius is splendidly defended at Antioch by
> the Jews*.

Diodorus, surnamed Tryphon, observed the people’s and soldiers’ hatred for
Demetrius. He went to Arabia and brought with him into Syria Antiochus, the son
of Alexander Bala, who had been raised there. His father’s friends and the
soldiers discharged by Demetrius immediately gathered around him. Jonathan
also followed his side, both because he had favored his father Alexander and
because Demetrius had repaid his great services with ingratitude. Defeated in
battle, Demetrius fled to the upper provinces of Asia, leaving Antioch and
Syria to Antiochus.

> Marginalia, printed p. 228: *Tryphon raises up Antiochus, the son of Bala,
> against Demetrius*.

After gathering soldiers from the upper provinces, Demetrius arranged for
Judaea to be invaded by a hostile army. Jonathan often defeated Demetrius’s
men, so that his great name was renowned everywhere. To establish his
position more securely, he followed the example of his brother Judas and made
an alliance through envoys with the Romans and Spartans. Yet this entirely
carnal undertaking, no less for him than for his brother, was the end of his
victories and the beginning of his calamities, without any benefit from his
gentile allies.

After Tryphon, under the auspices of the boy Antiochus, had defeated Demetrius
and driven him in flight to the upper provinces, he formed the plan of
transferring the kingdom to himself by removing Antiochus.

> Marginalia, printed p. 228: *Jonathan, allied with the Romans, is killed by
> Tryphon*.

To carry this out more safely, he decided that Jonathan, a brave man loyal to
the royal family, must first be removed. He therefore advanced toward Judaea
with large forces. Jonathan met him with an army unusual for the Maccabees,
forty thousand strong. Tryphon did not dare to engage Jonathan in open battle.
He feigned great friendship and, showing Jonathan every honor, persuaded him
to dismiss his army and come to him at Ptolemais as to his greatest friend.
When Jonathan came there with an escort of only a thousand soldiers, all of
them were killed by Tryphon and Jonathan himself was put in chains. His
brother Simon soon appeared with an army. To draw him too into his snare,
Tryphon declared that he detained Jonathan solely because Jonathan owed the
king one hundred talents in taxes and because he feared that Jonathan, now
provoked, would desert to the king’s enemies. If Simon would therefore send
him this money, together with two of Jonathan’s sons as hostages for their
father’s loyalty to the king, he was prepared to release Jonathan. Although
Tryphon’s fraud was quite transparent to Simon, he did not wish the people to
think that he neglected his brother’s safety for his own advantage. He
therefore sent Tryphon one hundred talents with two of Jonathan’s sons. After
receiving the money and the boys, Tryphon mocked Simon’s simplicity. He killed
Jonathan and his sons and returned to Antioch, rejoicing that Jonathan’s death
had freed him from a great obstacle to his plans.

While Demetrius was residing in the upper provinces, the Macedonian
inhabitants of many provinces secretly invited him to take up arms against
Arsaces, the proud and cruel king of the Parthians. Demetrius gained many
victories against him, but at last, by entering into a conference for peace,
he put himself into the snare of captivity. For several years he could not
free himself from it. Nevertheless, he was treated kindly enough in captivity
and, though a captive, even married the sister of the Parthian.

> Marginalia, printed p. 229: *Demetrius is captured in Parthia*.

Freed from fear of both Jonathan and Demetrius, Tryphon delayed no longer. He
pretended that the boy Antiochus suffered from a bladder stone and had
surgeons kill him during its removal. With no one opposing him, he occupied
the vacant throne with soldiers corrupted by gifts and hope. These matters
are treated extensively from Polybius, Justin, Josephus, the books of
Maccabees, and other sources by Tornellus, Salianus, Ussher, Capellus, and
others. We do not think it worth the effort to refute Salianus’s frequent
attacks upon Josephus on behalf of the author of the apocryphal Maccabees.

> Marginalia, printed p. 229: *Tryphon reigns after killing the boy*.

Simon erected at Modin a very magnificent tomb for his father and brothers,
and was chosen by the whole people, unanimously and with one accord, as both
leader and high priest. God attended him with such great kindness that he
completely freed the people, despairing after Jonathan’s death, from the Greek
yoke. The Jerusalem citadel was subdued by hunger, all the garrisons were
expelled from all Judaea, and cities everywhere were splendidly fortified
against enemy attacks. Under Simon the Jews enjoyed such profound peace that
they had not seen a greater since the days of Solomon. Simon himself resided
at Jerusalem, and his son John, a very brave young man, at Gaza, ready to
suppress at once anyone who, stirred from any quarter, might disturb the
people’s repose. But this tranquility did not last long. Tryphon, after
obtaining Demetrius’s kingdom and becoming a cruel and greedy tyrant, alienated
his soldiers. Observing this, Cleopatra, Demetrius’s wife, who lived at
Seleucia with her sons Seleucus and Gryphus, summoned Antiochus Sidetes—so
called from the city of Side in Cilicia, where he had been raised—and offered
him his brother’s kingdom and marriage to herself. Tryphon’s army immediately
deserted to Sidetes. Sidetes, therefore, surrounded by one hundred and twenty
thousand men, pursued Tryphon through the various hiding places he sought
until he compelled him to die.

Simon had long before written to Demetrius in Media, offering to uphold his
cause against Tryphon, and Demetrius had answered him in the friendliest
terms. Sidetes too at first cultivated Simon’s friendship zealously. But once
he had removed Tryphon, he immediately made war on Simon from a desire to add
Judaea to the rest of his Syrian kingdom, and sent Cendebeus with an army to
ravage Judaea. Simon’s sons bravely repelled him and expelled him from Judaea.
While Sidetes himself was preparing to besiege Jerusalem with all his forces,
Ptolemy, a Jew and Simon’s son-in-law, treacherously seized Simon with his sons
Judas and Matthias and killed them. John escaped, however, drove Ptolemy out
of Judaea, and was ordered by the people to act as high priest and leader in
his father’s place. He long defended Jerusalem splendidly against Sidetes’s
power, but at last, almost broken by a scarcity of grain, made peace with him.
The terms were easier because Sidetes was preparing an expedition against the
Parthians to free his brother Demetrius. John accompanied him with his troops
into Parthia and Hyrcania and from this obtained the surname Hyrcanus. At the
beginning of the war all went prosperously for Sidetes, and he defeated
Phraates the Arsacid in three battles. But after he distributed his troops
into winter quarters and they oppressed many of the cities, the inhabitants
formed a conspiracy and almost all the troops were cut down. Sidetes himself
was also killed while fighting bravely.

Before this slaughter Phraates, in order to draw Sidetes back to his own
territory, had released Demetrius with a Parthian guard to recover Syria for
himself. But when Phraates learned of Antiochus Sidetes’s death, he sent men to
bring Demetrius back to him. It was too late, however, for

Demetrius had reached Syria and disregarded the Parthians’ commands.

Demetrius had scarcely taken his seat again on the Syrian throne when he was
summoned to Egypt by his mother-in-law Cleopatra. Ptolemy Euergetes, or
Physcon, had oppressed the people with such tyranny that, weary of him, they
offered the kingdom to his wife, sister, and glory. Supported especially by
Jewish soldiers, she forced her husband Physcon to flee to Cyprus. Out of
hatred for Cleopatra he cut apart their common son, shut him in a chest, and
sent him to his mother as though he were a New Year’s gift. Enraged by this
gift, she summoned Demetrius to Egypt and promised him the kingdom. Yet
Demetrius had learned such pride and cruelty among the Parthians that he had
become intolerable to everyone. The Syrians therefore rebelled when he
departed for Egypt. To strengthen their rebellion, Ptolemy sent them a
pretended youth, Alexander Zebina, whom he claimed Antiochus Sidetes had
adopted before his death. The highly seditious Antiochenes admitted him as
king without hesitation and supported him with all their strength against
Demetrius. Demetrius was routed in battle and fled to Ptolemais to his younger
wife Cleopatra. So far was she from admitting her fugitive husband that she
even arranged for him to be killed at Tyre.

This Cleopatra had four sons. Tryphon had killed the eldest, Alexander Bala’s
son. By Demetrius she bore Seleucus and Antiochus Gryphus, so called from his
hooked nose, and by Demetrius’s brother Antiochus Sidetes she bore Antiochus
Cyzicenus. After his father Demetrius had been killed by his mother, Seleucus
called himself king. But his mother immediately killed him too because he had
dared to put on the diadem without consulting her. This Proserpina granted the
kingdom to her third son, Gryphus, on the condition that the title of the
kingdom belong to him but its substance and authority remain with her.

> Marginalia, printed p. 230: *Seleucus, son of Demetrius, is slain by his
> mother*.

Meanwhile Alexander Zebina held most of Syria, and Hyrcanus the high priest
had made an alliance with him. But Zebina, becoming fiercer after gaining
power, alienated his followers, so that Gryphus easily defeated and killed
him.

> Marginalia, printed p. 230: *Antiochus Gryphus removes Zebina*.

As Gryphus’s power increased, he became burdensome to his mother and she
attempted to poison him. Her son, however, discovered the fraud and forced her
to drink the poison. His youngest brother then lived at Cyzicus on the Pontus.
Gryphus attempted to remove him with poison lest he someday rise against him.
But Cyzicenus made these plots a just cause for open war against Gryphus.

> Marginalia, printed p. 230: *And his own mother as well*.

Gryphus’s wife was Tryphaena, daughter of Ptolemy Physcon. When Physcon was
dying, he left to his wife Cleopatra, with whom he had now been reconciled,
the power to choose which of their sons she wished to make king of Egypt. She
loved the younger Alexander more than the elder Lathyrus, but the people
forced her against her will to proclaim Lathyrus king. Cleopatra made him,
once he was king, dismiss his sister and marry their other sister Selene. This
Cleopatra, dismissed by her husband and brother, went to Antiochus Cyzicenus,
married him, and brought her father’s Cypriot army to his aid. Strengthened by
these forces, Cyzicenus fought a battle with his brother Gryphus. Cyzicenus
was defeated and escaped, but his wife Cleopatra was captured through the
most diligent search of her sister Tryphaena and cruelly killed, although
Gryphus strongly opposed it. Cyzicenus gathered his army again, defeated
Gryphus and expelled him from the kingdom, and stabbed the captured Tryphaena
to death.

> Marginalia, printed p. 230: *He is defeated by his brother Cyzicenus*.

As soon as an opportunity was given her, the elder Cleopatra expelled her son
Lathyrus from Egypt so that she might reign alone, although she granted the
title of king to her younger son Alexander.

> Marginalia, printed p. 230: *Ptolemy Lathyrus is expelled from Egypt by his
> mother Cleopatra*.

These disorders among his neighbors afforded John Hyrcanus secure peace at
Jerusalem, though he by no means spent it in idleness. He subdued the
Idumaeans, compelled them to be circumcised, and made them coalesce into one
people with the Jews. He also took Samaria after subduing it by famine and
utterly destroyed it together with the temple on Mount Gerizim. During his
rule the sects of the Pharisees, Sadducees, and Essenes arose. Hyrcanus died
in great prosperity after a high priesthood of thirty-one years, leaving five
sons. The eldest, Aristobulus, enjoying royal wealth, assumed the name and
diadem of king. But this ambition turned out badly for him, for within a year
he laid down his life together with his kingdom.

> Marginalia, printed p. 230: *John Hyrcanus’s principate flourishes in
> Judaea*.
>
> Marginalia, printed p. 230: *John’s son Aristobulus calls himself king*.

He loved his brother Alexander and had admitted him to a share in the
kingdom, but his wife, called Salome Alexandra, could not endure this
partnership and continually contrived accusations against Alexander. While
King Aristobulus was gravely ill, Alexander returned with a splendid victory
over the Ituraeans. Immediately after the battle he went up to the temple with
his soldiers, still armed, to sacrifice. Alexandra whispered that Alexander
was approaching armed in order to kill Aristobulus. Aristobulus summoned
Alexander to him but instructed him to come unarmed. Yet Alexandra secretly
sent contrary instructions to Alexander through the same messenger: namely,
that Aristobulus wished to see his brother in his new armor. Aristobulus
ordered his guards to kill Alexander on the way if he came armed. Suspecting
no fraud, Alexander approached his brother armed as he was, in accordance
with Alexandra’s counsel, and was stabbed by the guards near the entrance of
the palace. Aristobulus was driven to remorse for this crime and, tormented at
once by disease and conscience, vomited a great quantity of blood. The boy
who carried it in a basin slipped and fell in the same place where Alexander
had recently been killed, spilling the blood. Those who saw Aristobulus’s
blood poured over his brother’s

blood cried out loudly as though terrified by divine vengeance. Once the
cause of the cry had been discovered through threats, Aristobulus died in
great anguish of mind.

> Marginalia, printed p. 231: *He himself dies of anguish of mind*.

With this tragic restoration of the Jewish kingdom we shall close the history
of this epoch and hasten to the next, in which dominion over the world was
transferred to the Romans. The princes of Egypt, Asia, and the rest of the
East—not excepting Judaea—had been ruined by every kind of thoroughly
abandoned wickedness, so that by their conduct they demanded of divine
justice that they be driven from their principalities and subjected to most
savage tyrants. For they had all become porridge sufficiently prepared to be
chewed and swallowed by the iron teeth of the Roman beast.
