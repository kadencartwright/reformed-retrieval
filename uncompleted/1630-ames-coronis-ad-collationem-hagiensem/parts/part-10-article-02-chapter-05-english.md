### Chapter V

#### Fourth argument

Page 187.

All those for whom Christ died can freely say, “Who condemns? Christ died for
us” (Rom. 8:34). But all and each of mortal men cannot say this, but only the
faithful elect; otherwise this argument would be too weak for the faithful to
console themselves with it. Therefore Christ did not die for all and each,
but for the faithful elect alone.

Here the Remonstrants observe first of all that this argument can be turned
back upon its own authors, because they themselves do not deny that Christ
died in some respect for all. But this inversion is far too strained. For our
men concede this only with respect to the intrinsic nature or sufficiency of
this death, and to the intention of God by which he willed that it should be
sufficient for all—not with respect to an intention and will to save all and
each through this death. But the intention of the one dying, joined with the
price of the death, is here opposed by the Apostle to condemnation, which
consists not only in the deserts of sin but also in the will of the judge.

The Remonstrants distinguish concerning the mode or kind of saying, “Christ
died for us.” There is, they say, one kind of saying this before faith, which
has regard only to procurement: in this way all can say it as an historical
narration, and so forth. There is another kind, the very saying of it which
arises from faith and follows application: in this way no one unless made a
believer and a partaker of Christ can say it unto true consolation.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 136 | printed p. 126]

I confess that this distinction, rightly understood, has its place and use;
but it does not solve the proposed argument. For the force of the reasoning
lies hidden in this knot: what all can equally know to be true, all can equally
say; but all, according to the Remonstrants’ position, can equally know this
to be true, that Christ died for them; therefore all can also equally say it.
Furthermore, what all can equally know and say, they can equally know and say
that its consequence is certain and brought about. Therefore, if all can
equally say, “Christ died for us,” they can also say, “Who is it who
condemns?” For acquittal from condemnation is a consequence brought about by
Christ’s death, most certainly granted to all those for whom he died, and the
proper correlate of his procurement, as the Apostle teaches not only by
affirmation but, more effectively, presupposes by interrogation: “Who is it
who condemns? Christ is he who died.” But the Remonstrants press their
objection by denying
this: “Ought not those apostates of whom the Apostle treats in Hebrews 6:6 and
10:29 to have acknowledged that Christ once died for them?” I reply: 1. In the
Remonstrants’ judgment, those apostates were once true believers. It therefore
does not follow, as they conclude from this, that unbelievers as well as
believers can say this, if the apostates could once say it. 2. No one ought to
say directly, or is even able to know, that Christ died for him according to
the intention of God before he is rooted in faith. Therefore apostates,
who did not have root in themselves, cannot say it (Matt. 13:21). 3. Someone
can treat with contempt the blood whose profession had separated him from the
world, although it was never poured out for his salvation.

They say that the consolation of believers drawn from Christ’s death will be
sufficiently firm, although he did not die for them alone: 1. Because the
false consolation of an imaginary faith does not render void the true
consolation of true faith. 2. Because the consolation of believers does not
rest upon Christ’s death alone,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 137 | printed p. 127]

but also upon his resurrection and intercession, which pertain to believers
alone. But 1. An argument common to all people can no more of itself furnish a
consolation peculiar to some than it can be confused with a peculiar
argument. 2. Their true statement must be observed: to say that Christ died
equally for all is an imaginary faith, and the consolation following from it
is false. For this is what they mean, if they frame the comparison correctly
and according to the sense of the argument. 3. What they themselves note must
also be weighed and pressed: death, resurrection, sitting at the right hand
of God, and intercession are joined by the Apostle in Romans 8:34 and also
connected with election, as is manifest from verse 33: “Who will bring
charges against God’s elect?” 4. That final claim—that Christ’s death pertains
to many to whom his resurrection and intercession do not pertain—deserves not
so much to be refuted as to be hooted away by all Christians. For 1. if he did
not rise again for all for whom he died, then he underwent death for some for
whom he did not overcome death. 2. He offered himself on earth for some for
whom he did not present himself before the face of the Father in heaven,
although Arminius himself concedes that this presentation is a part of his
offering. 3. Then some of them are thoroughly and in every way in their sins
(1 Cor. 15:7). 4. No power and grace of the Spirit is ever communicated to
some of them, for this depends upon Christ’s ascension (Eph. 4). What, then,
will become of universal grace sufficient for every person? It would be
endless to recount everything that tears this invention down; it will be
enough to admonish from the Apostle in Hebrews 9:12 that redemption itself is
not completely obtained except through the entrance of our supreme High
Priest into the heavenly sanctuary with his own blood. Nevertheless, the
reader must be admonished in a word how the Remonstrants, in their reply to
the Walachrians, strive by some muddled distinction or other to escape the
odium of this invention.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 138 | printed p. 128]

“Christ died,” they say on page 51, “adequately for sinners; he rose again and
interceded with the intention of saving, adequately for believers.” Here the
mediation is divided as it were into parts. To the first, namely death, they
leave no intention to save and make it concern the reprobate themselves; but
to the latter, namely resurrection, they ascribe the whole intention of
salvation and will have it concern believers alone. Hence it manifestly
follows that, in their position, not only the value of the death and its
application, as they wish to seem to think, but also death itself and
resurrection are distinct and disjoined in intention, end, and object—which
they deny on the same page. This distinction is therefore so nearly nothing
that they themselves do not know where they stand.
