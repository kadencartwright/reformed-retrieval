29. There remains a certain shadow of antiquity cast by the name of some
Orpheus—if it please the gods. Although it does not obstruct the light of our
case, it must nevertheless be dispersed, lest it seem to obstruct it for the
unlearned. I deliberately said “by the name.” I have no more managed to see
the man himself—described not merely as ancient but exceedingly ancient, a
master of the Greek language, according to Suidas a contemporary of the
Israelite Judges, and moreover a good theologian (which the best of theologians
would not otherwise have added), and finally redolent of authority through the
very grayness of his verse—than that traveler once managed to see “a Man in
General” in Utopia. I admit that a certain little verse struck my ears:

> Lord of ether and Hades, sea and earth.

Someone perhaps wished to seem an Orpheus after enumerating what he took to be
the elements, but the very sound of the noise betrayed and ruined him. It is
clearer than day that these lines belonged neither to the Orpheus introduced
with such pomp nor to any genuinely ancient writer. I will not ask here what
answer can be made to Aristotle, who—if Cicero knows anything—does not merely
say but teaches that Orpheus the poet never existed. I pass over Herodotus's
testimony, which recognizes no Orpheus earlier than Homer; Sextus does not
hesitate to subscribe to it, although otherwise withholding assent. Nor do I
press the great silence about so singular a writer in Plato. Plato praises
Homer so often, though much farther removed from his philosophy, but does not
even name this man,

[Marginalia: Bilson 37.1; *On the World*, “he could not see,” 1; Herodotus,
*Euterpe*, p. 69; Sextus Empiricus, *Against the Mathematicians* 6.14.]

except as a lyre-player in a fable together with Phemius. My object is to show
this: since Suidas lists seven men of this name, how can it be established that
these lines belong to the Orpheus whom he mentions last and says flourished
under the Israelite Judges? This is especially doubtful because we find no
poems of his in Suidas, but only discourses *On the Knowledge of God*. Grant
that he wrote poems: Suidas teaches in the same place that more works were
falsely assigned to Orpheus than were written for any other man. What authority
then moves us to believe these particular lines are neither counterfeit nor
spurious? The subject itself, I suppose, tastes of antiquity. Yet even if we
grant that giving the elements the names of gods was an ancient custom—though
Herodotus denies that this was practiced in Greece before Homer and Hesiod—I
think no one can be produced before the age of Diogenes Laertius who called
Hades “air,” as our poet does. Diogenes did, I admit, take Empedocles' *Aidonea*
for air. We have seen, or will soon see, how correctly. Meanwhile it can safely
be affirmed that, however many pupils he had, he had no master in this doctrine.

Perhaps the words parade gray hairs. Far from it. This writer calls God a
“tyrant,” but that word was unknown in Orpheus's age and arose much later from
an event. The inhabitants of the Tyrrhenian shore of the Adriatic, prevailing
by their fleet, cruelly practiced piracy. The vowel in *Argo* itself denies
that this happened before the Argonautic expedition in which Orpheus took part.
The custom therefore arose that one who ruled at home after their manner was
called not a Tyrrhenian but a tyrant, one letter being removed and another
added, as Stephanus says. Hippias testifies, and Homer's interpreters confirm,
that this happened no earlier than Archilochus. Suidas himself finally admits
it, although he appears especially eager to proclaim the word's antiquity.
His text reads: “The poets before the Trojan times called kings tyrants.” It
therefore seems to say that poets earlier than the Trojan era gave kings the
name of tyrants. The whole passage, however, is mutilated and corrupt and must
be supplied and corrected from the ancient scholiast on Sophocles from whom it
was evidently taken. The scholiast says: “It belongs to poets after Homer to
call kings earlier than the Trojan era tyrants; this name was spread among the
Greeks very late, in the age of Archilochus, as Hippias the Sophist relates.”
It is evident that the name “tyrant” is more recent, for neither Homer, Hesiod,
nor any other ancient writer names a tyrant

in his poems. Suidas must therefore be read and understood this way. Otherwise

Whoever, then, the man was—and it is not clear—who tried to force Hades as air

## On the Descent of Our Lord Jesus Christ to Hell: Book II

1. Our original proposition—that Hades is *adamah*, that is, earth—has now
been established with the agreement of letters, prophecies, opinions,
religions, Greeks, Egyptians, barbarians, and Latins. It is consequently easy
to see that all nations in every age drew both their knowledge of nature and,
above all, their sacred rites and ceremonies from one and the same fountain
and source. On this subject, the most obscure of all, ancients agree with
moderns, barbarians with Greeks, Greeks with Hebrews, and everyone with
everyone else.

It remains to discuss this name's modification. Since our whole discussion
must be derived from the supreme Judge's sentence upon sinful man—“You are
Earth and will return to earth”—I propose first to explain what the name Earth
denotes. I will not pursue everything, nor even much, but sample only what
appears directly relevant to the proposed question. Sacred writers understand
Earth in different ways, whence not only Gentiles but also our theologians
have departed into various opinions about Hades. We will therefore observe,
wherever needed, what the teaching of the Law or the Gospel transmits about
this subject. Then, lest our discussion lack anything, we will compare profane
things with sacred, and modern with ancient. Thus, while we establish that what
the Prophets call *Sheol* and the Apostles *Hades* is Earth in each case, we
will also show that later writers transferred to Hades what the ancients said
about Earth. We hope by the very same work that error will receive medicine
from truth and truth light from error. Images rebound more brightly from
mirrors when some metal is poured onto the transparent body, giving the gaze
something on which to rest when it would otherwise penetrate through it;

[1611 Book II, printed p. 2 | Google Books RA1-PA2]

similarly, although the authority of ancient things shines clearly enough by
itself in divine oracles, somehow it strikes the eyes more forcefully when the
rays of a correct but not yet accepted opinion strike either ancient fables or
modern conjectures.

2. As one enters sacred history, a twofold Earth immediately appears at the
threshold. One was created with heaven at the very beginning of time, admitting
no sight and adorned with no form; the other displayed itself visibly in the
order of the elements only on the world's third day when the waters fled.
Because wise men interpret that first Heaven in different ways, the boundaries
of the first Earth are also narrowed or widened by their differing opinions.
Those who understand Heaven as that supreme light nearest God, bodiless,
removed from the senses, and reached by the mind's gaze alone, take “earth” as
all matter assigned to the world's generation: the entire formlessness from
which not only our earth and the other elements but the visible heaven itself
were to be fashioned. Those who permit nothing to be named here except what is
accessible to sense establish in heaven the matter of all heavenly things. By
“Earth” they understand not everything corporeal but everything subject to the
alternation of generation and corruption. Augustine reports both opinions and
embraces the first, chiefly, it seems, because the Prophets speak of the
“heaven of heaven”—the heaven we do not see, as he explains, for which Earth is
everything we do see.

For me it is enough that all agree on this: whatever was not heaven was Earth
at the beginning. Whether it occupied the places of all bodies or only the
elements, it wholly lacked light and form. It existed in such a state that God
is aptly said to have created two things, Heaven and Earth, in the beginning:
one close to himself and the other close to Nothing. That was Earth. We must
next examine the Earth that still exists. This too has no single constant
meaning but is used sometimes universally and sometimes properly. In the first
way Earth denotes the lower of the universe's two great parts, whence we so
often read “Heaven and Earth.” In the second it denotes the lowest portion of
the lower part, the usage by which we constantly encounter “Sea and Earth.”
After the Prophet said, “Praise the Lord from the heavens,” and assigned to
the heavens the angels,

[Marginalia: Ps. 113; *Confessions* 12.15.2; *Literal Meaning of Genesis* 3.6.]

[1611 Book II, printed p. 3 | Google Books RA1-PA3]

sun, moon, stars, and heavenly waters, he said, “Praise the Lord from the
Earth, depths, fire, and stormy winds.” We may therefore state without doubt
that what properly belongs to the other elements is assigned to Earth under a
common principle. The Preacher's proclamation, “All things were made from
dust,” belongs here too, though Moses plainly denies that fish at least owe
their origin to anything but water. The other meaning is: “God called the dry
land Earth, and the gathering of waters he called Seas.” Here one single,
fixed nature is designated by its proper name: immobile dryness, separated
from liquid moisture and driven to the lowest part of the world.

This Earth was good from the beginning and, while it remained good, was
adapted by the best Creator and Governor for the human race's origin and
preservation. Earth supplied the material for man's formation—a fact clearer
than speech and more certain than testimony can make it. Once man was formed
and animated by life's spirit, it gave him not merely a dwelling of blessedness,
the garden planted by God, but under the Lord's provision supplied everything
that could delight the senses or nourish life. Yet when man sinned, the God
who had earlier seen Earth as good said, “Cursed is Earth because of you.”
What had been prepared for life and pleasure was turned by the first parent's
crime into punishment and destruction. Whatever life remains to mortals after
their departure or expulsion from that region of delights is claimed by the
thorns and thistles, pains and sweats, of the ancient curse. What remains after
death becomes food for the serpent as the nature from which it was taken
dissolves into dust.

In sum, sacred literature considers Earth in two ways: in itself and in man.
Our account of its first formlessness and of the universal or particular
element belongs to the first. Under the second fall the material from which man
was formed, Paradise where he was placed, the place of trial into which he is
born, and the place of corruption to which he returns in death. This is the
whole Mosaic doctrine of Earth if we rest in the judgment of the senses and
the sound of words.

3. Yet Moses' whole narrative of the world's origin contains everything in
pairs. It does not cling to sensible things but leaps toward higher realities,
to which we rise through contemplation and study by mind and understanding
alone. Indeed, throughout the Law one thing is said to be shown and another
understood,

[1611 Book II, printed p. 4 | Google Books RA1-PA4]

while truth remains in both. I will establish this with adequate authorities:
first Christ and his disciples, who transfer many things apparently narrated
in the common manner to hidden meanings; then the most ancient Christians,
who were so persuaded that Scripture had more than one sense that they placed
in the rule of faith itself—something to be imbibed with the first milk of
religion—that Scripture comes from the Holy Spirit and also has another hidden
sense. Arabs make a similar judgment, holding the entire Hebrew Law to be
metaphorical and allegorical—not that it contains nothing plain and proper,
but that in many passages a hidden truth lies concealed beyond the sense
immediately presented. I add the Hebrews, the best interpreters of good
mysteries and the worst of bad ones. They teach that nothing in the Law is so
small that the same thing is not also great. A small thing is what the word
indicates; a great thing is what the mind alone attains. They therefore argue
that the letter *beth*, the sign of two, was prefixed to *Resith* at the
beginning of Mosaic history to warn us that its narratives should be referred
not only to sense but much more to the mind. No stroke of the Law is useless;
*Resith* by itself contains no less than *Bresith*. They derive this from other
passages and especially Solomon's, “Iehova possessed me at the beginning of
his way.”

Since universal agreement thus confirms that Earth has a mystical and
spiritual meaning, our purpose requires us to investigate it. Its meaning
appears manifold through different comparisons. First, as heaven mystically
designates eternity, purity, constancy, and everything that emulates form and
goodness, so Earth opposed to heaven shadows what is fluid, impure, mutable,
and whatever approaches the nature of evil and privation. This is Scripture's
first comparison, Earth with Heaven. The second compares Earth with Paradise.
The question whether God's Paradise, where Adam was placed, was terrestrial or
heavenly and perceived by sense or understanding long exercised commentators.
Many things in Moses seemed to rise above history and unable to stand in the
bare verbal sense. Yet first things were so fitted to middle, middle to
neighboring, and all to all by the law of history that no force of ingenuity,
though vigorously attempted, could tear them apart. At last it was established,
settling the whole dispute, that Paradise was corporeal

[Marginalia: Origen, *On First Principles*, preface 1; R. Joseph Castilion in
*Boronoth*; Prov. 8.22; Augustine, *On Genesis* 8 and *City of God* 13.12.]

[1611 Book II, printed p. 5 | Google Books RA1-PA5]

without preventing a spiritual and heavenly blessedness from being understood
in it. Earth must be treated similarly, whether the Earth to which man was to
go or the one to which he was to return. Others long ago observed that this
passage expresses both departure and return by the same word: the ancients
rendered our “You will return to earth” as “You will go to earth.” There is
therefore an Earth of departure, reached by those leaving Paradise, and an
Earth of return, reached by those leaving the world. Punishment pursues
wretched mortals in both: the first supplies a place where the living are
afflicted, the second one where the lifeless decay.

4. But this—to speak with the Hebrews—is the “small thing,” falling beneath
the senses and referring to bodies. Whoever wants the “great thing” must leave
the senses. Since place is to bodies what state and condition are to bodiless
things, he must behold the condition of souls in the place of bodies, according
to the Prophets' usual mode of teaching. They never set rewards before the
good or punishments before the wicked without speaking of God as a man, the
soul as a body, or the future age as this life. Human things are thus spoken
while divine ones are taught; sense is stirred while understanding is warned;
temporal things are indicated while eternal things are implied. The sentence
of the curse mentions the highest sterility amid the greatest labor and no
pleasure without bitterness even from its fruit. Though these attacked the
sinning farmer in their literal sense, closer consideration finds them rather
in the spiritual field. What thorns of cares, thistles of fears, fires of
trials, floods of pleasures, and droughts of frustrations are there! Earth is
subdued much more easily than life, and the storm of heavenly wrath rages not
so much against the year's produce as the soul's.

What follows death must be interpreted similarly. The event confirms the
heavenly utterance as bloodless corpses dissolve into dust, yet one who
investigates Scripture's mind must not stop in this decay. The account must be
transferred from body to soul: if return to Earth announces bodily evil, we
must understand some ruin of the soul in the same return; if rest from troubles
is suggested for the body, refreshment is likewise implied for the soul wearied
by the world's sweat.

[Marginalia: Ambrose on Luke 4.4; Hilary, p. 120.]

[1611 Book II, printed p. 6 | Google Books RA1-PA6]

5. It cannot be denied that an extreme plague stands out in this Return, yet
God's promise, not merely his threat, also lies hidden in the words. Indeed,
promises precede the threats, and necessarily so. How could human weakness
bear the curse's burden without hope's aid? The seed of redemption was promised
before the sinner's condemnation to life was inflicted. Likewise, the good in
this return was indicated by a kind of nod before death's corruption was
uttered aloud. To prevent apparent contradiction, understand that the first
parent wore two persons and received evils entangled with goods. He was both
earthly from Earth and the Image of the one to come. In each respect the sense
is twofold, so that he is consecrated not only under the private relation of
the first and second Man but embraces the whole race generated by the one
through nature and regenerated by the other through grace. The opposed things
we present therefore belong partly to Adam and partly to Christ: to Christ in
the curse as Adam's son, and to Adam in consolation as Christ's son.

In sum, this sentence threatens and promises the same things to the same man if
we behold both men as one, but different things to different men if we assign
each his own. It threatens corruption—what is more abject?—and promises Rest—
what is more desirable? He whose mercy is over all his works so arranged that
in our race's gravest fall nothing would be taken from the heavenly word, yet
hope would mingle with punishment, aid with burden, and consolation with grief.
The difference is perhaps that punishment is signified verbally and thus
strikes the senses, while refreshment is wrapped in the tacit argument that
every return goes back to the point of departure. This may be because punishment
spreads to all but refreshment does not, or because, though Law has promises
and Gospel threats, it seems proper and natural for the former to terrify more
clearly and the latter to console more openly. Yet I must not conceal that the
restoration of an exiled race seems not merely implied here but proclaimed
aloud. What do I hear? “You will sweat and labor until you return to Earth”—as
though God said: “After you return there, you will rest from every sweat and
labor. You will return not only to the earth from which you were taken, that
is, to dust, but also to this Earth from which you were removed: Paradise.”

[1611 Book II, printed p. 7 | Google Books RA1-PA7]

This comes first, and those who taught that human death was given by God not so
much for punishment as remedy appear to have followed it. “You are Earth and
will return to Earth” follows with a different intention. To what kind of Earth
will you return? To what you now are: Earth granted as food to the serpent and
cursed in your work. We earlier received from one fertile word a twofold Earth,
one of Departure and one of Return. From the whole speech we may likewise
receive a twofold Return: from Death to the garden of pleasure, and from life
to earth's prison. The words truly translated “until you return to the Earth
from which you were taken” can in an entirely different sense be read, “Until
you flee back to this Earth from which you were led captive.”

6. I impose no force upon the words; the meaning offers itself freely. It may
be new and suspect in these times, but rests upon the most ancient language
and surest doctrine. Consider the patriarchal words. Hebrew *schub* has broad
scope and is used of many returns, but at its root it stands opposed to
*schabab*, to lead captive, and properly denotes return from captivity,
especially where return, dwelling, and rest occur. Four words—*schabah*, lead
away; *schub*, return; *schab*, dwell; *schacah*, rest—are wholly entangled and
cohere, not by grammarians' curious subtlety, as some allege, but by kinship of
origin and scriptural usage. Man was therefore to return from bondage. Where?
*El-haadamah*: others render “to Earth”; I, “to this Earth,” expressing the
pronoun *he haiediakh*, a construction familiar to Greek and Latin as context
requires. Rebecca's *benoth haarets* is translated by the Greeks “daughters of
this land”; Joshua's *haarets hattobah* by the Latins “this best Earth.” Next
comes *ci mimmennah lukkah*, either “because you were taken from it” or “because
you were carried away from it.” The verb is known in both bad and good senses:
sometimes taking material, sometimes dragging into bondage. Solomon calls
those led to death *lakukhim*. Jeremiah likewise has *lukkekhu* for sons led
into captivity. Countless examples could be produced, but testimonies should
be reserved for doubtful matters. These sufficiently show that the first part
of the divine sentence

[Marginalia: Ambrose, *On the Good of Death*.]

[1611 Book II, printed p. 8 | Google Books RA1-PA8]

may refer not only to man's falling back into Earth as the element from which
he was composed, but much more to his fleeing back into Earth as the region
from which a conqueror violently carried him captive. I said “may”; Scripture
cries that it must. For Scripture makes the promise of Earth the good's final
end, and slavery and exile the evil's extreme. Both must be traced to the
author who, stripped of goods and wrapped in evils, propagated knowledge of
good and evil. More plainly: the first promise of Earth was made not to
Abraham but Adam, and the history descends through so many migrations and
returns as the story of mankind, not merely Israel. The events of Israel and
God's chosen yet offending offspring truly happened in their own fashion, but
the event was accomplished in the first parent and recalled by image in the
rest, at differing times and under differing enemies, so that memory of the
first exile would never fade and hope of the last return would always be fed.
We praise God as this doctrine's author, interpreting his promises not by
words but deeds. To teach Israel to strive not toward the Jerusalem in slavery
but the one above, he promised a land flowing with milk and honey but gave the
worst province of all—rocky, mountainous, naturally thirsty, watered by
heavenly rain and consoled for its few springs by cisterns.

Indeed, on a human estimate of the divine gift he did not give it. Though the
Lord told Abraham, “I will give you and your seed forever all the Earth you
see,” Abraham never possessed a foot of it. He dwelt as a stranger in the
promised Earth, aspiring in faith to the city founded by God. What of his
descendants, as numerous as the stars and seashore sand? They all died in faith
without receiving the promises. No one should be less fair than Jerome, who
traversed all Judea and reports it only 160,000 paces long and 40,000 wide,
including many regions, places, cities, and towns never occupied by Jews but
only divinely promised. Judea supplied a shadow to refresh believers for a
time; the reality appeared only when they heard the voice, “Today you will be
with me

[Marginalia: Gal. 4.26; Jerome on Isa. 49, book 13; Ambrose, sermons 68 and
372; Gen. 13; Acts 6; Heb. 11.9–10; *To Dardanus*.]

[1611 Book II, printed p. 9 | Google Books RA1-PA9]

in Paradise.” From the first man's departure to the second man's return, a
continuous figure of this had run among the Jews. Thus when the Prophets
celebrate the return from captivity, they explicitly mention Adam and Paradise.
The Psalm congratulates the Savior: “You ascended on high, took a multitude of
captives captive, and, receiving, gave gifts, *baadam*.” Isaiah consoles the
church: “He made its desert like Eden and its wilderness like Iehova's garden.”
What follows? Shall we seek elsewhere for the Earth promised to Abraham but not
for the promise made to Abraham? We perceive Paradise in Israel's Earth; shall
we not perceive Paradise's former inhabitant in Israel?

7. Let the Promise itself bear the meaning, for words can pull one whom facts
do not lead. Adam heard, “Until you return to this Earth”; Israel heard, “I
will cause you to return to this Earth.” How little—how nothing—does one see
who does not recognize the parent's likeness in the son? The speaker and words
are the same; the return here is no different from there, nor the Earth to
which they return. Scripture made the very word of return with which God
addressed man proper to Israel's condition, refusing to transfer it to
anything except Israel's restoration from captivity—that is, man's redemption
from the curse. Why should anyone twist toward fear what is proposed to the
Israelites' father for hope? Why should things God blended in names not also be
felt to coalesce in nature, especially when he granted this same sentence not
only as a promise to Israel but as consolation to descendants many centuries
later? Drawing from the treasures of divine knowledge, Solomon prayed for the
people of God laid low by enemies: “You will cause them to return to the Earth
you gave their fathers.” He did not repeat God's words without purpose or
mention the fathers rashly. Anyone who excludes the first father from their
number was long ago expelled from the church with Tatian. Since it is certain
that mercy began with the same man from whom punishment originated, and that
the chain of salvation could reach the sons only when joined to the father,
there can be no dispute about “Until you return to Earth.” All the rest breathes
threats; this alone seems to breathe rest. For “The woman's Seed will crush

[Marginalia: Ps. 68.18; Isa. 51.3.]

[1611 Book II, printed p. 10 | Google Books RA1-PA10]

your head” names the author of salvation and the tyrant's ruin, not salvation
itself and the captive's liberation. Death brought upon death is stated, but
life restored to the dead is not. The author of slaughter can be handed over
to death while his victim does not revive. Redemption therefore required not
only that the serpent hear his destruction but that man hear of return to his
homeland through the promised Seed. These words did that, as their wording
shows and the Hebrew doctors appear to have understood even before Christ.
They offered Paradise to the pious departing life, and their common people
absorbed the hope, for nothing was more widespread among them than hope of
returning to Paradise after this life. Truth itself appears to confirm it,
since Christ would otherwise have promised a dying thief a Paradise of which
he had heard nothing in life.

If someone thinks the Hebrews could hope for Paradise from Scripture but not
this passage, let him produce another that promises it. If he cannot, let him
believe the early Christians who explicitly affirm that Moses hands down two
Paradises here: one inhabited in this life and the other in the next. Anastasius
Sinaita says Moses taught man's two placements in Paradise: a literal one in
the terrestrial Paradise at the beginning and a mystical one in the heavenly
Paradise after death. Theophilus of Antioch similarly writes: “When man was
newly created in the world, Genesis recorded as an enigma and mystery that he
was placed in Paradise twice. The first entrance occurred; the other will
follow the universal resurrection and last judgment. As an imperfect vessel
is reshaped and recast by the craftsman's hand to become new and whole, so man
after death will be remade by God's power and found new, pure, righteous, and
immortal in the resurrection.” Eucherius adds: “He is dismissed from the
Paradise of blessedness to work the Earth—that is, to labor in this body and
lay up merit for returning to the blessed life

[Marginalia: Anastasius, *On the Doubled Name of God*; *To Autolycus* 3;
Eucherius on Gen. 1.6.30.]

[1611 Book II, printed p. 11 | Google Books RA1-PA11]

signified by Paradise.” Paulinus of Nola agrees: “So that we may receive what
is ours, namely Paradise's dominion and eternal life, if we are purified and
recover possession after this earth's condemnation, then, restored from exile
to our homeland or brought from long wandering to our native home, we may
truly say, ‘God is our portion in the Earth of the living.’”

Eucherius says there are three opinions about Paradise: some take it only
corporeally, others only spiritually, and others both ways. Testimonies teaching
that Moses handed down two Paradises in Genesis destroy the first view. Where,
if not in “Until you return to Earth”? Franciscus Georgius recently tried to
confirm the second with arguments from Origen and Jerome. Methodius and
Epiphanius say Origen overthrew Paradise's history by locating it not on earth
but in the third heaven and understanding angelic powers by its trees and the
waters below the firmament by its rivers. Yet Sixtus of Siena cites Origen's

8. The other member of the heavenly sentence is wholly spent in announcing
punishment without limit of duration or consolation to diminish it. It does
not say “until you return,” but simply “you will return to Earth”—not the Earth
whose common name embraces matter and homeland, but Earth considered only as
matter or something baser than matter. Earlier it was *Adamah*, whose twofold
meaning we saw; here it is *gnaphar*, properly Dust, never used for good. This
is fitting: evil is the symbol of Matter and Earth of matter, but Dust denotes
Earth with no regard for form, goodness's seat—only Earth's smallest particles,
as though the matter of Matter. This clause therefore contains the ultimate
evil and nature's extreme abasement. If Scripture threatens man with anything
in or after death, it must be sought in this fatal dust. As others saw, the
Lord drives Adam by these words into every mutation of death: Death is the
name, Earth the sign; the law confirms the sentence and the sentence interprets
the law. More plainly, as Augustine explains it, the Lord condemns Adam to the
