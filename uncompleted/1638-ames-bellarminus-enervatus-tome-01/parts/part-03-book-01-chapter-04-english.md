### CHAPTER IV

#### On the Perspicuity of Scripture, Book 3, Chapters 1 and 2

##### THESIS I

Bell.: *Scripture is not so clear in itself that, without explanation, it
suffices to settle controversies of faith.*

Prot.: Nor is this our position, if all controversies concerning faith are
understood. For Scripture says absolutely nothing about various controversies
except implicitly, and so it cannot settle them without some explanation. But
we deny that a praetorian and judicial explanation is necessary. Bell.: *The
question is whether Holy Scripture is in itself most easy and most clear, or
whether it needs interpretation.*

Prot.: That question also needs its own interpretation. For none of us has
ever thought that all parts of Scripture are equally easy and clear, that any
part is equally easy for everyone, or that the interpretation which God
entrusted to the ministers of the Church is superfluous. But, admonished by
Jerome on Hosea 2 that *obscurity arises in three ways—from the difficulty of
the subject matter, the teacher's lack of skill, or the learner's excessive
slowness*—we concede: 1. That certain things contained in Scripture are
difficult, whether because of their majesty, such as the mystery of the Holy
Trinity, or because for another reason they are remote from our perception,
such as predictions of future things. 2. That for carnal people destitute of
the Spirit of God all spiritual things are difficult and obscure, and thus
even for the regenerate in part, because they are still carnal in part. 3.
That for those whose senses have not been exercised in Scripture various
phrases are somewhat obscure. 4. That the Holy Spirit explained certain
things less clearly because they are less necessary to know. Nevertheless, we
contend: 1. That all those things which it is necessary for salvation to
understand are set forth most clearly

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 50 | printed p. 40 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

in Scripture. 2. That there is no such obscurity in Scripture that because of
it less learned people should refrain from reading Scripture, as though they
could receive no benefit from it. 3. That light is brought to nearly all
difficult passages of Scripture by other, clearer passages. 4. That no
interpretation of Scripture is to be admitted which cannot, at least as to its
meaning, be clearly proved to agree with Scripture itself.

2. Bell. 1: *In Psalm 119 David prays for understanding and for the unveiling
of his eyes, and so forth.* Prot.: 1. On the Roman Catholics' position, there
was no need for this prayer, since the Supreme Pontiff was at hand, from whom
David could easily have obtained the interpretation of all the things of
which he was ignorant. 2. Because David was illuminated only in part, he
therefore seeks greater light for his mind from that source, so that he might
perceive the will of God still more deeply from his Word. What does this have
to do with the matter? Bell. 2: *In the last chapter of Luke, the Lord
interpreted the Scriptures for his disciples.* Prot.: 1. No one denies that
the Scriptures are to be interpreted. 2. Those very Scriptures which were
then difficult for the disciples have now been rendered clear to believers
through those same disciples. Bell. 3: *The eunuch in Acts 8 denies that he
can understand Scripture unless someone shows him.* Prot.: He could not
understand that particular passage accurately without help. Nevertheless, he
did not refrain from reading, nor did he seek any praetorian interpreter.
Bell. 4: *In the final chapter of his second epistle, Peter says that certain
things in Paul's epistles are difficult to understand.* Prot.: Yet the same
Peter calls the prophetic word a shining lamp to which all believers ought to
pay attention, 2 Peter 1:19. So far is he from frightening people away from
reading Scripture because some matters are difficult. Nor does any of the
Fathers whose statements Bellarmine appends ascribe such obscurity to
Scripture that he says its reading is therefore useless to the common people.

3. Bell.: *Scripture treats of the most exalted matters by far.* Prot.: 1. If
it is necessary that obscurity be present wherever exalted matters are
treated, then the decrees of councils and the rescripts of pontiffs are most
obscure, for they treat of the most exalted matters. 2. An exalted matter can
easily be explained insofar as it is necessary for

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 51 | printed p. 41 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

faith, though it is difficult to explain for scientific knowledge. 3. It is
not required that we understand accurately everything handed down in
Scripture, but that we believe it. We briefly call attention to this statement
of Victorinus the rhetorician, together with Jerome in the preface to book 12
of his commentary on Ezekiel: *The obscurity of volumes arises from three
things: either the greatness of the subject matter, the teacher's lack of
skill—a will to teach clearly is presupposed—or the hearer's dullness.* We
plainly confess the first and last concerning Scripture; the authority of God
easily excludes the second.

4. Bell.: *There are many things in Scripture which at first sight appear
contradictory. Some are ambiguous. Some sentences are incomplete. Some
sentences are out of order. Some phrases are peculiar to the Hebrews. Finally,
there are very many figurative expressions.* Prot.: 1. There is nothing here
which is not found in human writings, in councils, in pontifical rescripts,
and in the Fathers' commentaries. They therefore show no peculiar obscurity in
Scripture because of which it should not be read by the common people. 2. If
the obscurity of Scripture consists in things of this kind, then they have no
need of any interpreter other than one practiced in them. To what end, then,
is the judicial power of the Roman Pontiff? 3. The figures used in Scripture
do not always produce obscurity, but often aim to make what is taught more
easily understood, according to that saying of Christ in John 3: *If I have
told you earthly things and you do not believe, how will you believe if I tell
you heavenly things?*

##### Our Arguments

5. Prot. 1: The commandment is not hidden from you, and so forth, Deuteronomy
30:11. Bell.: *Some understand this passage of the ease of fulfilling the
commandments, not of understanding Scripture. Others, however, understand it
of the ease of knowing the commandments of the Decalogue, which are most
easily understood.* Prot.: 1. If it is easy to fulfill the commandments, then
it is easy to understand them. These things are joined in the text, verse 12:
*Who will declare it to us so that we may do it?* Nor can anyone fulfill in
obedience what he does not know. 2. Moses does not speak specifically of the
commandments of the Decalogue, but of the whole book of the Law, verse 10. 3.
If the Decalogue is easily understood, then the whole of Scripture is not
obscure; then neither tropes nor Hebrew phrases produce obscurity; and,
finally,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 52 | printed p. 42 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

controversies can concern the easiest matters, as in the case of images, which
are forbidden in the second commandment.

6. Prot.: Scripture is bright, a lamp, a light, and so forth, Psalms 19 and
119, verse 6. Bell. 1: *Those passages do not concern all the Scriptures, but
the Lord's commandments.* Prot.: 1. The apostle Peter expressly affirms this
of the prophetic books, 2 Peter 1:19. 2. Psalm 19 concerns that entire doctrine
which converts the soul, verse 8. Bellarmine himself, in his commentary on
verse 9, includes even ceremonies under the Law. And a little earlier in this
very chapter, concerning Psalm 119, these are Bellarmine's words: *By the name
of the Law David does not understand those ten commandments alone, but all the
divine Scriptures.* Bell. 2: *Scripture is called a light and a lamp not
because it is easily understood, but because, when it has been understood, it
illuminates the mind.* Prot.: 1. These two things do not conflict with each
other, but agree perfectly. 2. The apostle expressly says that the gospel is
an illumination in itself, even when it is not understood, 2 Corinthians 4:4;
and the psalmist affirms that the light of Scripture is such that its entrance
illuminates the simple—that is, causes them to understand, Psalm 119:130.

7. Prot.: The apostles are the light of the world, Matthew 5:14. Therefore,
their doctrine—that is, Scripture—is also light. Bell. 1: *The passage concerns
the light of life and morals.* Prot.: 1. Life without doctrine is not light.
2. The light of doctrine belongs more properly to the apostles than does the
light of life. 3. Paul interprets it of doctrine, 2 Corinthians 4:6. 4. Christ
himself joins these things in verse 19: *Whoever shall do and teach them.*
Bell. 2: *The doctrine of the apostles illuminates when it is understood.*
Prot.: But it also belonged to the apostles' office to apply every effort so
that their doctrine might be understood, 2 Corinthians 3:2; and in that part
of their office they were also light.

8. Prot.: The prophetic word is a shining lamp, 2 Peter 1:19. Bell.: *Because
it illuminates the understanding.* Prot.: But the apostle says that attention
must be paid to that word as to a lamp, so that the illumination of the
understanding is to be sought from it.

9. Prot.: If our gospel is veiled, it is veiled to those who perish, and so
forth, 2 Corinthians 4:4. Bell.: *The apostle does not speak of understanding
the Scriptures, but of knowledge and faith in Christ.* Prot.: 1. The apostle
speaks expressly of his doctrine and preaching, by which he had manifested
the truth

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 53 | printed p. 43 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

to every human conscience, verse 2. 2. Without understanding the doctrine of
the Scriptures, there is no knowledge of or faith in Christ. The apostle joins
these things; Bellarmine separates them.

10. Prot.: The summary of the whole of Scripture—the Decalogue, the Creed, the
Lord's Prayer, and the sacraments—has the clearest testimonies in Scripture.
Bell. 1: *It does not therefore follow that the whole of Scripture is clear.*
Prot.: Nor do we infer this; but it follows that the things necessary for
salvation are clearly explained in Scripture. Bell. 2: *If such clear
testimonies about all these things existed, then all controversies would be
ended.* Prot.: As though perverse people could not raise controversies about
clear matters! Bellarmine said above that the commandments of the Decalogue
are most easily understood. Yet later he disputes in defense of the worship of
images, against the express words of the second commandment.

11. Prot.: In the New Testament Scripture is clear. Bell.: *In the New
Testament, even if many do not understand the statements of Scripture,
nevertheless even country people and women understand the mysteries of
redemption themselves.* Prot.: That is, they understand the very things which
are spoken of in Scripture, but not the meaning of Scripture. As though it
could in any way happen that someone who understands the things themselves
does not understand the statements which declare those things, uttered in a
known language.

12. Prot.: The Fathers of the early Church read the Scriptures without
commentaries. Bell.: *Living commentaries assisted the earliest Fathers—that
is, the apostles and their disciples. Afterward, they began to write
commentaries.* Prot.: 1. The apostles and apostolic men did not have leisure
to deliver commentaries on whole books to each person who approached them; nor
did all pastors have leisure to approach the apostles and apostolic men. 2.
Origen first broke the ice, as Beatus Rhenanus says, in expounding the
Scriptures; he lived around the end of the second century. But in his
commentaries he handled a large part of Scripture in such a way that he seems
not so much to have brought it light as to have poured darkness over it. *For
he roams through the open spaces of allegory and makes his own ingenuity into
mysteries of the Church.* Jerome, commentary 5 on Isaiah. Nevertheless, those
who immediately followed drew from him. As Jerome observes in the preface to
Jonah, *they did not so much open the meanings of Scripture as obscure them,
so that their interpretation itself*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 54 | printed p. 44 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*needs interpretation, and the reader departs much more uncertain than he was
before he read it.* Thus it is most certain that there was a time when the
Scriptures were read without commentaries, and also a time when they were
understood with greater difficulty because of commentaries.
