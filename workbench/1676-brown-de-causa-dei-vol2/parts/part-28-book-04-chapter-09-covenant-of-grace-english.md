# CHAPTER IX

## The covenant made with the Israelites was the covenant of grace

We have now treated the Decalogue insofar as it is law and signifies to us
God's will concerning human duty. It follows that we should consider it
insofar as it bore a relation to that covenant which the Lord made with the
Israelites on Mount Sinai.

Just as the character of the covenant, superadded to the law of nature
according to God's mere good pleasure, was distinguished from the character
of that law—so that this agreement could have been absent while the law
remained firm and perpetually binding—so also the character of the covenant
annexed to the law renewed on Mount Sinai can be distinguished from the
character of the law. For even if primal man had perfectly observed the
natural law, he could not for that reason have expected the reward of life
unless a promise had been annexed by virtue of the covenant made. As is well
known, the reward of eternal life was to be given to primal man, according to
the nature of a covenant and compact, not because of the *worthiness of the
works*, ἀξίᾳ, but because of ἀξιώσει, the divine *deigning* that rewards beyond
merit. We have already treated this law, insofar as it is the standard and
rule of our duty and prescribes what must be done and omitted, as far as the
scope of our undertaking permits. It now follows that we should say something
about this other character—namely, that of the covenant—as far as it serves
our purpose.

No one denies that God made a covenant with the Israelites, since Scripture
testifies to it so often. See, among other passages, Deuteronomy 4:13, 17; 2.
Exodus 24:3–8 and 19:4, 6; Deuteronomy 29:10–13; Jeremiah 11:3–5 and 31:31–32;
2 Kings 18:12; 2 Chronicles 6:11; Deuteronomy 26:17–18; and Jeremiah
34:13–14. We must first see, however, what kind of covenant it was, and then
what relation the Decalogue bore to that covenant.

People hold various opinions about the nature of that covenant made with the
Israelites. We say that it was the covenant of grace, and prove this with the
following arguments, with this preliminary qualification: here we are not
speaking of the Decalogue considered in abstraction or separately, but of
that entire dispensation of which the promulgation of the Decalogue formed
only one part.

*Argument 1.* The covenant which was the same in substance as that covenant
which the Lord Jehovah had made with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob is the covenant
of grace. The covenant made with the Israelites was such a covenant.
Therefore, and so forth. Scripture abundantly confirms the minor premise:

[Printed p. 106 | IA PDF 114 | IA leaf 114 | IA decausadei02brow]

Deuteronomy 7:8, 11–12: *Because Jehovah loved you, and because he keeps that
oath which he swore to your fathers—and you shall observe the commandment and
those statutes and judgments which I command you today, so that you do
them—it shall therefore come to pass, when you have heard these judgments and
have observed and done them, that Jehovah your God will keep with you that
covenant and kindness which he promised by oath to your fathers.* The covenant
made with the Israelites, therefore, accorded with that oath which God swore
to their fathers—that is, to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—and was the same
covenant which he had promised by oath to those same fathers. Thus
Deuteronomy 29:12–13 says: *That you may enter the covenant of Jehovah your
God, with his oath, which Jehovah your God makes with you today, and that he
may establish you today as a people for himself and may himself be God to you,
just as he said to you and just as he promised by oath to your fathers,
Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.*

Furthermore, circumcision, which was the sign and seal of the covenant made
with Abraham—and is sometimes itself called the covenant in Genesis 17:10,
14 and Acts 7:8—was also the sign and seal of the covenant made with the
Israelites. For it continued under this covenant, and its nature had not been
changed. The covenant was therefore the same in substance. Distinct
administrations of the same covenant sometimes require distinct and very
different seals. How much more would distinct covenants require a change of
seals? Yet we do not see that done here. This covenant was therefore not
distinct in substance from the earlier covenant established with Abraham.

Moreover, that covenant made with Abraham included the Israelites themselves.
For it was an everlasting covenant, made not only between God and Abraham but
also between God and Abraham's seed throughout their generations. It also
concerned those to whom God would give the land of Canaan, as may be seen in
Genesis 17:7–8, 13. I do not linger over that objection from Deuteronomy
5:2–3, where Moses addresses the Israelites in this way: *Jehovah our God
made a covenant with us at Horeb. Jehovah did not make that covenant with our
fathers, but with us.* For I anticipated it when I added the words *in
substance*. With respect to various circumstances, I grant that those
covenants were distinct. Moses spoke this way with respect to these
circumstances, as the Dutch Annotations and other commentators rightly
observe. See further 1 Chronicles 16:16–17; Jeremiah 11:3–5; Exodus 2:24–25,
3:14–15, and 6:2–8.

As for the major premise, its truth is evident from the fact that the covenant
which Jehovah struck with Abraham was a covenant of grace. This can be
demonstrated in many ways:

1. It was a compact ratified by God εἰς χριστόν, *unto Christ* (Galatians
3:17). That is, it was either established in Christ or directed toward
Christ. See Beza and the Dutch Annotations on the passage.

2. The blessing given to Abraham in Genesis 12:3—*All the families of the
earth will be blessed in you*—was evangelical, indeed the sum of the gospel,
according to Paul in Galatians 3:8: *Since Scripture foresaw*, he says, *that
God would justify the Gentiles by faith, it preached the gospel beforehand to
Abraham, saying, All the Gentiles will be blessed in you.* [I willingly
retained, says Beza on the passage, the verb *to evangelize*, which, as we
have now often said, is specially consecrated to the teaching concerning
gratuitous reconciliation through Christ. And I think the Apostle used it
deliberately to show that this teaching is by no means new, but was revealed
even to Abraham himself.] See also Marlorat on the passage.

3. This blessing of Abraham was obtained by Christ's accursed death. For the
Apostle speaks as follows in Galatians 3:13–14: *Christ redeemed us from the
curse*

[Printed p. 107 | IA PDF 115 | IA leaf 115 | IA decausadei02brow]

*of the law by being made a curse for us—so that the blessing of Abraham might
come upon the Gentiles in Christ Jesus, and so that we might receive the
promised Spirit through faith.* It therefore pertained to the covenant of
grace. See further Acts 3:25–26.

4. As the same passage teaches, that covenant contained the promise of the
Spirit, to be received by faith—that is, the promise of spiritual things,
whether the blessing of eternal spiritual life or the Spirit himself, who
seals the promise in our hearts. Promises received by faith pertain to the
covenant of grace, whose condition is faith.

5. That covenant made with Abraham contained promises and is called *the
promise* in Galatians 3:17. Moreover, the word ἐπαγγελίας does not signify
just any promise, but one that is entirely gratuitous, according to Beza on
Galatians 3:14. Gratuitous promises pertain to the covenant of grace. See
further Galatians 3:18.

6. It was of the same nature as the covenant now established under the
gospel. For the Apostle's purpose in that third chapter to the Galatians is to
show that under the gospel we are justified in the same way as Abraham.

7. The condition of that covenant was faith. For *Abraham believed God, and
it was credited to him as righteousness* (Genesis 15:5–6; Romans 4:3). Hence
he is called *faithful* in Galatians 3:9 and *the father of those who believe*
in Romans 4:11. Circumcision was the seal of the righteousness of faith
(Romans 4:11). He was not justified by works (Romans 4:2), but by faith
(Romans 4:3), and this faith had Christ himself as its object (John 8:58).
Furthermore, the children of Abraham obtain the promised blessing by faith:
*Those who are of faith are blessed with that faithful Abraham* (Galatians
3:9). It is plain, therefore, that this covenant was the covenant of grace.

8. If that covenant was not the covenant of grace, the Gentiles under the New
Testament could not have been included under it. Abraham, however, was
appointed the father of many nations (Genesis 17:4–5), and the inheritance was
by faith so that it would be by grace, in order that the promise might be
secure to all the seed (Romans 4:17).

9. The land of Canaan itself (Genesis 12:7; 13:14–15; 15:18–21; and 17:8)
bore a relation to Christ and led people toward him. It is therefore called
*the land of Immanuel* in Isaiah 8:8.

10. That primary promise in Genesis 17:7–8, *I will be your God and the God of
your seed*, plainly demonstrates that this covenant was a covenant of grace.
For since Adam fell, Jehovah becomes God to no one except through the covenant
of grace confirmed in Christ. This is further evident from the fact that this
promise embraces all things: *justification*, for example (Jeremiah 31:33, 31;
Romans 3:29–30); *adoption* (2 Corinthians 6:16, 18); *sanctification*
(Ezekiel 20:12, compared with verse 20); *communion with God* (Exodus 29:45;
Leviticus 26:11–12; Revelation 21:3; 2 Corinthians 6:16); indeed *all good
things* belonging both to this life and to the life to come (Revelation 21:7;
Psalm 33:12; Hebrews 11:13–16), and *resurrection* itself (Luke 20:37–38;
Matthew 22:31–32).

11. Christ himself was promised in this covenant (Luke 1:54–55, 69, 72–73).

12. The promises made to Abraham in that compact were made to Christ
(Galatians 3:16)—that is, to Jews and Gentiles who are members of Christ. See
Beza on the passage; or they were made to Christ as Head.

*Argument 2.* This covenant contained promises which could be given and
obtained under no covenant other than the covenant of grace. Such are those
in Exodus 19:5–6: *Now therefore, if you diligently attend to my voice and
keep my covenant, you will indeed be my peculiar treasure above all other
peoples—and you will be to me a priestly kingdom and a holy nation.* Scripture
often mentions the former privilege to the glory of God's grace, as in
Deuteronomy 7:6; 14:2; 26:18; and Psalm 135:4. The latter is a purely

[Printed p. 108 | IA PDF 116 | IA leaf 116 | IA decausadei02brow]

evangelical promise, as may be seen in 1 Peter 5:9–10, and was obtained
through Christ alone (Revelation 1:5–6).

*Argument 3.* This covenant had a mediator: Moses as a typical mediator
(Exodus 20:19–20; Deuteronomy 5:22, and so forth; Galatians 3:19), and Christ
as the mediator typified (Deuteronomy 18:15–19). It was therefore the covenant
of grace.

*Argument 4.* The summary of this covenant proves that it was the covenant of
grace. Its summary is this: *I will be God to you, and you will be a people to
me* (Deuteronomy 29:11–13; Jeremiah 11:3–5; compare Leviticus 26:3, 10 and
Jeremiah 7:23). This is also the summary of the covenant of grace, as appears
from Jeremiah 31:33; Hebrews 8:10; and Ezekiel 36:28.

*Argument 5.* In this covenant God promised that he would circumcise their
hearts, and so forth (Deuteronomy 30:6). According to the Apostle in Hebrews
8:10, however, this is a promise of the covenant of grace.

*Argument 6.* The same is clear from comparing Deuteronomy 30:12–14 with
Romans 10:6–8, for the summary of the condition of the evangelical covenant is
explained in both places.

*Argument 7.* The ceremonial law which was given at that time plainly proves
that dispensation to have been a dispensation of the covenant of grace and,
consequently, the covenant made with the Israelites to have been the covenant
of grace. For those ceremonies led people toward Christ, the sole mediator of
the covenant of grace, and indicated that remission of sins was to be obtained
through the blood of Christ, foreshadowed by the blood of sacrifices. It is
therefore evident that the covenant of grace was then administered, although
obscurely, through shadows, types, and such elements of the world.

*Argument 8.* The sacraments annexed to that covenant were sacraments of the
covenant of grace. Therefore, the covenant itself was the covenant of grace.
The antecedent is plain, because circumcision was the seal of the righteousness
of faith (Romans 4:11), and the Passover prefigured Christ (1 Corinthians 5:7).
These were the sacraments of that covenant, and they were sacraments of the
covenant of grace because they bore a relation to Christ and to faith. A
covenant according to which salvation is obtained through faith in Christ is
the covenant of grace, as is confessed.

*Argument 9.* In this covenant there was remission of sins and repentance had
a place, as Moses and the prophets everywhere testify. In that dispensation
God is called the God who exercises mercy, the longsuffering God, who shows
mercy and kindness to thousands and forgives the sins of transgressors
(Exodus 33:19; 34:6–7). God presents himself in this way only under the
covenant of grace.

*Argument 10.* The token by which that covenant was ratified, established,
and confirmed plainly demonstrates that it was the covenant of grace. For
when the covenant had been proposed and the people had said with one voice
that they would observe all things, burnt offerings and thanksgiving
sacrifices were offered. With the blood of those sacrifices Moses sprinkled
the altar, then the book itself, and finally the whole people (Exodus 24:3–8;
Hebrews 9:18–20). By that sprinkling of blood, however, Christ's blood was
prefigured, through which alone remission of sins is obtained. See Hebrews
9:14, 22.

*Argument 11.* In this covenant God stipulated that the Israelites should
give themselves to God (Exodus 19:5; Leviticus 26:12) and believe in Christ.
For without faith they could not rightly make use of the sacrifices or
perform ceremonial worship. It was therefore the covenant of grace.

*Argument 12.* In making this covenant with them, God is called their God:
*I*

[Printed p. 109 | IA PDF 117 | IA leaf 117 | IA decausadei02brow]

*am Jehovah your God*, he says—not as Creator, but as Liberator and Redeemer,
for it is added, *who brought you out of the land of Egypt*. By saying this he
declared that he was their God by virtue of the covenant made with Abraham.
For this was the special promise of that covenant, so that we often see it
said that God brought them out of Egypt by virtue of that covenant. See
Exodus 2:24–25; 3:6–8, 15–17; Psalm 105:8–10, 37–38, 42–44; Jeremiah 11:5;
Exodus 6:5–8; and often elsewhere. See also Nehemiah 9:8–10.

Hence it is evident that this was the covenant of grace, because according to
the tenor of the covenant of grace, Jehovah was their God. Nor could he be God
in any other way to those who were sinners by nature and enemies of God. We do
not, however, say that God was the God of them all in a spiritual and inward
sense. For we know that very many were unbelievers, were destroyed in the
wilderness because of unbelief (Hebrews 3 and 4), and thus did not fulfill the
condition of the covenant (Psalm 78:8, 10, 22, 32, 36–37; 95:11; Jeremiah
5:21, 23, 26; Psalm 81:11–13; Jeremiah 7:23–24, 26, 29; 11:4; and so forth).

Nevertheless, we affirm that the covenant of grace was truly administered at
that time, just as we say that the covenant of grace, and no other covenant,
is truly administered now in the evangelical Church, even though very many
are in the covenant only ecclesiastically and outwardly, not inwardly. That
is, although they make a pledge, they have not yet truly fulfilled the
stipulated condition of the covenant and therefore do not yet have, before
God, a right to the good things promised under that condition. The Israelites
accepted the stipulated condition by profession and promised that they would
fulfill it. They were therefore truly under that conditional administration
of the covenant of grace.

Thus this title which God assumes for himself sufficiently proves that this
covenant was the covenant of grace. For after the fall, God is no one's God
except in the covenant of grace, so that he makes a covenant with them and by
virtue of the covenant grants or promises benefits to them.

As for what some assert—that God is called their God in a political and
ecclesiastical sense, or by reason of a political and ecclesiastical
covenant—we reply only that by this ecclesiastical and political covenant
they understand either some covenant really and substantially distinct from
the covenant of grace, or merely the outward administration of the covenant
of grace. If they mean the latter, we do not contradict them, because it is
then manifest from this title that God made the covenant of grace with the
Israelites. But if they mean the former, we cannot assent. For one thing, on
this account covenants are multiplied without Scripture leading the way. For
another, it would follow either that God did not make the covenant of grace
with them—which the brothers who assert this will not say—or that God made
two distinct covenants with them, either both with all the Israelites or one
with some and another with others. Yet I certainly do not know how they have
demonstrated these things from Holy Scripture. Furthermore, I would like them
to explain the nature of that political or ecclesiastical covenant and show
that it is of a nature different from the covenant of grace itself.

*Argument 13.* The covenant of works is never called a testament; only the
covenant of grace is honored with this title. But that covenant which was
made with the Israelites is called a testament. Hence we read of the Old
Testament. It was therefore the covenant of grace.

*Argument 14.* The Angel of the covenant, the Son of God himself and the
Redeemer of humanity, made this covenant with the Israelites, as we saw above.
He, however, could make no other

[Printed p. 110 | IA PDF 118 | IA leaf 118 | IA decausadei02brow]

covenant with them than the covenant of grace, of which he was the sole
mediator.

*Argument 15.* If this had not been the covenant of grace, Peter could not
have said in Acts 10:43 that all the Prophets bore witness to Christ—namely,
that *everyone who believes in him will receive remission of sins through his
name*.

*Objection.* The law under which the Israelites were placed is often opposed
to faith and the promise (Romans 10:5–6; Galatians 3:18; Romans 4:14).

*Reply 1.* The term *law* is taken either broadly or strictly. It is taken
*strictly* insofar as it denotes the covenant of works, according to which
life was promised under the condition of perfect obedience. It is taken in
this sense here when it is opposed to *faith* or *the promise*. Indeed, the
corrupt Jews—and to some extent also those against whom the Apostle disputes
there—dreamed of such a covenant, perverted the proper use of the Mosaic
covenant, and fashioned a covenant of works. They therefore believed that
they would be justified by works of the law and established their own
righteousness.

The term *law* is taken *broadly*, however, when the whole Mosaic economy is
under discussion. Taken in that sense, it does not properly denote the
covenant of works, but rather the covenant of grace; nor is it opposed to
faith or the promise, but embraces both. Unless this distinction is admitted,
it will be impossible to interpret many of Paul's statements rightly or
reconcile them with one another, and we shall yield to the Socinians, who
press from this that no grace or faith was administered under the law and
that the fathers under the law had nothing of Christ.

2. Paul establishes this opposition not only between the moral law and faith,
but also between the ceremonial law and faith. For he speaks of the whole
law, and the dispute had first arisen about the ceremonial law. The carnal
Jews had also abused this law and turned it into a covenant of works. Yet who
will say that the ceremonial law, considered in itself and as it had been
instituted, was opposed to faith or Christ, when it led people toward Christ?

3. Through abuse, the gospel itself can be turned into a covenant of works
and so opposed to faith, or to the true covenant of grace. Yet no one will say
that the gospel, freed from this abuse, is opposed to faith or the covenant of
grace.

4. That it does not follow from this opposition between law and faith or the
promise that the covenant of grace was by no means administered then—and that
*law* must be taken there in its broader sense—is clear from the same passages
from which the objection is drawn. For Romans 10 openly says in verse 4 that
*Christ was the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes*.
Does the covenant of works, considered in itself, direct and lead people to
Christ? Furthermore, in verses 6–9 of the same chapter Paul describes for us
from Moses the righteousness which is by faith, or the condition of the
covenant of grace. He could not have done this unless that Mosaic economy was
a dispensation of the covenant of grace. Finally, in verse 3 of the same
chapter, he reproaches the Jews for their ignorance of that righteousness
which God then required and which is therefore called the righteousness of
God. This righteousness, however, was the very righteousness of faith,
opposed to the righteousness acquired by works of the law. Compare Romans
9:31–32 with Romans 10:3.

As for Galatians 3, it is plain that *law* can and must also be understood as
the administration of the covenant of grace. For in verse 21 Paul openly
denies that *the law is contrary to the promises of God*, and in verse 24 he
says that *the law was a tutor unto Christ, that they might be justified by
faith*. This cannot be said of the law unless that law was the administration
of the covenant of grace.

Finally, as for Romans 4, it appears that he is not speaking of the law in its
broader sense when he says in verse 14: *For if those who are*

[Printed p. 111 | IA PDF 119 | IA leaf 119 | IA decausadei02brow]

*of the law are heirs, faith has been made void and that promise has been
rendered empty.* For in the same chapter he proves from the words of David
that justification and remission of sins come by faith, not by works of the
law. He also shows that the promise made to Abraham was the sum of the gospel,
or of the covenant of grace. And in Galatians 2:17 he proves that the law
could not render this promise useless and, consequently, that it was not the
law of works—that is, the covenant of works. For if it had been the covenant
of works, it would have rendered that promise altogether empty as far as the
Jews are concerned.
