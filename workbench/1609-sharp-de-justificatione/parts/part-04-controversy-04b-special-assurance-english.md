### Testimonies of the Fathers in confirmation of the same point

1. Cyprian, sermon 4 *On Mortality*: “What room is there here,” he says, “for
anxiety and worry? Who in these circumstances is fearful and sorrowful, except
the one who lacks hope and faith? It belongs to the person who is unwilling to
pass over to Christ to fear death. It belongs to the person who does not
believe that he is beginning to reign with Christ to be unwilling to pass over
to Christ. For it is written, ‘The righteous shall live by faith.’ If you are
righteous, you also live by faith. If you truly believe in God, why are you
not going to be with Christ, and why do you not confidently embrace the
Lord’s promise?”

2. The same author in the same place: “God promises immortality to you when
you depart from this world, and you doubt and waver. This is to know nothing
at all of God; it is to offend Christ, the teacher of believers, by the sin of
unbelief; it is to possess no faith in the house of faith when faith has been
established in the church.”

3. Chrysostom on Romans 5: “Therefore, even if you have not been especially
worthy, do not lose heart when you have so great a protection—namely, the
judge’s love or favor.”

4. The same author in the same place, on verse 2, at the words “through whom
we have access into the grace in which we stand”: “He said well, ‘in which we
stand,’ for such grace has no end and knows no limit.”

5. Ambrose on Romans 5: “Because it is impossible for those who are beloved to
be deceived, he wished to make us secure concerning the promise, because it is
both God who made the promise and he wishes to hold as beloved those to whom
he

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 53 | image 15575025 | printed p. 51]

made it.”

6. Augustine, sermon 28 *On the Words of the Lord*: “All your sins have been
forgiven. Therefore be confident—not because of your work but because of the
grace of Christ. For ‘by grace you have been saved,’ says the apostle.
Accordingly, this is not arrogance but faith. To proclaim what you have
received is not pride but devotion.”

7. The same author on Psalm 88: “God has said this; he has promised it; if that
is too little, he has sworn it. Therefore, because the promise is firm not
according to our merits but according to his mercy, no one ought tremblingly
to proclaim a matter about which he cannot doubt.”

8. Tertullian in the book *On Baptism*: “Faith possesses the complete security
of salvation.”

9. Bernard, sermon 3 *On the Fragments of the Seven Mercies*: “I consider
three things in which my whole hope consists: the love of adoption, the truth
of the promise, and the power of fulfillment. Now let my foolish thought
murmur as much as it wishes, saying, ‘Who are you? How great is that glory? By
what merits do you hope to obtain it?’ But I know confidently what I have
believed, and I am certain that he has adopted me in exceedingly great love,
that he is true in his promise, and that he is powerful in its performance.
Here is a threefold cord which is broken only with difficulty. I earnestly ask
that we firmly grasp it, lowered to us from our homeland all the way into this
prison, so that it may lift us and draw us all the way to the sight of the
glory of the great God.”

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 54 | image 15575026 | printed p. 52]

10. Hilary on Matthew 5: “The Lord has declared that the kingdom of heaven is
placed in himself. He wishes it to be hoped for without any uncertainty of an
undecided will, because otherwise there is no justification by faith if faith
is doubtful.”

11. Nazianzen in the consolatory oration *On the Great Calamity*: “Who knows
whether he may turn, repent, and leave a blessing? Yet I know this plainly: I
have divine clemency as my surety.”

### Objections

#### Objection 1

From Mark 1:40: the leper’s faith was of the sort that God required and for
whose confirmation he performed miracles. Yet the leper was uncertain and
doubted concerning special mercy, for he said, “If you are willing, you can
cleanse me.” Therefore true justifying faith is not a certain persuasion of
special mercy. The leper did, however, possess faith in omnipotence and deity,
because he obtained what he asked.

Response 1: as to the major premise, if the leper’s faith was only in
omnipotence and deity, it was not of the sort Christ required. The major
premise is therefore false, because Christ does not require a faith which
regards only omnipotence and deity.

2. Christ performed miracles in and before many who did not possess true
justifying faith. He also performed them for an end other than that all should
believe, Luke 2:24; Matthew 13:13. For many who did not believe unto salvation
were healed by Christ.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 55 | image 15575027 | printed p. 53]

3. The words cited do not prove the minor premise. Although faith may be firm
in its degree, it is not free from doubt even in the greatest believers. Nor
does it follow, “He doubted; therefore he did not believe”; or, “He possessed
faith in omnipotence and doubted Christ’s will in this particular act;
therefore he possessed no faith at all in special mercy.” For the confidence
of the godly is often hindered by distrust, as in Psalm 32:2, yet in the end it
emerges victorious, as in Psalm 24:6; Job 13:15; Psalm 23:4. Although it is
weak, it is nevertheless true faith.

4. The inference does not follow: “He obtained what he asked; therefore faith
in omnipotence and deity was true justifying faith.” For bodily goods which
they ask are often given to the ungodly and unbelieving, as Scripture
testifies.

#### Objection 2

If the remission of sins is doubtfully promised to those who repent, then no
one can by faith be certain of special mercy and salvation. But the former is
true from 2 Samuel 16:12; Joshua 14:12; Numbers 21:3; Daniel 4:27; Amos 5:15;
Joel 2:14; Jonah 3:9; Acts 8:22, where “perhaps,” “possibly,” and “who knows
whether this may happen” occur, which are expressions of one who doubts.

Response 1: these expressions do not belong to those who doubt absolutely but
to those who hope well, desire God’s aid, and commit events to a good outcome,
as in Genesis 18:29. These expressions are also often added both to indicate
the difficulty of the matter from a human point of view and to strengthen the
hearers’ minds in good hope, as in 1 Samuel 14:7; 2 Kings 19:4; Zephaniah 3:3.

2. In the cited passages the writer does not speak about the remission of

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 56 | image 15575028 | printed p. 54]

sins or repentance. Nor are these expressions used when spiritual things are
in question, but temporal things, such as the mitigation or removal of the
calamities of this life or the gift of some temporal benefit. Just as these
things are not to be asked for absolutely in prayer, so they are not promised
absolutely by the prophets.

3. In Acts 8, *ei ara*, as some maintain, is an expression of wishing. If it is
an expression of doubt, it regards either temporal punishment—the sense being
that it is possible for this to be forgiven to him by a prayer from faith,
even if feigned, for God sometimes spares hypocrites who display the
appearance of godliness, as he spared Ahab—or eternal punishment. The doubt is
not to be referred to the remission which comes from God, but to the faith and
repentance of Simon Magus, which were feigned.

#### Objection 3

From Luke 18:11: the Pharisee trusted that he was righteous and was not
justified, although he possessed faith in special mercy. The publican,
however, lacked that confidence in special favor, because he approached with
trembling and fear, yet he was justified. Therefore the person who possesses
confidence in special mercy is not justified, but the person who works out
his salvation with fear and trembling.

Response 1: what is asserted concerning the Pharisee is false. He trusted
that he was righteous on account of merits, and thus he is said to have
exalted himself in verse 14. This, however, follows from the passage: the
Pharisee exalted himself and thought that he was righteous before God on
account of his good works, and he was not justified. Therefore those who, like
the Roman Catholics, believe in a Pharisaical manner that they are justified
on account of works

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 57 | image 15575029 | printed p. 55]

are not justified before God.

2. What is said concerning the publican is likewise false. He placed all his
confidence in God and distrusted his own powers; therefore he was justified.
It is evident from his prayer that he possessed confidence in special mercy.
Finally, it does not follow, “He approached prayer with fear and trembling;
therefore he possessed no confidence in God.” Rather, the contrary follows,
because we trust God most when we distrust ourselves.

#### Objection 4

If in Acts and the epistles, in which the doctrine of faith is contained, and
in Acts 8:37, when the eunuch is baptized, there is no word of special mercy
but only of resurrection and deity, then justifying faith is not that by which
we believe God to be gracious to us, but that by which we believe Christ to be
God. But the former is true from the cited passage. Therefore the conclusion
follows.

Response 1: the minor premise is utterly false, for this is the end of every
sermon and apostolic epistle: that each hearer should apply special mercy to
himself by faith. Nor have there been or are there any faithful people except
those who apply Christ to themselves by faith.

2. The apostles taught that Christ is God and has risen so that each person
should believe these things, but they did not teach these things alone.

3. The eunuch’s confession concerned not only Christ’s deity, but he said, “I
believe the Son of God to be that Jesus Christ who was promised, as was also
foretold in that passage of Isaiah, now manifested and dead for the sins of
the faithful and for his own sins.” For in those words the sum of everything
to be believed is set down.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 58 | image 15575030 | printed p. 56]

#### Objection 5

Romans 5:17: Abraham believed the promise that he would become the father of
many nations, although he was sterile and old, and he was justified by this
faith. But here he believed not special mercy but omnipotence. Therefore the
conclusion follows.

Response 1: Abraham believed before that promise was made, Hebrews 11:8, and
he received other promises which he believed.

2. This very promise regards special mercy in Christ, who was to arise from
Abraham. The minor premise is therefore false. Finally, by faith he believed
this promise, but not this promise alone.

#### Objection 6

If special mercy were to be believed, it would have been mentioned in the
Apostles’ Creed. But not a word concerning special mercy appears in the Creed.
Therefore the conclusion follows.

Response 1: the minor premise is false. When it says, “I believe in God the
Father and the Son,” and so forth, it means not merely believing that the
Father created everything but not you, and that Christ died for the world but
not for you, but that he did these things for you as well as for the rest.
Thus Thomas Aquinas rightly teaches that there are three acts of faith: (1) “I
believe God”—that is, that he is powerful, just, and so forth; (2) “I believe
God”—that is, I believe that the things he says are true; and (3) “I believe
in God”—that is, I receive myself into God’s faith and acknowledge him as my
Father in Christ. This signifies the application of special mercy. So too
David in Psalm

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 59 | image 15575031 | printed p. 57]

22:1. Bellarmine therefore says falsely that the meaning of the article “I
believe the remission of sins” is not “I believe that my sins have been
forgiven,” but “I confess that in the catholic church there is the gift of the
remission of sins, which is received through baptism and the other
sacraments.”

#### Objection 7

As a cause, justifying faith must precede justification. But faith in special
mercy follows justification. Therefore faith in special mercy is not
justifying faith.

Response 1: as to the major premise, it is true that faith precedes
justification in the order of causes, but not in time, just as many causes
exist simultaneously with their effects.

2. The minor premise is false. Faith in special mercy is prior to
justification in the order of causes but simultaneous with it in time. For
when the faith by which mercy is specially applied is given, God then makes
and pronounces us righteous on account of Christ’s merit apprehended by faith.

#### Objection 8

What removes prayer, sacraments, and good works, and what produces carnal
security, is not justifying faith but something that hinders justification.
Faith in special mercy is of this kind. Therefore the conclusion follows.
Proof of the minor: if I believe that my sins have been forgiven in Christ,
why do I receive baptism, or why do I ask for the remission of sins in prayer?

Response 1: it is true that a doctrine which does these things by its own
nature is exceedingly wicked, but not one which does so not of itself but only
accidentally, because of the fault of people who abuse it. In this sense the
gospel is called

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 60 | image 15575032 | printed p. 58]

an aroma of death, and Christ a stone of stumbling.

2. The minor premise is utterly false, and its contrary is true. Those who are
justified by faith also receive the Spirit of God and are trained for every
good work.

3. Because we judge concerning justification and salvation from their
effects, it is necessary that we discern and preserve the effects in
ourselves. The more we discern them, the more spiritual security concerning
justification and salvation arises. And although justification is perfect
with respect to God, it has its degrees with respect to our perception and
application. Therefore, so that it may be perfected in its degrees, prayers
must be continual and every aid for strengthening faith must be employed.

A comparison: we are alive and the end of our life is certain. Does it
therefore follow that we ought not to preserve our life? Finally, sin must be
avoided because God is offended by it, the Holy Spirit is grieved, and bodily
afflictions are also acquired.

#### Objection 9

From Job 9:20–21 and 31:14.

Response: these passages teach nothing other than that our salvation is not
placed in our own righteousness, because our righteousness cannot endure
God’s judgment. Therefore nothing follows from a passage badly understood.

#### Objection 10

Proverbs 20:9: no one can say that he is pure from sin. Therefore believers
cannot be certain of the remission of sins.

Response 1: the words are cited badly, for even by Bellarmine’s testimony they
must be read in the active sense. Therefore this does not follow: “No one can
say that he has cleansed his heart; therefore we are not certain of the
remission of sins.”

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 61 | image 15575033 | printed p. 59]

2. Although we cannot say that we are clean by inherent righteousness, we are
nevertheless clean in Christ and can accordingly be certain of the remission
of sins. It is indeed true, if we regard our own righteousness, that in this
life we are not pure from sin with respect to its inherence, but we are pure
with respect to its guilt.

#### Objection 11

Proverbs 28:14: if those who fear are blessed, then we ought not to be certain
of our righteousness and life. But the former is true from the cited passage.
Therefore the conclusion follows.

Response 1: I deny the major premise. Fear is twofold: one kind is servile and
the other filial. The passage speaks about filial fear, which ought to be in
all the faithful lest they offend God. Two vices, however, are forbidden in
the cited passage: carnal security, which scorns God’s wrath against sins, and
confidence in one’s own wisdom or righteousness. Fear which is opposed to
hardness is commended. For the aim of this and similar passages is not that
believers should be cast into doubt concerning salvation, but that they
should keep their infirmity before their eyes, depend wholly upon God’s mercy,
and not become insolent through confidence in their gifts.

2. Fear in the faithful is not opposed to confidence, because they do not
concern the same thing. The certainty belonging to confidence concerns God’s
favor, about which they do not doubt, while doubt and fear concern themselves.
For when the faithful look at themselves, they fear lest they offend God and
grieve the Holy Spirit. These two things can therefore exist together

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 62 | image 15575034 | printed p. 60]

in the faithful. Thus something like a twofold person must be considered in
each believer, as in Romans 7; 1 Peter 8:10.

#### Objection 12

From Philippians 2:12; 1 Peter 1:17; Romans 11:20; 1 John 4:18: if we ought
always to fear, we cannot be certain of salvation. But the former is true from
the cited passages. Therefore the conclusion follows.

Response 1: I deny the major premise, because doubt concerning God’s grace is
not commanded, but distrust of our own powers, fear of sinning, diligence in
avoiding falls, and diligence in serving God. Carnal security is avoided in
such a way that, with respect to God, his promises are to be trusted
fearlessly, Philippians 1:6. The fear which is commanded is filial and ought
to be joined with faith, as in Psalm 2:11; 1 Peter 1:18.

2. In the passage from Romans, fear is opposed to pride. Paul addresses
boastful hypocrites and warns them by the example of the Jews not to exalt
themselves.

3. In the passage from John, he speaks about the servile fear of unbelief,
which is not in love.

#### Objection 13

Ecclesiastes 9:1: if a person does not know whether he is worthy of hatred or
love, then he cannot be certain of special mercy and salvation. But the former
is true from the cited passage. Therefore the conclusion follows.

Response 1: the translation is false.

2. This is the fallacy of arguing from a qualified statement to an unqualified
one. The passage says only that a person cannot know God’s love or hatred from
external things, since external things come upon both the ungodly and the
godly; indeed, the ungodly very often fare better in this life. But this does
not remove the certainty of special mercy. As to how these external things are
to be judged, see John 4:9; John 17:23; Ephesians 2:4; Psalm 73:17. Statements
of this kind are often found in that book, teaching that with respect to
external things there is no distinction between the godly and the ungodly, as
in Ecclesiastes 3:9.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 63 | image 15575035 | printed p. 61]

#### Objection 14

From Psalm 19:13: those who have hidden sins ought to fear lest they are
blinded and do not see their sins, as David’s example shows. Therefore no one
can be certain of the remission of sins.

Response: I deny the consequence, because all sins, whether hidden or public,
are forgiven in Christ. Nor does this follow: “David asks to be cleansed from
hidden sins; therefore he fears lest he be blinded and could not be certain of
remission.” Rather, because he prays in faith, he was certain of remission.
Finally, this fear does not remove filial confidence, as was explained above.

Instance: the person who does not know every offense does not know whether he
pleases God. But no one knows every offense. Therefore the conclusion follows.

Response: if known and unknown offenses were not forgiven in Christ, or if
remission depended upon our worthiness or contrition, the proposition would
be true. But all sins have been forgiven, Romans 8:1, and remission depends
upon Christ alone. The proposition is therefore false.

#### Objection 15

From 1 Corinthians 4:4: if we are certain of salvation, then we judge
ourselves. But in the cited passage we ought not to judge ourselves. Therefore
the conclusion follows.

Response: I deny the connection. God is the one who judges us by his verdict,
while through the Holy Spirit we perceive the testimony of this verdict in
our hearts, Romans 8:16.

2. Paul is arguing against the perverse judgments of the Corinthians, who
condemned his doctrine in his absence. He teaches that in the discharge of
his office he is conscious of no crime, yet he does not assume the authority
to justify himself but leaves judgment to God. Therefore he is speaking not
about the judgment or righteousness of his person, but of his office.

3. Suppose that he is speaking about personal righteousness. He still does
not doubt his justification before God,

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 64 | image 15575036 | printed p. 62]

but says that he is not justified by works. Although he is conscious of no
crime before the Corinthians, nevertheless he cannot stand before God’s
judgment.

4. This does not follow: “We ought not to judge others; therefore we ought not
to acknowledge the testimony of the Holy Spirit and of our conscience
concerning a judgment approved by God.”

#### Objection 16

Bellarmine, book 3 of *On Justification*, chapter 5, calls this a theological
demonstration. No one is justified without faith and repentance, Deuteronomy
4:29; 2 Chronicles 6:36; Isaiah 1:17–18; Ezekiel 18:27. But no one knows with
the certainty of faith that he possesses faith and repentance, because
nothing is found in Scripture concerning our particular faith and repentance.
Therefore no one is certain with the certainty of faith.

Response 1: I deny the minor premise. Every promise pertains both to all and
to each individual, and the person who believes and comes to his senses knows
that he believes when he applies the promises to himself.

2. The measure of faith and repentance is not prescribed by God’s word.
Rather, their faith and repentance—or their sufficiency—consists not in their
measure but in their truth. But if justification took place on account of the
merit and perfection of faith and repentance, as the Roman Catholics
maintain, then faith and repentance would have to be complete in every degree.
That does not occur in this life, and consequently there would be no
justification. But justification takes place solely on account of Christ’s
merit.

#### Objection 17

The person who does not know the mind of the Lord cannot be certain of
salvation. But we do not know the mind of the Lord, Romans 11:34; Isaiah 40:16.
Therefore the conclusion follows.

Response: it is true that the person who knows neither God’s revealed nor his
secret will cannot be certain of salvation. But in the gospel we know his
revealed will concerning grace and salvation, 2 Corinthians 3:14. Therefore
we can be certain of it.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 65 | image 15575037 | printed p. 63]

The cited passages, however, concern the secret will. Thus the minor premise
is false.

#### Objection 18

Only the one who is the *kardiognōsēs*, the knower of hearts, knows faith and
repentance and those who will certainly be saved. But God alone is such a one,
not we. Therefore the conclusion follows.

Response 1: the one who is the knower of hearts alone knows these things,
unless he has revealed them to others and made others certain concerning
them. But God reveals this to each of the faithful, 2 Corinthians 13:5; 1 John
3:19.

2. God alone can know *a priori* and immediately, but we know *a posteriori*,
from the effects and mediately.

#### Objection 19

We cannot be certain of election. Therefore we cannot be certain of grace.

Response: I deny the antecedent. If we know that we have been justified, we
also know that we are elect, as the apostle teaches in Romans 8:30. But we know
that we have been justified, as was proved above, and the apostle commands us
to test whether we are in the faith, 2 Corinthians 3:5.

Instance: if our election must be made firm by us, then we cannot be certain
of election. But the former is true from 2 Peter 1:10.

Response 1: the conclusion should rather be that we can be certain if we can
make it firm.

2. If election had to be made firm with respect to God or in itself, it is
true that we could not be certain. But if it is to be made firm with respect
to our perception and that of others, the assertion is false. The apostle
speaks in this second way. From works as testimonies of faith we judge
concerning faith; from the testimony of the Holy Spirit, concerning effectual
calling; and from calling, concerning election, Romans 8:1, 5, 9, 14; 1 John
3:24 and 4:13. The contrary is taught in Matthew 7:16. With respect to God and
in itself, however, election is firm, Romans 9:11 and 11:29.

#### Objection 20

From 1 John 3:21: “If our heart does not condemn us, we have confidence before
God.” Therefore, by the contrary, if our heart condemns us, we do not have

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 66 | image 15575038 | printed p. 64]

confidence. No one is without accusation in his own heart. Therefore we
cannot possess confidence in special mercy.

Response 1: John speaks about sin against conscience. Those who commit it
cannot be certain of the remission of sins, but insofar as they commit this
sin they are condemned by their own conscience. By contrast, a good
conscience is joined with faith and confidence, and the contrary is taught in
1 Timothy 1:18 and 1 John 3:24.

2. The godly are accused by their own heart but are not altogether condemned;
rather, they are absolved. They are indeed terrified when they regard
themselves, but when they regard Christ they possess certain peace and
confidence, Romans 5:1 and 8:1; Ephesians 3:12.

#### Objection 21

The person who does not possess perfect obedience cannot be certain of
salvation. But no one possesses perfect obedience. Therefore the conclusion
follows.

Response 1: I deny the major premise. It would be true if the grace of
justification depended upon the worthiness of our works. But it depends not
upon us but upon Christ’s merit alone. God accepts that merit, and thus we are
certain and have peace.

2. I deny the minor premise. Every faithful person who apprehends Christ by
faith possesses his righteousness, which is perfect; and because they possess
it, they are justified and saved.

#### Objection 22

If the promises have conditions belonging to our powers and depend upon our
works, then we cannot be certain of salvation and the remission of sins but
must doubt, Matthew 5:20, 19:17, and 10:12; Romans 8:17; 1 Timothy 2:5 and 12;
John 15:16. Therefore the conclusion follows.

Response: I deny the minor premise. The promises do not have conditions

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 67 | image 15575039 | printed p. 65]

belonging to our natural powers, but conditions belonging to the Holy Spirit
in us. The cited passages must be explained in this way: God requires duties
from the faithful and gives them the power to perform them—that is, both to
will and to accomplish.

Instance: although there must be no doubt on God’s part, there must be doubt
on our part, because on account of our infirmities we may fail to fulfill the
conditions of the promises.

Response 1: there must be no doubt on account of our infirmity, because the
truth and fulfillment of the promises do not depend upon us, whether worthy
or unworthy, but upon Christ the mediator. Because we are unworthy, he was
appointed mediator, and on his account God is gracious to unworthy sinners.

2. The conditions of the promises are faith, repentance, patience, and so
forth. No determined measure of these things has been established such that a
person who has not attained it cannot determine that he will be saved. These
things are required in each person and are divinely granted according to
measure—greater in some and smaller in others—so that the person who believes
even in the smallest degree possesses life. Progress in faith and repentance
must nevertheless be made and asked from God, Mark 9:24.

### Objections of the devil and the flesh against the certainty of salvation

#### Instance 1, of conscience and the devil

The person who is entirely defiled by sin is not worthy or certain to be
saved, but rather to perish, Romans 5:12; Psalm 143:2. But the consciences of
all testify that they are defiled by sin. Therefore salvation is not to be
certainly expected.

Response: the proposition is true of people as they are by nature, if they
remain such and unless their sins are forgiven on account of Christ. But no
one elect to life

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 68 | image 15575040 | printed p. 66]

remains such. The sins of each of the elect are forgiven on account of
Christ, and each is sanctified by Christ’s Spirit. Finally, none of the
justified are wholly defiled by sins. In justification the guilt of every sin
is removed completely, and in sanctification the inherence of sin is removed
in part. Those who are in Christ, therefore, are not worthy to perish.

#### Instance 2

God, who is supremely just, leaves nothing unpunished. But remnants of sin
with respect to its inherence remain in you. Therefore he will punish you as a
sinner.

Response: it is true that God is supremely just and punishes all our sins
either in us or in another. He punished them in another—namely, Christ, who is
our surety—as in Romans 3:25; 2 Corinthians 6:21; 1 Peter 2:24. Because God is
supremely just, he does not wish the same debt to be paid twice. But Christ
paid it perfectly once, Romans 4:25 and 5:19; Galatians 3:13; 2 Peter 1:24.

#### Instance 3

It is not enough that someone else has made satisfaction for you; it is
required that you yourself keep the law, and the person who does not keep the
law is accursed, Galatians 3:10. But no one can keep the law. Therefore you and
everyone else are accursed.

Response: Christ not only endured the punishments for my sins but also
perfectly fulfilled the law for me, Matthew 3:15. He was made a curse for me
so that he might free me from the curse, Romans 5:8–10; Galatians 3:13. I,
however, appear before God clothed with this righteousness of Christ, and thus
there is no one who can bring a charge against me, Romans 8:33.

#### Instance 4

The law requires your own righteousness and says, “You shall not covet,” and
so forth. Scripture also says, “The soul which sins shall itself die,” Ezekiel
18:18; Romans 2:8. Therefore the conclusion follows.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 69 | image 15575041 | printed p. 67]

Response: but the gospel has revealed this mystery of obtaining salvation in
Christ, through the Son of God himself, who fulfilled the law for me and bore
the punishments of the law which I violated, John 2:2. Therefore Christ
himself is the end of the law, Romans 10:4. He was sent by the Father for this
purpose: that he might relieve from this burden me and others who had been
cast down to hell under the burden of the law, Galatians 4:4–5.

#### Instance 5

Sins which God abhors remain in you, Romans 7:17–18. How, therefore, could you
stand before God?

Response: I have and perceive in myself the struggle between the flesh and
the Spirit, Romans 7:15; Galatians 5:17. But these sins do not enter into the
account before God; they are forgiven because I am grafted into and united to
Christ, Ephesians 4:16; Colossians 2:7, 19; Romans 8:1. Nor do sins reign in
me. Through the Holy Spirit they are gradually purged away—by inception in
this life and by consummation in the life to come.

#### Instance 6

You are subject to afflictions and death, which are testimonies of God’s wrath
and are sent on account of sins, Romans 5:12; Ephesians 5:6. Therefore your
sins have not been forgiven, but God is angry with you.

Response: although afflictions and death entered the world on account of sins,
God does not always send them upon us in order to punish us as a severe judge,
but to discipline and test us as a most merciful Father. In this way our faith
may become known to others and serve as a good example, 1 Peter 2:19 and 3:14;
James 1:2–4. We may also learn to hate sin, despise the world, know ourselves,
and know God, 2 Samuel 7:14; 2 Corinthians 6:9; Hebrews 12:6. He also makes us
conformable to Christ’s image, Romans 8:29. Finally, in us, though weak, he
displays his own ineffable power and the efficacy of his Spirit, since by his

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 70 | image 15575042 | printed p. 68]

power alone we can endure all the gravest things, 2 Corinthians 4:7 and 12:9;
Colossians 1:24. Afflictions therefore do not harm the faithful but greatly
benefit them, and necessarily serve to advance their salvation, Romans 8:28.
Nor is death any longer death to the faithful, but a passage to life, since in
Christ the sting of death has been removed, 1 Corinthians 15:55.

#### Instance 7

Many perish whom Christ’s righteousness does not benefit. But you are no
better than they are. Therefore you will perish with them.

Response: it is true that by nature I am no better than they are, but by God’s
grace I am what I am. Those who are condemned are unbelievers, but I possess
faith by which I apprehend Christ and rest content in him. Therefore I cannot
perish, Romans 8:38.

As to how we know that we possess faith, see the doctrine of faith above.

#### Instance 8

Your faith is weak and often scarcely appears.

Response: it is indeed often weak but always true, and I pray with the man
spoken of in Matthew 9:24, “I believe, Lord; help my weakness.” Even weak faith
apprehends the whole Christ with all his benefits. What is weak in us is
complete and perfect in Christ. Finally, if I possess even the smallest spark
of true faith, I can be certain and secure because the gifts of God are
without repentance, Romans 11:29. Just as fire can exist where there is no
flame, and as trees in winter truly live although they bear neither leaves nor
fruit, so my faith can be true although it does not always appear in its full
vigor.

#### Instance 9

But you were long a hearer of the gospel

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 71 | image 15575043 | printed p. 69]

and yet were not truly called, but believed very late.

Response: a late calling does not remove the certainty of calling. God calls
whom he wishes and when he wishes, as is taught in the parable of the laborers
in Matthew 20. Often clearer, certain testimonies of faith and salvation are
found in those called late than in those called earlier, as can be seen in
Paul and others. Finally, even if I am called with the thief at the very point
of death, I can be certain of salvation.

#### Instance 10

“But,” says Satan, “if sins cannot harm you after you have believed, sin
securely.”

Response: the inference does not follow, because those who are children of
God are led by God’s Spirit and walk according to the Spirit, not according to
the flesh, Romans 8:1, 14. Those who are justified are also sanctified. We
also draw a conclusion concerning our salvation from its effects—that is,
from works, 2 Corinthians 13:7. Finally, sins harm us and are therefore to be
fled, because by them we offend our God, grieve the Holy Spirit, and procure
and acquire temporal afflictions or chastisements for ourselves.

#### Instance 11

To determine certainly that you will be saved is to search God’s hidden
things, which is rash presumption.

Response: to search God’s hidden things *a priori* is rash presumption, but not
to search them *a posteriori*—that is, from God’s word and the effects in us,
and especially from the gospel. In the gospel he has revealed his will
concerning our salvation most fully and plainly: concerning the redemption of
the human race, all the elect being called in time, true faith and repentance
being given through hearing the word, and the fact that all and each who
believe will be saved. I know with even greater certainty that I am one of
their number.

#### Instance 12

Arrogance is a vice displeasing to God, but humility pleases God. To determine
that you are certain of salvation is the greatest arrogance, while to doubt
is modesty. Therefore the conclusion follows.

Response: I deny the minor premise. True humility consists in despairing of
one’s own powers, resting securely in God’s mercy, and saying with the
prophet, “Do not enter into judgment with your servant, for no one living is
righteous before you.” Indeed, not to rest securely in God’s promises is the
greatest arrogance and contempt for the divine majesty.

#### Instance 13

Humility consists in acknowledging your sins and infirmity.

Response: the acknowledgment of sin does not remove faith and confidence in
God’s mercy and salvation. True humility consists not only in acknowledging
sin and infirmity, but also in fleeing to Christ and resting securely in his
most perfect satisfaction.
