[Marginalia: Of the apostles.]

As for the apostles and their testimonies, two things are worthy of note.

First, in most of Paul's epistles, after explaining the doctrine of the gospel in their earlier chapters and vindicating it from the corruptions of false apostles and others, he treats holiness of life and conformity to the divine law in the later chapters. By this he would openly show that obedience to the law is not opposed to the grace of the gospel but harmonizes with it most amicably, and would especially set this truth in a clear light.

Second, it should also be observed that if the apostles had gone about abrogating the law, they would readily have admitted this when their adversaries charged them with it. Like the Antinomians, they would also have had an easy answer ready: that the law—namely, the moral law—had been abrogated for them and that they were held bound only by the law of the Spirit and of love, so that they might behave less wickedly. But nothing of the kind ever escaped those vigilant men, such as our own dreamers, to speak with Jude, babble and openly hawk about.

[1679 new-series fol. 32 | IA leaf 352]

Indeed, on the contrary:

First, they recoil from and detest this impious invention. “Do we then make the law void through faith? By no means.” God forbid. Romans 3:31. For although the expression “having abolished” occurs concerning the ceremonial law in Ephesians 2:15, and concerning the moral law with respect to its former administration we read “that which is being abolished” in 2 Corinthians 3:11, nevertheless concerning the law itself, insofar as it is the covenant of works or insofar as justification might be expected from it, Romans 7:6 says, “we have been discharged from the law.” At least some expound that passage in this way. Yet there is another and preferable exposition given by others: they maintain that the law from which the apostle there says we have been freed is not God's law but the law of sin.

[Marginalia: See Beza on the passage.]

But as for the law being rendered null and void by the doctrine of faith or the grace of the gospel insofar as it is the rule of life and conduct, Christ repels even the very first Antinomianizing thought of this sort with “Do not think,” and the apostle abominates it categorically with “By no means.”

Second, on the contrary, they openly proclaim and defend the law's authority: “Rather, we establish the law.” For according to the doctrine of faith, as regards our justification, the righteousness of the law has been fulfilled in every respect by both the active and passive obedience of our surety. As regards the holiness that inheres within us, it has indeed been begun in us here and is at last to be perfected in heaven.

Third, they exhort us to render the obedience that is due, and do so by the force of the law and the authority of the lawgiver. “Owe no one anything except this, that you love one another. For he who loves another has fulfilled the law,” Romans 13:8. “Through love serve one another, for the whole law is fulfilled in one saying”—namely, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself,” Galatians 5:13–14. “Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right. Honor your father and mother, which is the first commandment with a promise,” Ephesians 6:1–2. And there are other passages of the same kind. These are indeed so compelling and invincible that Suarez himself, who tries to evade other testimonies, willingly yields the victory to these and confesses himself defeated.
