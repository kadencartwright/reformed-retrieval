It is asked whether the Word of God is the one and only instrument, both
preparatory and consummating, in generating faith, so that the Remonstrants
speak truly in the *Acts of the Synod of Dort*, articles 3 and 4, pages 62 and
128: that no immediate action of the Holy Spirit upon the mind and will is
required to produce faith, but the objective, moral, persuasive action of the
Word alone is sufficient.

We acknowledge that faith comes from hearing. Yet at the same time we also
believe that the Holy Spirit has certain immediate actions upon the mind and
will, and that these are not moral but real and physical actions, by which he
creates a supernatural power and habit in us through infusion.

1. For God does not draw people to himself by moral allurement alone, nor are
supernatural powers of believing infused merely by persuasion. An orator, by
declaiming, cannot restore the power of walking to the lame or an unimpaired
faculty of sight to the blind, nor can he raise the dead.

2. If the Word is the one and only consummating instrument of faith, no
actions nobler than those which can be performed by a pastor are required,
which is absurd.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 548 | PDF p. 599]

3. For God alone, through efficacious grace, brings about willing and doing.
But if there is no action here more eminent than the moral operation of the
Word, God through his grace brings about willing and doing in such a way that
ultimately it lies with the free will to will in response to God as he works,
to resist, and stubbornly to refuse.

4. God's action of generating faith is such an act of omnipotence as that by
which God raised Christ from the dead, Ephesians 1:19–20, and is not inferior
to an act of creation, 2 Corinthians 4:6. It is therefore irresistible. But if
the whole matter is accomplished only by the moral action of the Word, both
preparatory and consummating, there will be no action of omnipotence here.

5. Nor do we acknowledge anything enthusiastic here, although there is an act
of omnipotence which the moral causation of the Word cannot reach. For, first,
enthusiasm exists when inspiration is expected apart from every action of the
Word of God. Here we teach that the moral action of the Word is necessary,
although we require a more eminent action—namely, a real and physical one.
Second, enthusiasm exists when something to be done apart from or contrary to
God's Word is expected. But we expect the same infusion of the Spirit of
regeneration and bestowal of a new heart that God promised us in the Gospel
covenant, and not a different one.
