Martinez, in the same place, section 8, number 26: “The Fathers more than once
come upon an immediate and partial power of nature for supernatural acts. A
human will does something in fulfilling the precepts, just as grace does, and
indeed immediately, just as grace does. Otherwise grace alone would
immediately do the whole. Augustine, *On Rebuke and Grace*, chapter 2: ‘They
are acted upon so that they may act.’ Number 38: ‘Therefore, just as the
immediate and native activity of the eyes renders a human being capable of
seeing light, even

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 469 | PDF p. 520 | split word joined: absente]

in the absence of light, so the native faculty of the will renders a human
being capable of preserving rectitude even in the absence of grace. Yet there
is this difference: light, which completes the eyes' immediate activity, is
owed to the eyes; grace, by which the power to preserve rectitude is
completed, is not owed to a human being. Neither the will alone nor grace alone
is the adequate cause of salvation.”

Reply 1. Grace alone acts immediately in the genus of efficient cause—causing,
determining, inwardly teaching, and effecting the willing. The will alone also
acts in the genus of vital cause, formally and freely willing. Thus neither
one alone does the whole.

2. If a native faculty renders a human being capable of preserving rectitude
even in the absence of grace, then a human being not drawn by the Father—that
is, in the absence of grace—is capable of coming to Christ, that is, of
believing. What else did Pelagius teach? Nor does light add any real power to
the eyes.

Martinez, section 9, number 40: “If ‘to help’ denotes an immediate action in
God, it will also denote one in the human being. A purely remote cause does not
help.” He answers, “It does something, not the whole.” Thomas, 1, p. 9:23,
article 8, to the last argument and 2: “Someone is helped by another in two
ways. 1. Insofar as he receives strength from him, and thus to be helped
belongs to the weak. And thus, ‘Who helps the Spirit of the Lord?’—that is, no
one. 2. Someone is said to be helped by another through whom he executes his
operation, as a master does through a servant. In this way God is helped by us
insofar as

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 470 | PDF p. 521 | split word joined: exequimur]

we execute God's ordinance. For we are God's helpers,” 1 Corinthians 3:9.

Reply: Thomas speaks about God insofar as he uses means, namely, about natural
help, not the help of grace, which is the present question. For in believing
we do not help God or God's grace, nor do we furnish the help of grace. Thus
we love God in our heavenly country and enjoy God. Yet would we be said to
help God in vital actions of this kind? Certainly not.

Martinez, section 10, number 44: “Ownership in a good work does not belong to
a human being by reason of undergoing, as this or that color is called a human
being's own, but by reason of acting, namely, by title of the dominion of
liberty. John 15: ‘And that your fruit may remain.’ Revelation 2: ‘I know your
works.’ Augustine, *To Simplicianus*, book 1, question 2: ‘That we should will,
he willed to be both his and ours—his by calling, ours by following.’ The
ownership of grace, or of God as supernatural helper toward a saving act,
shared with the human being, testifies to an immediate action and power of
God's helps distinct from the power of free Choice. 2. If free Choice does not
flow into the act immediately through itself but through grace, by grace's
immediate action alone, no other ownership or possession in a work of piety
can belong to free Choice by title of a definite action than that which
belongs to grace. Therefore free Choice itself, as distinct from grace, can by
no right of ownership share a supernatural act

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 471 | PDF p. 522]

with divine grace. But just as the whole action and the whole power belong to
grace alone, and belong to free Choice by denomination from grace only, so the
whole ownership and possession will belong to grace alone and to free Choice
only through the possession and ownership of grace.”

Reply: Thus the Jesuits strive to divide the glory of Conversion between God
and a wretched human being, so that they begrudge God and God's grace the sole
glory. But one thing is a half share in the action, another a half share in the
glory. Likewise, one thing is the vital act of believing from created free
Choice, and quite another the act of conversion through the efficacious
calling of grace. For this argument does not prove that ownership of the
action of knocking through inward grace belongs to free Choice, while the
action of opening through the same inward grace belongs both to Grace and, in
a divided share, to the creature also. Unless that is proved, it does not
follow that part of the glory of conversion belongs to God's Grace and part
to the creature's free Choice. Rather, the glory is to be ascribed wholly and
entirely to God.

2. The ownership of an action is one thing physically and another morally.
With respect to the former, the Jesuits must certainly concede that the
physical ownership of the actions of plowing, speaking, walking, and even
hoping is divided between God and the human being. Yet they by no means prove
that the moral glory of such actions is thus to be divided. Indeed, we do not
deny that free Choice immediately and actively flows into the act

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 472 | PDF p. 523]

of believing, but as moved, bent, and determined by inward grace, which brings
about willing and working. Yet from this it does not follow that the moral
ownership of the action of believing is divided between God and creatures.

3. Nor do we deny that the act of believing is ours and that some moral
ownership is to be ascribed even to free Choice. For Christ praises the faith
of the Canaanite woman, the Centurion, Abraham, and others, and rewards it.

Martinez, section 11, number 47: “Grace is not the total but a partial cause of
the supernatural act. 1 Corinthians 15:10: ‘Not I, but the grace of God with
me.’ Bernard calls us fellow soldiers and Jerome companions with God in the
supernatural work.”

Reply: And Calvin calls us partners in labor with God. Yet because the
principal causality lies with God as he efficaciously calls and brings about
that we will and believe—even though we, and we alone, are the ones who will
and believe—God's grace is therefore rightly called the adequate and complete
cause that dominates, determines, and effects the willing. Here free Choice
has no fellowship, although in another genus it has partnership: as a moved
and efficaciously determined cause, it has a partial subordinate influence and
a partial lower activity, which Our theologians by no means deny. Nor does the
habit alone do the whole. Indeed, the habit is not illuminated as the
intellect is, not aroused as the will is, nor does the habit

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 473 | PDF p. 524]

perceive the supernatural object. The habit does not formally choose, reject,
will, desire, or suspend an act; nor is the supernatural habit a vital
principle. Rather, free Choice alone is such a principle, by which a living
being moves itself from within. Nor is the supernatural habit as such free;
it only helps free Choice.

It is asked: Is the whole gracious operation of God which the Holy Spirit uses
in converting a human being merely moral, so that God acts here only by
inviting, persuading with reasons, and enticing both with threats and with
promises and commands? Or does God act through the real and physical
efficiency of grace, by infusing a habit of grace and physically and really
predetermining the will? The Arminians complain of the wrong we have done them
by teaching that they hold the whole business of conversion to be accomplished
by a suasive and moral action. Thus Corvinus, *Against Molina*, chapter 45,
sections 2–3 and 7, and Gevinch., page 301. But the following makes clear
that this is their meaning. 1. In chapter 10, 11, section 4 of their
*Confession*, they require only two things for engendering faith: 1. Probable
arguments. 2. Docility. And in the *Hague Conference*, page 122, they say it
can be disputed whether persuasion is not the noblest and most fitting action
of God which he can use in converting a human being. They also teach that
willing people are made from unwilling people by enticement alone. And
Arminius, *Against Perkins*, pages 751 and 770, §, acknowledges in the
Father's drawing no

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 474 | PDF p. 525 | split word joined: nullam]

other action than persuasion. Next, they acknowledge no action administered
through the Word other than suasive action, “for the nature of the Word,” they
say, “is to act only suasively.” We maintain the contrary.

1. If God acted here only suasively, God's action in converting would be no
greater than a preacher's, Cicero's, or Demosthenes', and indeed no greater
than the Devil's. Pastors would thus be said to create a new heart in us, make
the dead alive, and regenerate the carnal through the mere letter of the Law
and Gospel. This is what Pelagius taught, as Augustine says in *On Grace,
against Pelagius and Celestius*, chapter 3.

2. God infuses the Spirit of supplication, Zechariah 12:10, gives a new heart
and removes the stony one, Ezekiel 36:26, and makes us alive, Ephesians 2:4–5.
Therefore, he does not act by moral action alone, because life is never
infused by merely moral action.

3. Persuasion adds and infuses no inward powers into the faculties of the soul
and corrupt free Choice. For by speeches, however golden, you will not restore
sight to a blind person, the power of walking to a lame person, or wealth to a
poor person.

4. To suppose that God regenerates in no way other than by morally persuading
also supposes that the Intellect, Will, and Affections are corrupted by nature
in no way other than through the mind's mere darkening and the lack of written
revelation, and that our inability to perform supernatural acts—Faith and

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 475 | PDF p. 526 | split word joined: resipiscentiam]

repentance—is no different from the natural inability of a human being to
learn the Mathematical disciplines and Metaphysics.

5. It would be as easy for us to be converted as for a drunkard to embrace
Cicero's counsel concerning drunkenness.

6. A person who embraces moral counsel is a more principal and efficacious
cause of the duty advised, when he performs it, than the person who counsels
him. Thus no other glory for conversion would be owed to God than for the
discovery of the Gospel, while the glory of Faith and of embracing the Gospel
would be owed to free Choice alone.

7. No one who persuades has dominion over the will. Therefore, if God is only
a persuader here, God will not be Lord of conversion and salvation, nor will
it lie with him that these people are converted rather than more people or
others.

8. I pray in vain for the grace of one who only persuades, who is a bare
persuader, so that I may obey his persuasion, since it lies with me alone to
obey or not obey.

It is asked: Following Bellarmine's *On Grace and Free Choice*, book 1, chapter
13, do the Arminians ascribe enough to God because they attribute to human
beings and the Devil an outward persuasion proceeding from the letter alone,
but to God an inward persuasion by which he always infuses light and breathes
in the beginning of a good will? Suárez likewise makes the distinction in *On
the Helps of Divine Grace*,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 476 | PDF p. 527]

book 3, chapter 15, number 17. We deny that enough is ascribed to God here.

1. If this infusion of light is objective and comes from the letter of
persuasion alone, it is merely outward persuasion, as they call it. But if
this infusion is the real bestowal of a new power upon free Choice, then it is
not persuasion but a physical action and more than persuasion. For no
persuasion infuses new powers into us.

2. Pelagius acknowledged inward persuasion in this way. Augustine, *Against
Pelagius and Celestius*, book 1, chapter 7: “He helps us through his teaching
and revelation when he opens the eyes of our heart—when he illuminates us by
the ineffable gift of heavenly grace—when by the revelation of wisdom he
arouses the stupefied will to desire God.” Indeed, gold inwardly entices the
thief's mind in this way, but only objectively.

It is asked: Does God also inwardly call all those whom he outwardly calls, so
that there is in them a power to obey if they will? The Remonstrants say yes;
we deny it.

1. God outwardly called the Jews through the Word, signs, and trials, “and yet
he did not give them a heart to know, ears to hear, or eyes to see, to this
day,” Deuteronomy 29:3–4. That is, he did not call them with an inward calling.

2. In John 12:37 God outwardly called the Jews through Christ's preaching and
excellent miracles, but by no means inwardly. Verse 39: “For they could not
believe, because God blinded their eyes.”

3. In 1 Corinthians 2:14 the natural person is outwardly

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 477 | PDF p. 528 | split word joined: vocatur]

called, otherwise he would not regard the Gospel as foolishness. But he is not
inwardly called, because he cannot understand the things of the Spirit of God.

4. All who have heard from the Father are inwardly called and come to Christ.
But most of those who hear God calling outwardly through preachers are not
inwardly called and do not come to Christ. Therefore, and so forth.

5. Some people hear the mysteries of Christ, but they are not revealed to
them because God has not inwardly revealed them, and because “no one knows the
Father except the one to whom the Son reveals him,” Matthew 11:25–27.
Therefore, some are outwardly called who are not inwardly called.

6. Christ preached is a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Greeks.
Therefore, they are outwardly called. But to those called—namely,
inwardly—both Jews and Greeks, Christ is God's power and wisdom, 1 Corinthians
1:23–24.

7. Those who at the preached Word are more insensible than stone and have
made their faces harder than adamant, Jeremiah 5:8; who are as incapable of
the Word as infants scarcely weaned from milk, Isaiah 28:9; who are blinded, 2
Corinthians 4:4; hardened, Matthew 13:15; and who mock the Word that outwardly
calls, Acts 17:32; Acts 26:24; and 2 Peter 3:1–2—those whose heart is covered
with a veil because they are destitute of the Spirit of the Lord who removes
the veil, 2 Corinthians 3:14–16—are outwardly called but not inwardly.
