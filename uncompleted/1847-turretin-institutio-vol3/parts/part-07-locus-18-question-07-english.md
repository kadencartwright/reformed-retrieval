QUESTION SEVEN

ON THE INVISIBILITY OF THE CHURCH

Is the true church rightly called invisible? We affirm against the papists.

[Marginalia: Origin of the question.]

I. In order to state this intensely disputed question properly, the origin of
the controversy must be examined, and the occasion from which it first seems
to have arisen must be investigated. The papists suppose that we call the
church invisible in order to answer the question they raised against us:
where was our church before Luther? They think that because we could not show
that our church had been continuously visible in the world, we therefore
devised this distinction and said that one church is visible and another
invisible. But they interpret our position wrongly. For even if our church
had formerly been as numerous and conspicuous as it is now, we would
nevertheless say that the catholic church is invisible. That other question
belongs to another controversy, concerning the church’s perpetual eminence
and conspicuousness with respect to its outward state, which will be treated
in what follows.

II. The controversy’s true origin, then, must be traced to the following
event. At the beginning of the Reformation the papists debated Luther and
others from the Scriptures concerning indulgences, justification, and human
satisfactions—the subjects on which the question was first raised. Because
they could not easily extricate themselves, they wished to drag their
opponents before the church’s tribunal so that those questions might be
decided by its pronouncement. But they acknowledged no church other than a
visible assembly outwardly professing the faith, consisting of bishops,
clerics, and others over whom the pope presided as head. Luther not only
refused to admit this, but also denied that such people either were or ought
to be called the church. The true church, he maintained, must not be measured
by outward profession or subjection to the Roman pontiff, but solely by
inward faith and piety, which cannot fall under the senses.

Bellarmine acknowledges this in book 3, chapter 2, *On the Church Militant*:
“When Erasmus objected to Luther in book 4, *On the Bondage of the Will*, that
it was not credible that God had forsaken the church for so long, Luther
answered that God had never forsaken the church, but that what is commonly
called the church—that is, the pope and bishops—was not Christ’s church.
Rather, the church was certain few godly people whom God preserves as
remnants.” From this arose the Question concerning the church’s invisibility.

III. Once the distinction between the church’s inward and outward states has
been asserted, and the church’s constituent parts have been established as
only the godly and faithful who are elect and called, this question might seem
less necessary. For if it is true that only the faithful constitute the
church, and they are known to God alone and cannot be known certainly and
individually by anyone else, it is clear that the church is rightly called
invisible. Bellarmine admits this in book 3, chapter 10, *On the Church*: “If
those who lack inward faith are not and cannot be in the church, there will no
longer be any question between us and the heretics concerning the church’s
invisibility,” because, as he immediately adds, “No one can know with
certainty who are truly righteous and godly among so many who outwardly
display righteousness and piety.” Nevertheless, because this is one of the
most serious questions discussed here and can assume several different
relations, it must be treated distinctly.

[Marginalia: State of the question.]

IV. Before everything else, it must be observed here that the distinction
between the church’s visibility and invisibility is derived from the twofold
calling directed to it—the outward calling through the Word and the inward
calling through the Spirit—and from the twofold form that consequently
emerges, one inward and essential, the other outward and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 30 | IA leaf 64 | PDF p. 64]

accidental. With respect to the outward calling and form, namely, the
preaching of the Word and the profession of faith, the church certainly
comes within the senses and is called visible. But the same church is
invisible with respect to the inward calling and mystical communion with
Christ through faith. It cannot fall under the senses, but is known to God
alone, who knows those who are his.

It is therefore evident that the distinction between visible and invisible is
not the division of a genus into species, as though we fashioned two species
of churches opposed to each other, as our opponents attach to us by slander.
It is only a qualification of the subject according to its different
relations. Just as the very same man can be called invisible and visible *in
different respects*, so the same church is rightly called visible with
respect to its outward form and invisible with respect to its inward form.
Meanwhile, because the visible church of the called also contains ungodly
people and hypocrites who are not in the church of the elect, the designation
*visible* extends more broadly than *invisible*, because many are called but
few are chosen (Matthew 20:16).

V. To perceive this more clearly, we must see in how many ways the church can
be called visible or invisible. 1. The church can be called visible with
respect to its material, or the people who constitute it, since they come
within sight. 2. It can be called visible with respect to the outward form,
namely, the preaching of the word and the administration of the sacraments,
which is also presented to the outward senses in particular assemblies. 3.
It can be called visible with respect to the splendor with which it shines
and is rendered conspicuous and illustrious in the world.

Conversely, it is called invisible: 1. With respect to its inward form and
the faithful as such who constitute the church. They do not come within the
senses but are known to God alone, namely, by certain and distinct knowledge
of the individuals. 2. With respect to catholicity. Although particular
churches are visible, the catholic church, insofar as it denotes the
collection of all the churches in the world, can never be seen in its entirety
at once—neither as regards the part already triumphant nor as regards the part
that is still to come. 3. With respect to dispersion and decline, whether
because rising errors obscure its purity, or because raging persecutions
disturb and take away its peace and liberty, when the days of Elijah arrive.
In those days the godly are forced to flee into the wilderness and hide
there, cannot be recognized by anyone, and religion seems to be altogether in
exile. The first invisibility is properly called invisibility of form or
essence; the second, invisibility of parts; and the third, invisibility of
marks and sacred exercises.

VI. With these points established, the state of the question is easily
gathered. First, the question here does not concern visibility or invisibility
of the third order, which concerns visible splendor or the church’s
concealment and dispersion. This belongs to another question concerning the
church’s perpetual splendor and eminence, which concerns its outward state
and will be examined later. Second, the question also does not concern the
invisibility of the parts and of people as such. No one denies that those
parts are sometimes visible and sometimes invisible according to the
difference in their condition, or that the people as such who constitute the
church are always visible.

The question instead concerns the first kind of invisibility, which regards
the faithful as such, and regards people not merely as those who constitute
the church materially, but considered precisely insofar as they constitute it
formally. It does not concern the outward form, which consists in the
profession of faith and use of the sacraments and which we freely confess to
be visible by its nature. It concerns the inward and essential form. Thus the
question is reduced to these terms: Is the church that composes Christ’s
mystical body rightly called invisible—not with the invisibility of parts,
material, or marks, but with the invisibility of its inward form and as such?
The papists deny this. Bellarmine says in book 3, chapter 2, *On the Church
Militant*: “The church is an assembly of people as visible and palpable as
the assembly of the Roman people, the kingdom of France, or the republic of
Venice.” Hence they customarily denounce the invisible church as a Platonic
idea, a mere fiction, or a

[1847 vol. III printed p. 31 | IA leaf 65 | PDF p. 65]

chimera of the Protestants. We, however, affirm it, led by the strongest
reasons.

[Marginalia: The invisibility of the church is proved. 1. From the nature of
the church, which consists of the faithful alone.]

VII. The first reason is drawn from all those passages and arguments by which
we proved in Question III that the church properly so called consists only of
the elect and faithful. For since they as such are not visible, the church
itself, which is constituted from them, also cannot be visible in this sense,
but only invisible.

[Marginalia: 2. From Romans 2:28.]

VIII. Second, the same thing is confirmed from Romans 2:28: “For a true Jew
is not one openly, but in secret; nor is true circumcision that which is in
the flesh and letter, but the circumcision of the heart which is in the
spirit.” Just as the apostle signifies a member of the true church by *Jew*,
as elsewhere he signifies the same by *Israel*, *children of Abraham*, and
*circumcision* (Romans 9:6; Galatians 6:16; Philippians 3:3), so when he says
that the true Jew is not the one who is one openly but the one “in secret,” he
indicates that the truth of Judaism is not to be sought from lineage or an
outward profession before men, but from the inward faith and piety of the
heart, which is manifest to God alone and not to men. Nor is the true,
saving, and God-pleasing circumcision the circumcision of the flesh, which is
nothing but an empty outward ceremony, but the invisible, inward
circumcision of the heart which is effected through the spirit, and which God
primarily and for its own sake commands and approves (Deuteronomy 10:16;
Jeremiah 4:4).

This is confirmed from 1 Peter 3:4, where the apostle calls the gift of
regeneration with which the faithful ought to be adorned “the hidden man of
the heart, which consists in the incorruption of a gentle and peaceful
spirit,” that is, in the never-perishing excellence of holiness and Christian
virtues. This is contrasted with gold, silver, costly clothes, and other
transient and corruptible things in which worldly women seek their adornment.
He adds that it is “the hidden man of the heart” because the truth of faith
and regeneration is situated in the heart and can be known to none but God
and the believer.

The statement about giving the conqueror a white stone and inscribing on it a
“new name” “which no one knows except the one who receives it” pertains to the
same point (Revelation 2:17). For just as that new name is nothing other than
the name and dignity of the children of God (John 1:12; Romans 8:16; 1 John
3:1), so when it is said that no one knows it except the one who receives it,
its invisibility is indicated plainly enough. This is confirmed from 2
Timothy 2:19, where God is said to “know those who are his,” that is, certainly
and infallibly. This does not belong to human beings except according to the
judgment of charity, in which they can often be mistaken.

[Marginalia: 3. From the church’s form and attributes.]

IX. Third, all the things that constitute the church properly so called are
inward and invisible: election and effectual calling, union with Christ, the
Spirit, faith, regeneration, the inscription of the law on hearts, and
rational and spiritual worship. Hence the church is described as the bride
of Christ, who is “beautiful and glorious within” (Psalm 45:14), that is, who
does not shine with gold, gems, and outward ornaments, but with faith, piety,
love, and the other virtues that do not fall under the senses.

It is described as a *spiritual house and tabernacle in the spirit*
(Ephesians 2:21; 1 Peter 2:5), not because the church consists only of
spirits, but because the people who compose it do not constitute it simply as
human beings visible to bodily eyes, but as spiritual people regenerated by
the Spirit who do not fall under the senses. It is described as *holy
Jerusalem descending from heaven*, which is seen in the Spirit (Revelation
21:10). The earthly mountain is contrasted with it (Hebrews 12:18) as a
visible and tangible mountain to which the Israelites had approached with
bodily movements when the law was given. That earthly mountain is contrasted
with the invisible and mystical mountain symbolically designated by Zion, to
which effectually called believers approach by faith: “You have not come to a
mountain that can be touched,” which can be seen or touched by the outward
senses like Mount Sinai, “but you have come to Mount Zion, the house of the
living God, the heavenly Jerusalem.” Nor can it be said, as

[1847 vol. III printed p. 32 | IA leaf 66 | PDF p. 66]

Bellarmine wishes, that this must be confined to the church triumphant. It
also extends to the church militant, both because the faithful are said to
have already approached it and because of the other predicates added there
which apply to the church militant.

[Marginalia: 4. From Christ the head.]

X. Fourth, the head of the church is invisible; therefore, so is his body.
For although Christ was visible on earth and is also in heaven as man,
nevertheless as mediator and head of the church he cannot fall under the
senses. The same must be said of the church, which is his body (Ephesians
1:22). The people who constitute it are indeed seen, but they are not seen as
believers and members of Christ. Hence, just as the church’s unity and
gathering into one mystical body is spiritual through faith, so also must its
form be spiritual.

I know that the papists press the pontiff upon us as the head of the church,
but we will demonstrate the vanity of that fiction in its proper place. The
matter itself makes the truth sufficiently plain, because since the church is
Christ’s body—not a natural but a mystical body—its head must likewise be
homogeneous with it and mystical, which plainly cannot be said of the pope.

XI. Fifth, because the catholic church is an object of faith, since we are
commanded in the Creed to believe it, it therefore does not fall under the
senses. “For faith is the demonstration of invisible things” (Hebrews 11:1).
The ridiculous exception of Bellarmine and Turrianus must not be heard: that
the church is indeed seen, but its holiness is believed, so that the article
ought to be conceived in this form: “I believe the catholic church to be
holy.” This would be as though only the church’s holiness, and not also its
existence, were set forth to be believed. On the contrary, both the subject
and the attribute are presented for belief, because neither falls under the
senses.

But Bellarmine himself, seeing the vanity of this answer, adds that it is
better to say that something in the church is seen and something is believed.
“We see,” he says, “that assembly of people which is the church, but we do not
see that this assembly is the true church of Christ; we believe it.” This
candid confession of the truth cuts the throat of his position and confirms
ours. Perron’s answer tends to nothing else when he says “that faith concerns
either invisible things, or things apprehended under invisible conditions,
such as those under which we consider the church when we believe it to be
Christ’s bride, God’s temple, and so forth. Otherwise,” he says, “believing in
Christ would not have been an article of faith while Christ was on earth, nor
could baptism be believed, as was decreed at the Council of Constantinople.”

For this is precisely our meaning: faith believes that people are Christ’s
church not as human beings or as those who profess the faith, but as those
engrafted into Christ through faith and love. Thus Christ was believed while
he was on earth, not as man but as the Son of God and redeemer; and baptism is
believed, not as an outward washing of water but as the sacrament of grace.
This is the point of what Pererius notes from Cajetan in his comments on
Romans 8: “Two things are to be considered in the object of faith, one of
which is formal and the other material. The material object of faith can be
seen, but the formal object is invisible.” Thus the church is seen with
respect to the material, but believed with respect to the formal.

[Marginalia: 6. From Luke 17:20.]

XII. Sixth, because the church is the kingdom of God, which does not come
“with observation,” that is, with worldly and royal splendor that comes
within the senses and can be observed, as though it were a temporal and
earthly kingdom such as the Jews dreamed of and still expect today. “For
behold, the kingdom of God is within you” (Luke 17:21). This is either because
the kingdom is spiritual and inward, erected in human souls and occupied with
the things pertaining to the soul, not “in food and drink,” that is, outward
and bodily things, but “in righteousness, peace, and joy through the Holy
Spirit” (Romans 14:17), as the Ordinary Gloss has on this passage: “It will
not be capable of observation because my kingdom is not temporal but
spiritual, which has already begun by faith”; or because Christ had already
begun to establish his kingdom among the Jews themselves through

[1847 vol. III printed p. 33 | IA leaf 67 | PDF p. 67]

the proclamation of the gospel received in their souls. This could not have
been said if that kingdom had been visible. Bellarmine excepts that *kingdom
of God* can mean either grace, through which God reigns in human hearts, or
Christ himself. But however it is taken, our position is confirmed, because
it cannot be seen in whose hearts Christ reigns through grace.

XIII. Seventh, because the true worshipers who constitute the true church
properly so called are only those “who worship in Spirit and truth” (John
4:23). They therefore cannot fall under the senses, since hypocrites furnish
the same outward appearance. Bellarmine does not deny this when he says “that
this passage teaches that the principal worship of God in the church would be
inward, but that it does not therefore follow that the church itself is
invisible, nor that outward worship ought to be rejected and outward temples
destroyed, since the Lord is speaking not of the place of prayer but of its
mode and rite.”

But, 1. if the principal worship in the church is inward and invisible, then
surely the church—which properly is and is called the church because of and
with respect to that worship—is rightly called invisible. 2. Although it also
has outward worship, since this is nevertheless not the principal worship, it
cannot and ought not be judged from outward worship who is properly a citizen
of the church. 3. Christ speaks not only about the mode and rite of prayer,
but also about its place, when he shows that henceforth God’s worship will be
attached to no place or seat. “The hour is coming,” he says, “when you will
worship the Father neither on this mountain nor at Jerusalem, but the true
worshipers will worship in Spirit and truth.” “In Spirit,” that is, in
spiritual worship, in contrast to the carnal and outward worship that
prevailed under the Old Testament (Hebrews 9:10). “And truth,” that is, in
true and real worship, in contrast to legal shadows and figures and to the
hypocrisy of outward actions with which carnal Jews were content.

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions.]

XIV. Although in Scripture the word *church* is often taken for a visible
assembly that can fall under the senses in two ways—both with respect to the
people who constitute it, who are visible, and with respect to the outward
form and order flourishing in it, which consists in the profession of faith
and participation in the sacraments under lawful pastors—and although in this
sense the ministry of the gospel is called a lampstand shining in the church,
which certainly can be seen by the bodily senses, it does not follow that the
same word does not elsewhere denote the church insofar as it is invisible
with respect to its inward form and the word is employed in its proper
meaning, as could be evident from the passages cited above.

Thus Bellarmine could only falsely say that Calvin was unable to produce a
single passage of Scripture in which this name was ascribed to an invisible
congregation (book 3, chapter 12, *On the Church Militant*). The various
passages he adduces from Numbers 20:4; 1 Kings 8:14; Matthew 18:17; and Acts
15:3; 20:28; and 18:22 do indeed show that the name *church* is used of an
outward and visible assembly, but they do not prove that this is the word’s
only and proper meaning, since its other meaning occurs more frequently
elsewhere.

XV. “A city set on a mountain which cannot be hidden” (Matthew 5:14) must not
be understood of the catholic church, but properly of pastors and teachers,
or of the church’s ministry. For Christ is addressing his apostles and
disciples. Just as he had previously called them “the salt of the earth and
the light of the world,” so now he adds that “a city set on a mountain cannot
be hidden.” He teaches that the light of the world is of such a kind that it
ought to shine, and that just as a city set on a mountain is hidden from no
one but is conspicuous to all far and wide, so their faith, piety, and life
are exposed to everyone and all their deeds and words are taken as an
example.

Hence he wishes to show them how carefully they ought to take pains not to
allow anything that might offend people, but instead diligently to devote
themselves to making the light of their good works shine before men, so that
men may be led to glorify God. Chrysostom and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 34 | IA leaf 68 | PDF p. 68]

Theophylact observe this well on the passage: “Do not suppose that you will be
hidden in a corner; you will be conspicuous everywhere. See, therefore, that
you live blamelessly, lest you be a stumbling block to others.”

XVI. The passages Isaiah 2:2–3; Daniel 2:35; and Micah 4:1–2 show the church’s
breadth and brightness under the New Testament through the preaching of the
gospel, in contrast to the times of the Old Testament and in relation to
earthly kingdoms and empires. But they do not imply either that this splendor
will be constant and perpetual or that the church of true believers as such
is visible. Outward glory and splendor do not engraft us into Christ’s
mystical body so that we become members of the true church; rather, inward
glory of faith and regeneration, which is known to God alone, does so (2
Timothy 2:19).

Add that it is certain that the prophets speak in this way not properly but
figuratively and mystically. Under the symbol of earthly things—namely, the
outward splendor of the world’s kingdoms—they foreshadow the spiritual and
heavenly glory of the gospel, adapting themselves to the undeveloped
condition of the Jewish people and to the manner of the Old Testament, in
which carnal things were shadows of spiritual things. Thus by Levitical and
bodily worship is understood evangelical worship in spirit and truth
(Zechariah 14:20; John 4:24); by pure offerings are signified prayers,
thanksgivings, and alms (Hebrews 13:15; Psalm 141:2); by splendor and glory,
the light of good works (Matthew 5:14) and the glory of God’s image in
holiness and righteousness (2 Corinthians 3:18); by riches and treasures, the
riches of grace and glory and the treasures of wisdom and understanding
(Colossians 1:27; Ephesians 2:7); by the tabernacle, the church (Ephesians
2:22) and the hearts of the faithful in which God has fixed his perpetual
dwelling (Ephesians 3:17; 1 Corinthians 3:16); and by Israel according to the
flesh, Israel according to the Spirit and the spiritual seed of true
believers, whom he chose for himself as his possession from eternity and will
preserve forever (Galatians 6:16).

XVII. Although circumcision was a visible sign of the visible Jewish church
with respect to its outward state, it does not follow that the true church as
such was visible. For not all those circumcised in the flesh belonged to the
true church, but only those who were Jews in secret, circumcised in heart
through the Spirit (Romans 2:28). In the same way baptism is now an outward
sign of the church with respect to its outward and visible form, but not of
the catholic and invisible church considered with respect to its inward
state.

XVIII. The statement that every believer is bound to join the church under
the peril of eternal death does not imply that the church must therefore be
visible. 1. This must be understood of the catholic church, not of every
particular and visible church. 2. Inward communion through faith is meant,
not always outward communion through profession. The former is absolutely
and simply necessary, so that no one who has not been united to Christ and
made a member of his body can be saved, because salvation is given to no one
without faith in Christ (Acts 4:12; John 3:16). But this does not presuppose
the church’s visibility, because the union must be effected by this bond of
the Spirit.

Outward communion with a visible particular church, however, is not
absolutely necessary, since it is sometimes impossible, as when a Christian
is held captive among unbelievers. It is necessary only conditionally: namely,
if that church is known to us and if we are not kept from its communion by
some insuperable hindrance. But if we do not know it or cannot join
ourselves to it, we are not bound. Otherwise Elijah and the seven thousand
who joined no assembly would be condemned; innumerable believers would be
condemned who, whether detained in prisons and mines or held captive among
the Turks, cannot join any assembly. Bellarmine states in chapter 6, reply to
the first objection, that “a person unjustly excommunicated can be saved,
since he is still in the church in spirit or desire, although not in body or
outward communion.” Therefore the necessity of communion with the church
cannot and ought not be understood of outward communion with the visible
church, but of inward and spiritual communion with the catholic church.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 35 | IA leaf 69 | PDF p. 69]

XIX. It is one thing to know where the true church is, and another to know
which individuals are its genuine members. The former is necessary for the
person who ought to join the church, but the latter is not immediately
necessary. For although I cannot discern with certainty which particular
people among the faithful who constitute the church are true believers, it
is enough that I know from the marks of the church where they are and in what
society they dwell, so that I may join myself to it.

Christ’s fold must indeed become known to a believer so that he may join it
with a general and indefinite knowledge, knowing that it possesses the marks
of the true visible church and that true believers are in it. In the same way
a pastor must know Christ’s fold in order to furnish food to the sheep who
are in it. But it is not necessary for the individuals to become known to him
by special, distinct, and definite knowledge, so that by a judgment of truth
he may hold with certainty that these and those people are the true church.
This belongs only to Christ, the chief shepherd, who infallibly knows his
sheep. It is enough for us to know this by a judgment of charity, which can be
liable to error.

XX. Our opponents wrongly insist that there can be no society of people
unless they recognize one another, and that they cannot recognize one another
unless they see one another. As to the first claim, who does not know that
there is society and communion between the church triumphant and the church
militant, between perfected spirits and possessors of heaven on the one hand
and wayfarers on earth on the other, even though there is no knowledge
between them? Certainly there are some exchanges of duties that can be
practiced between acquaintances and neighbors, but there are others by which
we embrace people entirely unknown to us when we commend them to God in our
prayers and are joined with them by the bond of the Spirit.

As to the second claim, it is false that no one can be recognized except by
the outward senses. For just as Christ is not known according to the flesh,
neither are believers. Thus I know Paul and the other saints not by outward
and visible signs, but by communion of the Spirit. The same must be said of
true believers, who are united to and recognize one another by hidden and
invisible bonds. I admit that visible signs are required in particular
churches to establish outward communion, but the situation of the catholic
church, which does not fall under the senses, is different.

XXI. So far is it from being possible to infer the church’s visibility from
Christ’s words, “Upon this rock I will build my church” (Matthew 16:18), that,
on the contrary, this passage strikes at the throat of the papal cause. For
the building must be of the same kind as the foundation and the building’s
construction. But the building’s construction is spiritual and invisible,
since it is made by the cement of the Spirit and the bond of faith and love,
through the effectual and inward calling that engrafts and builds us upon
Christ as living stones. The foundation also is not visible, but hidden in
God. For although Christ as man was once seen on earth, he could not be seen
as the church’s foundation and head.

Nor, if the confession of faith made by Peter was outward and perceptible,
does it follow that the church’s foundation was visible. For the confession
as such and in itself is not the foundation, but is so by reason of the thing
or doctrine declared by the confession. Its true and saving application to
found the church is effected not in the mouth through a confession, which can
be false, but in the heart through faith. It is evident from the attribute
added—that the gates of hell will not prevail against it—that this passage
cannot be understood of the church as visible. For Satan can and often does
prevail against visible particular churches, but he can never prevail against
the catholic church, which consists only of the elect, because it is built
upon the rock (Matthew 7:24).

XXII. The nature of republics and earthly kingdoms is one thing, and the
nature of the church another, because Christ’s kingdom is not of this world
(John 18:36). It therefore does not follow that because republics are always
visible or consist in outward and visible government, the church does as
well. For although the inward affection by which citizens are united to one
another contributes much to the tranquility of a republic, the essential
form of the republic does not consist in it, but in

[1847 vol. III printed p. 36 | IA leaf 70 | PDF p. 70]

the outward bond of society through outward observance of the laws, which can
remain intact without inward affection. But the situation is different in the
church. Since its formal character consists in the mystical communion of the
faithful with Christ and one another, merely outward communion with him does
not make anyone a member of the church. Indeed, apart from such outward
communion, true believers who live in places far apart are members both of
Christ and of the church itself. This differs from what happens in a
republic, where those absent for a long time lose the rights of citizenship,
or at least do not enjoy them.

XXIII. Although visibility belongs to the church by reason of the ministry
and outward means, it does not follow that the church properly so called is
visible by reason of its essence. For the outward ministry of the word must
not be separated from the inward efficacy of the Spirit, by which true
members of the church are constituted. Nor is the contrary proved from the
fact that the ministry of the word is common to the reprobate and the elect.
By right it belongs only to true believers, for whom Christ appointed it
(Ephesians 4:12); it is directed to others only accidentally, because they are
