Meanwhile Ptolemy Philadelphus flourished peacefully in Egypt. He received his
surname either by antiphrasis or by preeminence, because he pursued his brothers
with excessive hatred or his sister with excessive love. Fearing him, his
brothers Ceraunus, Meleager, and Alexander fled to Macedonia, where all of them
were raised to the throne and killed by the Gauls within a few months. He also
seized and killed two other brothers who remained in Egypt. But he married his
sister Arsinoe, who had first been the wife of Lysimachus and afterward of
Ceraunus, and loved her above all mortals. By her he fathered his successor,
Ptolemy Euergetes; after her death he fashioned her image from the Twins and
built a temple for its worship.

> Marginalia, printed p. 214: *Philadelphus’s hatred for his brother and lust
> for his sister*.

The poet Lycophron, wishing flatteringly to proclaim the sweetness of
Philadelphus’s character, resolved his name by anagram into “from honey,” and
Arsinoe’s into “Hera’s violet”: the humanity and virtue of Philadelphus are
indeed greatly celebrated by learned men. He was certainly a supreme lover of
books and men of letters, and he built the renowned library of Alexandria, in
which Aventinus reports that there were seven hundred thousand manuscript
volumes.

> Marginalia, printed p. 214: *A friend of flattering men of letters*.

Demetrius of Phalerum had for a long time governed the Athenian republic
temperately and prudently under Cassander. When Demetrius dissolved the Greek
oligarchies, he fled to Ptolemy son of Lagus and lived with him in great honor
and intimacy. When Ptolemy disclosed to him his plan to prefer his younger son
Philadelphus to his elder son Ceraunus, Demetrius of Phalerum strongly
dissuaded the king from the unjust attempt. For this reason, according to
Diogenes Laertius, as soon as Philadelphus came to the throne he imprisoned
Demetrius, who died there from the bite of an asp. According to others,
however, Demetrius remained in as great honor with the son as with the father

until his death, and the prefecture of the library was entrusted to him.

At the urging of Demetrius of Phalerum, Philadelphus wrote to Eleazar, the
Jewish high priest, asking him to send the divine books to adorn his library,
together with interpreters to translate the Hebrew into Greek. To obtain this
favor from the Jews more readily, he redeemed 120,000 Jewish captives in Egypt
for four hundred talents and sent many golden vessels to the temple, above all
a table of the greatest value. Its value, I say, arose not only from the
weight of fifty talents of gold, but especially from its remarkable
craftsmanship and the immense number of gems set into it—five thousand in all.

> Marginalia, printed p. 215: *The Greek translation of the Bible by the
> seventy interpreters*.

The high priest sent the law, written in letters of gold upon the purest
parchment, together with seventy-two men skilled in the sacred and Greek
languages, six from each of the twelve tribes. Ptolemy received them with
great honor and entrusted them with the task of translating the sacred books.
They all completed the translation in seventy-two days. The version, called
that of the seventy interpreters by a rounded number, was delivered to the
king to be deposited in the library, and the interpreters returned to
Jerusalem after each had received three talents together with other gifts.

Aristaeus, Ptolemy’s envoy to Eleazar, recounts these things fully in his own
work, and Josephus repeats them from him. The ancient Fathers of the Church not
only accepted them but added more of their own. Justin Martyr maintains that
each of the seventy interpreters was assigned his own cell apart from the
others, in which each remained until the work was finished. When all had
completed their work in seventy-two days, each man’s labor was read publicly
before the king and many Jews and Greeks. By a very great miracle, not a word,
syllable, or letter differed among so many interpreters separated from one
another, who had made no mutual comparison of their work.

> Marginalia, printed p. 215: *Many fables about its translation*.

Jerome ridicules the invention of the cells and also the miracle of so exact
an agreement among the seventy-two interpreters without any communication
between them. Not a word of this little tale is found in either Aristaeus or
Josephus. Indeed, those two testify to the contrary: every day after greeting
the king, the seventy interpreters met in one hall and worked there together
until the third hour after noon, their ninth hour of the day.

Ludovicus Vives and Leo Castrius reject the book of Aristaeus as spurious, but
Bellarminus, Possevinus, and others promote it as genuine, and John Donne
translated it into English as such. Scaliger too, following Laertius, regards
the whole part played by Demetrius of Phalerum in Philadelphus’s library as
fictitious. Certainly the king’s gifts were so magnificent, and the devotion
to God that Josephus reports from Aristaeus so great, that they scarcely seem
credible. I do not doubt the translation itself by the seventy-two
interpreters, but I cannot bring myself to believe many traditions about its
circumstances which Augustine and other Fathers embraced with amply human
credulity. Perhaps more will be said about this in the questions.

It is certain that a great company of poets, philosophers, and learned men in
every discipline flowed to Ptolemy from Greece and elsewhere. He often held
very familiar conversations with them about the finer points of literature
until late at night. But, as Plutarch observes, none of them dared utter a word
about the man’s real vices. When the poet Sotades blurted out a verse against
his incest with Arsinoe, he immediately paid for his frankness with
imprisonment and a cruel death by being thrown into the sea.

The claims made about him also seem excessively grand. Theocritus in his
Idylls not only sings of his power, subjecting 33,339 cities to his empire;
Jerome also relates that his armed forces and resources comprised 200,000
infantry, 20,000 cavalry, 2,500 ships, four hundred elephants, and two thousand
scythed chariots. Beyond his immense treasury, Jerome assigns him an ordinary
revenue of 14,800 talents and 4,500,000 measures of grain.

> power*.

In his last years Philadelphus waged a difficult war with Antiochus Theos. It
was always customary for the kings of Egypt and Babylon to fight over Syria.
After Seleucus was killed by Ceraunus, his son Antiochus Soter—so called
because, after defeating the Gauls by means of elephants, he had saved Asia
from Gallic servitude—did not trouble Ptolemy, since many wars pressed him at
home. But when he died after a reign of nineteen years, his son by his
stepmother Stratonice, Antiochus Theos, first given this title by the Milesians
because he had subdued their tyrant Timarchus, repeatedly made war upon
Ptolemy. At last, when both were exhausted, peace was concluded on these
terms: Ptolemy gave his daughter Berenice to Antiochus Theos as his wife with
an immense dowry, for which she was called the Dowry-Bearer. From this
marriage, as Daniel foretold, came new tragedies and more disastrous wars.

> Marginalia, printed p. 215: *To have peace with Antiochus Theos, he gave him
> his daughter Berenice the Dowry-Bearer as wife*.

Antiochus still had a living wife, Laodice, by whom he had fathered two sons,
Seleucus Callinicus and Antiochus Hierax. When the new bride Berenice arrived,
Laodice was held in contempt and regarded merely as a concubine. She therefore
brooded upon a great mass of anger

until her son Callinicus came of age; then she poisoned her husband Theos and
cruelly killed Berenice, her little son, and her Egyptian attendants. As though
these crimes were glorious victories over enemies, she gave her son the
surname Callinicus, “Glorious Victor.”

> Marginalia, printed p. 216: *Laodice kills Berenice*.

Philadelphus had died after a reign of forty years. But when his son Euergetes
learned of his sister’s brutal murder, he flew to Asia with an army, inflamed
by justified fury. Most cities of Asia allied themselves with him against the
parricide Callinicus. He defeated Callinicus in a great battle, captured his
mother Laodice, and subjected her to the ultimate punishment. From the spoils
of Asia he carried forty thousand talents and most of the statues of the gods
back into Egypt, especially those which Cambyses had carried from Egypt to
Persia. For these benefactions he received the surname Euergetes,
“Benefactor,” although others prefer to say that he was so called for the
three hundred talents and many other gifts with which his royal generosity
helped the Rhodians—almost utterly destroyed by an earthquake—recover their
former prosperity.

> Marginalia, printed p. 216: *Ptolemy Euergetes kills Laodice and plunders
> Asia*.

Revolts in Egypt recalled Euergetes from his possession of Asia. On his way,
at Jerusalem, he offered sacrifices and costly gifts to the God of Israel for
his victory. Nevertheless, as a sign of subjection he exacted an annual
revenue of twenty talents from the high priest, who was then the supreme
magistrate.

In Ptolemy’s absence Callinicus prepared a great fleet to punish his rebellious
subjects. A storm sank almost all of it, and the king himself barely escaped
Neptune’s jaws with a few men. His subjects, seeing that the gods had exacted
sufficiently great punishments from the king for his crimes, put aside their
anger and returned to his favor. Ptolemy returned to Asia and defeated
Callinicus a second time. Callinicus summoned his brother Antiochus Hierax to
his aid. He was called Hierax, “the Hawk,” because, although still quite
young, he was preparing to swoop like a hawk upon his brother Callinicus and
snatch away his entire kingdom. When Ptolemy saw that the brothers had united
against him, he granted Callinicus a ten-year peace. Meanwhile Callinicus,
having detected Hierax’s designs against him, took up arms against him. Hierax
joined the Gauls to his forces and defeated Callinicus in battle through their
strength. Thinking Callinicus dead, the Gauls seized Hierax, intending that,
with both brothers and in them the entire royal line cut off, they themselves
would rule Asia. But Callinicus appeared and restored his army. Hierax
redeemed himself from Gallic captivity with much gold and, fearing his
brother, fled to Ptolemy in Egypt. Ptolemy threw him into prison; after nine
years’ captivity he escaped, only to be stabbed to death by robbers.

Callinicus, broken so many times on land and sea, lost the majesty of his
empire. Eumenes, king of Pergamum, occupied almost all western Asia as far as
the Taurus. Arsaces the Parthian joined many provinces of eastern Asia to the
Parthian kingdom.

If we believe Trogus Justinus, the Parthians were Scythian exiles settled
between Hyrcania and Media. They had no reputation at all in the age either of
the Persians or of the Macedonians, before the days of Seleucus Callinicus or
at least those of his grandfather Antiochus Soter. At that time Arsaces united
many provinces firmly and established the Parthian kingdom with both great
prudence and courage. Callinicus led an army toward Parthia to subdue him, but
when battle was joined Callinicus was defeated and captured. Arsaces,
however, treated him humanely and released him. While returning home he fell
from his horse and died in the nineteenth year of his unfortunate reign,
leaving two sons, Seleucus and Antiochus.
