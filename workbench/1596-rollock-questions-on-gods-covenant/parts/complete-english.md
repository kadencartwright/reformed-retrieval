## Author's dedicatory epistle

[Digital frame 3; sig. A2r]

TO A GOOD MAN AND ONE SERIOUSLY DEVOTED TO GOD,
a most honored citizen of the city of Edinburgh,
who has served once and again, with distinction,
as provost of that city,
WILLIAM LITTLE.

When I considered in my mind, excellent sir, under whose name above all I
wished this little work—composed by me during a few spare hours—to appear, no
one came to mind more suitable than you. No good citizen of this city fails
rightly to proclaim that you have deserved exceedingly well of your
commonwealth; we in turn have found you most devoted to our commonwealth of
learning. To speak of myself, from the time when I first came to this city and
was placed over this academic commonwealth, I have had you as my constant
supporter and, I might almost say, as a father to me. For, to confess the
truth, you have testified your love for me by such frequent and exceptional
services that, by your own kindness, you have bound my heart, all my duties,
and everything I have toward you forever.

I acknowledge indeed that this little work of mine is neither worthy of your
name nor equal to your services toward me. Yet experience has taught me that
you value everything of mine not according to its own worth but according to
your singular affection and love for me. I have therefore ventured to offer
you this little effort of mine, however small it may be,

[Digital frame 4; sig. A2v]

as a witness to my affection for you in return and a pledge of some greater
work, if anything more worthy of you should hereafter be able to proceed from
me to you.

You have, then, excellent sir, in these few pages some questions concerning
God's covenant and the sacraments. They are elementary indeed, but I hope they
will not be useless to children and less advanced readers. They are intended
especially for your son—who is also our pupil—the boy of good promise and
character, WILLIAM LITTLE. We ourselves can testify sufficiently how earnestly
you have always desired his education in sound learning, and above all in
piety and holiness, of which you have here several choice foundations. Thus,
if he does not eventually turn out to be both a good man and a good citizen,
that is, a man like his father, the blame must now be assigned not to his
father but to himself. Farewell, then, in the Lord, excellent sir and my
dearest friend, and live long for your commonwealth and for us.

Edinburgh, the thirteenth day before the Kalends of March.

Most devoted to you in the Lord,
Robert Rollock.

## Questions and answers concerning God's covenant and the sacrament which is its seal

### First, God's covenant in general; then, the covenant of works

[Digital frame 5; sig. A3r]

[Marginal summary: What a covenant is in general]

**Q. What is a covenant made between God and man?**

**A.** It is that by which God promises some good thing to man under a fixed
condition, while man accepts the condition.

[Marginal summary: How many kinds]

**Q. How many kinds of covenant are made between God and man?**

**A.** There are two: the covenant of nature or of works, and the covenant of
grace. Galatians 4:24.

[Marginal summary: What the covenant of works is]

**Q. What is the covenant of nature or of works?**

**A.** It is God's covenant in which he promises eternal life to man on
condition of good works proceeding from the powers of nature,

[Digital frame 6; sig. A3v]

while man accepts that condition of good works. Leviticus 18:5; Romans 10:5;
Galatians 3:12.

**Q. Was a severe threat not also added to the promise of this covenant?**

**A.** It was.

**Q. In what form?**

**A.** “Cursed is everyone who does not continue in all the things written in
the Book of the Law, to do them.” Genesis 2:17; Deuteronomy 27:26; Galatians
3:10.

[Marginal summary: Its foundation]

**Q. What is the foundation of the covenant of works?**

**A.** A good, holy, and unimpaired nature, such as existed at man's creation.
For if God had not originally made man in his own image—that is, wise, holy,
and righteous by nature—he certainly would not have made with him this
covenant whose condition is the holy, righteous, and perfect works of nature.
Genesis 1:26–27; Ephesians 4:24; Colossians 3:9.

[Marginal summary: Its promise]

**Q. What is promised in the covenant of works?**

**A.** A blessed life to be lived forever. Romans 10:5; Galatians 3:12.

[Marginal summary: Its condition]

**Q. What is the condition in the covenant of works?**

**A.** The condition is good works, which must proceed from the good, holy,
and unimpaired nature on which

[Digital frame 7; sig. A4r]

the covenant of works itself is founded. Romans 10:5; Galatians 3:12.

**Q. What does this condition exclude?**

**A.** This condition excludes from the covenant of works, first, faith in
Christ and, second, the works of grace and regeneration. Romans 11:6.

**Q. Why are these excluded?**

**A.** Because the powers of nature with their works cannot coexist with
Christ's grace and the works of grace. Romans 11:6.

**Q. What are the articles of this covenant's condition?**

**A.** They are contained individually in the individual commandments of the
Decalogue. Hence the tablets on which the Law was written were called the
tablets of the covenant. Exodus 19–20; 32:15; Hebrews 9:4.

**Q. Is the condition of works therefore a condition of meritorious works?**

**A.** By no means. They are duties which testify to gratitude toward God the
Creator. Romans 11:35; Luke 17:10.

**Q. But the works of a holy and unimpaired nature had to be perfect, holy,
and good.**

**A.** It does not follow from this that they had to be merits. Properly
speaking, merit requires a work that is not owed and to which a reward is owed
according to the order of justice. Romans 4:4. To the one who works—to the one
who merits—a reward is reckoned as a debt.

[Digital frame 8; sig. A4v]

[Marginal summary: The reason for the name]

**Q. Why is it called the covenant of works?**

**A.** Its name is taken from the covenant's condition, namely the works of a
good and unimpaired nature.

[Marginal summary: When it was made]

**Q. When was this covenant of works made with man?**

**A.** It was made at man's first creation. Genesis 1:27ff.; 2:15ff.

[Marginal summary: How it was made]

**Q. How was it made, and in what order?**

**A.** God first engraved his Law in the created man's heart, that is, the
articles of his covenant's condition. He then said, “Do and work according to
the prescription of my Law—for it is written on your heart—and you will live.”
Man accepted the condition and pledged that he would fulfill it. Genesis
2:15ff.

**Q. You maintain, then, that this moral Law was engraved on man's heart at
creation, and consequently that the individual articles of this covenant's
condition were known by nature itself?**

**A.** I do. The remnants of that knowledge of the Law, which still persist
after the fall in corrupted nature, are evidence of it. Romans 1:19–32; 2:14.

[Marginal summary: Whether it was repeated]

**Q. Was this covenant of works repeated afterward?**

[Digital frame 9; sig. A5r]

**A.** It was continually repeated from creation and man's fall until the
coming of Christ. It was first handed down by the living voice; then engraved
on tablets of stone by God's own fingers; afterward delivered and written by
Moses; and finally repeated and written by each of the Prophets in his own
place and order, as is evident from the Old Testament.

[Marginal summary: How it was repeated]

**Q. In what manner and order was it repeated?**

**A.** You have the order in Exodus 19 and 20. First God said through Moses,
“If you keep my covenant, that is, my Law, you will surely be my peculiar
treasure.” Then all the people said with one voice, “Whatever Jehovah says, we
will do.” When the people's pledge had been reported to God through Moses, God
proclaimed from Mount Sinai his Law, that is, the articles of his covenant
which the people were to observe.

**Q. But you said before that the Law preceded and the covenant followed?**

**A.** It amounts to the same thing whether the words of the covenant, which
contain the pledge and the answering pledge, precede the Law—that is, the
proposal of the articles of the condition contained in God's covenant—or
follow it.

[Digital frame 10; sig. A5v]

[Marginal summary: The purpose for which it was repeated]

**Q. For what purpose was it repeated after man's fall to the ancient Church
and people, since after the fall the condition of nature's good works is
impossible for man? Romans 8:3.**

**A.** Its principal purpose was not that people should be justified through
it and live. Rather, convicted in their consciences and pressed by that
impossible condition of nature's good works, they were to flee to the covenant
of grace. Deuteronomy 10:16; Romans 3:19; 7:7ff.

[Marginal summary: Whether it was to prepare them]

**Q. You maintain, then, that the covenant of works was repeated to the
ancient people chiefly to prepare them for the covenant of grace?**

**A.** I do.

[Marginal summary: Whether it did so for everyone]

**Q. But not everyone was prepared or fled to the covenant of grace?**

**A.** True. The elect were prepared, while the reprobate were eventually
driven to despair. Romans 11:7.

[Marginal summary: Whether there was a covenant of grace]

**Q. Was the covenant of grace also made with the ancient Church and people?**

**A.** It was, although mention of it was obscure within the doctrine of the
covenant of works and of the Law. Genesis 3:15; 49:10; 22:18; 26:4.

[Marginal summary: Whether it was broken]

**Q. Did man therefore fall away from this covenant of works?**

**A.** He certainly did. Romans 3:4.

**Q. When did the first man fall from this covenant?**

[Digital frame 11; sig. A6r]

**A.** Immediately after creation and the making of the covenant itself.
Genesis 3:1ff.; Romans 5:12ff.

[Marginal summary: Whether his descendants defected]

**Q. Did his descendants also defect from this same covenant?**

**A.** After this covenant had been repeated to the people, they indeed
pledged that they would fulfill its condition, but they did not keep their
promise, nor could they. Exodus 32:1ff.

[Marginal summary: What followed]

**Q. What followed man's defection and violation of the covenant?**

**A.** Both kinds of death: first, the death of the body; second, that of body
and soul. This occurred by the force of the threat joined to the covenant's
promise. Genesis 2:17; 3:7ff.; Romans 5:12.

**Q. But he did not die immediately when the covenant was violated and
broken?**

**A.** Both bodily and spiritual death nevertheless began then, to be
completed in their own due time. His body became subject to mortality, and his
soul lost God's image, that is, the life of God which is manifested in
holiness, righteousness, and wisdom. Genesis 3:7; 1 Corinthians 15:42–43;
Ephesians 2:1; Colossians 2:13.

[Digital frame 12; sig. A6v]

[Marginal summary: Where the doctrine is found]

**Q. Where do you have the doctrine of this covenant of nature?**

**A.** It is taught fully in the Old Testament, that is, in the writings of
Moses and the Prophets, a large part of which is devoted to explaining the Law
and the covenant of works. It is comprehended in summary form in the
Decalogue.

### God's gracious covenant

[Marginal summary: What the gracious covenant is]

**Q. After the covenant of works had now been broken and man lay prostrate and
dead, was he himself the first to think about his liberation or redemption?**

**A.** He gave no thought at all to his own redemption. Genesis 3:8ff.;
1 Corinthians 2:14.

**Q. How, then, was he freed and redeemed from so great a death?**

**A.** By God's grace and mercy alone, which went before him beyond and
contrary to every expectation, since he was spiritually dead. Genesis 3:8;
Ephesians 2:1ff.; Colossians 2:13.

**Q. What was this prevenient grace of God?**

[Digital frame 13; sig. A7r]

**A.** God gave man his Mediator, Jesus Christ. Genesis 3:15; Romans 8:3;
Galatians 4:4; 1 John 4:9.

**Q. But the Son of God was not manifested in the flesh immediately after
man's fall, although the Mediator must be both God and man in one person?**

**A.** Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever, and Christ is
the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world. That is, the power and
efficacy of Christ's flesh and cross, which were to come only at their
appointed time, were already present from the beginning. Hebrews 13:8;
Revelation 13:8; 1 Corinthians 2:3–4.

**Q. When at last was the Son of God manifested in our flesh?**

**A.** When the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, born of a woman.
Galatians 4:4; Ephesians 1:10.

**Q. What part did the Mediator, now given by the Father, undertake in order
to redeem man?**

**A.** He transferred to himself man's sin, God's wrath, and both bodily and
spiritual death, which man had incurred by his own desert. Deuteronomy 21:23;
Galatians 3:13.

**Q. What follows from this?**

[Digital frame 14; sig. A7v]

**A.** For our sake he was made subject to the covenant of works and the Law,
in order to redeem those who were subject to the Law. Galatians 4:4–5.

**Q. When Christ our Mediator was thus placed under the covenant of works and
the Law for our sake, did he fulfill the covenant of works and the Law?**

**A.** He did, and in two ways.

**Q. What were they?**

**A.** Both by acting and by suffering. Romans 8:3, 5; 1 Peter 2:22ff.;
Galatians 3:13.

**Q. Would it not have been enough if for our sake he had acted well and
holily throughout his whole life, without also suffering death for us?**

**A.** It would not have been enough. Not even all his most holy and righteous
works would have satisfied God's justice and wrath on account of our sins,
much less merited for us God's mercy, reconciliation, righteousness, and
eternal life. Hebrews 9:22–23.

**Q. Why is that?**

**A.** Because, once we had broken the covenant of works, God's justice
necessarily required the ultimate punishment of death, in accordance with

[Digital frame 15; sig. A8r]

the threat which, as we said above, was joined to the covenant's promise.
Deuteronomy 27:26.

**Q. I will grant you that, once the covenant of works had been broken, none
of our works, even if they had been most holy and righteous, could have
satisfied divine justice and wrath. Yet I do not see why the works of the
Mediator—which were the works of God and nevertheless also those of a most
holy and innocent man—could not, I say, have satisfied God's justice and
wrath?**

**A.** Just as our works could not have done so, neither could even the most
holy works of the Mediator, God and man, have satisfied God's justice. Once
violated, that justice necessarily demanded unto death either the sinful man
himself or a Mediator in his place, so that God might be both just and true.
Hebrews 9:22–23; Romans 3:26.

**Q. If I grant you that the Mediator's death and suffering satisfied God's
justice and wrath on account of our sins, grant me in return that by his holy
and good works the Mediator merited for us all mercy, reconciliation, and
grace. In other words, grant that satisfaction and merit should be divided
between his works and suffering, so that you may say that by his suffering he

[Digital frame 16; sig. A8v]

satisfied justice and wrath, while by his holy and good works he merited grace
and reconciliation.**

**A.** I do not grant even this. 1 John 1:7.

**Q. At least you cannot deny that the Mediator's good and holy works
accomplished some part of both satisfaction and merit?**

**A.** Speaking properly, I say that those works did not accomplish even some
part of either satisfaction or merit, because throughout Scripture I see
Christ's satisfaction and merit ascribed to his suffering and cross, not to
his actions. Romans 3:25; 5:19.

**Q. You maintain, then, that by his suffering and death alone the Mediator
both satisfied divine justice and merited grace?**

**A.** I maintain that both satisfaction and merit are properly attributed to
the Mediator's death and suffering alone. Romans 3:25–26; 5:19.

**Q. Then I see no use for all the many and great works which our Mediator
performed throughout his whole life?**

**A.** That does not follow either.

**Q. What, then, will you say was their use?**

**A.** I say that the holiness of Christ's person as God and man, together
with all his holy works,

[Digital frame 17; sig. B1r]

as works of so holy a person, was the foundation of the satisfaction and merit
which properly belong to his suffering alone. 1 Peter 3:18; Hebrews 7:26;
9:13–14.

**Q. Explain this a little more clearly, please, if you can.**

**A.** This is what I mean: the dignity of Christ's most holy person, together
with the works proceeding from so holy and divine a person, was the reason why
his suffering possessed the power and efficacy by which he not only satisfied
the justice and wrath of God for our sins, but also merited for us new grace
and mercy.

**Q. I understand. You mean, then, that through that holiness of his person
together with that of his works Christ was made a fit, suitable, and
efficacious sacrifice—that is, both satisfactory, if I may so speak, and
meritorious?**

**A.** I do. For such a high priest was fitting for us: devout, innocent,
undefiled, separated from sinners, and made higher than the heavens. Hebrews
7:26.

**Q. How many effects, then, does the death of Christ the Mediator have?**

**A.** Two. First, it satisfied God's wrath for man's sin; second, it merited
new grace and mercy. Romans 3:25; 4:23.

[Digital frame 18; sig. B1v]

**Q. What is the cause of such great efficacy in Christ's death?**

**A.** First, the dignity of the person, who is God and a most holy man; then,
the severity of the punishment and death which he suffered to such a degree.

**Q. What grace, first of all, did he merit for man by his death?**

**A.** The grace of reconciliation with God. Romans 5:10.

**Q. How was reconciliation with God effected?**

**A.** Reconciliation was effected by striking a new covenant with man.
Jeremiah 31:33; Hebrews 8:8.

**Q. What was that new covenant?**

**A.** What people commonly call the covenant of grace, or the gracious
covenant.

[What the gracious covenant is]

**Q. What is the covenant of grace?**

**A.** It is the covenant by which God promises man righteousness and eternal
life, on condition of faith in Christ the Mediator; man, in turn, accepts the
condition and undertakes that he will believe. Habakkuk 2:4; Joel 1:31; Romans
9:10; Mark 16:15. [The references are reproduced as printed.]

[What its foundation is]

**Q. What is the foundation of the gracious covenant?**

**A.** The first is Christ's satisfactory and meritorious death; the second
is God's mercy, which Christ's death merited. The covenant of grace arises
immediately from this foundation of mercy. Romans 3:24.

**Q. Does the foundation of the covenant of grace, then, differ

[Digital frame 19; sig. B2r]

from the foundation of the covenant of works?**

**A.** Indeed, the foundations of the two covenants are as much in conflict
with one another as nature and grace are opposed to one another; these can in
no way cohere in the work of redemption. Romans 11:6.

[What is promised]

**Q. What things are promised in the covenant of grace?**

**A.** Righteousness and eternal life. Romans 5:21.

**Q. What is this righteousness that is promised?**

**A.** First, it is not inherent righteousness of the sort that was lost in
Adam, but the righteousness of God—that is, the righteousness which God
imputes through faith. It is as much more excellent than inherent
righteousness as Christ was more excellent than Adam; and without it man
cannot appear before God and his tribunal. Romans 3:22; 5:12ff.

**Q. What is this life that is promised?**

**A.** It is the effect of Christ's imputed righteousness. It is begun in this
life through inherent holiness, but is perfected after this life through
glory. Romans 5:21; 6:22; 8:30.

[What the condition is]

**Q. What is the condition of the gracious covenant?**

**A.** The very name “gracious covenant” implies either that there is no
condition (for when someone promises that he will give something freely,

[Digital frame 20; sig. B2v]

he properly requires no condition), or at least that the condition of the
gracious covenant is entirely gracious—that is, it comes from God's grace
alone. Isaiah 55:1; Romans 3:24.

**Q. What is that condition which you call gracious?**

**A.** It is faith in the Mediator. Romans 10:6; Galatians 3:14.

**Q. Is faith, then, from grace?**

**A.** It is, and solely from grace, not also from the powers of nature.
Philippians 1:29.

**Q. Therefore this condition of faith in Christ and in God's mercy in him
excludes those good works of nature which were set down as the condition in
the covenant of works?**

**A.** It plainly does. For Christ and the mercy of God, which are the objects
of faith, cannot coexist with the powers of nature or the works of nature in
man's justification. Romans 3:28; 11:6.

**Q. Does faith also exclude the works of regeneration, or of grace?**

**A.** It excludes these as well. For not even works of regeneration can
contribute as any part of the cause and merit for justifying and saving man.
Romans 4:2–3; Philippians 3:8.

**Q. You maintain, then, that faith alone—that is, Christ alone apprehended
by faith—constitutes the condition of the gracious covenant?**

**A.** I do,

[Digital frame 21; sig. B3r]

because that which is the condition in the gracious covenant is also the cause
of man's justification—that is, of the fulfillment of what was promised in
the covenant of grace. Faith alone justifies, however; that is, Christ alone
apprehended by faith. Therefore faith alone is the condition of the gracious
covenant. Romans 4:13ff.

**Q. Do you maintain, then, that in reality the condition of the gracious
covenant and the cause for which God fulfills the promise of the gracious
covenant—that is, for which he actually justifies us according to the formula
of the covenant itself—are one and the same?**

**A.** I do. For in this covenant these three are one and the same in reality:
the foundation of the gracious covenant, the condition of the covenant, and
the cause for which God fulfills the covenant's promise to us. For Christ
crucified is both the foundation of the gracious covenant, its condition, and
the cause of the fulfillment of the thing promised in the covenant. Galatians
3:8–9; 2 Corinthians 1:20.

**Q. Is there no distinction among these three?**

**A.** They differ only conceptually. For Christ crucified, as the covenant's
foundation, is considered absolutely. As the covenant's condition, he is
considered as one yet to be apprehended by faith, since a condition concerns a
future thing yet to be performed. Finally, as the cause for which God fulfills
the covenant's promise, he is considered

[Digital frame 22; sig. B3v]

as already apprehended through faith at a past point in time. Romans 3:24,
28.

**Q. Therefore nothing takes place in the gracious covenant apart from
merit?**

**A.** Nothing. For the covenant itself is founded upon merit, the covenant's
promise is made under the condition of merit, and, finally, merit is the cause
for which the promise is fulfilled. Hebrews 9:15ff.; 2 Corinthians 1:20;
Galatians 3:17; Romans 4:13; 10:9–10.

**Q. How, then, can it be called gracious when grace and merit are so greatly
at odds with one another?**

**A.** What is merit with respect to Christ the Mediator is itself sheer grace
with respect to us. The name “gracious covenant” is assigned to this covenant
not with respect to Christ, but with respect to us. Romans 3:24; 4:16.

[The use of the works of regeneration]

**Q. What, then, is the use of works of regeneration, if they constitute
neither any part of the condition that is in the gracious covenant nor any
part of the cause of our justification and salvation?**

**A.** That we may be most grateful to God for our calling, which comes
through the gracious covenant struck with us, and for our justification. 2
Corinthians 5:15; Ephesians 4:1.

**Q. Yet throughout Scripture you will find the reward of eternal life
promised under the condition of works

[Digital frame 23; sig. B4r]

of sanctification. Are these promises not promises of the gracious covenant?
If they are, must not works of regeneration constitute at least some part of
the condition that is in the gracious covenant?**

**A.** Those promises do not belong to the gracious covenant, just as they do
not belong to the covenant of works.

**Q. Why is that?**

**A.** Because, while the promise of the covenant of works repeated after the
fall is made to a man not yet justified and reborn, just as the promise of the
gracious covenant is, these promises are made to a man who is already
justified and reborn. Romans 3:23–24; 1 Timothy 4:8.

**Q. To what end are these promises made to those who have already been
justified and reborn?**

**A.** So that the reward of eternal life, when set before them, may stir
those already justified and reborn to works of regeneration. 1 Timothy 4:8;
Galatians 6:9.

**Q. Do you maintain, then, that three distinct kinds of promises are found
in Holy Scripture, and especially in the New Testament?**

**A.** I do.

**Q. What are they?**

[How many kinds of promises there are]

**A.** The first belongs to the covenant of works, the second to the gracious
covenant, and the third kind consists of those promises by which men who have
already been

[Digital frame 24; sig. B4v]

called, justified, and reborn are stirred to duties of gratitude toward God
the Redeemer. These occur throughout that part of evangelical doctrine which
concerns life, holiness, and good morals. Indeed, you will see this third kind
of promises scattered especially among the exhortations and precepts delivered
concerning a holy life.

**Q. What, then, are the heads of the condition that is in this gracious
covenant?**

**A.** The things which we must believe (for we can call these the heads of
the condition) concerning God, Christ the Mediator, and his benefits. These
are explained fully in the gospel, but are embraced in a few words in what
they call the Apostles' Creed.

[The reason for the name]

**Q. Why is it called the gracious covenant?**

**A.** Because either there is no condition of the covenant, or at least the
whole condition is gracious.

[When it was struck]

**Q. When was this gracious covenant first struck with man?**

**A.** Immediately after man's fall. Genesis 3:5. [The reference is
reproduced as printed.]

[How it was struck]

**Q. Was it also first struck in explicit words?**

**A.** By no means. Rather, at the beginning an obscure promise was made of
the woman's seed—that is,

[Digital frame 25; sig. B5r]

of Christ and his benefits. Genesis 3:5. [The reference is reproduced as
printed.]

[Whether it was repeated]

**Q. Was this gracious covenant repeated afterward?**

**A.** It was repeated continually from man's fall until the manifestation of
Christ. Only then did it begin to be proposed most clearly and openly, for
then people began to say, “Whoever believes in Christ crucified will be
justified and live.” John 3:18; Romans 10:9–10.

**Q. Therefore that gracious covenant with the ancient people and this
covenant of grace which was struck with the Church itself at Christ's coming
are the same in substance?**

**A.** Both are the same in reality and substance, but differ in accident.
Because of this accidental difference, the former was called the old covenant
or Testament, but the latter the new covenant or Testament. Hebrews 8:8–9ff.

**Q. By the name “Old Testament,” do you mean the covenant of grace?**

**A.** The name “Old Testament” extends more broadly to the Law, or covenant
of works, together with the covenant of grace joined to the Law. The Apostle
teaches in Hebrews that the name “Old Testament” is to be understood in this

[Digital frame 26; sig. B5v]

way. Hebrews 8–9.

**Q. Therefore the covenant of grace struck with that ancient people is also
called the Old Testament?**

**A.** It is.

[How it was promulgated after Christ's coming]

**Q. After Christ's manifestation and his ascension into heaven, what order
did the Apostles observe in promulgating this gracious covenant?**

**A.** First they evangelized—that is, first they preached Christ humbled,
then Christ glorified, and thirdly they taught the benefits flowing from
Christ's death and glory. Then they concluded their gospel with this promise:
“Whoever believes in this Christ, first thus humbled and then glorified, will
become a partaker of Christ and of all his benefits.” In these words we have
the gracious covenant. Acts 2:14ff.

**Q. Was this gracious covenant never, at any time before Christ's
manifestation in the flesh, proposed and expounded clearly and explicitly?**

**A.** Before Christ's coming it was never proposed as clearly as it was
promulgated after his coming. Yet this cannot be denied: the nearer Christ's
coming was, the more clearly the Patriarchs and Prophets indicated

[Digital frame 27; sig. B6r]

this covenant. Moreover, the last Prophets spoke of it most clearly of all.
Hebrews 1:1; John 1:18; 2 Peter 1:19.

**Q. Which of the Prophets, then, spoke of it most clearly?**

**A.** Paul cites this saying from the prophet Habakkuk: “The righteous will
live by faith.” Romans 1:17. Likewise, from the prophet Joel: “Whoever believes
in him will not be ashamed.” Romans 10:11. In these sayings, indeed, we have
the formula of the gracious covenant. Therefore, according to Paul's
testimony, these two Prophets spoke of the gracious covenant most openly.

[To what end it was repeated]

**Q. To what end was it repeated?**

**A.** Precisely the same end for which it was first struck with man
immediately after the fall: namely, that man might be justified through faith
and live. Jeremiah 31:33; Hebrews 8:8.

**Q. Do you maintain that in this respect this repeated gracious covenant
differs from that repeated covenant of works?**

**A.** I do. For, as we saw above, the covenant of works was not repeated with
man for the same chief end for which it was first struck.

[The punishment and the condition connected with this covenant]

**Q. What if, after breaking that covenant of works, man had not accepted the
condition of this gracious covenant?**

[Digital frame 28; sig. B6v]

**A.** Then he would never have been justified and saved. John 3:18; Mark
16:16.

**Q. Did the first man believe for himself and his descendants when he
accepted the condition of the gracious covenant?**

**A.** The covenant was indeed struck with Adam and his descendants or seed;
but faith and grace in Christ are not propagated to descendants in the way
that nature, whether good or evil, is transmitted to them. Genesis 5:3; 17:7;
Romans 5:12; Philippians 1:20.

[The punishment of one who falls away from this covenant]

**Q. What if someone, after undertaking that he would believe, has begun to
believe, to be enlightened in mind, and in his heart to taste the good word of
God and the powers of the age to come—what if, I say, he afterward slips and
falls away from God?**

**A.** He is not renewed again through repentance, nor is some third covenant
struck with him. Hebrews 6:6; 12:17.

**Q. Therefore the breaker of this gracious covenant is in a worse place and
condition than man was after breaking that first covenant of works. For after
the covenant of works was broken, there was room for repentance and for God's
mercy in Christ. But if the covenant of grace is broken, you say that there
will be no room for repentance?**

**A.** It is true that the breaker of the gracious covenant is in a worse
place and that his sin is unforgivable, because it is the sin against the Holy
Spirit. A necessary characteristic of that sin is that whoever has sinned
against the Holy

[Digital frame 29; sig. B7r]

Spirit is so punished by God with final blindness that he can never repent of
his sin earnestly and sincerely. Matthew 12:31.

**Q. You say, then, that no third covenant can be struck with man once this
second covenant, which is called gracious, has been violated?**

**A.** I do. For if we sin deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the
truth, no sacrifice for sins remains, but only a certain fearful expectation
of judgment and a raging fire that will devour the adversaries. Hebrews
10:26–27.

**Q. Therefore can the covenant not be renewed without a victim and a
sacrifice?**

**A.** God cannot be reconciled to a sinner and covenant-breaker without a
sacrifice by which satisfaction is made to his justice and wrath. That first
covenant did not require a Mediator and sacrifice, because man was not a
sinner when that covenant of works was first struck with him. When the
covenant of works was repeated to the Israelite people, it was not done by God
himself immediately, but through the ministry of Moses as intermediary; this
was an indication that God was at odds with the people because of their sins.
Galatians 3:19.

[Digital frame 30; sig. B7v]

The second covenant, which we call gracious, required a Mediator and
sacrifice, because man had sinned and sin is not expiated without a sacrifice.
For remission does not take place without the shedding of blood. Hebrews 9:22.
Now if a man falls away from this second covenant, because no sacrifice can be
found, there will therefore never be any remission of sin: no third covenant
is to be sought.

**Q. But if someone falls away from that covenant of grace—for you seem to
maintain that someone can fall away from it—does it not follow that faith in
Christ, the Holy Spirit, and grace, once received, can be lost, although the
Apostle says that God's gifts in Christ are such that he cannot repent of
them? Romans 11:29.**

**A.** For the purpose of the present treatment, we make faith twofold. One is
temporary, which is also called hypocritical; the other is permanent, also
called *ANUPOKRITON*, that is, unfeigned. Temporary and hypocritical faith can
be lost, because it is superficial and does not put down deeper roots in the
heart. Permanent and unfeigned faith, however, can never be utterly uprooted
and expelled from the heart, because it is not superficial but puts down
deeper roots in the heart. Therefore, whoever has received this gift of
unfeigned

[Digital frame 31; sig. B8r]

faith can never fall away altogether from God's covenant and fall into what
they call the sin against the Holy Spirit. 1 John 3:9; Luke 19:26; John 10:28.

[Whether the covenant of works has been abolished for those under the covenant]

**Q. Has that covenant of works been abolished for those who are under the
covenant of grace?**

**A.** So far as justification or condemnation is concerned, it has been
abolished. But with respect to conversion, faith, regeneration, or the
mortification of the flesh, it still has some use of its own even among
believers. Romans 1:5; Galatians 3:13; 4:5; Romans 3:31; 7:14ff.

**Q. Explain what you mean.**

**A.** I will. First, those who are in Christ are justified and saved by faith
in Christ alone, and not by works of the Law. Romans 3:28. Next, there is no
condemnation for those who are in Christ, for Christ became a curse for them.
Galatians 3:13. Moreover, even those who are in Christ, since they do not
believe perfectly, are not perfectly converted, and are not perfectly reborn,
need the terrors of the covenant of works continually. Romans 7:18. This is so
that they may be stirred to make continual progress in faith, conversion, and
sanctification.

[Digital frame 32; sig. B8v]

[Whether the moral Law itself has been abolished]

**Q. Was that moral Law, which contained the heads of the condition required
in the covenant of works, not abolished and abrogated together with the
covenant of works?**

**A.** Insofar as the moral Law serves the covenant of works, its status is
altogether the same as that of the covenant of works: it is abolished insofar
as the covenant of works has been abolished, and remains insofar as the
covenant of works remains.

**Q. Is the moral Law considered in another way than insofar as it has regard
to the covenant of works, namely, insofar as it contains the heads of the
covenant of works' condition?**

**A.** It is.

**Q. How?**

**A.** After Christ's coming it began openly to serve the covenant of grace
and to contain the heads and rules of Christian duties and of works no longer
of nature but of grace and regeneration. Therefore the moral Law remains,
serving and ministering in a certain manner to the covenant of grace itself.
It remains, I say, no longer inscribed on tablets of stone, but on fleshly
tablets of the heart. Jeremiah 31:33; Romans 3:31; 2 Corinthians 3:2.

**Q. Where, then, is the doctrine of this covenant which you call gracious
found?**

**A.** In both Testaments, the Old and the

[Digital frame 33; sig. C1r]

New, although it is handed down more clearly and fully in the New Testament
and the writings of the Apostles. A brief summary of this covenant's doctrine
is contained in what they commonly call the Apostles' Creed.

Thus far on God's gracious covenant.

ON THE SACRAMENT IN GENERAL

[Whether both covenants were sealed]

Question.

**Q. Did God seal both his covenants—the covenant of works and the covenant of
grace—with a Sacrament, that is, with his own seal?**

Answer.

**A.** He did indeed seal both these covenants of his with a sacrament, as
with his seal. Genesis 2:9; 17:11; Romans 4:11.

**Q. I would like you to show me the manner in which that first covenant of
works was sealed.**

**A.** The covenant of works was sealed in this manner, and with the following
form, as it were, observed. The covenant's words were put forward in this
form: “Will you,” God said to man, “act rightly and fulfill my Law, in order
that you may live?”

[Digital frame 34; sig. C1v]

The man answered, “I will.” God answered, “On that condition and law, I surely
promise you eternal life; and so that you may be all the more persuaded of
this, I seal this covenant of mine for you with my own seal.” Meanwhile, he
presented his seal and sacrament. Genesis 2:15–17; 17:11; Romans 4:11;
Revelation 2:7.

**Q. With what sacrament did he seal that covenant of works?**

**A.** Without doubt, the trees of the Garden of Eden were likewise
sacraments and seals of God's covenant: such were the tree of the knowledge
of good and evil and the tree of life. Genesis 2:9; 3:22.

**Q. How, and in what form, was the covenant of grace sealed?**

**A.** In precisely the same form in which the covenant of works was sealed.
For God said, “Will you believe in Christ the Mediator?” The man answered, “I
will.” Then God said, “On that law and condition I will justify you and give
you eternal life; and so that you may believe all the more, I seal this promise
of mine for you with this seal or Sacrament of mine.” Genesis 17:11; Acts
8:37–38.

**Q. With what sacraments did God seal—and

[Digital frame 35; sig. C2r]

does he still seal—his gracious covenant?**

**A.** With various sacraments from the beginning onward. Genesis 2:9; 17:11;
Exodus 12:1ff.; 1 Corinthians 10:1ff.

**Q. Since God's Word alone, without a seal and sacrament, is firm and
immutable in itself (for God is not a man, that he should lie), why was a seal
added to his Word and to the promise made in the covenant? It is as though his
Word needed some seal by which it might be made more sure, and as though it
were not firm and established enough by itself.**

**A.** The seal was added to God's Word and promise, not for God's sake or for
the sake of his Word. The Word alone, without a Sacrament, could certainly
have sufficed for man, if it is considered in itself and in its own firmness
and certainty. Rather, it was added for man's sake, for man is weak by nature
and his faith required those helps of the sacraments. Romans 3:4; 4:11;
Hebrews 6:17–18.

**Q. Therefore, in creation before the fall, while man was in the state of
innocence, did he suffer from this weakness and need these helps to confirm
his faith more fully?**

**A.** Man was indeed created without sin, but not without all weakness and
mutability of nature. Therefore in the first creation he was liable to

[Digital frame 36; sig. C2v]

change. Hence, to establish him in his faith so that he would not change it,
God gave him his sacrament together with the word of the covenant. James 1:13;
Job 15:13; 4:18; Matthew 19:17.

**Q. As for man's weakness after the fall—or rather, his unbelief—there is now
no question that it is the reason God added his seal or sacrament to his
gracious covenant?**

**A.** None. Romans 3:10, 23; Genesis 6:5.

**Q. Is some measure of unbelief therefore mixed with faith for as long as
man enjoys this life?**

**A.** It is mixed with it. 1 Corinthians 13:9; Ephesians 3:19; Colossians
1:9.

[On the sacraments of the New Testament in general]

**Q. Leaving aside those ancient Sacraments, both of the covenant of works and
of the covenant of grace, I would like you to teach me something about the
sacraments of the covenant of grace and of the New Testament which Christ
instituted at his coming.**

**A.** I will now teach those things about them in general which, when rightly
understood, will make the particular doctrine of Baptism and the Lord's Supper
easy to know.

**Q. Where will you begin the general doctrine of the sacraments of the New
Testament?**

**A.** I will begin with the things themselves signified in the sacrament.

[What things are signified]

**Q. How many things signified are there in all?**

[Digital frame 37; sig. C3r]

**A.** They can all be reduced to four. The first thing signified is Christ
himself, God and man. The second is the cross or suffering of Christ. The
third is the benefits flowing from Christ's suffering, such as the remission
of sins, justification, the gift of the Holy Spirit, regeneration, and,
finally, eternal life. The fourth thing signified is the inward application,
first of Christ, then of the cross, and lastly of his benefits. Romans 6:3;
Galatians 3:26–27; Romans 4:11; 1 Corinthians 10:4; 11:24; Acts 2:38.

[What the signs are]

**Q. Now, to pass from the things signified to the sacramental signs, I ask
first: what do you call signs in a Sacrament?**

**A.** I call not only the elements themselves signs, but also the rites and
all the external actions. 1 Corinthians 11:23–26.

**Q. I would like you to explain what signs the individual things signified
that were enumerated above have in each Sacrament, both in Baptism and in the
Lord's Supper.**

**A.** I will explain them.

**Q. To begin, then, with Baptism: in Baptism, what is the sign of Christ
himself and of the substance, if I may so speak, of his blood?**

**A.** Water is the sign of Christ and of his substance. Matthew 3:11; John
7:33; 3:5.

[Digital frame 38; sig. C3v]

**Q. In Baptism, what is the sign of Christ's cross or suffering?**

**A.** The pouring of water, by which the shedding of God's blood is
signified. Isaiah 44:1–3.

**Q. In Baptism, what is the sign of those benefits which flow from Christ's
suffering?**

**A.** The sign of the suffering and of the benefits is the same, namely, the
pouring of water.

**Q. Finally, in Baptism, what is the sign of the application of those three
spiritual things?**

**A.** The sign of the application of those three things is one and the same,
namely, the sprinkling or washing with water, by which water is outwardly
applied to wash the person being baptized. Hebrews 12:24; 1 Peter 1:2; Titus
3:5; Acts 22:16.

**Q. You have spoken of the signs of those things that are in Baptism. Next I
would like you to tell me what signs of the same things are in the Lord's
Supper.**

**A.** I will.

**Q. In the Lord's Supper, then, what are the signs of Christ himself—that is,
of his body and his blood?**

**A.** Bread and wine. Matthew 26:6ff.; Mark 14:22ff.; Luke 22:19ff.; 1
Corinthians 10:16ff.; 11:23ff. [The first reference is reproduced as printed.]

**Q. In the Lord's Supper, what are the signs of Christ's cross and
suffering?**

[Digital frame 39; sig. C4r]

**A.** The breaking of the bread and the pouring of the wine. The proof texts
are as in the preceding answer.

**Q. In the Lord's Supper, what are the signs of Christ's benefits?**

**A.** The same things that are signs of his suffering, namely, the breaking
of the bread and the pouring of the wine.

**Q. Lastly, in the Lord's Supper, what are the signs of the application of
those three spiritual things?**

**A.** There are several signs of application, each of which signifies the
application of all three of those spiritual things together. Specifically,
they are: 1. The giving of the bread and wine on the Minister's part. 2. The
receiving of them on the people's part. 3. The eating of the bread on the
people's part. 4. The drinking of the wine on the people's part. The proof
texts are those cited above from Matthew, Mark, and so forth.

**Q. From what you have said about the sacramental signs, my first observation
is that in each Sacrament the sign of the suffering or cross and the sign of
Christ's benefits are one and the same.**

**A.** You observe correctly. This is because all Christ's benefits flow from
his suffering. 1 John 1:7.

**Q. My next observation is that in each Sacrament the sign of the application
of all three spiritual things together is one and the same.**

[Digital frame 40; sig. C4v]

**A.** This too you observe correctly, for Christ, his cross, and his benefits
are applied in one and the same operation. Galatians 3:26–27ff.; Romans 8:32.

[Whether the signs in the sacrament are bare signs]

**Q. I now understand what the signs are in each sacrament and which spiritual
thing each signifies. I would now like to learn from you whether these signs
are bare and idle signs or not.**

**A.** They are not; rather, they are signs that effectually accomplish
something, and organs and instruments of the Holy Spirit. Romans 4:12; 6:4;
Titus 3:5.

**Q. I would like you to explain this to me.**

**A.** I will. In each sacrament, those things which are signs of Christ, his
cross, and his benefits—that is, of the heavenly things themselves not yet
applied—are also instruments of the Holy Spirit. By them he illuminates our
minds and effectually works in us the knowledge of those spiritual things,
which is the first part of faith. Romans 4:11.

**Q. What do you say of the signs, in each Sacrament, of the application of
those spiritual things?**

**A.** These too are instruments of the Holy Spirit, by which he works not
only knowledge in our minds, which is

[Digital frame 41; sig. C5r]

the first part of faith, but also inwardly works application in our hearts—
namely, the very application which they signify and the knowledge of which he
first works in our minds. And this is the second part of our faith, namely,
inward application. Romans 4:11; Galatians 3:27.

**Q. Therefore it is properly from these signs of the application of those
spiritual things that the sacraments possess the quality of being called
exhibiting, applying, and conferring signs, *SPHRAGIDES*, or seals—for these
are the names by which they are commonly called.**

**A.** They do possess it. For the signs of the application of those spiritual
things are the things through which the Holy Spirit works not only knowledge,
which belongs to the mind, but also application, which belongs to the heart;
and through them he exhibits to us Christ, his cross, and his benefits. Romans
4:11.

**Q. Thus far you have spoken first of the things signified, and then of the
sacramental signs. I would now like to know from you the causes for which the
signs you cited in each sacrament represent those signified heavenly things as
signs, and effect the knowledge and application of them as instruments of the
Holy Spirit.**

**A.** I will not fail you in this part either.

[Digital frame 42; sig. C5v]

[The causes both of the signification and of the operation of the signs]

**Q. How many causes are there in the sacraments for which the signs represent
those signified things and effect the knowledge and application of them?**

**A.** There are two causes. The first is the analogy and proportion between
the signs and the things signified. The second is the spiritual and
supernatural relation between the signs and the things signified, from which
arises what they call the sacramental union. 1 Corinthians 10:16–17; 1 Peter
3:22.

**Q. I would like you to explain this analogy or proportion of which you
speak.**

**A.** It arises from a certain natural property of the thing that is the
sign, by which the thing that is the sign becomes similar to the thing
signified. For example, in Baptism there is a proportion between the water and
the Lord's blood, because water has a natural property—namely, a fitness for
cleansing or washing away the body's filth. Through this the water becomes
similar to the Lord's blood, by which our spiritual filth is washed away.
Likewise, in the Lord's Supper there is an analogy and proportion between that
bread and the Lord's body, because bread has a property—namely, a fitness for
sustaining and nourishing this natural life. Through this the bread becomes
similar to the Lord's body, by which we are nourished unto eternal life. The

[Digital frame 43; sig. C6r]

same judgment applies to all the other signs in the Sacraments. In all of them
there is an analogy or proportion between the things that are signs and the
things that are signified. John 8:12; 1 Corinthians 10:16–17.

**Q. Do you maintain that this analogy and proportion between the signs and
things signified is so necessary that without it the signs cannot signify and
represent those spiritual things?**

**A.** I do. For unless analogy and proportion obtain between any sign and any
thing signified, that thing certainly cannot be a sign of it. For example, a
stone cannot be a sign of a man when there is no analogy and proportion
between the stone and the man.

**Q. You have spoken of analogy. Now I would like you to treat the second
cause, which you said was the cause of the signification and operation of the
signs in the sacraments, namely, that spiritual relation.**

**A.** I will treat it too.

**Q. What, then, do you call this spiritual relation?**

**A.** That by which a sign is no sooner presented to these bodily eyes in the
Sacrament than the spiritual thing signified is represented to the mind and
the eyes of faith.

[Digital frame 44; sig. C6v]

For this is the property of things that are related: one of the related things
is no sooner known than the other enters our mind. Genesis 17:11; Romans 4:11;
Ezekiel 20:12. In all these passages mention is made of a sign or seal which is
related to something else.

**Q. From where does this spiritual relation, as you call it, arise?**

**A.** Not from the nature of the thing itself which is the sign, from which
we said the analogy arises, but from the Lord's own institution and promise,
joined with prayers. For by the Lord's institution the thing which is the sign
is transferred from common use to sacred use, and becomes a sacrament—that is,
a sign related to the signifying of a spiritual and heavenly thing, and
sacramentally united with it. For example, because the Lord so instituted it,
the water of Baptism now represents his blood, and has therefore been made a
sacrament of the Lord's blood. 1 Corinthians 11:23ff.; Romans 4:17.

**Q. You do not maintain, then, that analogy alone makes something a
sacramental sign?**

**A.** I do not. For something which has an analogy to something else because
of some similarity does not immediately

[Digital frame 45; sig. C7r]

become a sign of it. Rather, for it to be a sign, some institution and
ordinance, either of God or man, is required. Otherwise, to be sure, every
piece of bread would be a sign and Sacrament of the Lord's body, since there
is no bread which is not proportional to the Lord's body because of some
similarity. John 6:55; 1 Timothy 4:4–5.

**Q. Do you maintain, then, that this spiritual relation is so necessary that
without it there is no Sacrament?**

**A.** I do. For unless any signifying thing has a certain relation to any
thing signified, it certainly cannot be a sign of it. A sign is always related
to something.

**Q. Since this relation which makes a sign or sacrament arises from the
institution of God and Christ, which they call consecration, I would like to
know how a Sacrament is instituted.**

**A.** After giving thanks, the Lord pronounced the words of institution, by
which he explained the use of the external things. Examples are the use of the
water in Baptism and of the bread and wine in the Lord's Supper. Thus, when it
is said in the Supper, “This is my body,” the sense is: “This bread is the
sign or Sacrament

[Digital frame 46; sig. C7v]

of my body, and now has this use: that it represent my body, because I so will
and institute it.” 1 Corinthians 11:23ff.

**Q. Do you maintain, then, that the institutor and consecrator—that is, the
maker of the Sacrament as Sacrament, who transfers the element from common to
sacred use—is the Lord himself?**

**A.** I do. He first did this by himself at that Last Supper; now, however,
he does the same thing daily through men—and not through any men whatsoever
without distinction, but through those lawfully called, that is, through his
Pastors and Ministers. Otherwise it is not a Sacrament, but a profanation of
the Sacrament. Matthew 28:19.

**Q. Should we think that this change of a thing, by which the thing itself is
transferred from profane to sacred use so as to become a Sacrament—should we
think, I say, that on this principle some change takes place in the thing?**

**A.** Indeed, it is a kind of change that no creature can bring about: by it
the thing which is changed not only represents, but also offers and effects;
and in our hearts it accomplishes the very thing that is represented.

**Q. Do you attribute to this change in the signs, and to what they call the
sacramental

[Digital frame 47; sig. C8r]

union, those metonymical expressions in which the external things are
predicated of the internal, and conversely?**

**A.** Those customary and metonymical modes of speech arise from the change
itself and the sacramental union. See Titus 3:5; 1 Corinthians 10:17, and
throughout elsewhere on these matters.

[How the sacrament differs from the word]

**Q. I have heard about the causes for which the individual signs in the
sacraments signify as signs and operate as instruments. I would now like to
hear from you in what respect the sacrament differs from the word. For the
word of the covenant or gospel signifies those spiritual and heavenly things
as a sign and operates as an instrument. The word of the gospel concerns
Christ, his suffering, his benefits, and the application of those same things,
not only as a representing or signifying sign, but also as an organ and
instrument of the Holy Spirit operating effectually in our minds and hearts.
Therefore it seems to me that, so far as their use is concerned, there is no
difference.**

**A.** The difference is that the sacrament added to the word signifies those
heavenly and spiritual things more fully, and more fully effects the knowledge
and application of them in our minds and hearts, than the word alone does.
Genesis 17:11; Romans 4:11.

**Q. Why do you say that the sacrament added to the word signifies and
operates more than the word alone?**

**A.** Because,

[Digital frame 48; sig. C8v]

while the word alone represents those spiritual things only to the ears, the
Sacrament added to the word represents the same things also to the eyes—to say
nothing here of the other senses. Nor need I mention that it is offered not
only to all generally, as the word is, but also to individual believers. Acts
2:38.

[How many uses the sacrament has]

**Q. Do you maintain that the Sacrament differs from the word only in this
respect: that when added to the word it signifies and operates more than the
word alone?**

**A.** With respect to this use of the Sacrament, which consists in signifying
and operating, the Sacrament differs from the word alone only according to
more and less, as they say. But there is also a second use of the Sacrament,
arising from this first use, which is in no way shared with the word but is
proper and principal to the Sacrament. Romans 4:11.

**Q. So much for the difference between the Sacrament and the word. Now I ask:
how many uses do you assign to the sacrament?**

**A.** Two.

**Q. What, then, is the first use of the Sacrament?**

**A.** It is that by which the Sacrament added to the word, as a sign,
signifies those heavenly and spiritual things more fully than the word alone;
and as an instrument,

[Digital frame 49; sig. D1r]

it more fully effects those same things: knowledge in our minds and
application in our hearts—that is, the *KOINONIA*, or communion, which they
commonly teach. Romans 6:3; Galatians 3:26; 1 Corinthians 10:16.

**Q. What is the second use of the Sacrament?**

**A.** It is that by which the Sacrament, because it signifies and operates
more when added to the word than the word alone does, confirms for us the word
of the covenant and gospel, and increases and assists our faith in the word of
the gospel—just as a seal affixed to a diploma gives the diploma greater
credibility. And this is the Sacrament's chief use. Genesis 17:11; Romans
4:11; Matthew 26:28.

**Q. From this second end it appears that the Sacrament serves the word of the
covenant and was ordained and instituted by the Lord for its sake.
Consequently, the word of the covenant or gospel is the primary and principal
instrument of the Holy Spirit by which he produces faith in our hearts; but
the Sacrament is only a secondary organ of the Holy Spirit, and less
principal. Matthew 28:19–20; Romans 10:8, 17.**

**A.** That is indeed so.

**Q. The second end which you have described raises this scruple for me. In
your doctrine of the gracious covenant you said that the covenant was

[Digital frame 50; sig. D1v]

sanctioned and made valid by the blood of Christ the Mediator, and established
in it as in its foundation. But now, in teaching the second end of the
Sacrament, you say that this same gracious covenant is confirmed by the
Sacrament. Consequently, you seem to me to maintain that the word of that
covenant is made valid and sanctioned both by a sacrifice and by a
sacrament.**

**A.** It is one thing for the covenant or Testament itself to be sanctioned,
made valid, and simply established; it is another for our faith in it to be
confirmed, which is the second and proper end of the Testament. For God's
covenant is sanctioned by the Mediator's sacrifice and blood alone, and is
founded on that alone. But by the Sacrament, as by God's sacred oath and seal,
our weaker and feebler faith in the very word of the covenant is confirmed and
increased. I will put it more clearly. The sacrifice of Christ the Mediator is
the cause—both satisfactory and meritorious, if I may so call it—which moved
and impelled God to strike this gracious covenant with sinful man. The
Sacrament, however, is a certain cause supplied by God himself which causes man
to believe more firmly in the promise of God's covenant. “This cup is the new
Testament through my blood”—that is, the seal of the new

[Digital frame 51; sig. D2r]

covenant which is sanctioned through my blood. 1 Corinthians 11:25.

**Q. I accept the distinction. I have also understood what you said about the
things signified in the Sacrament; about the signs and the causes for which the
signs powerfully signify those spiritual things and effect the knowledge and
application of them; about the distinction between Sacrament and word so far
as their use is concerned; and, finally, about the Sacrament's twofold end. I
would now be grateful if you would explain the Sacrament in general by its own
definition, or by some brief description.**

**A.** If you understand those two ends of the Sacrament which I have just
handed down, you have the definition. For from that twofold end I define the
Sacrament in this manner.

[What a Sacrament is]

**Q. What, then, is a Sacrament?**

**A.** It is a visible sign, instituted by God and operating effectually,
through which, when it is added to the word of the gospel, the Holy Spirit
represents those spiritual and heavenly things to us more fully, and effects
the knowledge of them in our minds and the application of them in our hearts
more fully than through the word of the gospel alone. In doing this, he
confirms our faith in the word of the covenant and gospel and causes us to
believe it more and more.

[Digital frame 52; sig. D2v]

**Q. What is the genus in this definition?**

**A.** The genus is sign.

**Q. What is the first differentia?**

**A.** That it is a sign by God's institution and ordinance, by which the
Sacrament differs from all common and profane signs.

**Q. What is the second differentia?**

**A.** That it is an instrument of the Spirit operating effectually, by which
the Sacrament is distinguished from all ineffectual signs.

**Q. What is the third differentia?**

**A.** That it is a visible sign, by which it is distinguished from the word
of the gospel or covenant, which is an audible sign.

**Q. What is the fourth differentia?**

**A.** It is taken from the first end of the Sacrament.

**Q. What is the fifth?**

**A.** It is taken from the second and principal end of the Sacrament.

**Q. I have the definition and its brief explanation. I now ask about the
name “Sacrament”: do you understand by it something complex and composed of
both the sign and the thing signified, or a simple sign which does not
comprehend the thing signified?**

**A.** From the definition I have given, it is clear that by the name
“sacrament” I mean

[Digital frame 53; sig. D3r]

a sign which is indeed related to the thing signified and sacramentally united
to it, but is not at the same time confused with it or comprehended together
with it. For related things must be defined and explained through their
relation to the things with which they are compared, but must not be confused
with them. Genesis 17:11; Romans 4:11.

**Q. I understand the sacraments of the New Testament in general. Will you
now also speak of them particularly?**

**A.** There will be a proper place to speak separately of the particular
Sacraments, that is, Baptism and the Lord's Supper. For now I only advise that
from what has been said about the genus itself one can easily gather what the
natures of the species are.

**Q. I do not now ask you for a fuller doctrine of the species, which I gladly
defer to another, more suitable time. I ask only that you draw out for me, in
a few words, definitions of the species from the definition of the genus,
adding their own special differentiae.**

**A.** I will do as you ask.

[What Baptism is]

**Q. What, then, is Baptism?**

**A.** It is the Sacrament by which the New Testament, now first perceived,
is confirmed to us; or by which our faith in the New Testament, now first
begun, is confirmed. For these are the same thing in reality. Genesis 17:10.

[Digital frame 54; sig. D3v]

Matthew 28:20; John 4:1.

[What the Lord's Supper is]

**Q. What is the Lord's Supper?**

**A.** It is the Sacrament by which the New Testament, heard for a sufficiently
long time already and preserved by faith, is confirmed; or by which the
progress and continuation of our faith in the New Testament is confirmed. For
these are the same thing in reality. 1 Corinthians 11:25–26, 28.

**Q. From where are these definitions taken?**

**A.** They are taken from the second and chief use of the Sacrament in
general, which is to confirm the New Testament, or the word of the gospel.

**Q. By what special differentiae do you restrict this general end?**

**A.** The special differentia of Baptism is that by it the New Testament,
first heard and received by faith, is confirmed. The special differentia of
the Lord's Supper is that by it the New Testament is confirmed—not as first
received by faith, but as preserved by faith over a long period. I will put it
more clearly: in Baptism the beginning of faith is confirmed, but in the
Lord's Supper the increase or continuation of faith is confirmed.

And thus far these matters on the Sacrament in general.

FINIS.
