Consequently, we have thus far seen how Downham proved that bishops are of
apostolic institution. Let us also see the argument of those who wish them to
have been instituted immediately by Christ himself. Although Downham proceeds
no further than apostolic institution, he nevertheless says that very many of
the Fathers assert that in the apostles and the seventy disciples Christ
instituted two ranks of ministers, bishops and presbyters. And those who say
that bishops succeeded the apostles consequently also say that when Christ
instituted the apostles, he instituted bishops. We showed above that the
seventy disciples were not called by Christ, while he was in the flesh, to a
stable and perpetual office, but to a certain temporary and general
announcement of the Messiah's coming. *Therefore, I will not deny a direct
succession to them through our presbyters because the seventy ceased to exist,
but because Christ did not institute them as a stable body of that number, as
he had made the stable college of the twelve apostles. Hence Matthias had to
be substituted in Judas's place, before they undertook the apostolic office
entrusted to them after the resurrection, Acts 1, certainly by Christ's own
inspiration and concurrence in those lots. And for as long as each of them
lived, he expressly exercised the apostolic office*

*entrusted to him with a general command. I do not find any such confirmation,
command, and general mission made by Christ to the seventy disciples, nor can
I affirm that the presbyteral order was directly, proximately, and immediately
instituted in them, as the episcopal order was directly and immediately
instituted in the twelve apostles,* says Spalatensis. We do not deny that
afterward all or some of them were transferred into the ordinary or
extraordinary function of evangelists or presbyters. *Indeed, before all else,
lest that action of Christ by which he chose the seventy-two be entirely in
vain, after Christ's ascension the holy apostles reunited those same seventy
disciples, as truly perpetual followers of Christ, even after the first
mission had been completed,* says Spalatensis. We will not reluctantly grant
that this could have been done. We will also grant what he says, that several
of those seventy disciples were likewise made general apostles by the
apostles—that is, as we speak according to Scripture, evangelists. But he
denies that they were called to the presbyteral order. Saravia admits that the
apostles did not receive the power of binding and loosing until after Christ's
death. For, he says, *The apostolate had its beginnings and progress, and after
the Holy Spirit was received it was in some measure different from itself.
Moreover, he promised that he would give them the keys of the kingdom of
heaven and the power of binding, which he did not give before his death,
because it was still joined to the Aaronic priesthood and was not to come to
an end until by the sacrifice of his body he abolished the Levitical victims
and transferred into himself the priesthood and whatever was joined with it.
And hence, while the old church still stood, they could not found a new one.*
If before Christ's death they did not have the power of binding and loosing,
they were not yet bishops. What if they were not even presbyters? For that
mission into the cities of Judea, which was common to them with the seventy
disciples, was not to the stable and fixed office of preaching the gospel, but
to that general announcement and proclamation of which we spoke above when we
treated the seventy disciples. Theodore Beza also denies that they had the
power to baptize. *Nor do I think it true that, as you say,* he writes, *the
power to baptize was also entrusted to them at that time, since not even the
slightest mention of it is made among the evangelists.* Saravia marvels that
Dr. Beza, who illustrated the New Testament with most learned annotations,
brought it back to the anvil three or four times, and read and reread it again
and again, could write so absurdly, *Since it is expressly read in John that
the Lord did not baptize, but his disciples did, John 4:2 and 3:22. This
mistake,* he says, *ought to warn Dr. Beza that he could err and that whatever
proceeds from him is not an oracle,* etc. He proceeds so shamelessly, as though
he were leading that most learned man captive in triumph—bold and insolent.
For the disciples to baptize was nothing other than to immerse while Christ
evangelized, as we said above. *He indeed taught and evangelized (for how would
Christ give his baptism without evangelization?), but these disciples, at
that time still entirely untrained and not yet sufficiently instructed to
evangelize, being truly*

say, so far as I know, that they were subject to the apostles, but that they
were lower by one rank. While the Lord lived on earth, both groups were the
first heralds of the gospel under his direction.* To what end, therefore, do

To prove that the bishop has directive and corrective power over presbyters.
The Fathers who wrote more than three hundred years afterward said that their
bishops succeeded the apostles and presbyters succeeded the seventy disciples.
They never said that they succeeded by Christ's institution, because Christ
never instituted *that the apostles should be succeeded by a second rank in
the church,* says Junius. *How, then,* you will say, *did they say that these
succeeded them? Namely, by human and not divine institution, by a certain
analogical and not proper rationale, by a certain common imitation and not by
a singular necessity of the church. The Fathers therefore said that these
succeeded them by likeness, not by parity, because some thought that ranks in
the church which were not equal but similar could be observed—not fully, but
in a certain manner—while others thought they ought to be observed. But if
there is any likeness, although it is only qualified*

*and most remote, it is nevertheless a likeness: if truly, a true one; if
imaginarily, an imaginary one. But this is a qualified, remote, and imaginary
likeness, from which, if some draw an absolute conclusion, they are absolutely
deceived.* Bellarmine says: *Bishops succeed the apostles in the same manner
that presbyters succeed the seventy-two disciples. But it is clear that
presbyters do not properly succeed the disciples, but only by likeness. For
those seventy-two disciples were not presbyters, nor did they receive any
order or jurisdiction from Christ.* You see how Beza, Bellarmine, and
Spalatensis agree about the office of the seventy disciples. But what did they
properly foreshadow? The apostles and their fellow workers, who were to be sent
throughout the world. But let us proceed to the full power of the apostolate,
which they received after the resurrection, when the keys which Christ had
previously promised were given to them; he sent them as the Father had sent
him, and poured upon them the gifts of the Holy Spirit. Did he not then
appoint the apostles as bishops? I answer that he did not properly appoint
bishops, but apostles. For the adversaries wish a bishop to be an ordinary
pastor who has power over other pastors. No apostle had such power as though
he had an ordinary episcopate enclosed within the apostolic function and
distinct from it. All the power which the apostles had over presbyters they
exercised as apostles, not as ordinary bishops. If you look to the etymology
of the word, that apostolic power can be called episcopal and the apostolate
an episcopate, as the apostolate of Judas is called an episcopate in Acts 1.
Bellarmine says, *To the point about Judas, I answer that in Psalm 108 it is
called an episcopate—not what we now properly call an episcopate, but any
prefecture whatsoever. For in Hebrew it is pekudah, which signifies visitation
or prefecture. And it is credible that Peter cited this psalm in Hebrew and
adapted that name of prefecture to the apostolate of Judas. But when Luke
reported these things in Greek, he followed the seventy translators, who
rendered it `τὴν ἐπισκοπήν`; by this word the seventy translators could only
have understood a prefecture in general, since in their time the episcopate
properly so called had not yet been instituted. Add that Cicero also uses this
name in book 7 of his letters to Atticus, when he says that Pompey appointed
him bishop of all Campania.* I add from Junius that the prophet wrote properly
against Doeg or some other wicked man and did not in the least look to the
history of Judas, although Peter adapts the prophet's words to Judas's
apostolate. Chemnitz also observes the same things in his *Examination*. *In
the Pandects bishops are officials placed over things for sale. And they more
readily adapted those terms to ecclesiastical ministry because they were then
known and common from the Greek version of the Old Testament. Pakod, pekudah,
and pekudim, which signify visitation, inspection, office, care, an entrusted
administration, and an assigned duty, the Greeks rendered `ἐπισκοπῶν`,
`ἐπισκοπήν`, and `ἐπισκόπους`. In Numbers 13, bishops of the army; in Judith 9,
Abimelech has as bishop*

*Zebul; in 4 Kings 11, bishops who are over the army. In the same place,
guards stationed over the house of the Lord are rendered by the Greeks, He

of the workers are called bishops; in Numbers 4, Eleazar's function or office
in the Lord's tabernacle is called `ἐπισκοπή`; likewise, in Psalm 106, Judas's

Judas's function, differently in his parallels, namely that David did not
prophesy here concerning Judas, but that the prophet's words were only
accommodated to Judas's apostolate by an aetiological kind of accommodation.

passage comes, the Greek word signifies not what we now properly call an
episcopate but any prefecture whatsoever. So far are we from denying that the
apostles had a prefecture that we attribute to them the highest prefecture in
the church under Christ, and we say that this apostolic prefecture was
extraordinary and temporary, higher than every ordinary dignity and power.
Casaubon says, *The fourth mark of the apostolate is a power far greater and
more august than was ever attributed to any other spiritual function. For to
the apostles alone it was said, As the Father has sent me, so I send you,

the blessed Chrysostom treats that passage in the homily which I have
mentioned, in order to express the greatness of the apostolic office he
establishes a comparison of spiritual dignities (which he calls `ἀρχὰς`) with
civil ones (which he calls `ἀξιώματα`), and says that among spiritual powers
the apostolate is a `ὑπατίαν πνευματικήν`, a spiritual consulship; that is, it
stands above all other spiritual functions just as consular power stands
above all civil magistracies.* Therefore, he says, Chrysostom calls the
apostolate the most spiritual of all powers, the power of powers, and,
furthermore, the head, root, and origin or foundation of spiritual powers,
because the apostolate contains all the lower powers which Paul enumerates in
that passage. Augustine says, *Who does not know that this primacy of the
apostolate is to be preferred to any episcopate?* But what does Tilenus the
disciple—or rather Saravia the master—reply to these things? *But you object
that I wrongly mix and confound extraordinary and temporary functions with
ordinary and perpetual ones. Therefore hear the apostle teaching the purpose
for which these offices and ranks were instituted by Christ, the end to which
they are to be referred, how long they are to be retained, and when they will
cease. Thus he speaks: For the restoration of the saints, for the work of the
ministry, for the edification of Christ's body, until we all attain to the
unity of the faith and knowledge of the Son of God, to a perfect man, to the
measure of the stature of the mature Christ, etc., Ephesians 4:12–14. Beza
himself—the founder of your discipline, or at least its restorer and most
vehement defender—testifies that this will happen only in the next age. As
long, therefore, as the church remains in this age, there is a place, just as
for the `καταρτισμῷ`, restoration, of the saints, the work of ministry, and
the edification*

*of the mystical body, so also for those offices and the various ranks of

us legates and
tribunes of Christ of exactly the same kind, whom we are persuaded can be
called `ἀμέσως`, immediately, by none but Christ himself, the commander of this
army,
furnished with the necessary gifts, and thus placed over his forces? Take the
matter, therefore, in this way: In the functions which you maintain were
extraordinary and temporary, we indeed acknowledge with you many extraordinary
and temporary gifts; but it is utterly false that the functions themselves,
taken `οὐσιωδῶς`, essentially, were instituted only for that very brief period
during
which the apostles lived in the flesh. They were indeed called and sent
immediately by Christ, his `αὐτόπται`, eyewitnesses, circumscribed by no fixed
boundaries,
but destined to traverse the whole world and illuminate it with the light of
their proclamation. They were so instructed and governed by the Holy Spirit
that in raising the spiritual building of the church they laid or built
nothing but pure, unalloyed gold, whether in the foundation or in the walls
and roof. In the beginnings of the church, these gifts were plainly necessary
for beginning the work, both for converting unbelievers and for confirming
believers while the rage and savagery of the enemies of the gospel increased.
To these were added other gifts—miracles, healings, tongues, understanding of
the most hidden things, prediction of future things, etc.—but these were also
granted to others and were not peculiar to the apostles. Afterward, however,
when churches had been established and the gospel carried throughout the
world, it was not necessary for those gifts to continue and endure, since the
tablets of faith were at hand for the declaration of doctrine and of the
discipline approved by Christ himself, and since both are sealed even today by
the miracles formerly performed. But the essential parts of the apostolic
office, which consist in the preaching of the word, the administration of the
sacraments, and the government of discipline, ought no more to vanish or be
abolished than Christ's church itself. Even you, Scottish brothers, do not
venture to deny an inequality of ranks between pastors who labor in the word
and presbyters placed over morals, and between these and deacons who bear the
care of the poor. This one thing you cannot bear: that pastor should excel or
preside over pastor, or presbyter over presbyter—as though, when Paul gave
commands to Timothy and Peter, calling himself a `συμπρεσβύτερον`, a fellow
presbyter, gave commands to other presbyters, the former had ceased to be a
pastor and the latter a presbyter; or as though what in the earliest times of
the church, in that golden age of the apostles, was of divine institution,
must today be regarded as a human or even satanic invention.* This is the sum

ranks. He also touches it elsewhere in other words, as we said above, where he
says that the extraordinary must not be confused with the temporary and that
Timothy and Titus succeeded to what was ordinary in the apostolate. *Therefore
both they and many others of the same kind, joined as fellow workers to the*

*apostles, are deservedly called their `συνεργοί`, colleagues, in that which
the apostolate
has as ordinary, essential, and perpetual; and for this reason, in certain
epistles, Paul places now the name of Sosthenes, now of Silvanus, and now of
Timothy together with his own in the superscription.* We treated above the
extraordinary vocation and temporary and extraordinary office, whether the
apostles and evangelists were bishops properly so called or not, and whether
their functions were to remain in the church, pages 140, 141, 142, 172, 173,
and 174. But because Tilenus repeats the same things in more words, we too will
add more. The apostle in Ephesians 4 does not mean that each of those offices
there enumerated is to endure for as long as the church remains in this age,
but that each serves the work of the church's edification until that entire
heavenly city is completed. That work will indeed be in perpetual motion until
all the faithful attain to the unity of the faith and knowledge of the Son of
God, to a perfect man. But in that work of edification some are founders and
others superstructure-builders. It is not necessary for the founders to be
superstructure-builders. Once the foundations have been laid, they have
completed their office, and their work endures in their work by power, though
not by present action. *And the wall of the city had twelve foundations, on
which were the twelve names of the apostles of the Lamb,* Revelation 21:14;
*and the foundations of the city's wall were adorned with every precious
stone,* verse 19. Field says that the apostles are called the church's
foundations for three reasons. First, because they first planted and founded
churches and converted unbelievers to Christianity. Second, because their
doctrine, received immediately from God, is the rule of faith for all
posterity and the immovable foundation upon which the church's faith rests to
the end. Third, because they were the heads, rulers, and pastors of the
universal church, in whom rested not only the supreme but also the first and
original government of the church, from whose broad and ample legation all
ecclesiastical power and authority, although in a lower rank and manner, was to
be derived to successors. You see, therefore, how the apostles served the
edification of Christ's body: as initiators of the work, laying the building's
foundations, upon which the evangelists built and ordinary pastors continue to
build, until the building is completed. When we ask whether we still have
apostles, prophets, or evangelists surviving, or brought back from the lower
world, Tilenus answers that the functions themselves, taken `οὐσιωδῶς`,
essentially,
endure, although many things in the functions were extraordinary and
temporary. He likewise answers that the essential parts of the apostolic
office themselves remain and ought no more to vanish or be abolished than
Christ's church itself. But the essential parts of the apostolic function
were not all ordinary and perpetual; some were extraordinary and some
ordinary. Neither the extraordinary vocation nor the extraordinary gifts are
parts of the apostolic function and ministry. The apostles had a function*

that was extraordinary and `οὐσιωδῶς`, essentially, different from the ordinary
function
of pastors and doctors. *Apostles and pastors, or bishops properly so called,
are distinguished in such a way that an apostle is plainly one thing and a
pastor or bishop another. And the apostle himself,* he says, *gave some to be
apostles, some prophets, others evangelists, and still others pastors and
doctors, Ephesians 4. What could be said more explicitly? He gave some to be
apostles and others pastors and doctors.* This is Sutclivius, one of the
hierarchical party, when about to prove that Peter was not a bishop. If an
apostle is plainly something other than a pastor or bishop, they do not have
all the essential parts of their offices in common; and it would certainly be
to mix and confound everything if we determined that functions plainly
different from one another agree in all their essential parts. Tilenus writes
thus elsewhere: *Peter's office, like that of the rest of the apostles, was
extraordinary and therefore temporary, not perpetual.* He does not say that
the vocation and certain gifts were extraordinary and temporary, but the
office itself. Likewise: *Peter is succeeded either as apostle, or as monarch
and ruler of the whole church, or as minister of the gospel. The apostolate
strictly taken is an extraordinary function, in which succession has no
place. A monarchy outside Christ is fictitious, alien to Peter himself, much
less fitting any successor. The ministry of the word, just as it is an
ordinary function in the church, is in this respect a succession to Peter
common to all pastors.* Tilenus copied these words from Junius's note 18. You
see that the apostolate strictly taken, and distinct from the pastoral office,
is a function in itself and an extraordinary one. It is certainly remarkable
if it is a function in itself but has nothing essential different from the
pastoral office. For what essential element will enter into the definition of
the apostolic function if all the essential parts of the office are common?
Tilenus either deceives or is deceived when he enumerates as essential parts
of the apostolic office the preaching of the word, administration of the
sacraments, and government of discipline. If by the government of discipline
he understands merely governing the church and ruling by discipline, it was
indeed an essential part of the apostolic office but one common to presbyters.
If by government of discipline he understands that supreme power and
prefecture which they had in the universal church above all ordinary pastors
and doctors, so far are we from denying that it is an essential part of the
apostolic function that we number it among the principal parts of the
apostolic office. The fallacy lies chiefly in this part. For that prefecture
and supreme power in the universal church was extraordinary and temporary,
and was by no means to be transmitted to any successors. For succession has
no place in extraordinary things. Succession pertains to order and
perpetuity, but the same thing cannot be both ordinary and extraordinary.
When Bellarmine had said that bishops succeed the apostles not properly, in
the manner that*

one bishop succeeds another and one king another, but by a certain likeness
and proportion—because, just as while Christ lived on earth the twelve
apostles were first under Christ and then the seventy-two disciples, so now
the bishops are first under the Roman pontiff, after them the presbyters, and
then the deacons, etc.—he says that they have no share in true apostolic
authority. For the apostles could preach throughout the whole world, found
churches, write canonical books, possessed the gift of tongues and miracles,
and had jurisdiction over the whole church, but bishops do not have these
things. What did Tilenus answer? *If he had been in his right mind when he
wrote those things, he would surely have considered that those things which
have a place in the ordinary ministry of the church and will remain until the
consummation of the age also pertained to apostolic authority: namely, to
preach the word of God, administer the sacraments, care for discipline, and
whatever things of this kind are perpetually necessary to the whole church
and thus common to all the church's pastors, extraordinary as well as
ordinary. The apostles' peculiar `γνωρίσματα`, marks, are these: 1. immediate
vocation by Christ; 2. immediate instruction by him; 3. `ἀναμαρτησία`,
freedom from error, in doctrine delivered by them either in writing or by the
living voice; 4. `προστασία`, prefecture and authority over the universal
church, however they subsequently divided provinces among themselves for the
convenience of their function.* You see that he numbers among the apostles'
peculiar `γνωρίσματα`, marks, the prefecture and authority over the universal
church, and
distinguishes it from the care of discipline, which here in the *Paraenesis*
he calls the government of discipline and in both places makes common to
ordinary and extraordinary pastors. That prefecture and authority over the
universal church is an essential part of the apostolic office and a certain
extraordinary `ἐπισκοπή`, oversight. *All the apostles, by reason of the
dignity and breadth of their office, presided over the whole world, so that
you may most truly say that the apostles were bishops of the whole world and
that the whole world was the apostles' `διοίκησιν`, diocese,* says Whitaker.
Junius speaks from the same understanding: *The episcopate of Peter, like that
of all the apostles, was catholic, not Roman or bound to the Roman soil or the
Tarpeian rock.* That catholic episcopate was extraordinary, temporary, and of
the essence of the apostolate. Junius says, *For an episcopate properly is
nothing other than an episcopate immediately instituted by God, purely
`θεόπνευστος`, God-breathed, and common—that is, without assignment to a
definite place.* Bellarmine also numbers this episcopate and apostolic
authority among temporary things, except that in Peter alone he imagines a
certain apostolate and universal episcopate which passed to Peter's
successors. *In those beginnings of the church,* he says, *it was necessary,
for the rapid dissemination of the faith throughout the entire world, that
the first preachers and founders of churches be granted supreme power and*

*liberty. But when the apostles died, apostolic authority remained in Peter's
successor alone.* Saravia and his disciples cast off all shame when they say
that apostolic authority continues in hierarchical bishops. Whoever properly
succeeds has equal authority with the one whom he succeeds. Thus a king
succeeding a king has the same authority, and so a proconsul succeeding a
proconsul. *But bishops did not succeed them in those things which the
apostles possessed extraordinarily—that is, in that power which is truly and
properly apostolic. For Bellarmine himself admits that bishops do not have
authority to preach throughout the whole world and to found churches. From
this it can be understood how inconsiderately certain petty theologians assert
that apostolic authority still remains in the church,* says Whitaker. Without
doubt he understands Saravia and his disciples by “petty theologians.” And
what is Tilenus to Whitaker but a petty theologian? Therefore that supreme
prefecture was extraordinary and temporary not only extensively but also
intensively—that is, not only because it was universal and they could found
churches among every people and preside over all pastors by apostolic
authority, but also intensively. For the nature of the apostolic power in the
particular stations where they stayed was no different from that which
belonged to it universally. From what has been said I infer: 1. That
prefecture and supreme power over the universal church and each of its parts,
as occasion required, was indeed an `ἐπισκοπήν`, oversight, if you look to the
word's etymology, but an extraordinary and temporary one, an essential part of
the apostolate, etc., and consequently not to be transmitted to successors.

ordinary, as it includes the presbyterate within itself. For the apostolic
prefecture and supreme power over the universal church cannot be at once
ordinary and extraordinary. Sutclivius says, *They say Peter ordained the
other apostles as bishops, but absurdly and foolishly. For to what end would
those who received all episcopal jurisdiction in the apostolate be ordained
as bishops? Is a king ordinarily created quaestor, or is a lower power
ordinarily granted to the supreme magistrate?* James, remaining at Jerusalem,
did not receive a new power distinct from the apostolic and supreme power in
order to become bishop, but only a determination of the supreme prefecture by
reason of a particular place—even if it were granted that that particular
church, rather than the province of Palestine and the Hebrew Jews, had been
assigned to him. Between the apostolic prefecture and presbyteral power there
is no intermediate power; whatever the apostles could do beyond presbyters,
they did as apostles. Even the presbyterate included in the apostolate was not
in the apostles as it was in presbyters, but in a higher manner and eminently,
as Baynes said above and Field also says in book 5 *On the Church*. They
preached the word not as ordinary presbyters but as apostles, as extraordinary
legates and `αὐτόπται`, eyewitnesses. The sum comes to this: the apostles had*

[Marginalia: *De Pont.* lib. 2, quaest. 8, cap. 3, nota 2.; *De Pontif.* lib. 2, cap. 1.; Pag. 116.]

[1623 printed p. 189 | IA leaf 201 | PDF p. 201]

a certain `ἐπισκοπήν`, that is, a certain prefecture and power over the
universal church and each of its parts, but this was properly apostolic and
belonged to the essence of the apostolate strictly and precisely taken. It was
therefore extraordinary and temporary, and they did not transmit it to any
successors. For it was personal, furnished with extraordinary gifts, and
necessary for a time to found the church on earth and begin the building of
the heavenly Jerusalem. Those, therefore, who argue from the apostolic rank
and the apostles' prefecture in order to establish an episcopal rank and the
bishops' prefecture over presbyters seem to me to rave without reason. Downham
is certainly ashamed to draw an argument from the episcopate of the apostles
in general. He adduces only James, whom he says was ordained bishop by the
apostles; but we showed above that this too is false and impossible. It is
therefore necessary to show, outside the apostles, bishops to whom they
entrusted a prefecture over presbyters; otherwise, how will it be established
that that prefecture was ordinary? Tilenus says that Timothy and Titus
succeeded to what was essential and perpetual in the apostolate. We say that
all pastors have so succeeded. Moreover, in Timothy and Titus there was also
an extraordinary prefecture—though not the supreme one, like the apostolic,
but one lower in rank. Apart from the extraordinary prefectures of both the
apostles and evangelists, they must show in Scripture one example of an
ordinary prefecture over pastors and the flock, if they wish to prove by
divine institution that their bishops are superior to presbyters by a greater
directive and corrective power; otherwise, their labor is in vain. What they
later either claimed for themselves by human institution or usurped does not
pertain to divine right. Is Tilenus, then, sufficiently sane when he says that
without this apostolic prefecture the church must vanish? For either he
understood this prefecture when he treated above the perpetual and ordinary
parts of the apostolic office, or he wandered beyond the olive groves.
Certainly the hierarchical party admits that episcopal government is not so
necessary to the church that without it the church must be abolished. See,
then, how far this sectary has advanced.

Others proceed further and prove from the figure of the Old Testament that
bishops are superior to presbyters in the power of order and jurisdiction by
divine right, as Bellarmine does in his book *On Clerics*. For there was one
high priest, Aaron, and those who succeeded him; there were also the lesser
priests, namely all the sons of Aaron and those descended from them; and,
finally, there were the other Levites, who derived their origin from Levi but
not through Aaron. But bishops, he says, are in the church what pontiffs were
in the Old Testament; presbyters among us are what the lesser priests were
there; and likewise our deacons are what their Levites were, as Damasus teaches
in epistle 3 *On Chorepiscopi*, Jerome in epistle 85 to Evagrius, Leo in the
epistle to*

[Marginalia: Cap. 16.]

[1623 printed p. 190 | IA leaf 202 | PDF p. 202]

the bishops of Germany, and others. Tilenus and other members of the
hierarchical party repeatedly press the polity of the Old Testament, in which
a distinction among ministers was instituted under the economy of the law.
Tilenus says, *There, under one and the same priesthood, we see unequal and
disparate ranks of priests and Levites established. There not only did the
priests stand above the Levites, but among the priests themselves there were
various ranks and orders. In the high priest or pontiff not only the rationale
of type but also of `εὐταξίας`, good order, was manifest. For he did not
typify Christ with respect to the royal and judicial power which Christ has,
since otherwise Christ would have possessed the dignity of king and priest
according to the order of Aaron as well as according to the order of
Melchizedek. Moreover, those who in the Old Testament are called fathers and
heads of the priests, and in the New `ἀρχιερεῖς`, chief priests, were divided
by David into twenty-four `τάγματα`, divisions (the Hebrews call them
machlekoth, mishmaroth, and Pekuddoth; the Greeks `διαιρέσεις`, `ἐπισκέψεις`,
`κλῆροι`, and `ἐφημερίαι`), so that each family in turn might discharge the
ministry during appointed weeks. All these were superior to the lower priests
not only in order but also in power.* Bellarmine argues from types and figures
when he wishes to prove that by divine right the Jewish polity was introduced
into the Christian church. For he says both that the Old Testament church was
a figure of the Christian church and that the Old Testament priesthood was a
figure of the New Testament priesthood. By priests, however, he understands
his sacrificing priests. If Bellarmine's assertion about a proper and
appointed type were true, his argument would have some appearance of divine
right. But it is denied. For the Old Testament church was not a figure of the
Christian church, if we wish to speak properly. *For a figure is not the same
as the thing figured. But the synagogue was no less a church than the assembly
of Christians is today, which is itself also called a synagogue in James 2:2.
The ancient church indeed abounded in types and figures, but it was not for
that reason itself nothing but a type or figure of the Christian church.* Nor
were the priests of the Old Testament types of the sacrificing priests, or
greater and lesser priests, of the New Testament. For then the high priest
would have foreshadowed some ecumenical pontiff, just as the Old Testament
church would have foreshadowed the visible universal church. But as to the
high priest, Tilenus says, *We acknowledge that he was a proper and appointed
type of Christ, and now that Christ has been exhibited we deny that there is a
place for shadows and types of this kind.* Junius says, *Nothing can be
necessarily concluded from a common figure or example, but from a proper
figure it is very well concluded that what was figured really exists or will
exist. In this manner, the high priest of old was a type and proper figure of
the Christ to come, or a legal shadow whose body is in Christ.* The same author
says elsewhere, *From the singular type of Christ's person an argument cannot
be drawn to*

*the rationale of our ordinary ministry; from a singular or personal case a
universal cannot be inferred, nor from a legal ceremony a perpetual
observance.* Our hierarchical party reasons not from an appointed and proper
type, but from a common figure and example. *For one figure is common, which
we call an example, while the other is proper or appointed, which we call a
shadow or type.* In the high priest, he says, one must consider not only the
rationale of type but also of order and polity. For he did not typify Christ
with respect to the royal and judicial power which Christ has, since otherwise
Christ would have possessed the dignity of king and priest according to the
order of Aaron as well as according to the order of Melchizedek. But since all
the saints are priests to God, did not that very eminence of the high priest,
in which they locate order and good arrangement, typify and foreshadow the
supereminent dignity of our high pontiff above all other priests, whose
priesthood flows into all the faithful and makes them priests and pastors in
an ethical, though not political, sense in external government? It does not
follow, however, that Christ would thus be a priest according to the order of
the Aaronic pontiff as well as of Melchizedek. For the high priest's power was
not royal, as Melchizedek's was, and consequently the high priest could not
foreshadow Christ's royal priesthood. The high priest had a certain dignity
and eminence above others even as a priest, though not a royal one. But grant
that in these ranks there was a separate rationale of polity and order,
distinct from every proper type. It still is not necessarily, nor even
probably, concluded from a common figure or example that the same polity and
the same ranks ought to be introduced into the Christian church. Bilson
himself admits that no necessary consequence can be drawn from the different
ranks of priests and Levites under the law of Moses. *For the tribe of Levi,*
he says, *was neither subject to the government of another tribe nor could it
lack all government without manifest `ἀταξία`, disorder. Therefore, like all
the other tribes, this one also had its own magistrates: a prince, elders,
judges, prefects, chiliarchs, and centurions, as was the custom of that
commonwealth.* He adds that the jurisprudence of the Jews, contained in the
books of Moses, comprised the form for governing the commonwealth, and that
the priests and Levites were accordingly placed on the same benches with the
judges as those most skilled in that learning; that this dignity was allotted
to them in their families by hereditary right and in respect of primogeniture;
and that *the ministries of the sanctuary and the rites and ceremonies of
sacrifices, from which all the other tribes except Levi were barred, were not
of one kind, so that it cannot appear surprising that different ranks of
ministers were distinguished according to the diversity of offices and
ministries, although it was always honorable to serve the Lord even in the
lowest. But in the church of Christ, the word and sacraments have been
entrusted without distinction to all pastors; since they are of one kind and
have no difference in administration or celebration, they likewise require no
disparate*

[Marginalia: Iun. *de pontif.* l. 1, ca. 6, not. 14.; *De gubernat.* cap. 2.]

[1623 printed p. 192 | IA leaf 204 | PDF p. 204]

*ranks of ministers.* The twenty-four families served an entire week in the
temple and are called weekly courses—by Luke `ἐφημερίαι`, by the Hebrews
*mishmaroth*, that is, `φυλακαί`, guards, and by the Talmudists stations—both
terms taken from military service, as Joseph Scaliger observed in his
*Isagogical Canons*. The ministry of the Levites serving in the movable
tabernacle is also compared to military service in Numbers 4 because of the
quasi-military order which the priests and Levites observed in the external
ministry. Where there was one common temple, a common priestly ministry, and
thousands ministering in each family, it is no wonder if there was a
subordination of ranks in such a priesthood and ministry. With the priesthood
changed, the law and polity suited to the condition of that church were
changed. I also say that not even a probable consequence can be drawn from the
different ranks in the Old Testament. Bilson says that although a necessary
consequence cannot be drawn, nevertheless the wisdom of God shining in the
Levitical polity must not be repudiated or lightly regarded. We say the same
and add that it must be adored, but not imitated where the same rationale does
not exist. First, because we have neither such a distinction of tribes nor a
common temple and common ministry in one temple for some universal or national
church. Second, because we do not have shadows and types as they did, upon
which that diversity depended. For although we granted that the rationale of
polity was distinct from the type, that diversity nevertheless depended for
the most part upon the type. Since there was one man who above the others
expressed a more vivid image of Christ, was he not for that reason also highest
in dignity in the sacred polity? Third, it is not permissible to introduce
any form of polity whatsoever into the Christian church merely because it is
praiseworthy in itself or elsewhere by reason of certain circumstances. For
that would imply that Christ did not provide as well for the Christian church
as provision had been made for the old one, although Christ *was faithful to
him who appointed him, as Moses also was faithful in all his house,* Hebrews
4:3, 5. Christ did not leave the church like a blank tablet upon which laws
concerning the ranks of ministers serving him in his church might be inscribed
at human discretion. And so much for the consequence of the argument. In the
comparison of ranks itself, one must also see what kind of analogy there is.
Bishops, they say, are in the church what pontiffs were in the Old Testament;
presbyters among us are what the lesser priests were there; and likewise our
deacons are what their Levites were. But *the use of the diaconate did not
exist in the Old Testament, because the law of God had provided for the poor
and afflicted in another way. Therefore our deacons cannot simply be what the
Levites were, but only in a certain respect, or, as they say, in a qualified
sense: namely, because their ministry is dative and accessory, just as the
Levites' ministry was accessory to the priests' work by the Lord's authority,
and because they serve tables.*

[Marginalia: P. 297.; Iunius *de Cler.* cap. 14, not. 13.]

*Moreover, there was among the priests an equal partnership of honor and
power, but unequal order, whether with respect to families or to time. By law,
ordinary ecclesiastical jurisdiction belonged to the assembly of priests, not
to the high priest alone.* But the hierarchical party attributes to a bishop
a superiority of directive and corrective power which the high priest alone
did not have over the lower heads of families, nor those heads over the lower
priests. This analogy is therefore imperfect and consequently does not prove
the point denied, even if we were to grant that a necessary consequence could
be drawn from the ranks under the Old Testament.

There are some who maintain that monarchy is the best form of commonwealth and
therefore should be erected in the church. But Sutlivius asserts that in the
judgment of political writers other forms are to be preferred to monarchy,
especially aristocracy. *They seem to have done this, first, because this kind
of government appears to have been the most ancient and most agreeable to the
order of nature; next, because the people of God first approved it; third,
because God prescribed to the Israelite people not a kingdom but government
by the best men; fourth, because reason commends this form as the best; and,
last, because divine and human testimonies teach that it is to be preferred to
all others.* He says that those who argue from bees, cranes, and herds have no
more wisdom than the beasts of the field; that according to Aristotle man
alone is a political animal; that animals lacking reason are not associated
by a fixed law; and that many kinds of animals are found which wander freely
without a leader. *Most animals are not at all `δεκτικά`, receptive, of any
government among themselves, such as lions, wolves, almost all wild beasts,
and likewise birds and fish. Among land animals, the greatest—the elephant—and
the smallest—the ant—seem to approach more nearly to democracy than to
monarchy. Those which represent some kind of monarchy and are ordinarily ruled
by one, such as oxen, sheep, horses, asses, etc., acknowledge no leader of
their own kind, `ὁμοειδῆ`, but are ruled by human guidance and command,* says
Tilenus. When Homer says that the rule of one is more useful than the rule of
many, he introduces Agamemnon speaking about the administration of military
affairs, which, Tilenus says, *because of sudden occurrences and the
uncertainties of war, is most safely entrusted to the command of one.* But
leaving aside the disputes of political writers, we say that the pattern for
the church's government must be sought nowhere except where it is expressed
in the Scriptures of the New Testament. Otherwise, we would imply that Christ
did not prescribe a form of polity for the church. Spalatensis says, *We ask
what kind of commonwealth Christ actually instituted to be retained
perpetually, not what kind human wisdom—which in divine matters is rather
foolishness—might think should be instituted.*
