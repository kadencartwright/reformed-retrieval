PROPOSITION SEVENTEEN.

ARMINIUS. As for the third question, what is disputed in it is not whether God, foreseeing the sin
of some, prepared for them the punishments they deserved, but whether, foreseeing the sin of those
thus passed over and left in nature, he prepared punishment for them from eternity.

JUNIUS. Those for whose sin God prepared deserved punishments are not elect. Therefore they are
passed over and reprobated. But it was demonstrated earlier that they have been passed over in a
manner befitting God’s wisdom.

ARMINIUS. It is not universally true that those for whose sins God prepared deserved punishments
are not elect. For he also prepared deserved punishments for the sins of the elect, both by laying
them upon Christ so that he might expiate those sins and by sometimes exacting them from the elect
themselves, so that they may learn how they deserved to be treated eternally, and how they would
have been treated if God had not determined to have mercy upon them. But it is true if it is
understood of preparation through the decree with which we are dealing [printed: *decretam*]. For
by that decree deserved punishments are not only prepared for sins but are also actually inflicted
upon sinners and inflicted eternally.

2. Rather, by that decree punishment is prepared for sin not as deserved or due, but as not to be
pardoned through the mercy which remits what is due to some. The order of election and of the
predamnation opposed to it requires this definition. For election pardons deserved and due
punishment. The predamnation opposed to it does not pardon deserved and due punishment. Therefore
it is inflicted through damnation—the execution of predamnation—not as deserved or due, but as not
pardoned.

3. Next, one must distinguish between the preparation of punishment made by God’s just providence
and the preparation of punishment made by the decree of divine predamnation which

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 70 | BSB canvas 82]

is opposed to election. All who repent and believe in the Son escape the former. No one escapes the
latter, because the decree of predamnation is irrevocable and peremptory.

4. But the question is not whether God prepared punishment for those who have been passed over in
a manner befitting God’s wisdom. For who denies that if some have been passed over, as indeed they
have, then they have been passed over in a manner befitting God’s wisdom? Rather, the question is
whether God, foreseeing the sin of those thus passed over and left in nature in the manner
explained, prepared punishment for them by the decree of predamnation. This does not seem probable
to me. I have brought forward arguments to prove this opinion of mine, which we shall now consider.

RESPONSE. When he says “of those thus passed over and left in nature,” he denotes a certain manner
of preterition and abandonment which he by no means recalls here. Its supplement therefore appears
to have to be fetched from the proposition most recently set forth, where it was indicated that
this abandonment consisted in the denial of a certain grace without which the law imposed upon
Adam was not performable; and for that reason he declared that that opinion fell into the former.
Therefore, I say, must this third opinion in like manner fall into the former according to its
present formulation by Arminius? It must. But what point was there, then, in bringing forward three
opinions as though they differed from one another, when he nevertheless frames their states in
such a way that, according to his own declaration, one falls into another?

What of the fact that in his declaration of opinion addressed to the Estates of Holland and West
Friesland, Arminius sets forth this third opinion without any qualification of the sort recalled
here, in the following manner? God, when he willed from eternity to make within himself a decree by
which he would elect certain human beings and reprobate the rest, regarded the human race not only
as created but also as fallen and corrupt, and therefore liable to the curse; that out of this fall
and curse he determined to liberate and save some freely through his grace, for the declaration of
his mercy, but determined by his just judgment to leave the rest in the curse, for the declaration
of his justice; and that he did so without any consideration of repentance and faith in the former
or of impenitence and unbelief in the latter. Add to this that the same Arminius later confesses
that this third opinion better avoids that difficulty—namely, the ordering of sin made by God so
that it should occur—but, in order to entangle its authors in the same difficulty at least by
consequence, he immediately adds: “unless its patrons should say something in their declaration of
predestination and providence from which even the very necessity of the fall could be inferred,
which nevertheless can arise only from some decree of predestination.” Come, then, let us see from
what Arminius wrings this out. “To this,” he says, “pertains first the description of divine
permission by which God permits sin.” To these words he attaches an exceedingly monstrous
definition of permission, without explaining at all from what author he received that description.
He proceeds as follows: “Permission is a withdrawal of divine grace whereby God either does not
disclose to the creature his will by which he wills that act to occur.” An astonishing definition,
I confess. For what? When I perform some act which God has nowhere prohibited, would you say that I
sin? Or, if I do not sin, how could God be said to permit me to sin? Those statements pertain rather
to the permission of an act than to the permission of sin. But they also proceed quite strangely
concerning the permission of an act. For if God has not disclosed to the creature that he wills
that act, how do you suppose that God wills that act to occur, since he has not yet disclosed it?
But only the latter part concerns the present matter: “Or,” he says, “he does not incline the
creature’s will to obey the divine will in that act, and if that very act occurs, the creature
indeed sins necessarily, but nevertheless voluntarily and freely.” Yet Arminius himself immediately
objects to himself that this description does not fit the permission by which God permitted Adam’s
sin, and confesses that this is so when he says: “We accept that this is so; nevertheless,” he says,
“it follows from this description that the other sins occur necessarily.” He thus declares that
this description applies to the sins which followed Adam’s fall, not to Adam’s fall itself. Perhaps
they meant Adam to have been much more capable of inclining his will toward the good in the state
of innocence than even God’s holiest children are today. Because of the flesh lusting against the
spirit, they require not only prevenient grace but also subsequent grace by which they may be kept
from sin. Therefore that argument does absolutely nothing to prove what Arminius intended—namely,
that the authors of this third opinion are burdened by the same difficulty which burdened the
authors of the former opinions. As for his further claim that it follows from this that the other
sins occur necessarily, although it does nothing for the present purpose, I shall nevertheless say
what must be said: this too is false. For one should not say that something follows from an opinion
when the opinion itself expressly declares it. But that opinion, as just quoted, had stated in so
many words that the creature indeed sins necessarily but nevertheless voluntarily and freely.
Therefore it no more follows from that opinion that sins occur necessarily than that they occur
freely. And there is a place where Arminius himself acknowledges that it is necessary that what is
permitted should occur. He also acknowledges that by reason of the divine decree it was necessary
that Christ’s bones should by no means be broken; and what sober person calls into question whether
the soldiers freely abstained from breaking Christ’s bones?

Let us consider the second argument. “Second,” he says, “to this pertains the contention of certain
persons that the declaration of God’s glory, which must necessarily be illustrated, is situated in
the demonstration of mercy and punitive justice. But this,” he says, “cannot occur unless sin—and
through sin, misery [printed: *miseriae*]—enters the world; and in this manner too sin is necessarily
introduced from the necessity of such a demonstration of God’s glory.” “Certain persons contend,”
he says. But what
does this have to do with the matter unless someone can be produced from among the patrons of the
third opinion who argues in this fashion? But who, I ask, are those who would have God’s glory
necessarily illustrated? By the same reasoning they would say that the world had necessarily to be
created, if Arminius should prove this. Yet I believe that he would by no means deny that God
created the world most freely, from which it will follow as established that something can occur
both necessarily and freely. But suppose that God intends a demonstration of his mercy and
punitive justice, and that this demonstration cannot occur unless sin enters the world. Even so,
one can still ask whether that intention preceded the entrance of sin into the world or followed
it. Unless you prove that the authors of the third opinion held that this intention in God preceded
Adam’s first sin, you wander entirely beyond the bounds. But this is by no means proved, nor do you
even make a serious attempt to prove it, conscious, as it seems, that those authors are accustomed
to place the intention of demonstrating mercy and punitive justice only after Adam’s sin. Yet,
contrary to all candor, you so confuse the matters and set them forth so incompletely that you wish
the appearance or suspicion of some contradiction to arise in the mind of an unwary and negligent
reader.

2. As for Junius, what he says is true; but one may doubt whether it is suitably stated for a
proper refutation. The preceding proposition treated the proper framing of the state of the
question, a matter about which Junius is silent.

3. Arminius denies that what Junius had said is universally true—namely, that those for whose sin
God prepared deserved punishments are not elect. If Junius had said “eternal punishments,” Arminius
would have had nothing to criticize. We shall nevertheless see how well Arminius has conducted
himself in his digressions. “He prepared deserved punishments for the elect,” he says, “by laying
them upon Christ.” Therefore, if Junius had expressed his meaning in this manner—“Those for whose
own sins God had prepared deserved punishments are not elect”—as he could easily have done if he

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 71 | BSB canvas 83]

had suspected that he was dealing with so captious an adversary, one who subjected grammatical
turns of phrase rather than the weight of the matters to examination, he would have wholly taken
that exception away from him. Observe in passing, I ask,
the subtle cunning of this doctor. He denied that Junius’s proposition was “universally true.” But
the form of the proposition is not universal but particular, and it cannot be contradicted except
by a universal affirmative. Arminius, however, did not venture to affirm the contrary universally.
He therefore said that it was not “universally true.” But you will reply on Arminius’s behalf that
this proposition has the force of a universal according to Junius’s meaning. I acknowledge this;
but according to Junius’s meaning, which is equally obvious to any reader, it is also universally
true. And Arminius soon confesses that it is true “if it is understood of preparation through the
decree with which we are dealing.” Therefore this proposition is false only with respect to a
decree about which Junius is not speaking. But what harm, I ask, does this do to Junius’s opinion,
provided the proposition holds true of the decree with which the present discussion had to deal?
Whether such thin and trifling things befit a weighty theologian I should like every reader to
consider and judge. At last Arminius adds that by the decree with which we are dealing deserved
punishments are not only prepared but also inflicted. He therefore maintains that something is
prepared through some decree but is nevertheless not actually brought about. In this way he
everywhere labors to introduce novelties into theology. We are accustomed, I confess, to
distinguish a decree from the execution of the decree; but that a decree of God should concern some
future thing which is never executed is a thesis Arminius could easily dictate but could not so
easily establish.

2. At long last he shares with us a specimen of his subtlety. “By that decree”—understand the
decree of reprobation or predamnation, as he later explains—“punishment is prepared for sin not as
deserved or due but as not to be removed through mercy.” He says that the order of election and of
the predamnation opposed to it requires this distinction, and supplies the reason: “For election
pardons deserved and due punishment; predamnation does not pardon it.” From this he concludes: “It
is therefore inflicted through damnation, the execution of predamnation, not as deserved or due
but as not pardoned.” Arminius labored to give birth to this distinction in his response to
Proposition Three, I believe in a dream—for waking people too have dreams—and he has remained
asleep in it until now. But I should very much like to know what point of theology is at last made
clearer by this tiny distinction. What of the fact that the members of the distinction are not so
opposed that the one does not include the other? For insofar as a punishment is said not to be
pardoned, it is thereby signified to be due. Further, what else is it to prepare punishment for a
sinner than to decree that one will not pardon it? Would someone really speak soberly if he framed
the matter in this fashion: namely, that God decreed that he would not pardon the punishment of
certain human beings insofar as it was not to be pardoned? Why, moreover, does he declare that the
order of election and predamnation requires this distinction? Who before Arminius ever wrapped
himself in so ill-starred a distinction? Did this really happen because they had by no means
grasped the order of election and predamnation? Again, let us devise something on the side of
election that corresponds to this because of the order of election and predamnation. Suppose that
election pardons and predamnation does not pardon. Does election therefore pardon a punishment as
one to be pardoned, while predamnation does not pardon it as one not to be pardoned? “It is
inflicted,” he says, “as not pardoned.” The subsequent distinction between a preparation of
punishment made by God’s just providence and a preparation of punishment made by the decree of
predamnation smacks, I confess, of Arminius, but by no means of a weighty theologian. For what?
Is not providence God’s decree? Otherwise preparation would not be ascribed to it. Indeed, an
action of this sort is customarily attributed, and is attributed by Arminius himself, to God’s
decree. But how can anyone escape what has been prepared for him by any decree of God? Will not
what God has decreed to be future come to pass in its own time? Again, it is certainly not the case
that the faithful and repentant escape all punishments; this is how we understood Arminius when he
made the distinction above. I ask, therefore, whether those punishments which the elect undergo
were prepared for them by the decree of predamnation or only by God’s just providence. He does not
say by the decree of predamnation, for no such decree extends to the elect. Therefore they were
prepared by God’s just providence; and therefore the faithful and repentant by no means escape the
punishments prepared by providence.

4. Come, then: from a consideration of your arguments and of our response to those same arguments,
let the intelligent reader judge whether what Arminius attributes to a patron of this third opinion
is probable or otherwise, since the formulation of that opinion smacks more of partisans of the
first opinion than of partisans of the third.
