VERSE 8. The individual things themselves grow weary; no one could express
it. The eye is not satisfied by seeing, nor would the ear be filled by
hearing.

From the attendant weariness of all the pursuits to which a man applies
himself, if you except heavenly things, he proves that happiness cannot be
acquired from any of them. Things that have weariness in themselves cannot
render a man blessed. But all human pursuits have weariness in themselves.
Therefore, and so forth. The proposition is omitted and is manifest in itself.
The assumption is contained in this verse. Here it should be noted that he
says all things are full of tedium and weariness, so that it may be established
as impossible to deny that there are many things from which we recoil in our
minds, yet to which we are driven by a certain necessity. Therefore, in order
to establish what is proposed here, it must be taught that trouble and
weariness are present even in the very things in which we take the greatest
delight. This is argued by an enumeration of the parts. Yet here sight and
hearing are a Synecdoche for all the means by which we perceive pleasure. The
rationale of this Synecdoche is expressed, because no one can encompass them
in discourse. Besides the fact that there are many things from which the eyes
and ears recoil, the very things that flood us with pleasure are not free from
trouble and weariness. For if the things we are held by zeal and desire to see
are not supplied to us abundantly in accordance with our wish and desire, we
are so tormented by longing for them that our condition would have been better
if we had never been given the opportunity to see them and take them in with
our eyes. But if they are supplied in abundance to the point of satiety, we
are seized by a certain disgust at seeing them. The same must be said about
hearing. For if something is pleasant for us to hear, we are so inflamed with
the desire to hear it further that the mind is restless until we satisfy our
desire. But if our ears are often struck with that same thing, we are dulled
by frequently hearing one and the same thing. The same can be said about
taste, smell, and touch, and about other things practiced in human life. The
Athenians, however, with such eagerness for hearing New things

were burning that, through zeal to hear new things, they did not have leisure
even to carry out the duties incumbent upon them by their calling. Acts 17.
And though the pleasure in enjoying these things may remain a little longer,
nevertheless, when illness presses, we are seized by nausea and tedium toward
all of them, so that it is burdensome and troublesome to hear, see, taste, and
smell them. To this belongs what is proclaimed in Solomon's proverb: a
honeycomb is hateful and nauseating to a person who is full, and so forth.
Proverbs 27:7.
See Job 33:19–20. Certainly in old age, the pleasure in all these things that
previously soothed us with a certain delight vanishes. Any happiness that can
be lost or interrupted is unworthy to obtain the name of happiness. But if
there is so much weariness in using those things, how much is there in
acquiring them? By contrast, however, those who are imbued with the true fear
of God are never seized by disgust at the things that nourish and sustain that
fear, nor is their mind ever offended by satiety. See the example of David,
who, though excellently instructed in the Law of God, is led by so intense a
zeal for it as if he had scarcely learned the first elements. Psalm 119:12,
19–20, 33–34. And just as there is no disgust or weariness in the course of
piety, so desire for the part of it that still falls short of completion and
perfection does not sting or burn us, because we are sustained by a sure and
undoubted hope of attaining it. Moreover, the heavenly graces already
obtained, which are not consumed as momentary things are, console us for the
absence of those that are still lacking. To this is added that true piety
equips us both with knowledge and with strength so that we can use those
momentary delights without disgust: it teaches us to be content with present
things and sets a limit upon our affections, which of their own accord run
rampant and extend themselves to infinity. See Philippians 4:11, 13; Hebrews
13:5.
