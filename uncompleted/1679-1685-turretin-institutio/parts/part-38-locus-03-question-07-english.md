QUESTION VII

ON THE SIMPLICITY OF GOD

Is God most simple and free from all composition? Affirmative against Socinus
and Vorstius.

I. The Socinians raise this dispute with us when they deny that simplicity can
be attributed to God according to Scripture and judge that it must be expunged
from the number of the divine attributes, *Racovian Catechism*, ch. 1. Their
end is none other than to undermine the mystery of the Trinity more easily by
positing that God's essence is composite. Vorstius also retained and
interpolated this error along with various others in his *Treatise on God* and
notes on Disputation 3, on the nature of God. The Remonstrants also join them,
for in their *Apology* they deny that God's simplicity is necessary to be
believed or that anything about it is contained in Scripture. Instead, they
say that the whole doctrine is metaphysical, whether one considers the word or
the thing. The orthodox, however, have steadfastly taught until now that God's
essence is supremely simple and free from all composition.

II. A thing is called simple in two ways: either absolutely and simply, or in
a certain respect and comparatively. That which is absolutely simple excludes

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 173 | IA leaf 227 | PDF p. 227]

composition in every genus of being. That which is comparatively simple
excludes composition only with respect to something. Thus heaven and the
elements are called simple bodies in comparison with mixed bodies, but they do
not exclude composition from matter and form and from quantitative parts.
Angels and souls are simple in comparison with bodies, but they too are not
absolutely simple, because they always involve manifold composition. Here the
subject is absolute simplicity, not comparative simplicity.

III. The simplicity of God, insofar as it is taken not morally but physically,
is his incommunicable attribute by which we conceive the divine nature not
only as free from all composition and division, but also as incapable of being
composed or divided.

[Marginal heading: God is proved to be most simple.]

IV. That this simplicity belongs to God is evident: 1. From his independence,
because composition belongs to the formal account of a produced and dependent
being, since nothing can be composed by itself, but whatever is composed must
necessarily be composed by another. Yet God is the first and independent
being, which acknowledges nothing prior to itself. 2. From his unity, because
he who is absolutely one is also absolutely simple and therefore cannot be
divided or composed. 3. From his perfection, because composition entails
imperfection insofar as it presupposes passive potency, dependence, and
mutability. 4. From his actuality, because God is the purest actuality, which
has no potency mixed with it and therefore rejects every composition. For in
God there is nothing that needs to be perfected or receives perfection from
another; rather, he is whatever he can be and cannot be other than what he is.
Hence he is customarily described not only by concrete names but also by
abstract ones: Life, Light, Truth, and so forth.

V. This is evident from the removal of all kinds of composition. There is no
physical composition from matter and form, since he is incorporeal; from
quantitative parts, which do not apply to God; or from subject and accident,
because nothing can accrue to the most perfect being by which he might be
further perfected. There is no logical composition from genus and difference,
because God is above every genus, nor is there in him a common nature that is
restricted by a difference. There is no metaphysical composition from
actuality and potency, since he is pure actuality, knows no properly so-called
change, and can neither have anything accrue to him afresh nor receive anything
from another. Nor is there composition from essence and existence, as there is
in created things, in which the account of essence is one thing and that of
existence another. Their essence can indeed be conceived without existence,
nor does existence enter into their definition, because they can either exist
or not exist and their existence in relation to them is something contingent,
not necessary. But in God essence cannot be conceived without existence, and
it is contradictory to conceive a God who does not exist. Hence philosophers
call him being through essence, that is, one who exists by the power of his
own essence and in whose essential account it lies always to exist. For this
reason God calls himself Jehovah, namely, the one who is, אהיה אשר אהיה, “I am
who I am,” to indicate that being belongs to him by a far different account
than it does to all created things—not participatively and contingently, but
necessarily, properly, and independently. Finally, there is no composition
from nature and subsistence. For the persons and the essence are not related
as real component extremes from which a third thing arises, as among humans
the individual whom we call Peter consists of human nature and Peter's
subsistence. Otherwise, not a Trinity but a certain quaternity would have to
be conceived in God. Nor does it belong to modes, such as the subsistences, to
compose, but only to modify.

VI. Now just as God rejects all composition in himself, so also his simplicity
prevents him from entering into composition with any created things so as to
have the character of some part, matter, or form. Contrary to this, the
Platonists once maintained that God was the soul of the world, and the
Manichaeans contended that all creatures were propagated from God's essence as
though by some transmission;

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 174 | IA leaf 228 | PDF p. 228]

both because he is utterly different from creatures and because he is
immutable and incorruptible and cannot coalesce into one with any mutable and
corruptible created thing. For every composition entails a change by which a
thing becomes a part of a whole when it was not so before.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VII. If all things are said to be from God, Rom. 11:36, this must be understood
not materially (ὑλικῶς), but creatively (δημιουργικῶς) and efficiently. We are
called God's race and offspring, Acts 17:28, not by
participation in the same essence but by likeness of image—efficiently, not
essentially. In the same way he is also called the Father of spirits, Heb.
12:9, with respect to creation, not composition. The Son of God is the
God-man (θεάνθρωπος), not through composition properly so called, but through
the hypostatic union. Through it the Word (λόγος) indeed assumed human nature
into one hypostasis, but he was not composed with it as part with part. Rather,
he is related to it as one perfecting and sustaining is related to an essential
adjunct that is to be perfected and sustained. Thus the human nature is indeed
perfected from this, but nothing is thereby added to the divine nature.

VIII. Where there are several real beings, there is composition, but not where
there are only several modes, because modes only modify and characterize the
essence; they do not compose it. But in the divine there is one essence and
three hypostases. These are modes which indeed distinguish the persons from
one another but do not compose, because they are not real beings that concur
in the composition of some fourth thing, since they have one common essence.
Rather, they are only modifications according to which the essence is
conceived to subsist in three persons.

IX. Simplicity and triplicity are so opposed to one another that they cannot
subsist together, but simplicity and Trinity are not, because they are spoken
of in different respects: simplicity with respect to the essence, but Trinity
with respect to the persons. In this sense, nothing prevents God, who is one in
essence, from being three in persons.

X. God's decrees can be considered in two ways: either subjectively, if it is
permissible to speak thus, that is, on the side of the internal act, in itself,
and in an absolute manner; or objectively, extrinsically, and relatively with
respect to creatures, in the manner of something relative. In the former
manner they do not differ from God himself and are nothing other than God
himself decreeing. In the latter manner, however, they differ, because they are
conceived as many, not on the side of the thing, since God decrees all things
by one most simple act, but on the side of the objects. In the same way God's
knowledge is occupied with innumerable objects without detriment to its unity.

XI. Thus God's decrees are free, not absolutely and on the side of their
principle, but relatively and objectively on the side of their terminus,
because no external object could exist that necessarily determined the divine
volition. For God needed nothing outside himself. Thus the decrees could exist
or not exist. Yet this does not prevent them from being called necessary with
respect to their principle and internal act, because acts of understanding and
willing could not be altogether absent from God. God could not exist without
understanding and willing. They are therefore necessary with respect to their
internal existence, but free with respect to their relation (σχέσιν) and
disposition outward. Nor is God's volition said to cease absolutely, but with
respect to the external object at which it terminates.

XII. God's decrees are immanent acts of the divine will, but they are not
properly its effects; and God should be called not so much their cause as their
principle. Hence they need not be posterior to God except in order and in our
manner of conceiving.

XIII. Although God's essence, considered simply in itself, is absolute and
implies no relation to creatures, this nevertheless does not prevent

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 175 | IA leaf 229 | PDF p. 229]

that same essence, if it is considered in relative opposition to creatures and
insofar as it determines itself as a vital principle to produce this or that
outside itself, from having some relation (σχέσιν) and reference to creatures.
Nor can that manifold relation produce composition in God any more than the
reference that omniscience and omnipotence have outwardly establishes a real
distinction between God and his omnipotence and omniscience.

XIV. Whatever is essential and absolute in God is God himself. Thus the
absolute attributes are really identified with the divine essence and are in
him essentially, not accidentally. If they are predicated of God in the
concrete, their subject is only one of denomination, not of inherence. But
whatever is personal and modal in God is indeed God himself in the concrete,
but not in the abstract.

XV. The relative attributes do not imply composition, but distinction,
because the formal account of relations is not to be in but to be toward. Nor
do they add a new perfection to the essence, but imply only the essence's
relation to other things. Paternity and dominion do not make the possessor
another, but dispose him otherwise without any change in the related term
itself.

XVI. The Son's personal property does not cause his essence to be other than
the Father's essence or cause the essence to become composite from simple. For
it adds nothing real to the essence, but only causes the Son to be distinct
from the Father; and distinction is not composition.

XVII. The Fathers often inculcate this simplicity of God.
Augustine, *On the Trinity* 5.1: “God's nature is simple, immutable, and
undisturbed; nor is he one thing, and what he has and what he is another.” And
in book 6, ch. 6, after teaching that no creature is truly and perfectly
simple, he adds: “But although God is called manifold, he is nevertheless
truly and supremely simple. For he is called great, wise, blessed, and true,
and whatever else seems to be said of him not unworthily; but his greatness is
the same as his wisdom. For he is great not in mass but in power, and his
goodness is the same as his wisdom, greatness, and truth. Nor is his being
blessed one thing and his being great and wise, or true, or good, or in sum not
existing another.” So Athanasius in his *Epistle to the African Bishops*: “God
is not composite, who composed all things so that they might exist, nor is he
such as the things made through his Word are. For he is simple substance, in
which there is neither any quality nor, as James testifies, any variation.”
