PARAPHRASE

OF THE BOOK

OF SOLOMON,

which is entitled

ECCLESIASTES.

CHAPTER I.

VERSE 1. The words of Ecclesiastes, son of David, king at Jerusalem.

So that the distinguished doctrine contained in this little book may receive
greater attention, I wish everyone to understand that this doctrine was
written by one who, on account of his sins—especially idolatry—had lived as an
exile from the Church, but through repentance (which he professed in this
book) joined himself again to her bosom by the ministry of the Prophets and
Priests. It was, moreover, written by one who was the son of that most famous
and celebrated man David; likewise by one who was a king; and finally by one
who exercised royal authority among the people of God.

VERSE 2. Uttermost vanity, says Ecclesiastes, uttermost vanity; all these
things are vanity.

The sum and chief point of this doctrine comes to this: all things under the
Sun, and everything that human skill or industry can seek out or accomplish,
are utterly vain, because they cannot actually furnish and fulfill the things
for which they were sought and obtained. Thus anyone who wishes to be and be
accounted blessed must lift his thoughts and pursuits above the Sun to the
highest heaven.

VERSE 3. What advantage comes to a man from all his labor at which he labors
under the Sun?

For it is plain that no advantage accrues to anyone from all the grievous
labor at which he toils beneath this spacious circuit of the Sun; so far is a
man from making himself blessed by his labors.

VERSE 4. One generation departs and another comes, although the earth remains
forever.

And even if some advantage toward blessedness should come from it, yet since
a man, hindered by death, cannot long enjoy it, how can he rightly be accounted
blessed because of these things? For when all the people now living have
yielded to death within a hundred years, more or less, another age and
another generation succeeds them, while the earth meanwhile remains firm and
fixed forever.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA3 | printed pp. 3–4]

VERSE 5. The Sun rises, and the Sun sets; and it hastens back to its place,
where it rises.

The heavenly bodies also—and the Sun above all—rise in the morning and set in
the evening, returning every day in a fixed sequence and with the greatest
speed to the place where their rising began.

VERSE 6. It hastens toward the south and circles toward the north; the air
hastens as it circles in its course, and the air returns according to its
circuits.

The wind likewise goes toward the south, circles toward the north, and turns
back again in a circuit.

VERSE 7. All rivers hasten to the sea, yet the sea is not filled; to whatever
place the rivers hasten, there they speedily return.

Although all rivers and streams empty themselves into the sea, the sea is not
therefore filled, because the waters that flow into it return again to the
rivers and streams from which they issued, through the pores and passages of
the earth, where they lay aside their saltiness. Thus the four elements of
which the human body consists and is composed, both all together and each
separately, possess a firmer and more enduring condition than the man himself
who coalesces and is constituted from them.

VERSE 8. All these things are wearisome; no one could express it. The eye is
not satisfied with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing.

All things under the Sun are full of trouble and disgust, even those in which
we take the greatest delight; and they are so numerous that no one could
encompass them in speech. Therefore, to leave that infinity aside, I shall
give an example from certain things that bring the greatest pleasure. The eye
is not satisfied by seeing, nor is the ear filled by hearing; and the same may
be understood concerning the rest (for it would be a long journey to go
through them all).

VERSE 9. What has been is the same as what will be, and what is done is the
same as what will be done; there is nothing new under the Sun.

It is not surprising that the eyes and ears are not satisfied, since there is
nothing new and unusual under the Sun by which we might be uniquely
delighted. For nothing has existed in any age which will not exist hereafter,
nor has anything been done in former times which will not be done hereafter;
thus there is nothing new under this Sun.

VERSE 10. Is there anything of which it is said, Look, this is new? It already
existed throughout the ages which were before us.

I would not indeed deny that something may be found which someone, deceived
in his opinion, declares to be new and never before seen or heard. Yet it is
certain that it existed in the ages gone by which preceded us.

VERSE 11. There is no remembrance of former things; nor will there be any
remembrance among those who come afterward of the later things that are yet
to be.

The only cause of this imagined and supposed novelty is that past things are
not remembered, because of the brevity of life and frailty of memory. In the
same way, among the descendants who succeed us there will be no remembrance
of the things that now exist. Hence something will appear new and unusual
to them which was nevertheless known in ages long past.

VERSE 12. I, Ecclesiastes, was king over Israel at Jerusalem.

Thus far the vanity of things has been discussed in general. It follows that
I should set out various opinions concerning the Highest Good—not all of
them, for who could set them all out in speech?—but the principal ones, and first
those which I learned by my own experience. And so that my discourse may have
greater authority, it must be remembered that when I investigated these
things I had returned to the bosom of the Church, from which I had lived in
exile because of my sins; furthermore, that I possessed royal dignity and was
placed over that people whom God chose peculiarly for himself; and that I was
in Jerusalem, the city which was the oracle of the whole world. No
assistance, therefore, was lacking by whose aid I might attain what I pursued.

VERSE 13. Applying my mind to seek out and explore wisdom concerning
everything done under the heavens (God gave this evil occupation to the sons
of men, that they might occupy themselves in it).

With the greatest care and diligence I applied my mind to investigate and
explore, by an orderly method and reason, rather than rashly and confusedly,
everything done beneath the spacious expanse of this heaven. Nor did I rashly
thrust myself into this pursuit without God’s calling, since God has commanded
mortals to occupy themselves in this work, which is full of troublesome
labor.

VERSE 14. I saw all the deeds that are done under the Sun; and behold, all
these things are vanity and affliction of spirit.

I therefore considered all God’s works created by God under this Sun, so that
I might know their causes and effects. But behold—something almost incredible
to say—so far was I from obtaining solid happiness from them that, on the
contrary, I discovered all these things to be vain for establishing it. I add
that they even produced torment and torture of the mind.

VERSE 15. What is crooked cannot be corrected, and what is lacking cannot be
brought into the number.

For although many things in human life are crooked and distorted, and
countless things are lacking in which piety and uprightness of life consist,
nevertheless this knowledge of natural things neither restores what is
crooked to the standard of rectitude nor supplies what is lacking.

VERSE 16. I spoke with my mind, saying: Behold, I have enlarged and added to
wisdom above all who ruled at Jerusalem before me, and my mind has perceived
much wisdom and knowledge.

If anyone should object that I failed to attain happiness in these and the
other things that follow, not through any fault of the things
themselves to which I devoted my effort, but through the weakness of my own
understanding, which failed to grasp what the things themselves abundantly
supplied, I want him to understand that I can truthfully affirm without
boasting, with my conscience bearing witness to me, that I surpassed in both
the magnitude of my resources and the abundance of my wisdom all those who
held the scepter at Jerusalem—to say nothing of the common people. Thus I
embraced ample wisdom and knowledge in my mind.

VERSE 17. I also applied my mind to knowing wisdom and the knowledge of every
kind of madness and folly; I learned that this too is affliction of spirit.

When I did not attain happiness in the contemplation and pursuit of Nature,
by which truth is distinguished from falsehood, I applied my mind to that
wisdom and knowledge by which good is distinguished from evil and justice
from injustice.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA7 | printed pp. 7–8]

To make greater progress in this undertaking, I considered in my mind every
kind of madness and folly, which are contrary to knowledge and wisdom, since
opposites placed beside one another become more evident. Finally, as in my
earlier investigation of Nature, I experienced that this pursuit likewise
brings not only no happiness but even sorrow and anguish of spirit.

CHAPTER II.

VERSE 1. For in an abundance of wisdom there is much vexation, and he who
increases knowledge increases grief.

Having learned by experience that very great trouble adheres to the
investigation of Wisdom, I also learned that sorrow and sadness of mind
increase in proportion to increased knowledge.

VERSE 2. I said to my mind, Come now, let me test you with gladness; therefore
enjoy the good. But behold, this too is vanity.

I resolved deep within my mind to enter upon another way of acquiring
happiness. Come now, then, my soul: why torture yourself by searching through
the treasures of knowledge and wisdom? Since that way did not succeed, I will
undertake another and test what you can attain by gladness. Therefore enjoy
pleasure of every kind: eat, drink, play; be cheerful and indulge your
appetite. For I resolved not merely to taste and sip lawful delights, but to
pour myself, as it were, into them, so that if any happiness could be attained
from them, I would attain it—especially since I was equipped with
incomparable abundance of every resource for every kind of honorable and
lawful pleasure. But behold, just like those former pursuits, this one too was
full of vanity. Thus you may readily know that the attempts of everyone else
who pursues happiness in bodily pleasure are fruitless.

VERSE 3. Of laughter I say, It is insane; and of gladness, What does it
accomplish?

Therefore, intending to protest and indignant that I had spent some portion
of time pursuing pleasure, I said to unrestrained laughter: You are indeed
insane, since you weaken reason and the light of understanding implanted in
human beings and drive them to madness. Likewise bringing a suit against
gladness, which produced the laughter, I said: What do you accomplish? What
advantage is perceived or derived from you?

VERSE 4. I explored with my mind by continuing drinking in my flesh, while
guiding my mind in wisdom. I explored by embracing folly until I should see
whether this is the good for the sons of men to do under the heavens during
the number of the days of their life.

I therefore continued in the pursuit of pleasures and applied my mind to
delicacies and delights in food and drink, so that I might soften the pains
which the very pursuit of wisdom brought. Yet I determined not to immerse
myself in them so far that I did not think the pursuit of wisdom—especially
of piety—should be joined to this pursuit of pleasure, which I eventually
discovered to be folly. By wisdom’s aid and benefit I would force pleasure,
otherwise wanton, within due order and the circuit of reason. I determined to
follow this pursuit only until I had discovered whether this was that good
which abundantly contained within itself everything by which someone may be
made blessed for as long as he lives here on earth; for I was certain that
another happiness is laid up for us in the age to come.

VERSE 5. I made very great works; I built houses for myself and planted
vineyards for myself.

To obtain the supports of this pleasure-loving life, I undertook great and
magnificent works. For I was not content with the things—royal and splendid,
to be sure—which my father left me when he died. I therefore constructed the
most spacious and stately houses, and planted vineyards from which the most
generous and delicate wine was supplied to me and my household.

VERSE 6. I made gardens and orchards for myself; in them I planted every kind
of fruit-bearing tree.

I made gardens for myself, filled with every kind of flower and herb, from
which the most fragrant and delightful scents were supplied. I also planted
the most delightful orchards, like paradises, and in them trees fruitful in
every kind, which produced fruit both in great abundance and of singular
quality, pleasing to the palate.

VERSE 7. I made pools of water for myself, to irrigate with them a grove
abounding in trees.

I dug out and excavated pools and fishponds, so that they might supply both
fish and water for irrigating the gardens and orchards. Those orchards and
gardens were so great and expansive that they seemed in some measure to equal
woods and forests.

VERSE 8. I acquired male and female servants, and I had servants born in my
house; I also possessed many herds and flocks, above all who were before me at
Jerusalem.

Although many home-born servants were born within my household, I also
purchased male and female servants, partly for cultivating the earth and
tending livestock, and partly for entertainment. Likewise I acquired herds
and flocks by which I might provide my household servants with food and bodily
maintenance, and these in such abundance as had never fallen to the lot of
anyone dwelling at Jerusalem.

VERSE 9. I also amassed silver and gold for myself, and the treasure of kings
and provinces; I acquired male and female singers for myself, and finally the
delights of the sons of men—music, and indeed of every kind.

I further amassed a great quantity of silver and gold, as well as precious
stones, spices, and costly ointments which kings and provinces brought to me
as honorific gifts when they were brought under my dominion. I also acquired
male and female singers, the delights of human beings, and finally musical
harmonies and every kind of concert.

VERSE 10. Thus I became great and increased these things more than anyone who
was before me at Jerusalem; my wisdom also remained with me.

I so increased all these things in number and magnitude that I surpassed
everyone who had ever lived at Jerusalem. Though surrounded by all these
pleasures, I did not depart from wisdom, which I had imbibed in early life as
though with my nurse’s milk; nor did I depart from the wisdom I had acquired
in the contemplation and investigation of Nature. Thus I was not deceived or
diverted from the design I had set before myself. For just as the enjoyment of
pleasure did not take wisdom from me, neither did wisdom restrain my pleasure.

VERSE 11. And since I did not withhold from my eyes anything they desired,
nor restrain my mind from any gladness, but my mind rejoiced in all my labor,
and this was my portion from all my labor.

For when these things already mentioned and other entertainments of that kind
had been acquired, I continued to use them to such an extent that I denied
nothing to my eyes and restrained nothing which my mind desired.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA11 | printed pp. 11–12]

I was hindered neither by disease, nor war, nor death, nor any ill humor of my
mind—as nevertheless often happens—from using all these things obtained by my
great labor lavishly, to the highest delight of my mind. For this one thing
remained to me as my portion from all the labor I spent in obtaining them.

VERSE 12. I looked back upon all my works which my hands had made, and upon
the labor I had laboriously performed; but behold, all these things are vanity
and affliction of spirit, and there is no advantage under the Sun.

When I had considered all these things with myself and in my mind, relating on
the one side the works I had completed to the labor and trouble I spent in
completing them, and on the other side my thought to the pleasure I had
received, I reached this sum: all these things brought vanity in place of the
happiness I sought, affliction of mind in place of its tranquility, and
nothing at all in place of a reward for my labor.

VERSE 13. Therefore I turned myself to behold wisdom and every kind of madness
and folly (for what could the man do who followed the king? Only what has
already been done).

Therefore I turned my mind and thought to consider carefully the nature of
wisdom and, at the same time, of folly—or rather of madness—so that I might
render a still more exact judgment about them. If anyone should charge me
with rashness because I wish to pronounce upon both wisdom and the
pleasure-loving and therefore foolish life merely from my own experience, I
want him to understand that no mortal will be found who can surpass me—a king
of such wisdom, power, and wealth—in knowing these things. For even if they
put forth all their strength and sinews with the greatest effort, they will
nevertheless accomplish nothing that was not done by me before.

VERSE 14. I certainly saw that wisdom has an advantage over folly, as light
has an advantage over darkness.

Therefore, to undertake a fuller explanation of my judgment: when I embraced
the pursuit of wisdom and the pleasure-loving life within one and the same
circuit of vanity, I do not wish this to be understood as though there were no
difference between the wise man and the lover of pleasure, who is also a
fool. For there is as wide a difference between folly and even worldly and
perishable wisdom as there is between light, by whose benefit individual
things are distinguished from each other, and darkness, by which everything
is mixed together in disorder and confusion.

VERSE 15. The wise man is like one whose eyes are in his head, but the fool
walks in darkness; yet I likewise recognized that the same outcome comes to
all these.

For the eyes of the wise man’s mind are situated in his head as in a citadel,
from which he sees and makes provision for everything far off. But the fool,
however much the eyes of his body are illuminated, is nevertheless blind in
the eyes of his mind and, wandering rashly here and there as in a dark night,
stumbles everywhere. Yet wisdom’s power is not so great that it can govern the
outcomes of things. Just as wise and foolish differ greatly in one respect,
so in other respects they agree miserably; for I know well that in many things
one and the same outcome comes both to the wise man and to the fool.

VERSE 16. Therefore I said in my mind, The same outcome as comes to the fool
will also come to me; why then should I become wiser any longer? And I said in
my mind that this too is vanity.

Having therefore firmly established in my mind that the same thing which
comes to the fool also comes to me, though I surpass everyone in wisdom, I
began to ask myself why I had so greatly tormented myself in the pursuit of
wisdom.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA13 | printed pp. 13–14]

At last I concluded that this too was not far removed from utter vanity.

VERSE 17. For there will be no everlasting remembrance of either the wise man
or the fool, since in the days to come all the things that now exist are
delivered to oblivion; and the wise man himself dies with the fool.

To omit the many things in which the outcome is the same and common to the
fool and the wise man, this in particular is worth lamenting: the memory of
the wise man perishes just like the fool’s, since everything done in past
times is covered by oblivion in following ages. To this is also added that the
wise man dies just like the fool.

VERSE 18. Therefore I hated this life, because the work done under the Sun
seems evil to me; for all these things are vanity and affliction of spirit.

When I had learned by experience that the wise and the foolish meet the same
outcome, I became weary of my life, since everything done under the Sun seemed
troublesome to me, and all things were utter vanity and torments of mind.

VERSE 19. I also hated all my labor at which I labor under the Sun, because I
will leave it to the man who will come after me.

For this very reason, just as earlier, in my hatred of life, I followed the
guidance of corrupt reason and was overcome by a certain disgust, so I became
weary of the labor and trouble I endured while living on earth to obtain those
magnificent works. Especially since this is also added to the former
disadvantages: I must leave the things gained by my sorrowful labor to another
who will succeed me when I am dead.

VERSE 20. And who knows whether he who will rule over all my labor which I
have performed, and which I have acquired wisely under the Sun, will be wise
or foolish? This too is vanity.

And although it is grievous to the flesh not to be able to enjoy what I
obtained by labor, it is still more grievous and sorrowful that I am uncertain
whether the man who, at his own discretion, will obtain possession of
everything I acquired during this life by grievous labor and by employing
wisdom in the conduct of affairs, will be wise or foolish. And this too, like
the other
things I have mentioned, is full of vanity.

VERSE 21. Therefore I turned away, causing my mind to abandon hope concerning
all this labor which I performed under the Sun.

Therefore I exerted all my mind’s strength and sinews to cast away every hope
of receiving fruit from the labor with which I wearied myself in this life—I
mean the fruit which I had expected and promised myself.

VERSE 22. For there is a man whose labor is performed with wisdom, knowledge,
and equity; yet he must deliver it as a portion to a man who did not labor in
it. This too is vanity and a great evil.

Especially when added to those former disadvantages, I have discovered by
experience that a man, with wisdom and knowledge as his guides, and innocence
and integrity also as his companions, repeatedly acquires riches by grievous
labor, yet those who never spent any labor at all in acquiring them come into
their possession. This too is utter vanity and must be counted among great
evils.

VERSE 23. For what remains to that man from all his labor and from the
affliction of his mind, with which he labored under the Sun?

For absolutely nothing remains to this man from all the troublesome and
oppressive labor and anguish of mind which he undertook in this life.

VERSE 24. Since pain and vexation occupied him all his days, and his mind did
not rest even at night? This too is vanity.

This circumstance strikes him all the more grievously because the whole
course of his life has been spent in anguish and vexation, to such a degree
that, even when his body rested at night, his mind was not free from cares.
This likewise is completely full of vanity.

VERSE 25. It is not within a man’s power to possess this good: that he should
eat and drink and cause his mind to enjoy good from his labor; I have also
seen that this comes from God’s own hand.

Human misery in things acquired by labor does not consist merely in the fact
that a person often leaves them to fools or that unjust masters seize them by
force or fraud. Even while he retains all of them in his possession, it is not
within his own power and choice to possess this good: to use them. From the
great variety of the advantages of the present life, I will offer the single
example of eating and drinking. For so far is he from being able to flood his
mind with pleasant
and delightful enjoyment by eating and drinking, that even to eat or drink is
not within his power, because of the many things that happen between the cup
and the lips. For although these things may appear trifling and easy to do,
experience has taught me that they proceed from God’s hand or power and are
not placed within human power.

VERSE 26. For who would enjoy it, and who would immediately enjoy it, apart
from me?

For who is equipped with such an abundance and variety of foods and
condiments, and imbued with such wisdom for driving away the illnesses by
which an appetite for food is removed, as I am, who have experienced this
powerlessness in myself? Who, I ask, can exert all his mind’s sinews and
outstrip or surpass me?

VERSE 27. For to the man who appears good in his sight, God gives wisdom and
knowledge with gladness; but to the sinner he gives the occupation of
gathering and amassing, so that he may deliver it to the one who will appear
good in his sight. This too is vanity and affliction of spirit.

This human powerlessness appears more clearly because God bestows upon the man
whom he embraces by free grace the wisdom by which he obtains the necessities
for the food and maintenance of life; the knowledge by which he knows how to
use them; and the gladness by which he may pleasantly enjoy them. This becomes
still clearer from its opposite. For to the man who is a slave of sin God
indeed gives the wisdom by which, constantly occupied,
he gathers and amasses great wealth and treasures. Although the man himself
sets before himself this end for his accumulation—that by living comfortably
from these things he might transmit what remains to his descendants—God’s
purpose, which alone will stand, is nevertheless that everything should
devolve upon the man whom God holds dear by his free love. And this inability
to use and enjoy things gained by labor is utter vanity and vexation of
spirit.
