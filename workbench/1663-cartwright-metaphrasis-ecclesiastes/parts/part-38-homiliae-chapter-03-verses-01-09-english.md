CHAPTER III.

VERSE 1. To everything belongs its own season, and there is a time for every
purpose under heaven.

VERSE 2. A time to give birth, and a time to die; a time to plant, and a time
to uproot what was planted.

VERSE 3. A time to kill, and a time to heal; a time to break down, and a time
to build.

VERSE 4. A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to
dance.

VERSE 5. A time to cast stones away, and a time to gather stones; a time to
embrace, and a time to withdraw far from an embrace.

VERSE 6. A time to acquire, and a time to lose; a time to keep, and a time to
cast away.

VERSE 7. A time to tear, and a time to sew; a time to be silent, and a time to
speak.

VERSE 8. A time to love, and a time to hate; a time of war, and a time of
peace.

VERSE 9. What profit is there for the one who acts in that at which he himself
labors?

The summary is that all things which happen under the sun are subject to
change and the vicissitude of times, and for that very reason are subject to
vanity.

The parts are: 1. All things are subject to change, in eight verses. 2. The
corollary drawn from this, verse 9.

The former part contains two things: first, the general axiom concerning the
mutability of all things; second, the proof of the axiom by an induction of
its parts.

From verse 12 of the first chapter down to the opening of this chapter,
Solomon has confirmed from his own experience the vanity of everything under
the sun. Those facts would have been abundantly sufficient to eradicate vain
hope in perishable things, if we were not wretchedly infatuated with them and
bewitched by love of them. But the Holy Spirit, who writes through him and is
aware of our utterly vain vanity, proceeds to confirm that same vanity from
the things which Solomon experienced and observed outside himself in others.

From this we are taught first to look at ourselves and examine the things
which are either done by us or happen to us, especially those which can train
us in humility of mind and contempt of the world. Then we are to turn our mind
and thought to the things discerned in others. For our own things, being as it
were nearer to us, are known more certainly and affect us more powerfully than
the things of others. Hence the apostle says in Galatians 6:4, “Let each one
examine his own work, for then he will have a ground for boasting in himself
alone, not in another”; and in verse 5, “For each one will bear his own
burden.” Nearly everyone sins in this matter, for they look at the affairs of
others rather than

their own. They are no different from women who are inquisitive about other
families but dissolute in their own households. What is still worse, we
readily look at and ponder the good things within ourselves, passing over
those things whose consideration ought to train us in humility. In others,
however, we are especially intent upon the things found blameworthy, while
blind to those things worthy of praise which ought to stir us both to
imitation and to lowliness of mind.

This, however, must be understood so that it does not conflict with Peter's
counsel, by which he earnestly exhorts us away from the practice of `τὸ
ἀλλοτριοεπισκοπεῖν`, “meddling in the affairs of others.” For it is one thing
to observe the deeds of others and God's `ἐνέργειαν`, “working,” upon them—to
observe what resulted from things rightly done and what from the contrary,
and how they fell from wealth into poverty and from the summit of honor into
disgrace and contempt. It is another thing to thrust oneself into the affairs
of others when those affairs have no bearing upon oneself.

Then—and this is the chief point in this passage—he teaches that each thing
has its time, appointed by God: a time at which it begins to exist, a time for
which it continues in its own being, so to speak, and a time at which it
ceases to be. Thus there is nothing under the sun which does not receive, just
as it does its origin and progress, its destruction at a time both fixed and
determinate. The Euripus, which reverses its flow seven times a day, possesses
no greater changeableness and inconstancy than the things which are done here
under the sun. Hence every man is said to be altogether vanity, even when he
stands most firmly and enjoys affairs flowing according to his wish.

See Psalm 30:7–8, where a man is said to pass by as an image. He is said to be
an image or an idol rather than a true reality; likewise, a dream when someone
awakens; elsewhere, a shadow and a vapor which appears and vanishes. The
apostle affirms this of the whole world in 1 Corinthians 9, saying that it is
more truly appearance and `σχῆμα`, “form,” than some solid and permanent
reality. From this the apostle also derives the teaching that we should
neither be elated by prosperity nor cast down by adversity.

For who is so foolish and inconsiderate that he takes substantial joy in an
image as though in the welfare of the commonwealth whose image it is? Nor is
anyone so dull that, because he imagined in a dream that he would obtain a
kingdom, he therefore exults when upon waking he finds himself bound with iron
chains. Yet such is the condition of this world. Hence its conditions are
also compared to masks and masked actors on a stage. There an abject and
lowly man sustains the character of a king for a very little time, but lays it
aside as soon as the play is over. Another puts on the character of a master,
but once that character is laid aside he is soon recognized as a servant or
slave—and conversely.

When Solomon says that there is a time for every purpose, “purpose” is a
metonymy for anything which a man wills, plans, and decides. Even if he attains
it according to his wish and the desire of his mind, it nevertheless has a
certain time of its own: after remaining for that time, it soon passes away
and ceases to exist. Indeed, what is still more, it is often changed into its
opposite, as will be seen later.

That the time in which everything happens and is changed either for the
better or for the worse is also appointed by God is a teaching drawn from the
most deeply hidden and abstruse treasures of divine government. For there is
nothing so great or so small concerning which God, in his secret counsel
before the foundations of the world were laid, did not decree what would
happen and appoint the time and place in which the things were to be
accomplished. This is the deep of wisdom, the sea or abyss of divine
knowledge, whose ways cannot be traced and whose judgment cannot be searched.
It is the sea whose bottom is never reached.

This teaching utterly overthrows the idol of fortune and chance. While it
magnifies God's benefits toward his own people, it softens the bitterness and
harshness of calamities in adversity, even when those adversities have been
brought upon us by our own wrongdoing and negligence. Toward the ungodly,
however, it makes clear the severity of God's judgments.

Thus far concerning the first part.

The second part follows, concerning the confirmation of this axiom by an
induction of its parts. Here this general point must first be maintained: this
passage does not teach what ought to be done by God's command, but what, by
divine government and according to God's eternal decree, happens and comes to
pass in human affairs. Hence those men reason inopportunely from this passage
who wish to use it to promote dances and defend dancing. For what follows?
Is there some time at which a man is bound by duty to lose or destroy what he
possesses, just as he is bound to seek a thing which has been lost? Or is
there some time at which a man ought to hate another, just as he ought to
embrace him in love?

To this is added the fact that the word to which they attribute the meaning
“dancing” is the same word applied to frolicking calves, rams, and lambs.
Thus it is readily apparent that this dancing requires no art for regulating
one's steps, but denotes only joy revealed by some gesture of the body. The
same reply can be made to those who use David's dancing before the Ark as a
pretext for defending their own dances, for the same word is used there too.
Besides, it is improper to divert David's religious dance, by which he
rendered worship to God, into a defense of play and sport.

Next it must be noted that this change often proceeds from one contrary into
the other, so that not only are the things which we presently possess and
enjoy taken away from us, but we often plunge headlong into a condition of
life wholly contrary to the former one: from the greatest wealth into the
greatest and most extreme poverty, from complete fullness into hunger, and
from the summit of honor into the most abject and contemptible condition—and
conversely. Just as this change ought to compose us to moderation when we
enjoy prosperity, since it is easy to be stripped of that prosperity, so it
also ought to raise and encourage our minds toward the hope of better fortune.
Thus we need not be crushed beneath too great a weight of evils while there
remains the hope of one day emerging from evils and attaining the contrary
good.

Moreover, in this enumeration of the parts Solomon sometimes begins with
things that are sought and ends with things from which men recoil; conversely,
he sometimes begins with things that men flee and ends with things they
desire. By this he seems to indicate the order and `εὐρυθμίαν`, “harmonious
arrangement,” which God employs in governing the world. To some he first
offers honey to taste and afterward gives gall to drink; to others he offers
vinegar to drink and afterward refreshes and revives the same people with new
wine. In his incomparable wisdom he governs all of this so as to direct
everything to his own proper glory and to the salvation of his people.

David bore the Lord's yoke from boyhood and walked sorrowfully under God's
hand for many years; afterward, when he had become a man, he was refreshed by
God's many blessings. By contrast, Peter girded himself in his youth and
lived according to his own choice, but when overcome by old age he was girded
by others and led where he did not wish to go. This should warn us that if we
have passed our youth according to our pleasure, we should thereafter prepare
ourselves to endure afflictions which we cannot escape if we have regard to
God's election. Thus far concerning all the parts of the induction in
general.

These things, however, are not of one kind. Some are not within our control
but depend upon God's hand. No one can prevent himself from being born and
brought into the light, nor can anyone withdraw himself from the dominion of
death, although he might wish this ever so greatly. Other things are within
our control. Yet everything in both classes has its own time, at which it both
comes to exist and is removed from the midst. What was said about the
appointed time at which each thing comes to be applies especially to this
teaching about origin and destruction. For the days of man are numbered

and appointed by God. When men have fulfilled them, they cannot pass beyond
them. Accordingly, the statement that fifteen years were added to Hezekiah's
life as an increase must be understood according to Hezekiah's perception,
since a certain death—though one announced conditionally—had been declared to
him, and not according to God's decree. Just as men cannot pass beyond their
appointed time, so they cannot hasten the day appointed by the Lord. Hence
those who are gravely wounded often recover, while those who suffer a slight
puncture prove beyond recovery. To this point belongs the statement made
several times that the Jews could not seize Jesus or lay hands upon him
because his hour had not yet come. The things which follow and lie within our
control are partly household and partly political. Thus far concerning the
former part.

The corollary is that the vain confidence men place in the plans and labors by
which they torment and weary themselves is beaten down. Even amid the greatest
striving and struggle they can produce no result, both because of the
inconstancy of things and because nothing happens before that time which God
has decreed in his secret or revealed counsel. For that very reason plans and
labor must indeed be employed, but in such a way that, with prayers devoutly
poured out to God, men call down aid and blessing from heaven. For the good of
his people, God kindly disposes of the times and the moments of time, which he
holds enclosed, as it were, in his hand.

2. The p. 148 catchword and resumed text on p. 149 give `quam`; the p. 150

3. The Greek `τὸ ἀλλοτριοεπισκοπεῖν` denotes meddling in affairs belonging to
   others; `ἐνέργειαν` denotes God's working or activity upon them; `σχῆμα`
   denotes the world's form or outward fashion; and `εὐρυθμίαν` denotes an
   orderly or harmonious arrangement. All four expressions are retained.
4. The edition prints `Apost. 1. Cor. 9` in the discussion of the world's
   passing form, although the familiar Pauline wording occurs in 1 Corinthians
   7:31. The Latin retains the printed citation, while the translation neither
   silently corrects nor expands it.
5. The Latin preserves the edition's irregular `illum esse cum speciem` and
   `ut est ne tempus`, its mixed sequence `desinat ... desiit`, the question

   quos cum implebunt`, and the agreement mismatch in `consilium ... & labor

   silently altering the transcription.
6. In the stage comparison, the edition's compressed shifts among the lowly
