## CHAPTER XII

### Questions and Doubts Concerning the Sixth Epoch

### QUESTION I

#### On the Period of the Shins and Feet of Nebuchadnezzar’s Statue, Daniel 2:33

The first question is what the meaning of Daniel 2:33, 34, and the following
verses may be. Namely, what is intended by the iron shins and the feet partly
of iron and partly of clay in Nebuchadnezzar’s statue? Or, at what time did
that fourth, iron kingdom and the stone cut from the rock, which crushed the
whole image, arise? Answer. There was sufficient agreement here in the
opinion of all Christian interpreters, both ancient and recent, until Johannes
Bodin, a man of the highest talent and learning, raised a great, intricate,
and quite dangerous controversy concerning the four previously undisputed
monarchies. Jerome, Theodoret, Luther, Melanchthon, Bullinger, Calvin,
Münster, the first English Annotators in their comments on the passage, as
well as Funck, Sleidan, and other Protestants generally; and also Lyra,
Cornelius a Lapide, Sanctius, Tirinus, and all the Roman Catholic
commentators—understood by the iron shins and the fourth kingdom the Roman
Empire, which had abolished all the preceding kingdoms of the Chaldeans,
Persians, and Greeks. They interpreted the Stone as Christ, who by his gospel
raised up a spiritual kingdom throughout the whole world and at the end of
the world will reduce to nothing the remnants of the Roman Empire and all
other earthly empires and bestow an eternal kingdom in heaven upon his
saints.

> Marginalia, printed p. 231: *Johannes Bodin, the first to challenge Daniel’s
> four monarchies*.

But as soon as Johannes Bodin, in chapter 7, page 437, of his *Method of
History*, dared to assail the four monarchies as an old and false tradition,
he gained far too many learned followers: Franciscus Junius, Johannes
Piscator, Amandus Polanus, Robert Rollock, Giovanni Diodati, and both the
intermediate and the latest English Annotators. They all maintain that the
feet of the statue and the fourth kingdom signify not the Roman Empire but
the kingdoms of Alexander’s successors, all of which had vanished before the
advent of Christ.

> Marginalia, printed p. 231: *Many followed Bodin*.

Hugo Grotius, a man indeed of very great learning, but in his last writings
very often most absurd and most heretical, adds to the latter opinion in his
annotations on the passage that the Stone signifies the fifth kingdom, that
of the Romans, who first arose from the Palatine Hill and crushed all those
before them.

Josephus and other Jews agree with us that, just as the golden head signified
the kingdom of the Babylonians and the silver breast and arms those of the
Medes and Persians, so the bronze belly and thighs prefigured the empire of
the Greeks and the iron shins and feet that of the Romans. But they interpret
the Stone that crushes everything as their own nation, to which they dream
that God promises dominion over the whole world before the end of the age.
After explaining the rest of the image in *Antiquities* 10.10, Josephus
conceals his view about the Stone that would crush the Romans, then masters
of the world, whom he feared to provoke. But the Talmudists and more recent
rabbis have long since disclosed their dreams quite openly concerning a fifth
monarchy of the Jewish nation after the Romans, or a sixth after the Turks.

> Marginalia, printed p. 231: *The Talmudists’ fifth monarchy*.

The English Fifth Monarchists of today do not differ greatly from them. To
the other Jewish dreams of the millenarians they add the frenzy of the
Anabaptists, most dangerous to humankind, or at least to their neighbors: that
according to the prophecy delivered by Daniel’s pious hands, the kingdoms of
the world will be delivered into the hands of the saints. They hold
themselves to be the beginnings and firstfruits of the saints who will reign
in the world. By God’s extraordinary favor they have begun to crush the
powers of the world, and they will not cease until, with the wind of further
divine aid favoring them and armies of other saints raised up everywhere
throughout the world, all earthly empires are abolished together with every
civil law and the ecclesiastical ministry, and the government of the whole
world, both ecclesiastical and civil, is delivered into the hands of Christ
alone and his people.

> Marginalia, printed p. 231: *The English Fifth Monarchists*.

The one question we intend here is the controversy of Bodin and his
followers. They overthrow the four monarchies and, in order to show that
Daniel said nothing about the Romans, divide the empire of the Greeks in two,
assigning the belly and thighs to Alexander alone and the shins and feet to
his successors in Asia and Egypt. In this they oppose all the ancient
writers, all Roman Catholics, and the earliest and foremost doctors of the

[Printed p. 232 | IA leaf 262 | access PDF 262 | OCR object 261]

Reformed churches. We shall touch on the rest in the fewest words.

We shall not take up the Jews’ dream, which Calvin refuted fully enough in his
comment on the passage, although among the six sophisms of Rabbi Abarbanel
that Calvin presents, some seem so subtle that they rightly require a fuller
examination.

Hugo Grotius’s invention is far more repulsive. Can it truly be so? Must the
kingdom of the Greeks be divided, Alexander’s being taken as the third and
that of his successors as the fourth, so that the Roman people may occupy
Christ’s fifth place? Can the shadow of a single passage be named in the whole
Old or New Testament that even hints that the title of the Stone should be
ascribed to the Roman people? But let us grant that, because Romulus and Remus
pastured their sheep on the Palatine Hill and built Rome upon it and the six
neighboring hills, the Romans may by some misuse of language be called the
Stone. Yet in what way were they cut from the mountain without hands? Will
Grotius really persuade anyone of the divine generation of those bastard
twins, Romulus and Remus? As for his explanation that “without hands” means
nothing other than a people free from the yoke of kings and the hands of
tyrants, it demonstrates his incomparable boldness and impudence in invention,
without sacred or profane authority, without reason, and without any pretext
of any color whatsoever. But once we have digested this absurdity too, let
the learned man tell us in the third place what privilege the kingdom of the
city of Rome would have over the empires of the Chaldeans, Persians, and
Greeks, such that those should have existed by merely human right but this
one be considered established by a peculiar right from the God of heaven.
Just as the Jesuits today celebrate the Roman episcopate as one of divine
right while casting down all other bishops throughout the world to human and
ecclesiastical right, does Grotius wish the Roman Empire likewise to be
divine and all others human? Finally, we ask the most learned man whether,
with the blasphemous flatterers of that supposedly eternal city in antiquity,
he considers the Roman Empire eternal. Surely the spiritual, heavenly, and
eternal kingdom of Christ, the truest cornerstone, which the prophet Daniel
most clearly foretold, ought not to have been so shamefully obscured and
evaded by any Christian.

> Marginalia, printed p. 232: *Grotius’s absurdities*.

Grotius is not excused because he joins Christ with the Romans in the last
kingdom. Christ will certainly coalesce with the Romans in his scepter and
throne with far greater difficulty than iron and earthenware are fused in the
feet of Daniel’s statue. With the worst judgment, the learned man assigns the
first parts in the last kingdom that crushes all before it to the Romans, and
only secondary and collateral parts to Christ.

As for the English Fifth Monarchists, Cromwell recently refuted them sharply
enough in his second-to-last parliamentary speech. Nor does Spittlehouse, in
his deservedly bitter answer, satisfy any sensible person. We shall merely
dispose briefly of the arguments that Spittlehouse cites from a London sermon
of Thomas Goodwin for this fifth monarchy.

> Marginalia, printed p. 232: *Thomas Goodwin’s arguments for the fifth
> monarchy refuted*.

First. Scripture often intimates that when all nations are converted to
Christ, God’s people will obtain dominion everywhere on earth. Answer. We
responded at length to the scriptural passages that Goodwin and his colleague
Burroughs alleged for this most dangerous error in chapter 11, page 214, of
the *Dissuasive*, and still have received no reply.

Second. It is just that those who in former ages were oppressed by cruel
persecution under all the monarchies should at last, having struggled through
their miseries, take their turn at ruling on earth. Answer. If this argument
has any force, it concerns only the persons oppressed under the former
monarchies. Although Goodwin in his millenarianism wishes most of those
oppressed persons to rise bodily and enjoy a thousand years of peace on
earth, our Fifth Monarchists contend not so much for the glory of those long-
dead oppressed people as for the dominion of those alive today. Moreover, the
rest, dominion, and glory that God promises for the comfort of the oppressed
are supreme, perfect, and eternal in heaven, not lowly, imperfect, and
temporary on earth.

Goodwin’s third argument. What we lost in Adam will be restored through
Christ. But in Adam we lost earthly dominion over the creatures. Answer. The
major premise is true either in this life or in the future. Has any mortal in
this life ever obtained, or will any obtain, the integrity of a soul without
any indwelling sin and the health of a body without any disease or danger of
death, which we lost in Adam? The minor premise is also false. For although
before the fall we were free in Adam from every kind of servitude and dominion
over animals and earthly creatures had been given to man, no monarchy over
his neighbors was given to any race of men before the fall, much less was it
natural to every man.

Fourth. The promises of Isaiah 65 are fulfilled on earth, because verse 21
says that the Israelites will build houses and dwell in them, plant vineyards,
and eat their fruit, since houses are not built and vineyards are not planted
except on earth. Answer. Heavenly things are very often promised under the
figure of earthly things. That saying of the Gospel, “I shall drink it new
with you in the kingdom of my Father,” does not

[Printed p. 233 | IA leaf 263 | access PDF 263 | OCR object 262]

imply drinking wine in heaven. People do not sit on thrones, eat at a table,
drink from a fountain, or pluck trees of Paradise in heaven literally and
without a figure. 2. In that passage of Isaiah earthly benefits are promised,
which were indeed granted to the Israelites upon their return from the
Babylonian captivity.

Fifth. Whatever the saints expect as the answer to the petition “Your will be
done on earth as it is in heaven” will be fulfilled in this life. But the
saints expect the promises concerning Christ’s kingdom as answers to that
petition. Answer. The minor premise is false, for we expect many things
relating to Christ’s kingdom, not as answers to the petition just named, but
to the one before it, “Your kingdom come.” Certainly very many relate to
Christ’s spiritual and eternal kingdom and neither ought nor can be expected
here until after the Lord’s second coming for the universal judgment; nor will
they be fulfilled except in heavenly glory.

But let us approach the principal question. Is the Roman kingdom understood
by the fourth kingdom, or that of Alexander’s successors? It is hard enough
that some later theologians named above, following Johannes Bodin, abandoned
all the ancient and foremost doctors in the Reformed church. Yet they do so
quite modestly, conscious of how many and what sort of men they abandon and
by what great difficulties their new opinion is surrounded. But the arrogance
of the Intermediate English Annotators is scarcely tolerable. Not content to
condemn the sounder opinion of the ancients as error, they pronounce it an
error of such consequence that it harms not only the book of Daniel but all
Christianity. So far is the newest edition from correcting this most arrogant
judgment that it repeats it in the same words and even adds to its bitterness.
Against this English arrogance we set Calvin’s quite severe observation upon
the passage against men who think such things. “This,” he says, “is beyond
controversy: all who are endowed with sound judgment and sincerely desire to
explain the prophet’s meaning understand it of the four monarchies, in which
one followed another.” Luther too displays great confidence here. “Daniel
himself,” he says, “explains the statue quite plainly in the text, namely, that
four kingdoms or monarchies are signified by it: the first of the Assyrians
and Babylonians, the second of the Medes and Persians, the third of Alexander
the Great and the Greeks, and the fourth of the Romans. Everyone agrees upon
this interpretation and opinion; events and the facts themselves attest that
it should be understood of these kingdoms, and every history makes it clearer
than can be denied.”

> Marginalia, printed p. 233: *The Roman empire is understood by the fourth
> kingdom. Argument 1*.

But leaving authorities aside, let us weigh the force of the reasons. We
argue as follows for the ancient and common opinion.

First. The empire of one and the same people is not portrayed by the prophet
with different metals; several peoples who unite as one are expressed by one
metal. Gold is assigned to the Assyrians and Babylonians, and silver to the
Medes and Persians. But the Babylonian kingdom is not expressed by both gold
and silver, nor the Persian by both silver and bronze, for this would confound
the most beautiful distinction between the parts of the image expressly
drawn by the Holy Spirit. Therefore the empire of the Greeks, who were only
one people, and more truly one than the Assyrians and Chaldeans or the Medes
and Persians, is not portrayed by two metals, bronze and iron.

Second. There is a due correspondence between the parts of the image and the
things signified by them. Just as the first part, the “golden head,” included
not only Nebuchadnezzar but all his Chaldean successors, and the second part,
the “silver breast and arms,” included Cyrus and all his Persian successors,
so the third part, the “bronze belly and thighs,” includes Alexander and all
his Greek successors—provided that the later parts correspond to the earlier
ones, as must be assumed in an image so beautiful and so carefully ordered by
God himself.

Third. Every part of the image denotes an empire lasting many years, not one
king alone for a very few years. Thus the head denotes an empire lasting, if
not more than fifteen hundred years for the Assyrians, certainly seventy years
for the Chaldeans; the breast, the Persian Empire lasting more than two
hundred years; and the feet, even by our opponents’ admission, Alexander’s
successors for three hundred years. Will the belly and thighs, then, a great,
indeed the largest, part of the body, signify no kingdom but one king alone,
and one who reigned only six years?

Fourth is similar to the preceding. The belly and two thighs signify more
persons than one, since the head, breast, and feet denote many more.

Fifth. The empire signified by the shins and feet was only one, namely the
fourth kingdom. But the kingdoms of Alexander’s successors were not one but
several, commonly four or five: namely, Antipater’s in Macedonia, Lysimachus’s
in Thrace, Antigonus’s in Asia Minor, Seleucus’s in Assyria, and Ptolemy’s in
Egypt. Once divided, these dominions never united by any means into one, if
we except their common nationality. But if the unity of the Greek people
makes these five kingdoms one, the same reasoning will unite and identify
these five with Alexander’s kingdom.

Sixth. The fourth kingdom was stronger than all the preceding, just as iron
in hardness

[Printed p. 234 | IA leaf 264 | access PDF 264 | OCR object 263]

surpasses gold, silver, and bronze. But the kingdoms of Alexander’s successors
cannot be compared in strength with any of the preceding empires. Apart from
the fact that they were divided among themselves and weakened their common
strength by wearing one another down in continual wars, even if you suppose
them united, they fell far below the power of Nebuchadnezzar, Cyrus, or
Alexander.

Our opponents object that the kingdoms in Nebuchadnezzar’s statue are
represented not as they were in themselves but as they acted toward the Jews,
and that the Seleucids, because they treated the Jews worse than all others,
may therefore be described as more ironlike than the rest. There are three
replies to this objection. 1. It is asserted without proof and without any
argument that the image before us represents the kingdoms not as they were in
themselves but in respect of their conduct toward the Jews. 2. The Jews were
treated much worse by the Chaldeans and Romans than by the Seleucids. Although
very many Jews were tortured to death with the most savage torments by
Antiochus Epiphanes and the temple was most shamefully polluted, these evils
lasted very few years. They neither drove the nation from its home nor utterly
destroyed Jerusalem. But the Chaldeans and Romans all but eradicated the
Jewish people and destroyed their cities and temple. The former caused a
captivity of seventy years and the latter of sixteen hundred. 3. The kingdom
of the Seleucids was weaker than all the rest even in relation to the Jews.
For the Jews gained many splendid victories against the Seleucids but never
any notable victory against the Chaldeans, Persians, Alexander, or Romans.

We argue seventh. In the days of the kings of the fourth empire, Christ’s
spiritual kingdom was erected through the gospel, as Daniel 2:44 expressly
states. But Christ’s kingdom was not erected in the days of Alexander’s
successors. For our opponents admit that Christ was born not in their days
but at least twenty-nine years after the death of the last of them, namely,
after Cleopatra’s death and the Roman occupation of Egypt, the last Greek
province. Nor did Christ begin to preach the gospel before his thirtieth year.
Thus Christ’s ecclesiastical kingdom was not erected on earth until sixty
years after the days of all those kings.

The most recent English Annotators answer this argument by saying that the
preposition *in* is sometimes used for *after*. To prove it they cite Job
27:15, “They shall be buried *in* death,” meaning, they say, after death.
Reply. This is a bad interpretation of Scripture. Even granting that the
preposition *in* sometimes means *after*, we never read that “in the days” can
be rendered “after the days,” for that would be not *in* but *outside the
days*, truly the opposite of what “in the days” says. Nor does the passage in
Job support the point. All interpreters render the Hebrew ב there as *in* and
no one as *after*, and the text does not permit such a commentary. The Spirit
speaks there of the curses upon the posterity of the wicked and among them
names as their peculiar misery that they will be devoured by death, that they
will be buried in the sea of death, and that death like a beast, sea, or grave
will swallow them so that they will never emerge from it into life but remain
in eternal death. If you render the passage “they are buried after death,” the
intended meaning is completely overturned. Burial after death is a favor and
honor desirable to everyone, not a peculiar curse pronounced against the
posterity of the wicked.

Rejecting this English answer, the pious Rollock invents another: namely,
that the Messiah was born not after the period but in the period of those
kings, because it was under Herod, who was an Arab, while Arabia was among the
Greek provinces. Reply. Rollock has no follower in this invention, nor can it
be admitted. Herod had no connection whatsoever with Alexander’s successors.
He received his kingdom solely from the Romans, and he was not an Arab but a
Philistine, the son of Antipater of Ashkelon. Nor was Arabia ever subdued by
Alexander or his successors. In any case, before Herod’s kingdom the empire of
the Greeks had vanished completely.

Eighth. The fourth kingdom was destroyed by Christ incarnate and preached on
earth, that is, by the little stone after it had been cut from the rock and,
through the preaching of the gospel, had become a great mountain that filled
the earth. But the kingdoms of Alexander’s successors were overthrown before
Christ was born, not by the preaching of the gospel but by God’s common
providence, by which he builds and destroys every kingdom according to his
good pleasure. Christ had no more peculiar privilege in the overthrow of the
kingdoms of Alexander’s successors than in the overthrow of the three earlier
empires or of the first world before the Flood.

Ninth. In Daniel’s other prophetic figures the kingdoms of Alexander and his
successors are represented as one, not as different bodies. Thus in 7:6
Alexander and all his Greek successors are represented by only one animal, a
winged, four-headed leopard, just as the Babylonian kings are represented by
a lion and the Persians by a bear. But the fourth beast, so terrible and
cruel that it has no name and has no equal among all the animals, is
completely distinguished from Alexander’s four successors. Similarly, in 8:8
Alexander and his successors

[Printed p. 235 | IA leaf 265 | access PDF 265 | OCR object 264]

are most expressly joined in one he-goat. On its head Alexander himself forms the single
horn, and when that is broken, the successors become four horns growing in
the place of the broken one. These five horns standing upon the forehead of
one goat make the kingdom of the Greeks as much one kingdom as the two horns
on the ram’s head made one kingdom of the Medes and Persians.

Tenth. The fourth kingdom endures until the second coming of Christ, but the
kingdoms of Alexander’s successors were overthrown before Christ’s first
coming. The major premise is founded upon Daniel 2:44 and 7:7 and 9. In the
former passage the fourth kingdom endures until it is destroyed by Christ’s
eternal dominion, which is assigned to no other prince or people after Christ.
In the latter, the fourth beast is not abolished until Christ’s throne is set
up for the universal judgment.

The conclusion Luther and other German theologians draw here—that it is
certain the German Empire cannot be overthrown either by the Turks or by any
others until the end of the world—is not sufficiently sound. For what prevents
the Turk, who sits at Constantinople and represents himself as successor to
the Eastern emperors, from being regarded as the other, and especially the
iron, leg of the Roman principate, just as the prince of Germany sitting at
Vienna is almost clay and earthenware?

Finally, it contributes more to the glory of Christ the God-man that, having
become man through the incarnation, he should destroy the very great Roman
Empire and subject the other powers of the world to his mediatorial kingdom
than that by his divine and merely ordinary providence he should abolish the
kingdoms of the Greeks that had become extinct long before he was born. The
common opinion displays the honor and power of Christ the mediator, which the
opposing view greatly obscures and diminishes.

But let us see what our opponents offer here either for themselves or against
us. Piscator has only one argument and Rollock another, to which Junius adds a
third. Diodati and the most recent English Annotators borrow these without
adding anything of their own, but Polanus expands those three to eleven. We
shall try to dispose of them all briefly.

Polanus’s first argument is this. The Messiah was to come near the end of the
fourth kingdom and destroy all the empires represented by the image. But the
Messiah did not come near the end of the Roman monarchy, nor did he destroy it
at his coming. On the contrary, the Roman Empire flourished most greatly when
Christ was born. Answer. Christ was to come not near the end but before the
fourth kingdom ended, namely, in the days of the fourth kingdom, for he was
born while Caesar Augustus flourished. It certainly follows from this that
the kingdoms of Alexander’s successors could not have been that fourth
kingdom, since Christ was not born during them but long afterward, and
Christ’s kingdom was erected still longer afterward. Moreover, Scripture
nowhere says that Christ would destroy the fourth kingdom immediately at his
first coming, but only that he would destroy it. The stone cut from the rock
was to abolish the image, but not immediately, as soon as it was cut. Christ
was to do this at the proper time; he has long since accomplished it in part,
and will yet accomplish the rest before and at his second coming.

> Marginalia, printed p. 235: *Polanus’s opposing arguments answered*.

Second. The fourth kingdom was immediately divided into two legs, namely, the
two families of the Lagids and Seleucids, but the Roman Empire was not
immediately divided in two. Answer. The prophet does not say that the fourth
kingdom would be immediately divided in two. He does indeed represent the
fourth kingdom under the figure of two feet, but the pair of feet no more
implies the division of one kingdom into two than the pair of bronze thighs
cut Alexander’s kingdom in two during his lifetime, or the pair of silver arms
divided the Persian kingdom in two. Moreover, let us grant that Daniel
indicated that the fourth kingdom would be divided. Still, nothing implies
that this division would occur immediately at the kingdom’s beginning or
before it descended to the feet and toes. Only then was it to be divided, not
into two but into the ten toes, which some count as the kingdoms that have
arisen from the Roman Empire today. The Roman Empire was indeed often divided
in two in antiquity: two opposing parties existed in it from very ancient
times, those of Sulla and Marius, Cicero and Catiline, Pompey and Caesar, and
Augustus and Antony. For many centuries the division of the Eastern Empire
from the Western was also established, and to this very day the emblem of the
Roman Empire is the double eagle. As for Alexander’s successors, as can be
seen in the history, many years passed before they were divided in two.
Scripture represents them as four horns and four heads, never as two. And the
foremost horn was the Macedonian kingdom, more distinguished than either the
Egyptian or the Syrian until it was overthrown by the Romans under Perseus.

If you consider the Jews, the Egyptian Lagids sometimes ruled over them, but
all the persecution of the Jews came from the Seleucids alone and by no means
from the Lagids, so there is no suitable reason for dividing the kingdom of
Greece in two.

Polanus’s third argument. The families administering the fourth kingdom tried
to remedy their divisions through marriages. But

[Printed p. 236 | IA leaf 266 | access PDF 266 | OCR object 265]

we read of nothing of this sort in the Roman Empire. Answer. The marriages
mentioned by Daniel in chapter 11 between the Seleucids and Lagids must not be
understood as the mingling by human seed mentioned in 2:43, for those
marriages were celebrated when the kingdoms of Egypt and Asia were at their
greatest strength and wealth. But the mingling in chapter 2 occurred only at
the end of those kingdoms, when the vision had passed beyond not only the
feet and shins but down to the very toes. Thus Rollock, in his application—
more correctly than in his exposition—refers this mingling to human leagues
and alliances, not marriages, by which after the Council of Trent Roman
Catholic princes were joined in a futile attempt against the churches. But if
one prefers to understand the passage of marriages, as many celebrated
marriages among Roman princes can be named as among Seleucids and Lagids:
namely, those between Caesar and Pompey and Augustus and Antony, the rulers of
West and East; and especially among the toes, the last kings divided from one
another—namely, the kings of Germany, France, Spain, Britain, Portugal,
Naples, Poland, Denmark, Sweden, and Hungary. These marriages, contracted
under the pretext of establishing peace, have often had the opposite effect.

Fourth argument. The fourth kingdom had to be destroyed at Christ’s second
coming and therefore endure to the end of the world, but the Roman kingdom
perished long ago, removed by the Turks, the pope, and others. Answer. We have
said that the empire was not to be destroyed immediately when Christ was
born, but at the proper time after his first coming. Luther and others indeed
assert that it will endure until Christ’s second coming; in any case, it
endures to the present day. Whatever you make of the Turk who represents
himself as successor to the Eastern Roman emperor, the Germans certainly
boast without any dispute from anyone today that they have the Western Roman
emperor. The toes, some iron and some earthenware, also persist in the ten
kingdoms named, and it is not improbable that they will persist until
Christ’s second coming.

Fifth. The vision of the statue in chapter 2 is explained more clearly by the
later visions of the beasts in chapters 7, 8, and 11. Answer. This is the
chief argument of all our opponents, about which there will be more in the
following questions. For now, however, we deny that the visions of chapters 8
and 11 are the same as and explanations of the vision in chapter 2. The
manifest error will appear here to anyone comparing the passages. The vision
in chapter 2 was fivefold both in the sign and the thing signified, both in
the image and in the things represented by the image’s parts: the golden head
of the Babylonians, the silver breast of the Persians, the bronze belly of the
Greeks, the iron shins of the Romans, and the cornerstone, Christ the Lord.
But the vision of chapter 8 has no more than two parts in either the sign or
the thing signified, and not even those are complete. There is no word about
the first monarchy, the fourth, or Christ. It treats only the Persian and
Greek kings, and only a few of them, not all. The vision of chapter 8 begins
with Darius Codomannus, the ram Alexander overthrew, and ends with the little
horn, Antiochus Epiphanes. The vision of chapter 11, although it touches on
more kings of Greece, nevertheless does not proceed beyond Antiochus
Epiphanes. Thus we deny that the visions of chapters 8 and 11 are the same as
the vision of chapter 2. The case of chapter 7 is different; it indeed seems
capable of being fitted to chapter 2, where the fourth beast can rightly
represent the Roman kingdom. But we shall say more about that image shortly.

Sixth argument. The fourth kingdom could not have been that of the Romans,
because the Romans held only a few provinces of the Babylonian Empire,
namely, Syria, Asia Minor, and Egypt. Answer. It is enough for the fourth
empire to have been the Roman if the Romans possessed by far the greatest
empire, even if they lacked some provinces of the earlier empires. Second,
they possessed all the kingdoms of both the Lagids and Seleucids. After
Antiochus the Great was defeated by the Romans, the Seleucids, unable to
defend even Babylon itself against the Parthians, much less the eastern
provinces, made Antioch in Syria the capital of their kingdom. Finally, the
Romans conquered all Asia as far as the Euphrates even in Pompey’s time, and
would not concede when the Parthians requested that the Euphrates be made the
boundary of the Roman Empire. As for the city of Babylon, it was then of no
reputation among the cities of the East.

Seventh. Only the matters Nebuchadnezzar had thought about while awake were
revealed to him in his dream. While awake, however, he had contemplated the
imminent condition of his own kingdom, not the very distant times in which the
Romans would reign. Answer. The king indeed contemplated the times after
him, but who knows how far the thoughts of the greatest and most ambitious
man on earth extended? It is certain that God revealed to him not merely
later but last things, not only what would happen after him but what would
happen at the end of time, after the Roman Empire was finished. For Daniel
expressly addresses him concerning Christ’s eternal kingdom.

Therefore, as for the dispute many raise here against the Latin translator,
as though his bad translation had led the Latin Fathers into error,

[Printed p. 237 | IA leaf 267 | access PDF 267 | OCR object 266]

because he rendered Daniel’s passage as concerning not later but last times,
this rebuke
of the ancient translator is undeserved. In the original text itself there is
a manifest distinction between באחרית יומיא in verse 28 and אחרי דנה
in verse 29. The Latin translator rightly renders the former
expression, following the Greek ἐπ’ ἐσχάτων τῶν ἡμερῶν, as in many other
passages of Scripture without offending anyone, but he translates the latter
as “hereafter,” as his critics desire. This openly intimates that the divine
revelation extended far beyond Nebuchadnezzar’s thoughts to the last times of
Christ, after the end had been imposed upon the stage of all the world’s
kingdoms.

Eighth. Nebuchadnezzar saw only those kingdoms that were connected with his
own. Therefore he did not see that of Rome, which was twelve hundred miles distant
from Babylon. Answer. The distance between Rome and Babylon was not so great
that Roman arms did not reach there and beyond. Indeed, the Romans possessed
the most splendid Babylonian provinces: Syria, Egypt, Cyrene, Cyprus, Lycia,
Cilicia, Phrygia, Lydia, Pontus, Bithynia, Cappadocia, Armenia, Paphlagonia,
and many others. As for the more eastern provinces, the Parthians took them
away from Alexander’s successors themselves before Antiochus Epiphanes. And in
the statue Nebuchadnezzar saw the kings who followed him ruling to the very
ends of the earth, namely Alexander the Great, whose empire was no less
extensive than the Romans’ and much more extensive than the Babylonians’.

Ninth. Nebuchadnezzar saw only those empires in which the Jewish church,
dwelling among them, was afflicted until Christ’s coming. These were the
empires of Alexander’s successors, not that of the Romans. Answer. Canaan, the
home of the Jews, was as much part of the Roman Empire as of the Greek. The
Lagids, although sometimes against the Seleucids’ will, possessed Canaan but
never persecuted the Jews. The Romans, however, mistreated the Jews many times
even before Christ’s advent and finally crucified Christ, killed John the
Baptist and the apostles, utterly destroyed Jerusalem, and carried away the
people into a captivity that endures to the present day.

Tenth. The prophecies of chapters 7, 8, and 11 plainly prove that Daniel says
nothing about the Romans in chapter 2. Answer. No answer is needed here, for
this argument is a mere commonplace and repetition of the fifth, about which
enough was said above.

Eleventh. Events in the kingdoms of the Lagids and Seleucids corresponded
most exactly to the feet and shins of the statue. Answer. We have brought
forward many arguments in the constructive part to show why we deny this, and
we do not think it worthwhile to add more.

We shall not linger over what Johannes Bodin further adduces. He argues three
points at particular length. First, the Roman Empire was not a monarchy for a
long time. Second, the German Empire today is not the Roman Empire. Third,
other empires were formerly and are today greater than those of the
Chaldeans, Persians, Greeks, or Romans—namely, those of the Parthians,
Tartars, Ethiopians, Turks, and others.

All this has little bearing on our question. In any case, to the first we
reply: 1. Daniel speaks of supreme empires, whether they were monarchies or
had any other form. 2. Just as the other supreme empires turned into
monarchies, so the Roman Empire was reduced to a monarchy after Caesar. 3.
Bodin’s followers do not raise this dispute about the name, since in their
writings they use the phrase “four monarchies” no less than we do. Certainly
those whom they make Alexander’s successors, whether you make four or only
the two Seleucids and Lagids, do not properly constitute one monarchy.

> Marginalia, printed p. 237: *The first answered*.

To the second we reply: among the princes of Europe, indeed among all
mortals, it has been agreed without controversy for more than six hundred
years that the title, emblems, and rights of the Roman Empire are to be
assigned to the German prince. It was not fitting for Bodin to disturb that
universal agreement in order to flatter his fellow Frenchmen. In any case,
it is sufficient for us if the German emperor is counted as one of the ten
kings among whom the Roman Empire, declining toward its fall, was divided.

To the third we first reply: the great and little-known empires at the ends of
the earth—of the Tartars, Muscovites, Indians, Ethiopians, and the like—had
little relation to the Jewish church, which was Daniel’s chief concern, or to
Nebuchadnezzar’s subjects, of whom alone he seems to have been thinking before
his dream. Next, as for the Turks, many maintain that they succeeded no less
than the Germans to a part of the Roman Empire. Finally, although the empires
of the Tartars, Muscovites, the Mughal of India, and Prester John of Ethiopia
are immense today in the extent of their kingdoms and the multitude of their
peoples, they are not comparable in power, strength, and stable government to
any of the four monarchies before us.

As I was preparing to conclude the question, there came into my hands the
writings of three more men on this subject: Constantijn L’Empereur, Thomas
Parker of New England, the son of Robert the scourge of bishops, and Ephraim
Huit.

The first wrongly turns Rabbi Jacchiades’s correct interpretation of the iron
feet as the kingdom of the Romans into the kingdom of the Seleucids. In
support of his opinion he praises Franciscus Junius, to whom, on Joseph
Scaliger’s testimony, he assigns first place among all theologians after the
apostles. Answer. When extravagant praises exceed measure, they detract more
than they add to anyone’s name. Certainly in this question Franciscus Junius
served the church and truth poorly. What L’Empereur adds here on his own, he
calls a demonstration from Daniel 8:22. From it he argues as follows. The
fourth kingdom denoted by the iron feet immediately succeeded Alexander the
Great and consisted of four kingdoms weaker than Alexander’s. But this is not
true of the Roman Empire. Reply. The major premise of this demonstration is
plainly false. The fourth kingdom did not succeed Alexander the Great
immediately but after his successors had been overthrown, just as Alexander
succeeded Cyrus and Cyrus succeeded Nebuchadnezzar. In the passage cited,
Daniel touches upon only two kingdoms, the Persian and Greek, and says not a
word about either the first or the fourth. Moreover, Daniel plainly calls the
kingdoms of Alexander’s successors four, and they were weaker than their
predecessors. Daniel denies both points concerning the fourth kingdom, making
it both one and more powerful than all its predecessors.

As for Parker, he rightly interprets the shins and feet and the fourth kingdom
as the Romans, and argues soundly against making the Seleucid kingdom the
fourth, using nearly the same arguments upon which we have lighted. Yet,
struck by the unhealthy air of New England, he agrees with the Fifth
Monarchists that the little stone cut from the mountain and crushing the
whole image is to be understood not of Christ or of his spiritual kingdom
filling the earth after the incarnation, but of the saints separating
themselves from the Roman mountain and the kingdom of Antichrist. After
Antichrist’s fall, they will visibly occupy every kingdom even in this life by
divine power. Perhaps we shall say more against these fantasies hereafter,
when Parker reveals his opinions more openly.

> Marginalia, printed p. 238: *Parker’s view*.

The third, Huit, adds a few points to the preceding. He agrees with us in
rightly interpreting the iron shins as the Roman Empire, but with regard to
the stone crushing the statue he strays into New England’s delusions. Yet he
differs from Parker and the Fifth Monarchists in this: he assigns the last
kingdom solely to the Jews converted to Christ, for he wishes them, after
returning to Judaea, to rule over the whole world until the end of the world.
Perhaps we shall consider hereafter how slight his arguments are.
