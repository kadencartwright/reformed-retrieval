It follows now that we should consider the elect as united to Christ. Speaking
of the elect in the broadest and most general sense, however, they are the
blessed angels and human beings destined for salvation. As for the angels, it
seemed good to touch on very few things about them, with Holy Scripture leading
the way. We may say that the election of the blessed angels is God’s decree by
which he determined from eternity so to confirm certain angels in the good in
which they were to be created that they would never fall from it, unto the
enjoyment of eternal blessedness. Hence the Apostle calls them “elect angels”
in 1 Timothy 5: “I charge you,” he says, “in the sight of God and the Lord Jesus
Christ and the elect angels, that you observe these things.” Hence Augustine
teaches us in his *Enchiridion to Laurentius* that those angels who remained
with God when the others deserted him are not only happy in their present
enjoyment of God, but are also secure about their happiness always to come.
Their blessedness may therefore rightly be said to consist in these two things:
the perpetual communion with and enjoyment of God in Christ, and the most
firm certainty of the stability of that grace and happy state from which they
will never fall.

[Marginalia: Lecture XIX. On the elect angels. 1 Timothy 5:15. Augustine,
*Enchiridion*, chapter 39.]

Augustine does sometimes say that this was attributed to their merits: while
the others fell by free choice, they themselves stood with God by free choice
and persevered in their original state. Thus in his book *On Rebuke and Grace*,
where he compares humanity’s fall with the fall of the angels, he says: “Since
he”—that is, the human being—“did not will to remain”—namely, in the good in
which he had been established—“it is certainly his fault; it would have been
his merit if he had willed to remain. So the holy angels acted: when the others
fell by free choice, they themselves stood by the same free choice and merited
to receive the reward due for this abiding, namely such fullness of blessedness
that it is most certain to them that they will always remain in it.” Thus
Augustine. Although, I say, he speaks in this way, he undoubtedly speaks worse
and more harshly in this respect than he thinks. For at that point he does not
ascend all the way to God’s eternal counsel, but speaks of the event itself.
Elsewhere he likewise asserts that eternal life is given by God as a reward for
the good merits of human beings—that is, for good works—yet he does not mean
that it therefore ceases to be God’s free gift, since those good works too are
God’s gifts which God crowns in us. In this way he gave the Scholastics occasion
to philosophize at great length, ineptly and subtly, about the merits of the
good angels. Yet far be it from us, speaking properly, to attribute to their
merits this grace of eternal and never-changing happiness. On that basis it
would not truly be grace but a reward owed, as Augustine harshly calls it in
the passage cited.

[Marginalia: Augustine, *On Rebuke and Grace*, chapter 11.]

Rather, let us say that the blessed angels were chosen from all eternity for
that grace, not on account of any merits of theirs—for there were none, since
they themselves did not yet exist—but from God’s mere grace and gracious
regard. By this grace he decreed so to confirm them in the good, while the
others fell, that they would never fall, in order that in their eternal glory
and blessedness he might reveal the glorious riches of his grace and goodness
through all eternity and so be celebrated, praised, and glorified by them
forever. Thus their perseverance in their good and blessed original state was
founded first and principally not in themselves or their free choice, but in
the good pleasure of the God who elects and in gratuitous election.

[Marginalia: The true opinion.]

Moreover, they were chosen in Christ as their head. In Malachi 3 and elsewhere
he himself is called the Angel of Jehovah, and in particular Joshua 5 calls him
the prince of Jehovah’s army—not only because he was leader and head of God’s
people, but because, as that title seems to suggest to us, he is leader, prince,
and head of all the heavenly angels, who throughout Holy Scripture are commonly
called Jehovah’s forces and armies. Hence all the angels are subject to and
serve Christ as head and king. In him they were chosen from eternity and then
confirmed in the grace of their original state.

[Marginalia: They were chosen in Christ. Malachi 3. Joshua 5:13.]

Although they did not need a Redeemer and Mediator in the proper sense through
whom they might be reconciled with God after falling into sin, they nevertheless
needed a head and prince in whom their sure and unshaken stability would be
founded, since they had been created mutable and liable to fall by their own
nature. They therefore needed a firm and indissoluble bond by whose union they
might adhere steadfastly to God, namely the eternal Son of God. Just as they
had been created in original grace through him, so through him they might also
be confirmed and preserved eternally in the same grace.

[Marginalia: Anticipation of an objection.]

Bernard, in sermon 22 on the *Song of Songs*, goes further and asserts that the
angels too needed a Redeemer. But it is clear that he uses this name more
broadly, not only for a restorer of fallen nature and a deliverer from evil,
but also for a preserver in the good; not only for one who conquers, expels,
and drives out death already reigning and exercising dominion, but also for one
who averts and prevents a fall that would otherwise threaten. “In what way,”
he says, “will you say that the Lord Jesus Christ was Redeemer to the good
angels? He who raised fallen humanity gave the standing angel grace not to
fall, thus rescuing the former from captivity and defending the latter from
captivity. In this respect he was equally redemption to both, releasing that
human being and preserving this angel. It is therefore clear that Christ the
Lord was redemption to the holy angels, just as he was righteousness, wisdom,
and sanctification.” Thus Bernard. As for others who gather the same opinion
from Ephesians 1:10 and Colossians 1:20, where the Apostle mentions the
recapitulation or reconciliation of all things, both those in heaven and those
on earth, we have said above in the proper place what our judgment and that of
other most learned interpreters is about passages of this kind. Let this
suffice for the election of the blessed angels.

[Marginalia: Bernard. Transition.]

As for the election of saved human beings, we have already set out its form
and manner quite fully, in which its proper nature consists: namely, that it
is God’s acknowledgment of certain people—that is, his taking them above the
rest into love and grace, distinguishing them from the rest, and appointing
them to adoption as children, salvation, and eternal glory through Jesus
Christ. We have also treated its bond, the foundation of its execution, and
the means or subject in whom we are chosen: Jesus Christ himself, the God-man
and Mediator, the head and prince of the elect and predestined. It remains now
to touch on those of its causes which are referred to the categories of
efficient and final cause. Matter, properly so called, has no place here,
because election is God’s decree or counsel and is therefore properly referred
to action. The matter in which—that is, the subject in whom we are chosen,
Christ the head himself—has already been discussed. The matter about which,
that is, the subject occupied or object, consists in the elect themselves,
about whom we have spoken in part and will say more in the proper place.

[Marginalia: Proposition.]

As for the principal efficient cause, there is no need to dwell upon it. It is
plain and agreed by all that God the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit

[Marginalia: On the principal efficient cause.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 109 | De Prædestinatione p. 67]

chose us from eternity. Far be it from us to separate or pull apart from one
another those inseparable
persons of the glorious Trinity in this action. Whatever the Father does, the
Son and Holy Spirit likewise do, and they cohere in an indivisible union in
every work toward creatures, while always preserving the manner and order of
acting appropriate to the persons themselves. Although the Apostle attributes
this action of choosing, or decree of election, to the Father in Ephesians 1,
he does so because the Father is the fountain of deity and therefore also the
principle of every divine action, as we have already warned you.

It follows, then, that we should come to the antecedent or impelling cause,
that is, the cause for which God chose us or by which he was moved and impelled
to choose us. The gravest controversies over this both exercised the Church of
God in Augustine’s time and still disturb it today. Satan raises from hell the
errors long ago condemned and rejected by the orthodox Fathers and thrusts
them upon people who are wrongly curious and in this matter enslaved to the
judgment of the flesh. We, with all the orthodox, seek no cause of this matter
outside God himself, but assert that it is all placed in God’s own bosom, so
to speak: namely, in his mere good pleasure or free benevolence, as the Apostle
taught us above. We contend that no other cause is to be sought beyond this,
nor can any other cause outside God be found in creatures themselves. Others,
following their own understanding—that is, human ingenuity and the corrupt
judgment of our nature—rather than the light of Scripture, although they dare
to offer even the twisted and corrupted appearance of Scripture as a pretext
for their error, seek and allege the impelling cause of our election either in
human free choice and its right use and in good works foreseen by God, or in
foreseen faith, or in any other condition of this kind which God observed and
foresaw in the creature itself. Finally, others stray from the truth less
perniciously but no less unskillfully by making Christ’s death and merit this
cause.

[Marginalia: On the antecedent cause. Proposition of the differing opinions.]

We will therefore conduct this disputation by first rejecting and refuting the
false and fictitious causes commonly brought forward by others, whether
Papists or Ubiquitarians—that is, those who are either entirely or at least
partly Pelagians. Then we will both assert the true cause and establish the
cause asserted with the firmest reasons. Once errors have been removed from
the road like thorns and brambles, easier access to the truth itself will lie
open. As for the objections and instances with which they commonly attack the
truth, we warned above that these must be considered and resolved in their
proper place near the end.

[Marginalia: Division.]

First, then, as for human free choice and therefore the human will, and the
merits or good works which depend upon and proceed from it—we join these two
in this matter because any good works that may be granted through nature, as
they wish, and any human merits, as they maintain, necessarily presuppose the
right use of human free choice and the human will and depend entirely upon
it—what need is there to gather many arguments to show that these are not the
impelling cause of election, as though God foresaw something of this kind in
the people whom he chose?

[Marginalia: First, the refutative part. First member: on human free choice and
foreseen works.]

1. The Apostle himself in Romans 9 directly opposes the human will and God’s
mercy to each other. Speaking of election itself, he says, “It is not of the
one who wills or runs, but of God who shows mercy.” By willing and running he
understands every endeavor, exertion, and desire of our mind, as well as every
work and action of ours. He excludes all these from election as its causes and
attributes the whole of it to divine mercy, which freely took us up to be
chosen when we did not will or desire or think any such thing—for we did not
yet exist at all. This does not mean that God rejects anyone who wills and
runs rightly, as though a person wills well and runs while God is unwilling
and repels him. On the contrary, no one can will or run unless God gives and
graciously grants it. It means that God chose no one on account of that person’s
willing or running and was not moved by it, but acted from his mere grace and
mercy.

[Marginalia: First reason. Romans 9:16.]

2. Who does not see that God’s will, the first and principal cause of all
things, precedes the human will not only in time but wholly by nature? The
human will or the foresight of it therefore cannot have moved God’s will to
choose a person, since God could foresee no good at all in a person except
what he himself was going to give and implant. But before he decreed to implant
anything good in a person, he must by the order of nature have determined
within himself the end to which he would predestine that person—unless we wish
to be so impious and devoid of reason as to assert that God rashly decreed to
create a person before he knew to what end or what would happen to the person
once created.

[Marginalia: Second reason.]

3. What else would this be than to subject God’s grace to the human will and,
as Prosper says, “to make the beginning of salvation arise not from the one
who saves but from the one who is saved,” and thus to make our salvation depend
on the human will rather than the divine? This is as absurd and false as it is
to abolish election itself altogether.

[Marginalia: Third reason.]

4. As for foreseen merits or good works, in addition to what has already been
said, consider the Apostle’s argument in Romans 9 about the twins not yet born,
who had done neither good nor evil. Consider also Deuteronomy 7 and 9, where
the Prophet denies that Jehovah chose the Israelites and brought them into the
promised land because of their righteousness, merits, holiness, or any other
condition in them, but acted from his gratuitous faithfulness according to the
covenant made with the patriarchs. So too the Apostle sets grace and works in
diametrical opposition in Romans 11, asserting that “a remnant has been made
according to gratuitous election. But if by grace, it is no longer from works;
otherwise grace would no longer be grace.” Therefore, everything he asserts
about grace against the merit of works shows election to be gratuitous, since
election is the foundation of all grace, and not to have been made from
foreseen works. This is expressly taught by the notable passage in 2 Timothy
1: “He saved us and called us with a holy calling, not according to our works,
but according to his purpose and the grace given to us before the ages began
in Christ Jesus.”

[Marginalia: Fourth reason. Romans 9:11. Deuteronomy 7:7 and 9:4. Romans 11:5.
2 Timothy 1:9.]

5. Good works are an effect of election itself and depend upon it as their
cause. The Apostle testifies in Ephesians 1: “He chose us in Christ before the
foundations of the world were laid, that we should be holy and blameless before
him in love.” In the following chapter, “God prepared good works that we should
walk in them. And we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good
works.” We were therefore not chosen because he saw or foresaw that we would
walk in them.

[Marginalia: Fifth reason. Ephesians 1:4 and 2:10.]

Finally, it would follow that a person can merit something from God, contrary
to the Apostle’s statement, “Who first gave to him, and it will be repaid to
him?” It would likewise follow that our election depends on works, that is, on
ourselves, contrary to so many statements of the Apostle—

[Marginalia: Sixth reason. Romans 11:35.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 110 | De Prædestinatione p. 68]

—and also to that statement of the prophet Hosea in chapter 13: “Your destruction
is from yourself, O Israel; but in me is your salvation or help.” The flesh
would thus have something in itself and from itself in which especially to
glory before the Lord, if that impious, arrogant, and plainly diabolical dogma
were true: that God chose a person on account of his foreseen will, works, or
merits. Hence we see that the Jesuits themselves are now ashamed of this
doctrine and teach the contrary, as appears from Bellarmine, book 2 *On Grace
and Free Choice*, chapter 9 and following, although other Roman Catholics in
earlier times sometimes taught in this way, including Pighius, Catharinus, and
others.

[Marginalia: Hosea 13:9. 1 Corinthians 1, final verse. Bellarmine.]

Second, as for foreseen faith, before we teach that we were not chosen on
account of this as an impelling cause, we must first resolve the adversaries’
calumny. They commonly reproach us odiously with teaching that a few people
were chosen by an absolute decree of God without any consideration at all of
faith in Jesus Christ, while they teach that people were chosen under the
condition of faith, or, as others say, on account of foreseen faith. The
statement that people were chosen with respect to faith or under the condition
of faith can have two senses. The first is that faith is the impelling and
moving cause for which God chose people, so that God is said to have chosen
some above others because he foresaw that they would have faith. This is the
opinion of the adversaries today, which we condemn as plainly impious and
erroneous. The second is that people are chosen for faith as a necessary effect
and consequence of election and as a means of salvation, but not as the
efficient cause of election. We embrace this opinion as right, pious, and
orthodox, asserting that in this sense our election necessarily includes both
faith and good works, at least in adults, as means of salvation and effects of
election, not as causes and principles of eternal election. In this sense we
will also say that we are chosen with respect to faith in Jesus Christ—not as
a cause of election in any way, whether principal, instrumental, impelling,
occasioning, or external, and so forth, but as its effect and consequence
depending upon election.

[Marginalia: Second member: on foreseen faith. Distinction of an ambiguity.]

But we rightly reject as false, harsh, unsuitable, and absurd the expression
that we are chosen “under the condition of faith,” that is, provided we
believe. It seems to suggest that God decreed nothing certain about us from
eternity but left the matter at a crossroads and uncertain. We do say that the
promise is made to the believer and that salvation and eternal life are
offered under the condition of faith when that gratuitous covenant is
promulgated. But the matter is very different in the public proclamation and
preaching of the gospel, which is made to elect and reprobate alike, from what
it is in God’s secret and eternal election. In respect of antecedent causes we
say that this election is entirely absolute and has no cause outside God’s own
bosom.

[Marginalia: Note.]

With this preliminary established, it will now be easy to reject this fictitious
cause of our election. In addition to the arguments already brought against
foreseen works, we argue as follows. Those who wish foreseen faith to be the
cause of election must hold either that it is from nature—that is, from the
powers of our free choice, so that believing or not believing and receiving
and embracing or not embracing Christ offered in the gospel lie within the
human will—or that it is from grace. If they hold that it is from nature, they
fall into the former absurdity, by which our merits are made to precede and
anticipate God’s grace. They also contradict the many testimonies of Scripture
which assert that faith is God’s free gift. If they confess that it is from
grace, they must surely confess that it arises from the grace of election,
which is the first of all grace and the fountain of every spiritual blessing.
It will therefore be an effect of election, not its antecedent cause, which is
what had to be proved.

[Marginalia: Reason for the rejection. First reason.]

2. It is clear from various passages of Scripture that the grace of election
precedes faith: John 15, “You did not choose me, but I chose you.” It is likewise
clear from the notable passage in Acts 13 that faith is an effect of election:
“As many as were ordained to eternal life believed.” The corruption of this
passage by a certain apostate who tries to evade it is therefore plainly
detestable: “As many as were ordained,” he says, means as many as had ordered
and disposed themselves to embrace eternal life—as if people ordained
themselves to eternal salvation. That is the same as saying that people chose
themselves, rather than God the Father choosing them from eternity, as the
Apostle says. What could be imagined so absurd or devised so impiously that a
mind abandoned by God to itself in just judgment, seized and blinded by that
spirit of dizziness, would not readily admit and embrace it?

[Marginalia: Second reason. John 15:16. Acts 13:48.]

Thus whenever the Apostle asserts that mercy was shown to himself or others,
it was not because he was going to be faithful, but so that he might be
faithful, as Augustine rightly asserts in various places. Some attempt to
restrict these statements to his ministry or apostleship, as though the
Apostle meant only that he obtained mercy so that he might be faithful in
discharging that office. This is far removed from his meaning, since in his own
person he never separates or disjoins these two things: faith in Christ and
faithfulness, so to speak, in the ministry of the gospel. Rather, he understands
especially that grace by which, together with faith in Christ, he was also
given that office. It is as if the Apostle could have been a faithful minister
of Christ while not being a believing Christian, or as if he had obtained the
former by God’s mercy but possessed the latter from his own will or the powers
of his own choice, when it was given to him, as to the other believers, “not
only to believe in Christ but also to suffer for him,” as he says in
Philippians 1.

[Marginalia: 1 Corinthians 7:25. Anticipation of an objection. Philippians
1:29.]

Furthermore, if faith were the cause of election, it would much more be the
cause of effectual calling, which depends upon election and occurs in time.
There seems more reason and probability in saying that people are called to
Christ in time on account of foreseen faith than in asserting that they were
chosen from eternity on account of that same foreseen faith. Yet the latter is
false and absurd, for it is clear from Holy Scripture that we were called so
that we might believe, and this “according to the purpose and grace given to
us before the ages began in Christ,” as we saw above. Likewise, by effectually
calling us, God himself “made us fit to share in the inheritance of the saints
in light, rescued us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the
kingdom of his beloved Son.”

[Marginalia: Third reason. Romans 8:27. 2 Timothy 1:9. Colossians 1:12–13.]

Finally—what need is there of more?—it would follow from this opinion, as from
the former one, that election is not from grace or from the one who calls, but
from the one who believes. This amounts to saying not so much that God chose
us as that we chose him, contrary to the Savior’s statement cited above, and
thus making election depend on people themselves rather than on God, since the
first part in it is assigned to the person and the later part

[Marginalia: Fourth reason.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 111 | De Prædestinatione p. 69]

to God. Indeed, election would in this way be entirely abolished and overturned. No
one by his own nature believes in Christ or comes to him unless the heavenly
Father draws him. Only those to whom it is given to know the mysteries of the
kingdom of heaven know them, those to whom God the Father deigns to reveal
what is hidden from the rest, and he does this from his mere good pleasure, as
Christ himself teaches us in Matthew 11 and 13.

[Marginalia: Matthew 11 and 13.]

Third, as for the death and merit of Christ the Mediator, although we confess
that the error which makes Christ as Mediator, or his mediation, the cause of
our election is less pernicious than the former errors which seek that cause
in ourselves, not even this opinion can be consistent with the truth. It must
immediately be maintained, lest anyone take what we say adversely, that we are
not dealing here with the meritorious cause of our salvation, or what others
call its material cause. We profess with our whole heart that this cause is
Christ’s death and merit, and we acknowledge that Christ is from the Father
the whole, adequate, and sole principal cause in the entire business of our
salvation—that is, in the entire execution of the divine decree concerning our
salvation. For we assert that God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, as the first
and principal one choosing and decreeing, committed to Christ as Mediator the
tasks of saving, redeeming, calling, justifying, sanctifying, and glorifying
us. But here the question concerns the antecedent cause of the eternal decree
itself, namely election, not the cause of our salvation. We have often said
that it is wrong to seek this outside God’s own bosom, even in Christ the
Mediator or in his death and merit.

[Marginalia: Third member: on Christ’s merit. Preliminary caution.]

This is clear from the single reason that an effect of eternal election is not
its efficient cause. But Christ’s merit is an effect of election; therefore,
and so forth. The reason for the minor premise is that God did not choose us
because Christ was going to die for us and merit life by his death. On the
contrary, Christ died for us because God had chosen us in him—that is, because
God had ordained this most wise and glorious way of saving us through him and
had prepared this remedy for our evil and disease in his infinite mercy. By
the order of nature and our manner of conceiving, therefore, God first
determined to raise us from the fall and eternal death through Christ’s death,
before he decreed that Christ his Son should become man and be exposed to the
cross for us. He was not moved by Christ’s cross to choose us in him, as if it
were an antecedent cause of his will. This is clear even from John 3 alone,
where the Lord says that “God so loved the world that he gave his only Son,”
and so forth. He makes the gift of Christ the Mediator, and therefore of his
death, an effect of divine love, namely the love by which God loved us from
eternity and chose us for grace. Thus the gift of Christ sent into the world
was not the antecedent cause of that love or eternal election.

[Marginalia: First reason. John 3:16.]

Furthermore, as we taught above, Christ himself as Mediator was foreknown and
chosen from eternity as the head in whom the others would be foreknown and
chosen. His merit was therefore not an antecedent cause of our election for
which God chose us.

[Marginalia: Second reason.]

Once all fictitious causes have been removed—for we do not pause over other
causes that might absurdly be devised, such as dignity of race, prerogative,
the merits of ancestors, the right of primogeniture, and others of this kind,
nor dwell on refuting them, since it is plain that our God shows no partiality
and considerations of this kind carry no weight with him—it remains that the
sole antecedent cause for which God chose those whom he willed is his mere good
pleasure, gracious approval, or gratuitous benevolence.

[Marginalia: The latter, affirmative part.]

If this is compared with the rest who were not chosen, or the reprobate whom
God passed by, it may rightly be said to be founded on that supreme and
absolute right which he, as supreme Lord of all, has over every creature
according to his most free will, as the Apostle plainly teaches us by the
analogy of the potter in Romans 9. If it is then referred to us considered
absolutely, without regard to any good or evil in us, in the way we said a
person is regarded in that first act of the divine purpose and decree, what
other foundation will it have than God’s infinite goodness and grace taken
absolutely, by which he deigns to communicate himself to his creature and to
favor and will well to it because he himself is good and gracious—that is,
grace and goodness itself? Finally, if he regards us as fallen and wretched,
as necessarily occurred in our election in Christ, we will rightly say that
this good pleasure flowed from God’s infinite mercy and clemency, because he
is full of compassion and pity. His grace and favor toward people so wretched
was therefore necessarily joined with infinite mercy and compassion.

[Marginalia: Note this distinction. 1, 2, 3.]

Yet we must not disjoin or separate from one another these things which we
consider distinctly for the sake of order and easier understanding. Rather,
we should say that since we were all equal by nature, whether regarded as to
be created and liable to fall, or as created and fallen into sin and misery,
God by his supreme right and authority over all creatures, from his gratuitous
benevolence and infinite mercy, chose us above the rest, distinguished us from
the rest, and freely took us up in Jesus Christ for love, salvation, life, and
glory, leaving others in death and misery by his hidden but most just judgment,
because it so seemed good to him, as our Lord and Savior says in Matthew.

[Marginalia: The caution repeated.]

This is the true opinion concerning the cause of our election, and it can be
sufficiently clear even from what has already been said. Once those false and
fictitious causes sought outside God himself in creatures have been removed,
no other remains than God’s own good pleasure. The Apostle teaches this so
clearly and in such express words even in the opening of the Epistle to the
Ephesians that it is astonishing that any have ever been found who acknowledge
with their lips that Scripture is God-breathed, yet refuse to see so great a
light. Various arguments arise from this to prove that our election is merely
gratuitous, that is, that it has no impelling or meritorious cause outside God
himself. First, we are said to be predestined according to the purpose of him
who works all things, and so forth; therefore, by gratuitous benevolence.
Second, God is said to have predestined us in himself, that is, without looking
outside himself at anything in the creatures which might impel him to choose
us. Third, it was from eternity, before the foundations of the world were laid;
therefore, without regard to anything that existed in people themselves.
Fourth, it was unto holiness of life; therefore, not because he foresaw that
we would be holy. Fifth, it was unto adoption as children, that is, unto faith,
since we are adopted as children through faith; therefore, not because he
foresaw that we would believe. Finally, it was unto the praise of his glorious
grace; therefore, from his mere grace. For if election is not merely
gratuitous, God’s grace alone will not be exalted; human

[Marginalia: First reason. Ephesians 1:4.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 112 | De Prædestinatione p. 70]

ambition—or rather that sacrilegious human presumption—will seize and arrogate to
itself some part of this praise. Humanity and the flesh will thus have
something in which to glory before the Lord, which is impious and absurd to
assert.

Other statements of Scripture agree with this truth. Thus Romans 11 says that
“a remnant was made according to gratuitous election.” So do the passages in
Deuteronomy cited above, in which the Prophet denies that Jehovah chose the
Israelites and pursued them with love because they were more numerous or
stronger than the other peoples, or because of their righteousness or merits,
since he reproaches them for hardness of heart and a stiff neck. That is, he
removes every cause they could imagine in themselves for God’s having chosen
and loved them above the rest, in order to establish his mere and sole grace.
From this grace, according to the covenant made with their ancestors, he
deigned to choose and love their descendants, or their seed after them. This
is certainly a notable passage, which I advise you to consult for yourselves
at your leisure. Similarly in Luke 12 the Lord says, “Do not fear, little
flock, because it pleased the Father to give you the kingdom.” So too 1
Corinthians 4: “Who distinguished you from others? What do you have that you
did not receive?” By this everything in the person himself is removed from the
cause of election.

[Marginalia: Romans 11:5. Deuteronomy 7:7 and 10:15. Luke 12:32. 1 Corinthians
4:7.]

Finally, not to accumulate more, Romans 11—“Who first gave to him, and it will
be repaid to him?”—most clearly proves that all God’s gifts are merely free;
how much more election, the fountain and principle of all grace? So too that
notable epithet in Romans 9, by which the elect are called “vessels of mercy
prepared for glory”: they were therefore prepared for that glory from God’s
gratuitous mercy as the antecedent cause of that preparation, that is, of
gratuitous election. For what else is election than the preparation of grace
and glory, as we heard above in the proper place?

[Marginalia: Romans 11:35 and 9:23.]

It is now time to introduce Augustine’s notable argument, which we promised
above to bring forward, drawn from the gratuitous election of Christ himself
as man and the assumption of his human nature into personal union with the
Word. In chapter 15 of the same book *On the Predestination of the Saints*, he
most skillfully applies and adapts this argument to establish the purely
gratuitous character of our election against the Pelagians. “By grace,” he
says, “he is such and so great. Why is grace diverse where nature is common?
Who, I do not say as a Christian, but as a madman, would say these things? Let
the fountain of grace therefore appear to us in our head, from whom it diffuses
itself through all his members according to each one’s measure. By the grace
by which that man was made Christ from his beginning, every person who is a
Christian becomes one from the beginning of his faith. The Christian is reborn
of the same Spirit from whom Christ was born. The forgiveness of sins is
effected in us by the same Spirit by whom it was effected that Christ had no
sin. God certainly foreknew that he would do these things. This, therefore, is
the predestination of the saints, which shone forth most greatly in the Holy
of Holies. Who among those who rightly understand the declarations of truth
can deny it?” A little later he says: “This human nature was predestined to so
great, lofty, and supreme an elevation that there was nothing higher to which
it could be raised, just as for us the deity had no lower depth to which to
descend than to assume human nature with the infirmity of the flesh even to
death on the cross. Just as that one was predestined to be our head, so we many
were predestined to be his members. Here let human merits, which perished
through Adam, fall silent, and let God’s grace, which reigns through Jesus
Christ our Lord, God’s only Son, reign. Whoever finds in our head antecedent
merits of that singular generation may seek in us, his members, antecedent
merits of our repeated regeneration. That generation was not repaid to Christ
but bestowed, so that he might be born from the Spirit and the Virgin, free
from every obligation of sin. So too our rebirth from water and the Spirit was
not repaid for any merit, but freely bestowed. And if faith led us to the
washing of regeneration, we must not therefore think that we first gave
something so that saving regeneration would be repaid to us. The one who made
for us the Christ in whom we believe made us believe in Christ. The one who
made the man Jesus the founder and perfecter of faith makes in people the
beginning and perfection of faith in Jesus. For so he is called, as you know,
in the Epistle to the Hebrews.” Thus far Augustine’s outstanding words. Let
this suffice for the antecedent cause of our election and therefore for its
efficient cause as a whole.

[Marginalia: Second reason. Augustine, *On the Predestination of the Saints*,
chapter 15. Note those antitheses carefully. Hebrews 12.]

As for the final cause, the only cause remaining for us to touch upon, there
is no need to dwell on it, since we discussed it when explaining the Apostle’s
own words. Everyone of sound mind agrees that this counsel of election cannot
be without its end, since God does all things wisely and we defined election
above as God’s most wise decree or counsel. He must therefore have set before
himself an end fitting for his counsel. Indeed, since the work of blessing
people with eternal glory is by far the greatest and most excellent, he
undoubtedly set before himself the most excellent end. In the definition given
above we expressed it from the Apostle himself: “the praise of his glorious
grace,” that is, the eternal manifestation and celebration of God’s grace and
mercy in the Church. Thus the Apostle clearly says in Romans 9 that God “chose
certain people in order to make known the riches of his glory toward the
vessels of mercy which he prepared for glory.” Therefore, in forming this
counsel God set before himself as the supreme and most excellent end his own
glory in such abundant mercy toward his creature, or the manifestation of the
riches of his grace toward the elect in Jesus Christ.

[Marginalia: On the final cause. Romans 9:23.]

As for other ends which are made subordinate and inferior to this one, if we
wish to speak precisely and accurately, they are less properly ends in relation
to election itself. They are rather the execution and consummation of the
divine counsel, that is, of eternal election. Or at least, just as in logic,
both common and proper, we commonly distinguish an internal from an external
end—the former included within the scope of the art and produced and acquired
in it; the latter considered and obtained outside logic, in philosophy
itself—so in the decree of election we may call God’s glory the external and
principal end, while the internal end subordinated to it and contained within
the scope of election as God actually executes it is human salvation or glory,
which comprehends under itself all the other means leading to it. Hence the
Apostle calls the elect “vessels of mercy prepared for glory,” and Acts 13 says
that they were “ordained to eternal life.” Likewise, the elect—

[Marginalia: Note the other ends. Analogy. Acts 13:48.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 113 | De Prædestinatione p. 71 (through Lecture XIX close)]

—are said to be chosen for its means, namely, for holiness and good works, as
is clear from those often-cited
passages in Ephesians 1 and 2. That these are less properly called the end will
be clear from the same analogy of a building which I recall using above. A
person who has purposed in his mind to build a magnificent and costly house
has proposed as the end of this work either a dwelling or, in addition, a
display of his wealth and power in constructing such a building. The
construction of the house itself is not properly the end of the counsel, but
its execution, unless we wish to say that the execution of the very thing
decreed is the end of the decree. No one, however, would properly answer in
this way when asked about the end. For example, a king decreed to build a
palace. For what end? You would not answer correctly, “So that he may build a
palace.” Or he determined to wage war. For what end? You would not answer
appropriately, “So that he may wage war,” but rather that he might avenge
himself on his enemies, extend the boundaries of his empire, or something of
that kind. In the same way, God decreed to save and glorify people in and
through Christ, and thus to adopt them to himself in Christ—for this is to
elect. If you ask for what end, I would certainly not answer fittingly, “So
that he may adopt, save, and glorify them,” but so that he may glorify himself
in them and gloriously manifest his grace in and toward them.

[Marginalia: Ephesians 1 and 2. Examples.]

But, someone says, the Apostle says that we were chosen so that we might be
holy, and likewise prepared for glory; therefore, holiness and our glory will
be the proximate or nearest end of election. I answer that the final
conjunction “that” or *hina* does not always designate a properly so-called
end, but often the means appointed to an end, of which each later one may in
some sense be called an end in relation to the earlier ones. Since in that
counsel of eternal predestination there are many distinct stages which are not
only ordered but subordinated to one another—for example, creation, permission
of the fall, effectual calling, justification, sanctification, and
glorification, all of which the decree comprehends within its scope—we can say
that each later stage, at least among those appointed for their own sake, is
an end in relation to the preceding stages. This is so not so much in relation
to election itself as to the person chosen. For example, the nearest end of a
created person, speaking of the elect, is to be holy, and the further end is
to be blessed and glorious. Yet in relation to election itself these are
rather parts and stages of its execution than ends. Thus people are said to
be chosen for holiness, adoption, and glory as an end not so much of election
itself, which comprehends all these things within itself, as of the elect
themselves, insofar as they are still regarded as wretched, lost, polluted,
and alienated from God’s life in themselves and their own nature, excluded
from his communion and destitute of his glory.

[Marginalia: Objection. Note the explanation.]

Finally, even if we call these the proximate ends of election itself, we must
at least maintain that an end is always to be distinguished from the final
cause. We assert that only the supreme end deserves to be called the final
cause, since it is that for which the agent is said to do everything he does.
Although we customarily grant the name “ends” to the other inferior and
subordinate ends, we must by no means distinguish them with the name “final
cause.” Let this suffice for the discussion of the final cause of our election.
