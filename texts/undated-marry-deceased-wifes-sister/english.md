# Whether a Man May Marry His Deceased Wife's Sister

*A fresh translation of the complete French response at CO X/1 233–235. The
heading is CO's collected title; no date or subscription is transmitted in the
French body.*

[CO X/1, p. 233]

Concerning the question debated today—namely, whether it is lawful for a man
to marry the sister of his deceased wife—it has seemed to us that the man who
wished to affirm it ought to serve us as an example that those who study how
to please men will never be good servants of Jesus Christ, as Saint Paul says.
In the first place, we certainly wish that he had refrained from misapplying
the two passages he alleges in his preface, since in truth he corrupts them and
turns them toward a sense entirely different from the one in which they ought
to be understood.

For the rest, the principal matter would be easily settled if people were
disposed to acquiesce in truth and reason rather than wishing to give free rein
to a disordered appetite that aims only to bring great confusion into the
world. It is quite certain that when taking a brother's wife is forbidden, God
has declared that contracting marriage in that degree was contrary to decency
and consequently incest. The alleged difference between the reasons is of no
force, since one must adhere to the reason God sets forth. For if the brother's
shame is uncovered, so too will the sister's be.

One can, moreover, see and judge that God did not have regard to consequences
of murder or poisoning, or to living in common, but condemned the evil in
itself. To claim that it is not the same vessel is a sufficiently filthy
covering, one that hardly savors of modesty; and the same could also be said of

[CO X/1, p. 234]

the mother and daughter, or the aunt and niece. Yet everyone admits that God
has disposed otherwise concerning them, although Moses expressed nothing
about it. In fact, he does not speak with subtle method, but sets forth in
simplicity what ought to be quite sufficient for us.

Indeed, there would be no dispute about this were it not for the shadow or
pretext borrowed from what is written: “You shall not take as wife the sister
of the one whom you have while she lives.” If this law did not exist, everyone
would admit without contradiction that marriage with two sisters is unlawful,
as can easily be gathered. Now, under the pretext that there is a prohibition
only for the duration of the wife's life, they wish to conclude that it is
permitted after her death. But those who base themselves on such an exclusion
have paid poor attention to the legislator's intention. For the passage does
not speak of shame or incest; it only forbids the cruelty of setting two
sisters at odds and in contention. Thus a law concerned with humanity is
dragged in by the hair when it is mixed among the prohibitions of incestuous
marriages.

It is quite certain that God wished to make this exception within polygamy:
if the Jews wronged their first wives by introducing second wives into their
bed, at least such a conflict was not to arise between sisters. For this reason
the rabbis thought that God had expressly deprived the ancient people of the
excuse they might wrench from Leah and Rachel, as if the deed of the patriarch
Jacob had justified them. But in truth this law must not be restricted to full
sisters, since it includes cousins and the whole kinship, as though it were
forbidden to provoke the wife to the point of making her fight against her own
blood.

If this interpretation is not received, we shall practice the rule Saint Paul
gives us: to leave them as they are when they deliberately wish to remain
ignorant of what is sufficiently notorious. As for civil law, when everything
has been properly set out, custom and experience will testify that this
liberty was never received under the Roman Empire.

We do not answer everything brought forward to show that scandal ought not to
be feared in this case. It merely pains us that the writer has been so carried
away by the vehemence of his passions that he would almost have people believe
that Christendom cannot be saved unless this mixing is accepted. We readily
admit that when the pope made a ruling about it, he did so on the presupposition
that it was not forbidden by God's law. But the error is exposed by the direct
and natural interpretation we have just related. May God grant everyone the
grace not to become intoxicated with frivolous curiosities any more than with
foolish loves. For our part, we do not think we have advanced anything from

[CO X/1, p. 235]

our own imaginings or opinions, but have tried to conform ourselves to the
pure word of God, as we firmly hope that all people of moderate mind will
recognize.

## Translator's notes

1. The response speaks throughout as “we.” CO reports an old copy but no
   signature, named author, date, or named addressee. The notice does not show
   whether this is an authorial or corporate plural, so it has not been
   converted into Calvin's individual “I.”
2. “The same vessel” translates *ung mesme vaisseau*, a deliberately crude
   argument quoted and rejected by the response. The English keeps the
   metaphor rather than replacing it with a speculative anatomical gloss.
3. The phrase “the one whom you have” follows the response's French wording of
   the biblical prohibition. It is not substituted with wording from a modern
   Bible translation.
4. CO distinctly prints *gons* in the last sentence. The transcription retains
   that apparent error or copy-form; “people” renders its evident contextual
   value without changing the French to *gens*.
5. The response's classifications of affinity, incest, papal and Roman law,
   and permitted marriage are historical claims. This documentary translation
   is not contemporary legal or pastoral advice.
6. The English was translated afresh from [`french.txt`](french.txt) and
   checked against the facsimile. The Beaty–Farley translation was not
   consulted or used for wording.
