The second argument stands as follows. What preceded in Christ ought also to
precede in us. But in Christ sonship preceded first of all. Therefore it also
precedes in us, so that first, through adoption, we are made Christ’s brothers
and become God’s sons through Christ before we are made participants in
redemption, justification, sanctification, and the other benefits flowing
from adoption.

He tries to establish the minor premise with many considerations.

1. First, Christ was made the Son of God by his very incarnation. But he was
made our redeemer, and our righteousness in justification, by the obedience
of his life and death, which followed after his incarnation.

2. Further, he could not become our redeemer before he was our brother. For
under the law of Leviticus 25:25 and Ruth 2:19, the גאל הקרוב, the
avenger and near kinsman, is from among one’s brothers, and the redeemer from
among one’s blood relations. Therefore, in order that Christ might be our
avenger and redeemer, he willed first to be our brother. He is accordingly
often called both the firstborn and the firstfruits on the sacred page. In
the law, the firstborn and firstfruits were first consecrated to God and, by
being of the same genus, species, and nature as the rest, consecrated all the
younger brothers and the entire lump and harvest. In the same way Christ,
through his incarnation, first became our firstfruits, firstborn, and brother
so that by his later offering he might act as our avenger and redeem us by
his ransom (λύτρῳ), the price having been paid, Hebrews 2:11. Thus this
brotherhood does not arise from his offering, but the offering from his
brotherhood.

3. He further illustrates this from the analogy of a husband and wife, in
which the union of persons is prior to the sharing of goods. In precisely the
same way, through adoption, we are made participants in Christ himself—or,
as the apostle speaks of the matter in Ephesians 5:30, “members of his body,
of his flesh, and of his bones”—before we can participate through
justification in the benefits of his death and obedience.

4. He therefore concludes at last that, since adoption depends in no respect
upon Christ’s death but upon his personal property as the Son of God, whereas
justification rests wholly upon his passion, just as our participation in
his person precedes our participation in his death and passion, so adoption
precedes justification.

There is more here than I can pursue at length in every particular. Let us
therefore proceed more strictly. As concerns the major premise, it does not
appear universally true that everything which is first in Christ by the
order of nature holds first place in its application to us.

Rather, at least in some matters, the order is altogether reversed. Just as
in a representation of the rainbow the colors occupy a position contrary to
their position in the rainbow itself, so what is first in Christ will be
last in us. Thus the glory of the Son which he had with the Father from
eternity was among the first things, yet our partial participation in it will
be among the last, John 17:22, 24.

To approach more closely the matter now before us, suppose that one of a
king’s subjects has by his own fault become a rebel and is about to suffer
the ultimate penalty. The king’s son undertakes his defense, pays the ransom
(λύτρον), and even himself bears the punishments which the other deserved,
in order not only to redeem him from death and restore him to the king’s
favor, but also to raise him higher to the rank of an adopted son. Although
the king’s son was a son before he was a redeemer, the guilty man, once the
price has been paid for him and pardon granted, participates in redemption
and pardon before he rises to participation in sonship together with the
king’s son. The reasoning is similar—indeed, entirely the same—in the matter
before us. Once the foundation of this major premise has given way, the
entire superstructure of the minor premise, though supported by many props,
falls together with it.

1. As concerns the first consideration in its confirmation, I grant that
Christ was first—namely, from his first incarnation—the Son of God and,
with respect to participation in our human nature, the brother not only of
the elect but, in a more general sense, of absolutely all human beings. Yet
he became redeemer only at his death. I deny, however, that it follows that,
before his redemption is applied to us, we are either God’s genuine sons or
Christ’s true brothers with respect to saving grace. The apostle says in
Galatians 4:5 that he “redeemed those who were subject to the law so that
they might receive adoption.” Nor can any moment of time or reason be
assigned or truly conceived in which we are sons and heirs of God while not
yet redeemed, or in which apart from justification we remain subject to the
guilt of sins and eternal condemnation.

2. His next contention was that, in order to be our גאל, or avenger, it was
necessary that Christ should first be our brother by blood, and should be
both the firstborn and the firstfruits, so that he might consecrate all the
remaining offspring and harvest. But he could not do this except through the
intervention of his death. When its merit is apprehended by faith, we are
first freed in justification from the guilt of sin and condemnation and are
then made friends and sons of God in adoption. Thus the very learned man
spoke altogether rashly in saying that adoption neither depends upon nor
flows from Christ’s death.

3. As concerns the third consideration, drawn from the analogy of a husband
and wife—namely, that the union of persons was prior to the communication of
the benefits flowing from it—we say that this is indeed true, but is not
equally truly applied to the matter now before us. For we are united to
Christ’s person not by adoption alone, but just as much, if not earlier, by
justification. When the apostle says in 1 Corinthians 1:9 that we have been
called εἰς κοινωνίαν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, “into fellowship with Jesus
Christ,” neither there nor elsewhere does he restrict this fellowship to
adoption alone. Together with adoption, he leaves it common to justification
and sanctification, and to perseverance in them, according to Hebrews 3:14:
μέτοχοι γὰρ γεγόναμεν τοῦ Χριστοῦ, “we have become companions and
participants of Christ, if indeed we hold the beginning of his substance
firm to the end.” We have, I say, been made participants in Christ himself
just as much with respect to his righteousness through justification as with
respect to his sonship through adoption. Consequently, no priority over
justification accrues to adoption from this consideration.

The third argument by which he attempts to prove this priority is drawn from
the completely contrary order of things as constituted and as perceived. It
is framed as follows:

What is prior with respect to being known is posterior with respect to
being. But justification is prior to adoption with respect to being known;
therefore it is posterior with respect to being. Since we are first assured
of the remission of sins in justification and of the peace arising from it,
and only then are assured of our adoption, he therefore regards as an error
the priority of justification with respect to being, although from the
foundation just laid he ought to have concluded the complete contrary.

As concerns the major premise, I readily grant that things posterior by
nature are very often known first in act, as when we ascend from effects to
causes and from individuals to universals. It was therefore unnecessary for
him to prove this to us so laboriously, as he nevertheless does at greater
length. The inquiry into the first thing known and knowable belongs not to
these schools, but to those of philosophers and logicians. I note this here,
however: since our intellect is not always analytical, nothing prevents it
from sometimes observing the order of nature, so that what is prior by nature
and therefore in itself knowable first is also actually known first by us.
This is especially so in individual things or acts produced by us or in us,
whose manner and order of production, as they occur, become known. Since our
knowledge of these is from what is prior and from their causes, they are
known in the same order in which they are produced.

As concerns the minor premise, namely that justification is prior to
adoption with respect to being known, I say:

1. This knowledge or assurance that sins have been forgiven is not
justification itself. Since justification is the object of this knowledge,
it is indeed prior to that knowledge by nature and reason, but is not on that
account posterior to adoption. If it is said that it is posterior precisely
because it is known first, as was said above concerning the major premise,
that does not follow. But:

2. I say, secondly, that it still remains to be proved that justification is
always known before adoption, namely from the peace and joy which follow
justification. For:

1. God’s manifold (πολυποίκιλος) wisdom does not always keep the same
path in assuring believers of his love. As the means here are various, so
are the measures and methods. According as various objects first meet faith
in hearing, reading, praying, or meditating, so various arguments first make
believers assured of his love. To one person the arguments from remission of
sins in justification may occur first; to another, those from sonship in
adoption. Nothing certain concerning the matter before us can therefore be
established from this.

2. If we regard the things themselves, just as adoption and justification
exist simultaneously with respect to time, so the knowledge of both is also
simultaneous in time by the nature of the things. The same consideration
which proves adoption is naturally fit at the same time to confirm
justification, and conversely. If you say that, when the order of nature is
observed, we ascend from justification as what is known earlier to adoption
as what is earlier by nature, I answer: what else is this than begging the
question? It remains to be proved that the inference from adoption to
justification is not just as primary as that from justification to adoption;
for

[1679 printed p. 67 | IA leaf 75]

example, “I am a son; therefore my sins have been forgiven,” and “My sins have
been forgiven; therefore I am a son.” Or, if the inference from justification
to adoption is prior, it must be proved that it is not from what is prior,
because justification itself is prior to adoption in its own nature.

3. As for his statement that from peace and joy concerning the remission of
sins in justification we then at last become assured of our adoption, I say
two things. First, peace and joy flow just as immediately from adoption once
it is known, Romans 8:15, as from justification, Romans 5:1. A rebel rejoices
just as much—indeed, more—if he first learns that the king has adopted him
as a son than if he first learns only that the king has forgiven his crimes
and the punishments owed for them. Second, even if that peace flows
immediately and primarily from pardon obtained and justification first
known, and thereafter secondarily from adoption known, nothing prevents us
from saying that the order of nature is preserved in this way: the consequent
is proved from the antecedent, and adoption from justification.

The fourth argument is as follows: What is more general is also prior. But
adoption is more general than justification, because it comprehends
justification under itself—indeed, because it comprehends the whole of
redemption, of which justification is only one part. He tries by three
arguments to prove that the whole of redemption is contained under adoption.
We shall deal with them shortly. Meanwhile, two things must be said about
that general proof.

1. Something may be called more general, and said to comprehend more things,
in two ways: either because it contains several things in itself as in the
cause from which they are produced and come to be—and this is always prior,
as the most general cause is first—or because it contains several things in
itself as in the terminus in which they meet and are perfected. Whatever is
most general and comprehends all things in the latter sense is last of all.
In the former sense election contains in itself all the remaining things
subordinate to it and is therefore first of all. Glorification comprehends
all the intervening means as ordained to it; in glorification they all
terminate and are perfected. Nevertheless, and indeed for that very reason,
it is last of all. The major premise, therefore, taken universally and in
every manner, is not true.

2. In the minor premise it was said that adoption comprehends justification
and even redemption, which is much broader than justification, since it
contains within its scope justification, sanctification, and glorification
itself. I answer from the very learned man himself that *adoption* is taken
in two ways: either in a broader sense, insofar as, according to his opinion,
it contains in itself everything from calling through glorification; or in
a stricter and more proper sense, insofar as it signifies that by which we
are first enrolled in the number of God’s sons. We shall soon examine the
broader meaning. Only this must be said here: in the question we are now
treating, the dispute is not whether something which can in any manner be
called *adoption* is prior or posterior to justification, but whether
adoption strictly taken, by which someone is first enrolled in the number of
God’s sons, precedes justification properly so called. The very learned man
asserts that it does, because adoption so taken is nothing other than either
election to sonship before all time or effectual calling to it in time. We
deny both.

We deny the first because election and adoption are not the same thing. We
read of election to

[Marginalia: Argument 4, chapter 13.]

[1679 printed p. 68 | IA leaf 76]

adoption in Ephesians 1:5, προορίσας ἡμᾶς εἰς υἱοθεσίαν, “he
predestined us to adoption.” But election itself is nowhere called adoption
on the sacred page.

We likewise deny the second. By the idiom of Holy Scripture, calling is
never adoption itself, nor can it ever be so by the nature of the things.
Effectual calling posits something real and inherent in the subject, for it
is regeneration itself and begun sanctification. Although regeneration and
adoption both concern sonship and constitute a son, the former does so
really, by way of generation; the latter only relatively (σχετικῶς) and
by accounting.

But let us proceed to his arguments by which he tries to prove that adoption
contains the whole of redemption and all its parts—namely justification,
sanctification, and glorification—and is therefore more common and general
than them all and, on that account, prior.

The first argument is that adoption extends to the last benefit of our
redemption. For the redemption of our bodies, which is perfected on the last
day, is called our adoption in Romans 8:23 because it is the revelation of
the sons of God, as the same apostle had previously said in verse 19 of the
same chapter. Thus the whole of redemption and all its parts, justification
among them, are a revelation of adoption and are therefore posterior to
adoption by nature.

The answer is ready to hand: everything said here of adoption can be said
equally of justification, and therefore no priority accrues to adoption from
it. If adoption extends to the whole of redemption and all its parts because
it is manifested and fully perfected on the last day, this very thing also
belongs to justification. Although the removal of guilt is fully
accomplished here before God, it will also be manifested before angels and
human beings on that last day. In that sense the sinner will then at last be
justified. In this same sense very many of our theologians expound the
Savior’s statement in Matthew 12:32 concerning forgiveness of sins granted
even in the age to come—that is, manifested before all. Adoption and
justification are therefore altogether alike in this respect. They mutually
manifest and comprehend one another, because they are always indivisibly
connected both in themselves and in their subject. Thus this argument proves
neither the priority nor the greater extent of adoption.

2. The second argument, by which he proves that adoption contains all of
redemption as something less common and on that account posterior, is drawn
from the fact that Christ redeems only his brothers, Hebrews 2:17. Thus
brotherhood is the foundation of redemption, and adoption prior to
redemption. This was partly answered in the solution to the second argument,
and will be answered more fully in the reply to the fifth.

3. He tries to prove this thirdly from the definition of divine
predestination in Ephesians 1:5, Προορίσας ἡμᾶς εἰς υἱοθεσίαν,
“who predestined us to the adoption of sons.” Under the name of adoption are
contained all the good things to which we are predestined, because adoption
contains all of them in itself and under itself, namely the entire
inheritance. The righteousness in which we are made participants in
justification is only one part of this inheritance, according to Hebrews
11:7, where Noah is called “heir of the righteousness which is by faith.”
Through justification, therefore, we are made participants in one part of
this inheritance. But in order to be heirs, we must first be sons. Therefore
adoption is both broader than and prior to justification. I answer:

1. I think that enough was said earlier in answer to the passage in
Ephesians 1. From the fact that we are said to be predestined to adoption, it
does not immediately follow that adoption is broader than or prior to all
the other benefits which we enjoy through Christ. We are equally said to be
predestined to eternal life and chosen to salvation, 2 Thessalonians 2:13,
just as is said in Acts 13:48. Yet eternal life, which is last of all, will
not on that account be first. It could also have been said that we are
predestined to sanctification or holiness, as is indeed said in the
immediately preceding verse, Ephesians 1:4: “As he chose us in himself before
the foundation of the world was laid, that we should be holy,” and so forth.
Yet sanctification will not on that account be either broader or prior to
all the rest. These things are said synecdochically: when one is named, the
others are understood, not as though all the others were comprehended in it
as something prior and more general, but because of the indivisible
concatenation of them all, so that whoever has one has them all.

But why does he mention adoption rather than the others? I answer:

1. He could do this according to the matter at hand. In verse 3 he had
spoken of “the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ,” and hence the
mention of the adoption of sons was natural and easy.

2. Or where he speaks of adoption, he does not remain silent about the other
benefits. In verse 5 he speaks of predestination to adoption, but earlier in
verse 4, as we have said, he had spoken of election to holiness. I therefore
think that nothing is established from this passage in Ephesians.

2. As concerns the claim that the righteousness we obtain from
justification is a part of the inheritance, Hebrews 11:7, but that we must
first be sons in order to enter upon the inheritance, and therefore adoption
is prior: I say that in substance this is the same as the fifth general
argument by which he establishes the priority of adoption, and therefore the
answer will be the same.

This fifth argument is drawn from the covenant and the promise—the promise
of God—and is framed as follows:

We must be in the covenant before we are participants in its promises. But
through adoption we are in the covenant, or are covenant members, whereas
through justification we are merely participants in the promise. Therefore
adoption is prior to justification. The covenant is made with Abraham and
his seed, Genesis 17:7. His seed is the mystical Christ, Galatians 3:16—that
is, Christ himself and all believers united to Christ through adoption,
verse 29, and hence “children of the promise,” Romans 9:7, 9. We must
therefore belong to this seed and be sons through adoption before we become
participants in the promise. Among these promises, one of the chief is the
remission of sins, which is effected in justification, Hebrews 8:10, 12.
Hence it follows that by the order of nature we first become seed and sons
through adoption before through justification we obtain the promised
remission of sins. Thus, from first to last, adoption will be prior to
justification.

I answer:

This argument rests on the same foundation as some of the preceding ones:
in order that Christ may be our גאל, avenger, and redeemer, he must first by
the order of nature be our brother. Since justification is a part and

[1679 printed p. 70 | IA leaf 78]

promise of the inheritance, it is necessary that we should first through
adoption belong to the seed and be sons, in order that we may have a right
to that promise and inheritance and actually participate in it. The
foundation of the proof is, I say, the same in all these arguments. The same
answer must therefore be adapted to them all, and it is this one answer:

I acknowledge that the covenant, promise, and inheritance belong only to
sons and the seed. But this seed can be considered either in known and
designated being or in the being of actual existence. In designated being
they are sons before they are participants in the promise or remission, or
participants in justification, because none but sons are designated for the
promise and remission. But as concerns the being of actual existence,
according to the order and method of the divine economy (οἰκονομία), they
obtain remission in justification before they rise to the dignity of
sonship.

For example, suppose that a king resolves within himself and confirms by a
charter that he will raise to some dignity men descended from one family
alone. In this designated being they alone are, so to speak, candidates for
that dignity before perhaps they are even born, much less before they have
committed any evil. But if, once born, they become rebels and enemies, they
must first be granted pardon before being raised to that dignity. The matter
stands in precisely the same way here. Similarly, the end in intentional
being precedes the means, restricts them to itself, and orders them *a
priori*. But in execution and attainment, it follows them *a posteriori*.
Thus adoption follows justification.

The apostle’s statement in Galatians 4:6 may be adduced to the contrary:
“Because you are sons, God sent the Spirit of his Son into your hearts,
crying, Abba, Father.” From this passage some might wish to prove that
adoption is first of all, according to the statement in 2 Peter 1:3: “We
must first become sons of God before we are led by God’s Spirit,” so that it
is necessary to begin from adoption.

But in this way adoption itself would be prior to calling, which is openly
and consistently denied even by those who maintain the contrary opinion to
ours on this matter. They assign to calling the first place above all the
other benefits in the economy (οἰκονομίᾳ) of grace applied to us, and wish
calling to be either the same as adoption itself or at least simultaneous
with it.

If, therefore, the apostle speaks, as he appears to do, of those who were
already actually sons, must that sending of the Spirit not be understood of
the first sending by which they were first regenerated? For that sending of
the Spirit precedes actual adoption and does not follow it. Rather, it must
be understood of some secondary or later sending, by which those already
adopted received the Spirit of adoption so that they cried, “Abba, Father,”
as follows in the passage. For the Spirit is said to be sent into our hearts
not only in order first to dwell in them, but also to work something in them,
especially if it is something extraordinary.

If, however, the apostle is to be understood of that first sending of the
Spirit which occurs in calling, then his words “because you are sons” must
not be understood as though they were already sons in act with respect to
the being of actual existence, but as sons in known and designated being:
namely, with respect to God’s decree concerning their adoption as sons, or
with respect to Christ’s procurement. As Piscator says on the passage, the
right of sons had been procured for them by the death of the only-begotten
Son. This does not mean that they were actually adopted or sons before that
sending of the Spirit, since they were only then to be regenerated by that
preceding sending and so adopted as sons.

But this is more than sufficient to refute the very learned man’s arguments
by which he tried to prove that adoption is prior to justification by nature
and order.

[1679 printed p. 71 | IA leaf 79]

Let us now at last see what can be adduced for the contrary opinion. The
arguments will be very few.

1. I do not wish to linger on the point that, according to Arminius,
justification is to be referred to communion in Christ’s death, but adoption
to communion in his life. This is indeed entirely true. Although the life
Christ lived here for the most part preceded his death, the more excellent
part of his life, in which we have been made participants, followed his
death. At least the communication of his death was made to us before the
communication of his life, according to the apostle’s statement in Romans
5:10: “If, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death
of his Son, much more, having been reconciled, we shall be saved through his
life.” If, therefore, through justification we are more properly made
participants in Christ’s death, and through adoption in his life, the
priority of justification over adoption follows from this foundation.

2. The second and principal point is this: those who are still enemies and
hostile are not yet sons in act, whatever they may be with respect to the
Father’s decree or the Son’s procurement. But before justification we are all
actually enemies and hostile to God. This is proved as follows. If we are
enemies before actual reconciliation, then we are so before justification,
because reconciliation and justification are either the same thing, as we
disputed earlier, or at least we are reconciled when we are justified,
according to the apostle’s words in 2 Corinthians 5:19: “God was in Christ
reconciling the world to himself, not imputing their offenses to them.” But
before actual reconciliation we are God’s enemies, as the apostle testifies
in Colossians 1:21: “When you were formerly alienated and enemies in mind,
intent upon evil works, now he has indeed reconciled you.” Therefore the
point follows.

The very learned man regards this as exceedingly dangerous. He wrongly fears
that if God’s love is founded upon justification it is rendered insufficiently
free, although Holy Scripture teaches us to the contrary that God loved us
while we were still sinners, unrighteous, and enemies, Romans 5; and that,
while we were still polluted in our own blood, he washed and cleansed us and
covered our nakedness with more splendid garments, Ezekiel 16:6–10. He
therefore argues that justification is not the cause of God’s regarding us
as sons. On the contrary, because from love alone and mere grace he first
received us among his sons in adoption, he thereafter cleanses us from the
filth of sins in justification.

But there is nothing here for us to fear. For:

1. The priority of justification over adoption will detract nothing from
grace and God’s free love. Rather, it will illustrate them more, because he
was pleased to adopt as sons those who were in themselves so unworthy that,
in order to be adopted, they first needed to be absolved by his mere grace
from the guilt of rebellion. Adoption would be less free if in justification
there were, first, anything of ours; second, anything in itself of merit; or
third, any intrinsic causality of adoption in justification insofar as it is
ours. But justification in its entirety is God’s alone—“it is God who
justifies,” Romans 8:33. It has no savor whatever of merit, since it is the
pardon of demerit, Romans 4:7, and is altogether free, δωρεὰν τῇ αὐτοῦ
χάριτι, “freely by his grace,” Romans 3:24. Finally, in the divine
economy (οἰκονομίᾳ) of grace it indeed has precedence and connection,
but contains no intrinsic causality of adoption. By the nature of the matter,
a king’s grant of pardon from guilt does not immediately make someone the
king’s son. I therefore see no way in which this detracts at all from grace,
and cannot imagine one.

[1679 printed p. 72 | IA leaf 80]

Indeed, if something outside God first moved him, this would detract from his
primacy. If anything in us were the objective cause of his love, it would to
that extent render it less than altogether free. But the existence of an
order among God’s free gifts detracts nothing from his grace. Adoption is no
less free if justification precedes it than justification would be less free
if, according to our adversary’s opinion, adoption preceded it. I add further
that it is so far from being the case that adoption would not be free if it
flowed from justification, itself altogether free, that it is even more free
on that account. In the same way, a man is more—that is, under a double
title—his master’s servant if he was also born of the master’s own servant,
according to Psalm 116:16: “Lord, I am your servant; I am your servant”—a
servant under a double title—“because I am the son of your maidservant.”
Adoption is all the more free because it follows from justification, which
is itself entirely and in every way free.

2. It was objected that God loved us before he justified us, and indeed
justified us because he first loved us. But as far as we are concerned,
nothing was prior to justification except calling, which is the same as
adoption; therefore adoption precedes justification. I answer:

1. This begs the question both by supposing that adoption is prior, which is
the very point at issue, and by confusing adoption with calling, which was
denied earlier.

2. I say that, from God’s free election and Christ’s procurement, God indeed
loved us with a love of benevolence before justification. But while we are
still ungodly and unrighteous before justification, it is altogether
repugnant to God’s righteousness and holiness for him to love us with a love
of complacency. כי לא אל חפץ רשע: “For you are not a God who wills
iniquity, nor shall the evil man dwell beside you,” Psalm 5:4. In
justification, from the preceding love of benevolence, we cease to be guilty
and unrighteous; and thus in adoption the heavenly Father embraces us as
sons with the love of complacency.

3. Third, to prove the priority of justification over adoption, theologians
generally and commonly adduce that very well-known passage in John 1:12:
ὅσοι δὲ ἔλαβον αὐτόν, ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς ἐξουσίαν τέκνα Θεοῦ
γενέσθαι, τοῖς πιστεύουσιν εἰς τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ: “As many as
received him, to them he gave this right to be made sons of God, namely to
those who believe in his name.” Or, as the Vulgate has it, “He gave them
power to become sons of God, to those who believe in his name.” Here the
power or right to become God’s sons is said to be acquired from faith and the
reception of Christ which occurs in justification. They are therefore sons
from justifying faith. Thus justification is prior to sonship and adoption.

The very learned man replies with many points most worthy of notice. The two
chief heads of his reply are these:

1. Receiving Christ and believing in him in this passage denote not
justification but adoption.

2. The ἐξουσία, or power, to be sons of God signifies not adoption
itself, but the privileges which follow adoption or sonship.

His first point is that believing in Christ and receiving him by faith must
not be restricted to justification. For by faith we are not only justified,
Romans 5:1, “Being justified by faith,” and so forth, but also adopted and
made sons of God, Galatians 3:26.

[Marginalia: *Ci Lo El Chapphetz Reshang*; chapters 15 and 16.]

[1679 printed p. 73 | IA leaf 81]

“For you are all sons of God through faith in Christ Jesus.” Believing in
Christ’s name can therefore be referred to adoption; indeed, it must be so
referred in this passage. He proves this from the two parts of the description
set down there.

The first is receiving Christ. But to receive Christ is to come to him, John
5:40, 43. This is the work of the Father, who calls us to Christ in calling
and adoption and produces faith by which we receive Christ. Having been made
participants in the Son, we ourselves are made sons. Only then follows the
Father’s second work, by which he justifies us when we have already been
called and made sons by this reception of faith. He proves this from Matthew
11:28: “Come to me”—in calling and adoption—“and I will cause you to
rest”—namely, in justification. He also appeals to Philippians 3: “that I
may gain Christ and be found in him.” According to this interpreter, this is
adoption, with the end “that I may not have my own righteousness which is
from the law, but that which is through faith in Christ.” He expounds these
words of justification, which he thinks follows adoption both from the order
of the apostle’s words and from the nature of the matter itself. He regards
constituting someone righteous before he is a son as the same as placing an
accident without a subject, or accounting someone righteous before he was a
human being.

He also urges the other part of that description, in which those believing
in Christ’s name are called begotten of God. Since these words speak of
sonship, they must be interpreted not of justification but of adoption.
Accordingly, that reception of Christ through faith must not be expounded of
receiving his righteousness in justification, but of receiving Christ
himself in adoption, through which we are one with Christ and together with
him are thereby made sons of God.

We have quoted him more fully so that we might not seem deliberately and in
bad faith to omit anything in which the force of his answer consists. It
will be worthwhile to answer each point distinctly but concisely, so that we
may cast some light both on that outstanding passage of the evangelist and
on the controversy in which we are now engaged.

1. First, he says that the words there about receiving Christ and believing
in his name are wrongly restricted to justification, since they can be said
equally of adoption, because by faith, believing in and receiving Christ, we
are equally adopted and justified.

I readily grant this last point. Faith, insofar as it believes in and
receives Christ, causes us not only to be accounted righteous in
justification but also to be regarded as sons in adoption. From this bare
phraseology of believing in and receiving Christ, therefore, no sufficiently
effective argument can, in my judgment, be constructed to show that
justification in contradistinction to adoption is designated there.

But John speaks there of the first reception of Christ, as is plain from the
contrasting failure of others to receive him in verse 11. If the reception
of Christ which occurs in justification, though not the first reception
absolutely, is prior to adoption, it appears necessarily to follow that the
reception prior in justification is meant there.

I know well that he will deny this—namely, that the reception of Christ in
justification is prior to that which occurs in adoption. But I think we
already proved this sufficiently in the preceding dispute: through faith we
first receive Christ for the pardon of sins and release from guilt, before
receiving him to obtain the honor of sonship, which follows afterward.

[Marginalia: John 6:44; Galatians 3:26.]

[1679 printed p. 74 | IA leaf 82]

When he tries to prove that this reception of Christ must be understood of
the reception which occurs in adoption, because receiving Christ is coming
to him—the work of the Father who calls us in calling and adoption and
produces the faith by which we are first sons by adoption and thereafter
accounted righteous in justification—I answer:

1. Let receiving Christ be the same as coming to him, although this is not
expressly said in the passage he adduces, John 5:40, 43.

2. Let both occur in effectual calling, in which we come to God through
faith and receive Christ.

3. But the assertion that this is never said in Scripture of justification,
which the adversary affirms here, is analogous (ἀνάλογον) and, in my
judgment, hitherto unheard of. This phrase of receiving Christ does not occur
very often in the New Testament. It occurs in the passage of John now under
discussion, but its sense is precisely the question. It also occurs in
Colossians 2:6: “As you received Jesus Christ the Lord, so walk in him.”
Nothing there suggests that it should be understood of adoption rather than
justification. The same can be said of Matthew 10:40, John 13:20, and Luke
9:48, where the same phrase occurs. Finally, consider John 5:43: “I came in
my Father’s name, and you did not receive me.” Let this be the same, I say, as
verse 40: “But you do not wish to come to me that you may have life.” Yet we
come to Christ in justification, as will shortly be plain.

Grant also that it is understood of the reception and approach which occur
in calling, as he wishes. But what is there here about adoption, unless
calling and adoption are the same thing? He indeed affirms this, but we have
already denied and refuted it.

Finally, grant that it is also understood of adoption. Is it understood in
such a way that justification is altogether excluded? Will anyone besides
him say or believe that? I certainly do not. Nothing in the passage and
nothing in the nature of justification proves it; rather, they prove the
contrary. We receive Christ and come to Christ in calling, in justification,
and also in adoption. But that this occurs in adoption so as not also to
occur in justification, or first in adoption and afterward by the order of
nature in justification—of this there is neither trace nor footprint here.
Nor does he prove it from the two passages he adduces, namely:

Matthew 11:28, “Come to me, and I will cause you to rest.” According to this
interpreter it means: come to me in calling, specifically in adoption, and
then, as the effect, I will cause you to rest in justification. But what kind
of interpretation of these words is this—that *coming* is proper to
adoption and has no commerce with justification, which is found only in the
rest that is the effect of this approach?

On the contrary, we come to Christ through justifying faith, and insofar as
it justifies. “If anyone thirsts, let him come to me,” John 7:37; Revelation
22:17. “Let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, our
hearts sprinkled from an evil conscience,” Hebrews 10:22. Here is approach
to Christ through faith—through justifying faith—with the sprinkling of
Christ’s blood from an evil and accusing conscience. This occurs most
properly in justification, which removes crime and guilt and washes away the
accusation with Christ’s blood. This very thing presents itself in the
passage of Matthew itself: “Come to me, all you who labor and are
burdened”—namely, by the burden of the law and of sins, which properly calls
for justification and pardon—“so that under this title you may find rest for
your souls.”

[1679 printed p. 75 | IA leaf 83]

Coming, therefore, will not belong to adoption and rest to justification as
adoption’s effect. The coming itself belongs to justifying faith, and rest in
justification is the sweetest fruit of this approach through faith,
according to Romans 5:1: “Being justified by faith, we have peace”—not,
“Being adopted by faith, we have peace in justification.”

Nor does the other passage he adduces, Philippians 3:8–9, prove the contrary
any more: “that I may gain Christ and be found in him, not having my own
righteousness but that which is through faith in Christ.” What does he
finally gather from this? He takes “that I may gain Christ and be found in
him” as adoption, according to this interpreter, with the end “that I may not
have my own righteousness which is from the law, but that which is through
faith in Christ.” These latter words he expounds of justification, which he
therefore wishes to conclude is posterior to adoption. It is as though the
sense of the apostle’s words were: “I counted all things loss so that first,
through adoption, I might be found in Christ, and only then, as a consequence,
might be justified through faith in Christ.”

But this interpretation is as excessively ingenious as the former and is
altogether foreign to this passage, which treats entirely of justification
and must be so understood.

1. The controversy between Paul and the adversaries against whom he argues,
whom at the beginning of the chapter he called the κατατομή, or “mutilation,”
concerned justification, not adoption.

2. In justification we gain Christ, when even at the cost of all things we
make his righteousness our gain.

3. Through justification we are also found in Christ, since we are not
found in the guilt of sins. It contains a tacit reference to God’s judgment:
“Those whom he finds to be in Christ, in them he finds no condemnation.” So
Beza. Thus every believer will be able to say, “Only in Jehovah is
righteousness and strength for me; in Jehovah all the seed of Israel will be
justified and will glory,” Isaiah 45:24–25. Hence Calvin reads the apostle’s
words ἵνα εὑρεθῶ ἐν αὐτῷ not passively with others as “that I may
be found in him,” but actively as “that I may find in him,” since—although
Beza resists, while Budaeus notes and approves—this passive ending often has
an active meaning. He therefore renders εὑρεθήσομαι actively here: “I
shall find in him,” namely, righteousness. This serves our present thesis by
showing that these words must necessarily be understood not of adoption but
of justification.

4. The words which follow, “that I may not have my own righteousness which
is from the law, but the righteousness of God which is through faith in
Christ,” do indeed speak of justification. They do not, however, as he
wishes, state the end, effect, or consequence of the earlier words “that I
may be found in him,” which he attributes to adoption. Rather, they contain
an explanation of them. As concerns justification, we are found in Christ
when, having stripped off all our own righteousness with respect to every
confidence in it, we are clothed in Christ’s righteousness alone,
apprehended through faith and freely imputed to us by God. Thus the renowned
Beza writes on the words μὴ ἔχων: “He explains what it is to be found in
Christ.” I do not mean that being found in Christ belongs to adoption alone,
while only the following words about having God’s righteousness through
faith in Christ denote justification. Rather, both occur to believers in
their justification: they are found in Christ and have his righteousness,
the latter being the explanation of the former. Since this expression
“being found in Christ” has some obscurity in itself,

[1679 printed p. 76 | IA leaf 84]

light is supplied by the added periphrasis about not having one’s own
righteousness, but that righteousness of God which is through faith in
Christ. This speaks of and expresses justification, the subject of the
passage, more plainly.

To this he says that “to constitute someone righteous before he is a son is
the same as placing an accident without a subject, or regarding someone as
learned or righteous before he was a human being.”

I answer by denying it. A subject can be given for this accident—namely,
righteousness—although in the logical order the man is not yet a son of God.
This will become clearer if we regard either this righteousness or its
subject.

1. First, as concerns righteousness, it must be maintained that this is not
the righteousness by which we are righteous through sanctification—that is,
inherent righteousness—but the righteousness in which we are made
participants through justification, imputed in the pardon of sins. To assert
that only a son can be the subject of this righteousness is to beg the
question and merely affirm what is denied. I say to the contrary that someone
can be accounted righteous in this way while in the logical order he is not
yet a son. Indeed, he must first be so accounted so that only thereafter he
may be regarded as a son.

2. Thus, if we ask secondly about the subject of this righteousness, it will
not lack one. Although he is not a son, he is nevertheless:

1. A human being, who is not altogether nothing, but can quite suitably be
regarded as the material subject of this imputed righteousness.

2. He is also an enemy and guilty. This constitutes the subject or formal
object of this righteousness or justification, for God grants him pardon as
such. Someone is guilty in this way to whom a king grants such pardon,
although he has not yet adopted him as a son. Indeed, the king first grants
him pardon precisely so that he may then rightly adopt him.

3. Although he is not yet a son with respect to the being of actual
existence, he is nevertheless a son in known and designated being, by God’s
election and Christ’s procurement. Such intentional being, provided it is
joined with the physical existence of the person, is abundantly sufficient
for this accident to be placed in that subject. Let the man merely be a
guilty person actually existing and additionally designated beforehand for
sonship. Under this threefold description, he will be a subject sufficiently
capable of this righteousness in justification, although he is not yet a son
in act, especially since:

4. In this situation he is not only a human being actually existing, also
guilty, and designated for sonship, but already effectually called. On this
point I agree amicably with my very learned adversary: in the economy
(οἰκονομίᾳ) of grace—that is, in the execution of the divine decree
with respect to us and in the application of Christ’s merits to us—effectual
calling is first of all. Justification follows it according to the apostle’s
method: “Those whom he called, he also justified,” Romans 8:30. By calling
and regenerating he produces faith; from faith he justifies the person thus
called, or grants him Christ’s righteousness. So far is this accident from
lacking a subject that it has a fourfold one: a human being, a guilty person,
one designated as a son, and one actually called, although he is not yet a
son in act with respect to adoption. Thus, although no one can be learned
before he is a human being, someone can very well both be and be regarded as
righteous from imputed righteousness if he was formerly designated for
sonship and is now an actual human being, guilty, and effectually called,
even though by the order of nature he is not yet actually a son by adoption.

[1679 printed p. 77 | IA leaf 85]

Thus far concerning the first proof of the very learned man, by which he
tries to show that the words in John 1:12—namely, receiving Christ and
believing in his name—designate not justification but rather adoption,
because the reception of Christ pertains to adoption rather than to
justification.
