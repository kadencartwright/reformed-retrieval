[1632 witness | unnumbered dedication leaf following Google Books PP1]

To the most noble and eminent lords,

most prudent men,

most weighty counselors and most upright judges in the Supreme Court of
Friesland.

We are not pleading a cloistered case, distinguished gentlemen, one foreign
to your concerns, but one that longs to come before the highest judges and men
possessing sharp judgment. Various sacraments and sacramentals, of which no
mention occurs in Christ's testament, are imposed upon the papal world as
Christian. Is it lawful for them alone to have rites of the testament with
impunity?

They sell the transformation of common flour into the heavenly substance of
our Lord Jesus Christ. Should they not be numbered among those alchemists who
seek profit from imposture? Every day they devour what they worship, and
sometimes even leave it for mice to eat, thereby condemning the wretched
pagans and Muslims to the unhappy wish of Averroes: “Since Christians eat the
God whom they worship, let my soul be with the philosophers.” What reason can
be given why they should not be regarded as traitors to Christianity?

They profess that they sacrifice Christ himself, and boast magnificently of
so great a crime. What else would that great red dragon of Revelation 12 do,
if he had strength equal to his hatred?

They sell papal bulls, and many other scraps of the same bran, at a great
price to those credulous people who wish to be deceived—not without loss to
the commonwealth. Who does not recognize here the wandering charlatans whose
merchandise is human souls? Revelation 18:13.

But there is scarcely need for an accusation when a confession of the
greatest crime is available. “More tolerable,” says the Jesuit Coster in
*Enchiridion*, chapter 8, “is the error of those who worship as God a statue
of gold, silver, or some other material, as the pagans venerated their gods;
or a red cloth raised upon a spear, as is related of the Lapps; or living
animals, as the Egyptians once did—than the error of those who worship a
piece of bread.” Here surely, unless all the senses continually deceive us,
unless all the first principles of reason deceive us, and unless, finally,
faith itself concerning Christ's human nature is false, we have the culprit's
own confession that among the papists there is found an idolatry the like of
which was never seen or heard anywhere in the world.

If I offer more freely to the guardians of both tables what I had to say about
matters of this kind than my condition might counsel, no one ought to wonder.
I hope and ask, most eminent lords, that you will interpret this, whatever
duty it amounts to, fairly and favorably. I entreat God, the Best and Greatest,
in Christ, that he may deign to preside over and bless your counsels, so that,
as far as possible, every pollution of this province may be removed from its
midst. Franeker, four days before the Kalends of April, 1626.

Your eminences' most devoted servant,

WILLIAM AMES.

TOME THREE.

BOOK ONE.

CHAPTER I.

On the nature and definition of a SACRAMENT.

Everything that Bellarmine places in the definition of a sacrament from the
Catechism of the Council of Trent—“A sacrament is a thing presented to the
senses which, by God's institution, has power both to signify and to effect
holiness and righteousness”—can be admitted if taken in a sound sense. That
sense will have to be disputed afterward. But since he attacks many things
that our theologians customarily employ to explain the nature of a sacrament
more precisely, we shall examine them briefly.

Question 1 concerns the perceptibility of the sign.

THESIS I.

Bellarmine. For the nature of a sacrament, insofar as it is a perceptible
sign, it is enough that it be perceived by some sense. It is therefore enough
if it is perceived by the ears, even if not by sight and touch.

Protestant reply. Bellarmine is cunningly taking precautions here for himself
and for his fictitious sacraments—Penance, Matrimony, and the like—which have
no visible or tangible signs, but in vain. For: I. All the sacraments of the
Old Testament consisted of visible signs. II. If perception by the ears were
enough, there would be no difference between the preaching of the Gospel
(especially when personally applied) and the sacraments. On this reasoning,
when Christ said to the paralytic, “Your sins are forgiven you” (Luke 5:30),
he was administering a sacrament to him. III. All the scholastics require for
the existence of a sacrament an element distinct from the word—the former as
matter and the latter as form—according to Augustine's statement in *Tractate
80 on John*: “The word comes to the element, and a sacrament is made.” IV. The
same scholastics teach that it belongs to humanity's special instruction, and
even to a certain humbling of humanity, that in the sacraments a person is
helped through bodily things. Thomas, part 3, question 61, article 1;
Bonaventure, on Book 4, distinction 1, question 1. They also teach that the
consecration of a sacrament's matter is necessary, but words are not normally
consecrated. Finally, the Council of Florence decrees as a dogma of the Church
that all sacraments are completed by three things, namely things as matter,
words as—

[1632 witness | printed p. 2]

—form, and the person of the minister conferring the sacrament. It manifestly
distinguishes the thing and matter from the words.

Question 2 concerns institution.

2. Bellarmine. It is enough for a sacrament if it is established that God gave
a command about it, even if it is not stated so expressly in Holy Scripture.
An express command of God in the divine writings is not required. For whether
it is written or unwritten is incidental to its being a divine command or
institution.

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine is again providing for himself and his party,
so that without any testimony of Scripture, or even any institution by
Christ, they can impose their inventions upon the Church as sacraments. Thus
Alexander of Hales, part 4, question 9, member 1, is not ashamed to affirm:
“The sacrament of confirmation was instituted at the Council of Meaux.”
Bonaventure appears to hold the same in Book 4, distinction 7, article 1,
question 1. II. Bellarmine demands the concession of something that no sane
person will grant: that, while Scripture is silent, God's commands can be
known to us with the certainty of divine faith through other means. III. Holy
Scripture is degraded beyond every measure and reason if it is so imperfect
that it does not even expressly identify all the sacraments. IV. Although a
divine command can by its nature be unwritten, and at one time was unwritten,
the history of the Gospel has nevertheless been most fully written, along with
all those things that the apostles preached to the peoples as necessary or
useful to them—as Bellarmine concedes in *On the Word*, Book 4, chapter 11.
It is therefore not credible that Christ gave a command about some sacrament
which was not written down.

Question 3. On the promise attached to a sacrament.

3. Bellarmine. A promise of grace that is the effect of a sacrament, or a
promise concerning the sacrament's efficacy, is found in the word of God for
all the sacraments according to Catholics, who also acknowledge traditions as
the word of God. But a preceding promise which the sacrament seals as a seal
is not necessary, nor is it found in all the sacraments.

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine openly admits here that some of the papists'
sacraments are unwritten, since he concedes that no promise pertaining to them
can be found except in unwritten tradition. II. He wrongly separates things
that must be joined: the promise concerning the sacrament's efficacy and the
preceding promise to be sealed by the sacrament. For that promise belongs to
the institution as the sacrament's efficient cause, and the sealing itself is
the promised efficacy. III. It is also clear that the promise to be sealed
naturally precedes the sacrament, because the covenant—

[1632 witness | printed p. 3]

—precedes it, and the sacrament is so much the sign of that covenant that it
takes the name *covenant* from it. Genesis 17.

4. Bellarmine. The word which by divine ordinance is attached to and, as it
were, clothed with a sign so that a sacrament is thereby made is never a word
of promise, but an assertive or supplicatory word.

Protestant reply. I. The covenant is a word of promise and, by God's ordinance,
is clothed with a sign to form a sacrament. II. An assertive word is added as
the foundation and ground of the efficacy of the promise to be sealed in the
sacrament. It therefore does not exclude but entails the word of promise. This
is manifestly declared in those assertive words: “This is my blood of the new
covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins.” III. The
promise was customarily explained by the apostles before administration of
the sacrament. Acts 2:38–39: “Let every one of you be baptized,” and so forth;
“for the promise is made to you.”

5. Bellarmine. The promise attached to the sign in a sacrament need not be the
forgiveness of sins.

Protestant reply. It must be the promise of the new covenant: “I will be
merciful to their iniquities.” Hebrews 8:11.

Bellarmine. If this were true, it would also exclude the Eucharist from the
sacraments properly so called, because it was not properly instituted for the
forgiveness of sins.

Protestant reply. It was properly instituted to nourish faith concerning the
forgiveness of sins. Matthew 26:28.

6. Bellarmine. This promise is not God's benevolence.

Protestant reply. What do the promises of the new covenant express other than
the highest benevolence?

Bellarmine 2. The sacrament would then be the sign of a past thing, or rather
of an eternal thing.

Protestant reply. The scholastics themselves teach that a sacrament is the
sign of a past, present, and future thing. Thomas, part 3, question 60, article
3. II. An eternal thing—that is, God's love—is signified when its effects,
justification and sanctification, are signified.

Bellarmine 3. On this reasoning it would often happen that God testified
falsely, nor could anyone baptize another without sin. For in the baptism of a
non-elect person, God would testify that he loved someone whom he does not
love; and someone would rashly baptize another whom he did not know to be
elect.

Protestant reply. I. The sacraments are not complete and actual testimonies of
God's saving love except to those by whom they are rightly received. II.
Ministers are bound to regard as elect everyone who rightly professes faith.
They do not administer the sacraments according to their private knowledge,
but according to God's command. III. With respect to those who do not believe,
the sacrament's testimony concerning saving grace is only conditional: if
they believe.

[1632 witness | printed p. 4]

Question 4, and the principal question, is whether the sacraments seal the
promise of grace like a seal. Bellarmine denies it; we affirm it.

Bellarmine's arguments.

7. Bellarmine. If the sacraments confirmed God's promises like a seal, they
would be truer and more effective in persuading faith than God's very words
are. But this is false. Therefore, and so forth.

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine himself concedes a little later that perceptible
signs are in some way better known to us than the bare word: “Catholics say
that God wished to give his grace through perceptible signs, so that through
them he might instruct people and lead them to understand spiritual things.”
Surely that which leads to the understanding of something else must in some
way be more intelligible. II. What is not stronger in itself can be stronger
and more effective in relation to us. God's promise is as firm as his oath.
Nevertheless, wishing more abundantly to show the heirs of the promise the
immutability of his counsel, God gave assurance by an oath. Hebrews 6:17.

Bellarmine 2. Nothing greater or more effective than God's word can be
imagined.

Protestant reply. I. Yet the papists teach that, as far as we are concerned,
God's Word depends upon the Church's authority. II. Here one should not compare
the word and the sacrament as a thing really distinct from the word, but the
bare word and the word clothed with a sacrament. The latter can be called
greater and more effective in relation to us, because it testifies more fully,
or to more senses, and is better fitted to move our minds.

Bellarmine 3. Sacraments are mute, and mute signs.

Protestant reply. Sacraments enlivened by the word are living and clearly
speaking images. But here the marvelous perversity of the papists must be
observed. When the question concerns images prohibited by God's law, they do
not hesitate to affirm that “a picture sometimes teaches better than
Scripture” (Bellarmine, *On Images*, Book 3, chapter 10). Yet sacraments are
mute to them because, of course, they are all too keen-eyed and sharp-eared
among images, but altogether blind and deaf in the sacraments instituted by
God.

Bellarmine 4. Neither do we learn from the sacraments what God's promises are,
nor do we believe the sacraments more than the promises.

Protestant reply. I. From the sacraments we advance in our knowledge of what
God's promises are and what sort they are. II. Although we do not believe the
sacraments more than the promises, we nevertheless believe the promises more
because of the sacraments, since by the sacraments the promises are presented
as more credible to us. For they declare to us more fully that it is God's
will that we believe.

[1632 witness | printed p. 5]

Bellarmine 5. The sacraments depend simply upon the word.

Protestant reply. They depend upon the word that institutes them absolutely;
yet they confirm the word applied to me through faith.

8. Bellarmine. Nowhere in Holy Scripture are the sacraments called testimonies
of the promises.

Protestant reply. I. They are everywhere called God's covenant, because they
are signs of the covenant, and the covenant is a promise. II. Wherever they
are said to effect the things promised in the covenant, there in substance
they are said to confirm the promises to us as testimonies.

Bellarmine. Who could tolerate a person who said, “Baptism regenerates; that
is, it gives testimony to the divine will”?

Protestant reply. Everyone who understands and tolerates Scripture would
tolerate someone who said: “Baptism regenerates; that is, it gives testimony
to regeneration promised by God.”

9. Bellarmine. If the sacraments were only testimonies of the promise and of
grace, they would be either superfluous or at least of little necessity. For
we have other testimonies, far more effective, such as good works.

Protestant reply. I. Testimonies that are less effective can be very necessary
to us. II. Good works are testimonies not by divine institution but by their
own nature. III. Our works are not, properly speaking, God's testimonies but
ours. “There is a great difference,” Bellarmine says in chapter 17, “between
God's testimony and the testimony of our conscience.” Thus between God's
testimony and that of our works there is a much greater difference. IV. Good
works are not more effective testimonies than the sacraments are to true
believers who effectually partake of them.

Bellarmine. Many ungodly people are baptized with a feigned heart and receive
the Eucharist.

Protestant reply. Only those who are inwardly effectually baptized or fed have
testimony from God. Romans 2, final verse.

10. Bellarmine. If the sacraments were only testimonies of the promises,
infants would be baptized in vain.

Protestant reply. I. Bonaventure answers in Book 4, distinction 1, question 2:
“A sign has a twofold relation: both to what it signifies and to the one to
whom it is signified. The first is essential and the sign always has it in
act; the second it has as a disposition. It is called a sign from the first,
not the second. Just as another's faith suffices for an infant, so it suffices
that something be signified to the infant in another. Hence although it does
not signify to the infant in himself, it nevertheless signifies to him in
another.” II. Peter was not washed in vain when the Lord said to him, “What I
am doing you do not know now, but you will know afterward.” John 13:7.

11. Bellarmine. If the sacraments were testimonies of grace conferred on a
particular person, they would very often be false, namely in hypocrites.

[1632 witness | printed p. 6]

Protestant reply. They are not complete and absolute testimonies except to
believers.

Bellarmine 2. According to Luther's opinion, whoever does not believe the word
of the minister who baptizes makes God a liar. But if those words were
understood with a condition—if he believes otherwise—they would not be false,
nor would he make God a liar.

Protestant reply. I. This is the same teaching as John's in 1 John 5:10. II.
They are nevertheless understood conditionally with respect to those who do
not believe. Such people are said by the nature of the deed to make God a liar,
as Cajetan observes, because they do not effectually believe to be true that
which is conditionally affirmed.

Bellarmine 2. The Lutherans maintain that a person must believe absolutely
that he is righteous.

Protestant reply. Not except through faith in Christ, by which they are
righteous, or as a consequence of it.

Our arguments.

12. Protestant argument. Romans 4:11 expressly calls circumcision a seal of
the righteousness of faith received.

Bellarmine 1. Things said only of circumcision cannot rightly be extended to
all the sacraments.

Protestant reply. I. Whatever is said of circumcision and pertains to its
sacramental nature, which it shares with the other sacraments, is rightly
applied to all the sacraments. But circumcision's manner of signifying, apart
from its relation to the future, past, and similar modes, is of this kind.
Bonaventure says in Book 4, distinction 1, question 2: “It must be noted that
sacramental medicine has signification as part of its integrity, and this is
common and essential to all the sacraments.” II. Although everything said of
circumcision cannot rightly be extended to all the sacraments, whatever
perfection existed in circumcision is also found in baptism. But it is the
perfection of a sign to seal like a seal.

Bellarmine 2. No mention is made of a promise or covenant. Circumcision is
called the seal of the righteousness of faith either because it enclosed the
righteousness that was to be revealed in its own time, or because it was
given to Abraham as a sign and testimony of the righteousness that he had
acquired for himself through faith.

Protestant reply. I. It is a wretched evasion to say that no promise or
covenant is mentioned. For, as Cajetan observes on these words, the Apostle
took this from Genesis 17, where it is plainly said, “It shall be a sign of the
covenant.” The Apostle himself immediately adds that Abraham's fatherhood was
in some manner sealed: “that he might be the father of all who believe.” But
God placed the covenant in this very thing. Genesis 17:4: “Behold, my covenant
is with you, that you shall be the father of a multitude of nations.” II.
Bellarmine absurdly separates the righteousness of faith and the promise,
which the Apostle joins most tightly—

[1632 witness | printed p. 7]

—in Romans 4:13: “The promise came to Abraham through the righteousness of
faith.” III. To enclose and conceal righteousness does not belong to the
blessing of the covenant. IV. That last claim—that circumcision was given as a
sign or testimony of righteousness acquired through faith—is precisely what
we maintain.

Bellarmine 3. This was Abraham's privilege alone and cannot be transferred to
others.

Protestant reply. This directly contradicts those words of God: “This is my
covenant between me and you, and your offspring after you.” Genesis 17:10–11.
It also invents the claim that the very righteousness of faith was sealed to
Abraham alone, and thus overturns the Apostle's argument drawn from Abraham to
others.

Bellarmine. Not all the circumcised are fathers of all believers.

Protestant reply. All faithful people circumcised after Abraham receive the
seal of the righteousness of faith as Abraham's children, not as fathers.
Fatherhood was Abraham's privilege, but the sealing of the righteousness of
faith was not.

Bellarmine. Paul distinguishes a sign from a seal to indicate that circumcision
was always some sort of sign for everyone, yet was a seal of righteousness not
for everyone but for Abraham alone.

Protestant reply. Paul does not distinguish sign from seal, but explains the
more general term by the more specific. Thus Cajetan: “By receiving it as a
sign, he received it as a seal, a character, or an impressed mark.”

Bellarmine. Scripture nowhere calls circumcision a seal except in this place,
where Abraham is discussed. This is clear proof that circumcision was a seal
for Abraham alone.

Protestant reply. The Jews could have answered the Apostle in a similar way:
“Scripture nowhere says that faith was imputed for righteousness except in
one place, where Abraham is discussed. This is clear proof that faith was
imputed for righteousness to Abraham alone.” But the Apostle answers in verse
23: “It was not written for his sake alone.”

13. Protestant argument. The rainbow and circumcision are called signs of
covenants. Genesis 9 and 17.

Bellarmine. They were commemorative signs, not confirmatory signs; they aided
memory, not the confirmation of faith.

Protestant reply. I. They were not merely commemorative, for then they would
have regarded nothing present or future, which is manifestly false. II. Insofar
as a sacred sign commemorates the past and from it infers the present or
future, it also confirms faith. Thus Bonaventure in Book 4, distinction 1,
question 2: “The movement of faith is aroused through the presentation of the
sign, so that it seeks the thing signified.”

14. Protestant argument. Among human beings, too, it is customary for
covenants to be ratified by an external sign.

[1632 witness | printed p. 8]

Bellarmine. This is done because human beings can deceive or forget, but we
are entirely certain that God can neither deceive nor forget.

Protestant reply. I. We are not so certain about God's perfections, insofar as
they pertain to our consolation, that we have no need to be made more certain
from time to time. II. Cajetan says on Genesis 15: “It is not devoid of mystery
that the first principle of being descended from his exaltation to this: that,
in the likeness of a human covenant-maker, he also dealt with a human being so
as to present himself to the person as though bound by contract.” No one who
reads Scripture will deny that God descended this far, unless he is an
unbeliever, blind, or exceedingly shameless.

14. Protestant argument. In baptism, it is not the outward washing but the
pledge of a good conscience that saves us. This pledge is a testimony, and it
flows from God's testimony in the sacrament of baptism.

Bellarmine 1. Peter calls the inward washing the question, or answer, of a good
conscience toward God, because peace and quiet of conscience arise from that
cleanness. The answer of a good conscience is also a questioning before God,
because it dares to approach God with confidence.

Protestant reply. All these things are truly said, but the argument remains
firm, because the testimony of our conscience or spirit flows from the
testimony of God's Spirit. Romans 8:16. Therefore, if the testimony of our
spirit is an effect of baptism, it follows that in baptism the Spirit of God
provides testimony to us.

CHAPTER II. On the Causes of a Sacrament.

THESIS I.

Bellarmine. Concerning intrinsic causes, the first question is whether a
sacrament consists of things as matter and words as form.

Protestant reply. I. This can indeed be affirmed insofar as the celebration of
a sacrament is an ecclesiastical and external act. But as regards the complete
nature of a sacrament, by which it is what it is and does that for which it was
instituted, its form is rather that relational union which stands between the
sign and the thing signified. Accordingly, the words belong to the efficient
cause rather than to the form—or they merely signify and declare the form.
II. Not just any things and words, but those ordained by God for this purpose,
have the force of a cause in a sacrament. Thus Durandus on Book 4, distinction
1, question 3: “The principal cause why sacraments consist of these or those
things is divine institution.”

2. Bellarmine. It is probable that the sacraments of the old law did not have
things and words, but things alone.

[1632 witness | printed p. 9]

Protestant reply. This is not probable concerning the ordinary sacraments,
because in the very institution both of circumcision and of the Passover,
words explaining their nature were expressed. Thus Bellarmine himself at the
end of chapter 20: “We do not deny that, when he gave the signs to the fathers,
he explained their force.” Nor can a sacrament without a word, either expressed
or understood, be conceived.

3. Bellarmine. In all the sacraments of the new law, things are found as
matter and words as form.

Protestant reply. Matrimony and Penance, therefore, are not sacraments of the
new law or testament, because—as Durandus testifies on Book 4, distinction 1,
question 3—they do not consist at all of things and words, still less of those
instituted by God for such an end.

4. Bellarmine. For sacraments to be said to consist of things and words, it is
not necessary that the things are not words and the words are not things. It
is enough if one signified thing pertains to a thing and another to a word.

Protestant reply. According to Bellarmine's logic, then, it is not necessary
that matter not be form and form not be matter. It is not necessary that the
element and word to be joined in a sacrament were previously two things; nor
is it necessary for the element to have any manner of signifying other than
the one present in a word and received through the ear. But paradoxes of this
sort are admitted by the papists for this reason alone: that they may seem in
some way consistent with themselves when they defend those invented
sacraments whose things and words cannot be demonstrated from Scripture or
even by some plausible fiction.

5. Bellarmine. It is not necessary that in every sacrament things and words be
so joined as essential parts of the same thing.

Protestant reply. It is a monster of logic and sound reason for things and
words to constitute one effect or composite as intrinsic causes, matter and
form, and yet never to be joined. But this must be granted to
transubstantiation. By it, whether the thing of the Eucharist is bread and
wine, or that into which the bread and wine are changed, it cannot coexist
with the words that make the change, since those words put an end to the bread
and wine and themselves end when that into which they are changed first
begins to exist. Yet Scotus, in Book 4, distinction 8, question 1, denies for
this very reason that those words are the form of the Eucharist or belong to
its essence, and adds another reason: for then the Eucharist could not be a
permanent sacrament, but one passing away with the words.

Question 2. What word is it that, when joined with the element, makes a
sacrament?

6. Bellarmine. All Catholics teach that the word of a sacrament is a certain
few—

[1632 witness | printed p. 10]

—words prescribed by God, which are to be pronounced by the minister over the
matter.

Protestant reply. True Catholics, ancient as well as recent, teach that the
word of a sacrament is the words of institution applied to the element in
such a way that, with honor to God and the edification of the faithful, it can
be transferred from common to sacred use. But they deny that these words are
prescribed by God in fixed formulas; they deny that the words must necessarily
be few; and, finally, they deny that the words must necessarily be pronounced
*over* the matter for any reason other than that they are employed concerning
the matter before God and the Church.

7. Bellarmine. Words are required not only in baptism but also in the other
sacraments in the very celebration of the mystery.

Protestant reply. Beyond all doubt. But words whispered in the secrecy of the
Mass are not such words as are required, nor are particular fixed verbal
formulas necessarily required for the essence of any sacrament. All the
scholastics concede that Matrimony, too, which the papists number among the
sacraments, can be celebrated without sacramental words.

8. Bellarmine. A preaching word is not required for the essence of any
sacrament.

Protestant reply. A sermon is not required except for the sacrament's proper
well-being. Yet some preaching word—that is, a word of instruction concerning
the nature of the sacrament—is required for its essence to be rightly
celebrated, because the sacrament's signification cannot be celebrated without
a word of this sort.

9. Bellarmine. A fixed form of words is required for the essence of a
sacrament, one which does not so much instruct the bystanders as consecrate
and sanctify the element.

Protestant reply. I. Instructing the bystanders and consecrating the element
should not be opposed to each other, because through instructing the
bystanders about the element's appointment to sacred use, the element itself
is, at least in part, consecrated. II. The contrast “not so much ... as” has no
place where the bystanders are in no way instructed.

Bellarmine 2. The words prescribed by the Lord in baptism do not have the form
of instruction, but of invocation and blessing.

Protestant reply. I. It does not appear that particular words were absolutely
prescribed by the Lord in baptism; Bellarmine himself proves this in chapter
19 of this work. The Lord does not say, “Say, ‘I baptize you in the name of the
Father,’” and so forth; nor did the Lord say, “Say, ‘in the name of the
Father,’” and so forth. He said only, “Baptizing them in the name of the
Father,” and so forth. Those words do not compel us to say “in the name of the
Father,” and so forth. Therefore, although these words are supremely suitable
and, both by their own majesty and by usage, have gained the force of something
nearly necessary, nevertheless, if the same sense were explained in more—

[1632 witness | printed p. 11]

—words, the essence of baptism would not thereby be changed. Certainly in the
formula of the Greeks—“Let Christ's servant be baptized in the name of the
Father and of the Holy Spirit”—the essence of baptism is not changed, for the
Greeks are regarded as baptized. Alexander of Hales too, part 4, question 8,
member 2, article 3, along with other scholastics, maintains that the
perceptible form established by Christ was later changed by the apostles while
the same intelligible form remained. II. The words of this formula contain in
themselves the sum of that instruction which must precede: “Teach and
baptize.”

Bellarmine 3. The words said in the mystery of the Eucharist are called by the
Apostle words of blessing. 1 Corinthians 10: “The cup of blessing.”

Protestant reply. But those words are not the words which the papists maintain
are alone consecratory of the Eucharist—“This is my body”—but the preceding
prayer: “He blessed and broke it and said, ‘Take, eat; this is my body.’”
Chapter 11. From this it is most effectively proved that the words which are
consecratory in the sacrament are not a prescribed few words, but principally
the words of prayer, which have not been written down.

Bellarmine 4. “Name,” in the words of the sacraments, signifies invocation.

Protestant reply. Let it be so; but the whole invocation has not been
prescribed.

10. Bellarmine. Not only the things but also the words in the sacraments of the
new law have been determined by God, so that it is unlawful to change
anything.

Protestant reply. If by *words* is understood not letters and syllables but the
underlying meaning, and if the change is understood as substantial, we concede
all this. But ruinous changes are made by the papists and are commanded to be
made, as everyone knows. Some of their masters say that the formula of baptism
can consist in this: “I baptize you in the name of the Father, and of the Son,
and of the Holy Spirit, and of the Blessed Mary, and of Saint Peter,” if the
addition is made from devotion alone. Alexander of Hales, part 4, question 8,
member 3, article 2, section 3.

Concerning the ministerial efficient cause, the question is whether the
minister's intention is necessary for the sacrament's perfection.

11. Bellarmine. An actual or virtual intention is required to do what the
Church does, whether the true Church or a false one that he supposes to be
true; that is, to do the sacred work or ceremony that Christ instituted.

Protestant reply. An outward expression, by appropriate words and actions, of
the intention to administer the sacrament is indeed required. But such an
inward or mental intention is not required for the essence of the sacrament.

Bellarmine's arguments.

12. Bellarmine. Words can have various meanings. Something is therefore
required to—

[1632 witness | printed p. 12]

—determine their true meaning. But nothing can be devised by which it is
determined except the minister's intention.

Protestant reply. No invention is needed: Christ himself determined the whole
mystery. We do not ordinarily inquire into a herald's intention when he
announces something at another's command. It is enough to understand the
intention of the author who commands it.

13. Bellarmine. Ministers of the sacraments are God's instruments, but they
are living instruments that use reason, as when a king appoints governors and
judges in cities to administer justice in his name. But in such people the will
and intention to exercise the actions are required. For a person whom a judge
acquits without intending it is not truly acquitted, although outwardly he
appears acquitted.

Protestant reply. I. By precisely the same reasoning, the preaching of the
Gospel would cease to be preaching if the mental intention were removed,
although everything else was present. II. On this reasoning, too, the minister
would require not only an intention to perform the work but also an intention
directed to its end. Yet Bellarmine, with the majority of the scholastics,
denies this. III. The fallacy's ground lies in the fact that kings confer a
certain dominion on their governors and judges concerning the actions they
are to perform; but ministers are mere stewards of things whose entire
dominion remains in Christ alone.

14. Bellarmine. Performative words are not effective unless spoken by someone
who has the power and will to do what the words signify. But the words of the
sacraments are performative words.

Protestant reply. They are Christ's performative words, and their efficacy
depends on his power, not on the minister's intention.

Bellarmine 1. It is probable that in the minister himself there is also an
operative power, efficiently and instrumentally, as there is in the sacrament,
so that the operative power of the sacramental words depends upon the
minister, and those words have power only when joined with the power that is
in the minister.

Protestant reply. This opinion is not probable, because an impure and
unregenerate will would often possess an operative power of grace.

Bellarmine 2. At least sacramental words are not moved by God as instruments
except when pronounced by the person to whom he entrusted them.

Protestant reply. By this reasoning they depend no more on the minister's
intention than an edict depends on the herald's intention.

15. Bellarmine. Many absurdities would follow from the contrary opinion. A
person who washed someone in a bath for the sake of health while invoking God
with the words of baptism would baptize him; whenever those words “This is my
body” were read at the table, the table bread would be consecrated, and so
forth.

Protestant reply. They do not follow at all, because an outward sacramental
intention to perform the work is required, although the inward intention of
the minister performing it is not.

[1632 witness | printed p. 13]

Protestant argument. God's Word never requires such an inward intention of
the minister in order to produce its effect; therefore neither does the
sacrament.

Bellarmine 1. The word, indeed, when uttered with any intention whatever, can
arouse faith, yet it cannot have a sacramental effect. This is the difference:
no efficacy is required to arouse faith; signification is enough.

Protestant reply. I. Signification itself must be determined no less in the
word than in the sacrament, but from this determination Bellarmine earlier
proved the necessity of intention. II. The efficacy of God's mighty arm is
required for faith to be engendered and aroused through the word. Romans 1:16.
III. We have proved above, and shall prove later, that arousing and confirming
faith is a sacramental effect.

Bellarmine 2. The words of sermons are speculative words, but sacramental
words are performative words.

Protestant reply. I. Only to sophists with alien judgments are the words of
sermons merely speculative. James 1:22. II. The practical efficacy of both the
word and the sacraments depends upon one and the same Spirit and will—not the
minister's but the principal author's.

17. Protestant argument. If the sacrament's efficacy depended upon the
minister's intention, the certainty that a believer ought to have concerning
the sacrament's effect, and spiritual consolation, would perish, since no one
can be certain of another's intention.

Bellarmine 1. A person in this world ought not to seek an infallible certainty
of his salvation or justification, such as the certainty of divine faith.

Protestant reply. I. What, then, do those words of Peter mean: “Be diligent to
make your election and calling firm”? 2 Peter 1:10. II. The issue here is not
certainty of this or that fruit, but certainty of the sacrament itself and of
receiving some fruit from it.

Bellarmine 2. Human and moral certainty sufficient for a person's judgment is
possessed when there is no cause to doubt the minister's intention.

Protestant reply. I. Human and moral certainty admits various degrees. Not
every presumption suffices for peace of conscience in a question concerning a
means necessary to salvation. II. There are weighty causes for doubt, because
many of those who administer baptism are vain and thoroughly wicked men.

Bellarmine 3. Lutherans who are baptized as infants come to know this only if
they believe their parents or friends, who can nevertheless deceive them.

Protestant reply. The reasoning is different. For concerning some outward
fact, such certainty can be obtained from human testimonies—

[1632 witness | printed p. 14]

—as Bellarmine himself teaches in *On Councils*, Book 2, chapter 9—that it can
in some manner be compared with natural evidence, since it leaves no doubt in
our minds. But concerning another person's inward intention, we have no such
proofs.
