Verse 6. Instruct a boy according to the measure of his way; even when he has grown old, he will not depart from it.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 492 | printed columns 968-969]

In חנך על פי (*institue pro ratione*, instruct according to the measure) there is a metaphor transferred from corporeal matters to spiritual ones. For just as nurses bring food little by little to infants, whose mouths are narrow, so the easiest and briefest lessons must be instilled into boys, whose understanding is slight and meagre. Or, just as liquid is poured gradually into vessels whose mouth is narrow, so boys, whose capacity is more confined, must be instructed gradually by precepts.

The purpose is to stir parents toward the godly and prudent instruction of their children. Here are considered, first, an exhortation to parents concerning the godly and prudent education of their children; then the reason, or argument, upon which the exhortation rests.

In the exhortation, first, the subject to be instructed by parents is considered, namely, the boy. By this word is denoted one who moves himself, so that we may understand that as soon as he begins to exercise his strength a little, it is necessary immediately to make a beginning of instruction, Deut. 6.6. This very thing is taught still more expressly at 2 Tim. 3.15. Consequently, the error of those who determine that boys should be permitted every freedom of playing is not slight. They also determine that not the least trouble should be given them, by which they might be hindered from growing in stature, as they object.

But if someone should perhaps ask how this may be done when they understand so little of more sublime matters that they do not know how to determine even the distinction between right and left, it must be known that they possess greater understanding than is commonly thought. For if even the most eloquent adults have more understanding within them than they can express in words, how much more must the same be determined concerning infants, to whom, because of the weakness of their organs, no faculty of speech is granted? Yet it is evident from the following that they are endowed with some understanding: they deeply love those who nurse them, feed them, and carry them in their hands, but flee from those who perform no office of this kind, much more from those who do what is disagreeable. I ask also why it would not have been as easy to calm those peevish children by mention of divine wrath as by those silly, senseless, and wholly fictitious terrors of Dryad girls or of beggars who would come to carry them away.

The reason why he wishes boys to be instructed is this: they have pliant minds which, like wax, readily admit whatever image the person who impresses it upon them has in mind; whereas old people perceive the things delivered to them only with great difficulty.

Next, the manner and method of educating a boy in godliness and duty is indicated when he says that this must be done according to the measure of the boy and according to his capacity. Three things are required for the right instruction of a boy, as the Apostle indicates at Eph. 6.4. The first is that we imbue him with knowledge. That will finally be done according to the measure of his way if we disclose the things that are easiest, as is evident from the meaning of the word itself, by which it is intimated that he is to be nourished in godliness. The second is that we set forth in words the most familiar teaching. The third is that we do it gradually and, as it were, drop by drop, lest he be overwhelmed by a multitude of precepts, Heb. 5.12; 1 Cor. 3.12; Isa. 28.1.

Next, it is required that we apply παιδεία (*discipline*), both by setting forth rewards fitted to attract boys and by restraining those who transgress with a correction suitable for curbing them.

There follows an argument from the effect, by which he applies a goad to parents to perform this so holy office. Indeed, they will never afterward forsake the good way upon which they entered in childhood, not even in old age, when people are usually seen to decline and become children again and when the faculties of the mind are in great part blunted and weakened, much less in adolescence or

[1632 witness | PDF p. 493 | printed columns 970-971]

youth, in which, just as lessons are readily received, so after they have been received they are retained firmly and tenaciously. See Lam. 3.27. For what is customarily said of an earthen vessel—that it will long preserve the odour with which it was freshly imbued—we experience as no less true in people themselves. We see that they retain most firmly and for the longest time the things which they imbibed as boys or infants. Old age, by contrast, just as it admits with the greatest difficulty the exceedingly hard, metal-like impression of sound lessons, so allows it, once admitted, to be erased most easily and swiftly, as though it were made of wax or ashes.

From this those diabolical Proverbs are refuted: [*From a shaggy colt comes a noble horse; but from a holy young man comes an old devil.*] The former of these—without my opposing this divine Proverb to it or answering that the nature of man and horse is not the same in all respects—is entirely overturned by the comparison which they themselves adduce. For it is too well known to be decently denied that horses, unless they are tamed while still colts and accustomed to human use, never become noble and never obedient. The latter, however, is refuted by the constant testimony of Scripture, which affirms that one once imbued with the spirit of sanctification will always persist in the grace and favour of God.

This, however, like nearly all other Proverbs, must be understood as true ὡς τὸ πολὺ (*for the most part*). For sometimes, as is discerned in King Joash, those who were well educated in childhood degenerate. Conversely, people who have polluted themselves throughout the remaining course of life sometimes come to uprightness as their age declines, as we read happened in Manasseh.

Verse 7. A rich man will rule over a poor man; and one who receives a loan will be servant to the one who lends.

The purpose is to set before people's eyes, for detestation, the oppression of the poor by the rich.

It was said at chap. 18, verse 23, that a poor man addresses a rich man submissively and most humbly with supplicating words, but that the rich man answers harshly and peevishly. Nearly the same thing is set forth in this Proverb. The reason is that they are proud from confidence in riches and lofty in mind. Hence the Apostle earnestly admonishes them at 1 Tim. 6.17, 18 to take diligent care against elevation of mind. Without this care they raise their horns and butt the poor with them. For although people destitute of resources are not thereby diminished in status, rich people are nevertheless accustomed to regard them as slaves. Perhaps in words they make requests and are in some manner supplicants, but in such a way that, as someone aptly said, αἱ γὰρ δεσποτῶν δεήσεις ἀνάγκη εἰσί (*for masters' entreaties amount to compulsion*). For unless the poor devote to them the service they request, they immediately become inflamed and prepare themselves for vengeance.

But this injustice will not escape unpunished by God. By it they first arrogate to themselves a power which properly belongs to the Magistrate, and then affliction is accumulated upon the afflicted. For poverty is by itself a sufficiently harsh weapon of God; moreover, what else is depriving a person of liberty than furnishing more abundant material for grief? See what God has threatened against people of this kind at Ps. 109.16. Then there is another corruption: the rich, although wicked and foolish, nevertheless gain control of affairs ahead of the godly and prudent poor.

In the latter hemistich he appears to affirm something more severe and bitter concerning the person who receives a loan: that he will be a servant, and so forth. For the yoke of servitude is harsher than that of subjection, just as masterly rule is more constraining than royal rule. And if a rich man exercises dominion over a poor man upon whom he has conferred no benefit, how much more imperiously will he rule over the one upon whom he has conferred one? This, however, manifestly conflicts with that teaching of Christ at Luke 6.30, where he admonishes that one must give freely to the poor, without even expecting the principal.

But what is said about a poor man receiving a loan is also not rarely proved most true in the person who gives one. For if someone of lower condition lends to someone more powerful, he generally becomes the latter's servant in order to recover his money.

This Proverb ought to teach us not readily to cast ourselves into debt, as very many do. For besides the fact that receiving a loan is a sign of divine wrath, Deut. 28.44, the person who borrows something from others, especially from the rich, delivers himself in a certain manner into servitude, as though a man acquired for himself as many harsh masters as he has creditors. For this reason we ought to apply ourselves diligently to work so that everything necessary for life may be abundantly available to us. Nor should we live so luxuriously in food or clothing that we are finally compelled to enslave ourselves to others. In Judaea it was indeed established by a certain special arrangement that a debtor who was unable to pay should be compelled by law to serve together with his children. Among us matters are handled not much more mildly, or even more harshly, when he is committed to prison, where he can be of use neither to himself nor to others.
