QUESTION XVIII.

Was Peter at Rome, and did he hold the episcopate there for many years? We
deny against the papists.

I. Although the question of Peter’s stay at Rome may seem historical rather
than theological, nevertheless, because the papists rely especially upon the
hypothesis that Peter was at Rome and held the episcopate there for many years
in order to establish the primacy and monarchy of the pope, it is very
important to examine its truth or vanity more closely. In this way we may more
easily expose the impiety of papal tyranny. Nor is it surprising that the
adversaries are so agitated over this, for they know that the whole mass of
the pontifical hierarchy rests upon this foundation and must collapse when it
is removed. For if Peter was not at Rome and did not exercise the episcopate
there, this authority could not have been transmitted to the Roman pontiff so
that he would be regarded as Peter’s successor. Therefore, this matter must be
investigated all the more diligently so that it may be evident upon what a
fragile—indeed, false—foundation this whole mystery of iniquity rests.

[Marginalia: The positions of the parties.]

II. The adversaries agree among themselves on two things: (1) Peter was at
Rome and died there. (2) He held the episcopate there for many years and
neither changed his see nor transferred it elsewhere, but occupied it
continuously until the end of his life. Yet they disagree about when he came
to Rome and how long he occupied it. Some, following Jerome and Eusebius,
think that he came in the second year of Claudius. Others say the fourth year,
as Thomas, Bede, and the *Fascicle of Times*; others the thirteenth year, as
the *Passional on the Lives of the Saints*; some think he occupied it for only
twenty-three years, and others for twenty-five. The more common opinion,
followed by Baronius and Bellarmine, is that Peter, after remaining in Judea
for five years from the Lord’s passion, came from there to Antioch in the year
39 of Christ, undertook its episcopate, and discharged it for seven years;
that he then went to Rome, established his chair there, and occupied it as the
first of all; that meanwhile it happened that in the year 51 Peter was
expelled from Rome with the other Jews by the edict of Claudius, on which
occasion he came to the council held at Jerusalem that year; and finally,
after Claudius died, that he returned to Rome and, after presiding over that
see for twenty-five years, ended his life by martyrdom. We say two things.
First, that Peter was at Rome is doubtful and highly uncertain, and it is far
more certain that he never saw Rome. Second, that Peter was bishop of Rome is
false, and that he occupied the see there for so many years is utterly false.
It must be admitted that among our men there are great men who, moved by the
testimonies of the ancients, suspend judgment here, and indeed readily grant
that Peter was at Rome. Such are Junius, Scaliger, Casaubon, Ussher, Selden,
and others, although even they do not agree with the papists about Peter’s
Roman episcopate and the twenty-five years assigned to it. But not a few
others, no less distinguished, openly take the negative side here, such as
Illyricus, Velenus, Whitaker, Salmasius, Hospinianus, Sutlivius, Pareus,
Sibrandus, and others. They had already been preceded by men among the papists
such as Johannes Baptista Mantuanus, Michael of Cesena, Marsilius of Padua,
who debated this question before Emperor Louis two hundred years ago and
whose book is entitled *Defender of the Peace*, Johannes Aventinus, Carolus
Molinaeus, and others,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 145 | IA leaf 179 | PDF p. 179]

who were led by the doubtful reliability of the tradition. Their arguments
are neither few nor slight, and we join them.

[Marginalia: It is proved that Peter was not at Rome. 1. From Luke’s silence.]

III. The first reason is drawn from Luke’s silence in the Acts of the
Apostles. For if Peter left the borders of Syria, went to Rome, and blessed
the church founded there by his preaching and martyrdom; if in this place he
erected the pontifical see, the head of all Christianity, as they commonly
boast, why is Luke silent and neglects so great a matter, one so necessary for
Christians to know? Why, when he speaks about Peter’s first encounter with
Simon Magus, does he say nothing about the final contest, in which that magus
is said to have paid the penalty for his impiety while the Romans and Nero
himself looked on? Why does he relate so carefully Peter’s various journeys
to Samaria, Lydda, Joppa, Caesarea, and Jerusalem, yet does not even mention
his most momentous journey all the way to Rome? Why, when in Acts 15 he
presents Peter as present at the council, does he not commemorate his return
from the city and the chair he left behind? Finally, why, when speaking of
Paul’s entrance into the city, does he mention various others but say not a
word about Peter? Nor should it be replied here either that the argument is
negative and therefore has no force, because it is not unusual to use a
negative argument where no trustworthy affirmative testimony from the same
age can be set against it, and the reasoning rests not only upon the fact that
Luke is silent but also upon the right of the matter, because he neither could
nor ought to have been silent about so momentous a thing; or that Luke set
before himself chiefly the task of tracing the acts of Paul, his teacher, for
he himself declares that he wished to embrace apostolic history in general.
And if he was concerned to commemorate matters concerning Peter which were
far less important, why did he envelop this far weightier one in silence?

[Marginalia: 2. From Paul’s silence.]

IV. The second is from Paul’s silence in his epistles. For if Peter had been
at Rome, is it likely that Paul, writing to the Romans and greeting all who
were present, especially his *fellow workers*, would have omitted a greeting
to Peter, the most worthy of them all, and not even mentioned him? Baronius
objects in vain that it was unnecessary for Peter to be greeted through
others, since Paul could have sent another private letter to Peter by the same
messenger. This is a fiction which is rejected as easily as it is proposed.
Nor is it any better when he replies that Peter was then absent, having been
driven from Rome by the edict of Claudius. For besides the fact that this
flight of Peter from fear of the Claudian persecution is assumed but not
proved, it is certain according to Baronius’s computation that Claudius had
died by the time Paul wrote his epistle to the Romans. And if many Jews whom
the apostle greets were then at Rome, why not Peter also? If Aquila and
Priscilla, who had previously been driven out by Claudius’s edict, were
permitted to return to Rome, why not Peter? Was it fitting for a pastor to be
absent so long from his church? Then, if Peter had once come to Rome to found
the church, how does it happen that Paul, who in this epistle praises the
faith of the Romans and those through whom the cause of Christ had been
vigorously advanced among them, forgets so great a founder? Then how could he
profess that he did not wish *to build upon* a foundation laid by others?
Furthermore, how does it happen that in all the epistles Paul sent from Rome
he mentions Clement, Luke, Timothy, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Epaphras,
who worked with him, but everywhere maintains a deep silence about Peter? Nor
should it be replied that no mention is similarly made of Timothy, John, or
James in the epistles to the Ephesians and Hebrews, although they were leading
men and bishops in those churches. For the force of our argument is not
simply that Paul does not mention Peter, but that he mentions all the others
who were at Rome, especially his *fellow workers*, but never Peter, the most
worthy of them all and the one to be greeted first. Add that in the epistles
to the Ephesians and Hebrews Paul greets no one by name, having discharged

[1847 vol. III printed p. 146 | IA leaf 180 | PDF p. 180]

the duty with a general wish of greeting. Nor is it established that Timothy
or John was at Ephesus then. Finally, how could he say in the epistle to the
Colossians concerning Aristarchus, Mark, and Jesus, who were at Rome, that
“these alone from the circumcision” were his “fellow workers for the kingdom
of God,” and that they alone were his helpers? And in the later epistle to
Timothy, how could he say that no one *stood by him* in the defense of his
case, but that all had deserted him, with the Lord alone adding both strength
and comfort? Hence either Peter was not then at Rome, or Paul must be judged
guilty of great cowardice or unfaithfulness.

[Marginalia: 3. From Peter’s silence.]

V. Peter’s own silence supplies the third reason. For if Peter founded the
Roman church, why does he make no mention of it anywhere? Why does he not
recall this office of his at the beginning of the epistles to the dispersed
Jews, so as to procure greater authority for himself among them? Why, when
exhorting the elders to their duty, does he call himself a *fellow elder* with
them (1 Pet. 5:1)? Why, when he was compelled to be absent from Rome for a
long time, as the adversaries acknowledge, did pastoral concern not move him
to write to the Romans, his flock, as Paul did in order to strengthen and
increase their faith? These things are certainly such that, unless we wish to
be willfully blind, they clearly prove that Peter’s ecumenical pontificate at
Rome is a mere fiction.

[Marginalia: 4. From the chronology.]

VI. Fourth, if Peter was at Rome, he must have been there either under Gaius,
under Claudius, or under Nero, during whose reign Paul stayed at Rome, was
imprisoned, or was put to death. But he cannot be said to have been there
under any of them. Not under Gaius, because Luke’s history presents him at
that time in Judea and Samaria. Not under Claudius, whether in the second year
or any other time during his reign, because it is evident from Acts that
Peter was captured by Herod and cast into prison at Jerusalem in Claudius’s
fourth year, which was the last year of Herod Agrippa. Hence he could not have
set out for Rome before that year, as Valesius acknowledges. Again, Peter was
still at Jerusalem at the time of the apostolic council, which was assembled
six years later, that is, in the eighteenth year after Christ’s passion, the
ninth year of Claudius, and the fourteenth year after Paul’s conversion. Paul
went there with Barnabas, as Galatians 2:1 testifies. From there Peter went to
Antioch and remained there for some time. Therefore, he could not have been
at Rome under Claudius. For what Bellarmine alleges, that Peter returned from
Rome to Jerusalem because of Claudius’s edict by which the Jews were expelled
from Rome, is frivolous. For if he returned from Rome, why does Luke, who
commemorates the arrival of Priscilla and Aquila for this reason, make no
mention of Peter? Then, if Peter had already been constituted bishop of the
Roman church, which consisted not only of Jews but of far more Gentiles, why
did he desert that assembly? Ought not God to have been obeyed here rather
than men? And ought he not to have confirmed the faith of the faithful with
his own blood rather than shamefully desert them? Thus the event is assumed
without evidence for it, contrary to Peter’s fortitude and the duty of a good
pastor. Again, if he had been forced to withdraw, he would at least not have
failed in the duty of correspondence. Following Paul’s example and with even
greater right, he would have encouraged the Romans—his church and
*episcopate*, separated from him by the adversity of the times and divided by
the interval of places—with some epistle. Finally, if the ecumenical pontiff
had been established in his see, why is no mention made of so distinguished a
privilege at the Jerusalem council? Indeed, why does he not settle this
question alone by his own right, but leave this task to James?

VII. It cannot be said that Peter came to Rome or remained there under Nero,
Claudius’s successor. (1) Because then very few years remain for Peter’s
episcopate, unless we wish him to have been put to death under Vespasian or
Titus. Add that since Paul wrote to the Romans in Nero’s second or third year
and says not a word about Peter, it is evident that Peter was not then at
Rome; nor was he there when Paul came to the city in Nero’s sixth year;

[1847 vol. III printed p. 147 | IA leaf 181 | PDF p. 181]

nor when Paul wrote his six later epistles from Rome. For who would believe
that if Peter had been there at that time, Paul would have said absolutely
nothing about him? When Paul came to Rome, the brothers there met him at the
Forum of Appius. Peter certainly would then have met him if he had been
there, but no mention of Peter is made. Then, after the third day, Paul calls
together the leading Jews, but they, not yet Christians at the time, desire
to hear from Paul what he thinks. But if Peter was then at Rome and was bishop
there, would they not already have heard the Christian faith from him,
especially since he was their apostle? Bellarmine replies in vain that Peter
was absent at the time. For who would believe that Peter had left his church
for so long when he could have been safe there? If he was bishop of that
church, where should he have remained rather than at Rome? Otherwise, would
he not be accused of supine negligence?

[Marginalia: 5. From the distribution of the office between Peter and Paul.]

VIII. Fifth, the same thing is inferred not obscurely from the distribution
of the office made between Peter and Paul, concerning which Galatians 2:7–9
speaks. By this distribution, the special care chiefly of the Jews and “the
apostleship of the circumcision,” as Paul calls it, was entrusted to Peter,
while “the care of the Gentiles” and the preaching to the uncircumcision were
entrusted to Paul. This was not done absolutely to limit the breadth of the
apostolic office, which is ecumenical, but by a special dispensation for the
more convenient and ready diffusion of the gospel. Nor can it be doubted that
Peter diligently discharged that task among the Jews, whether in person in
Palestine, Syria, and so forth, or *by letter*, as is evident from both
epistles written to the Jews *of the dispersion*. With this established, who
could easily persuade himself that the apostle Peter, at the very time when
Paul and Barnabas were devoted to the Gentiles, neglected his own assigned
parts amid so great a harvest of Jews throughout Greece, Thrace, Asia Minor,
Syria, Babylonia, and the whole East, made long journeys to Rome to establish
his see and pontifical chair there, where there were very few Jews in
comparison with the Gentiles, and would never think of returning to the East
except through a certain accident, namely, the edict of Claudius, after which
he would retrace his step to the city and remain there for many years until
death? Who would not rather believe that, after the labors were distributed,
Peter exercised his ministry among the Jews, instead of setting the Jews
aside and administering his episcopate among the Gentiles for a full
twenty-five years? Nor does Bellarmine’s observation untie the knot: “This
compact had only this force, that Peter preached principally to the Jews and
Paul to the Gentiles, while it was nevertheless permissible for Paul to
preach to the Jews and for Peter to the Gentiles.” Although we do not deny
this, it must nevertheless be admitted that this would have been, as it were,
extraordinary and *incidental*, and that Peter’s ordinary see ought to have
been where there was the greatest multitude of Jews, namely, in the East, not
in the West and at Rome, which was the head of the Gentiles and the seat of
the empire, where there were few Jews and far more Gentiles.

[Marginalia: 6. From chronology.]

IX. Sixth, although it were established that Peter was at Rome, it is
impossible that he remained there for twenty-five years, as the adversaries
maintain. For from the eighteenth year of Tiberius, when Christ was crucified,
to Nero’s thirteenth year, when Peter is held to have been put to death by
him, thirty-six years are counted: namely, five years of Tiberius, four of
Caligula, fourteen of Claudius, and thirteen of Nero. But Peter did not leave
Judea before the twelfth year after Christ’s death, when he was captured by
Herod and cast into prison. This is abundantly evident from the Acts of the
Apostles, where his presence and deeds at Jerusalem and in Judea are recounted
in a continuous sequence (chapters 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12). Six years
later, that is, in the eighteenth year after Christ’s death, the Jerusalem
council was assembled. Paul went to it (Acts 15:2–3; Gal. 2:1), and Peter
attended it with the other apostles. Again, on the adversaries’ hypothesis, he
had to occupy the see at Antioch for seven years. Thus only eleven years
remain in which he could have remained at Rome, not the twenty-five they
would have. It is far more likely that he spent those years elsewhere in the
East, where the ministry to the Jews

[1847 vol. III printed p. 148 | IA leaf 182 | PDF p. 182]

entrusted to him could be better exercised.

[Marginalia: 7. From Clement.]

X. Seventh, Clement’s testimony in his first epistle to the Corinthians does
not contribute a little to this. There he speaks about both apostles, Peter
and Paul, and about the labors and martyrdom of each. But he speaks briefly
about Peter and designates neither the place of his martyrdom, his journeys to
the West, nor his episcopate at Rome. Yet he speaks far more fully about Paul
as one put to death after Peter, as greatest of all in his labors, as one who
was carried through Rome to the limits of the West, as the teacher of the
whole world, and as one who suffered under the emperors, namely Nero. “On
account of wicked envy,” he says in paragraph 5, “Peter endured not one or two
but many labors, and, having thus suffered martyrdom, departed to his due
place of glory. Because of envy Paul was cast into bonds seven times, beaten
with blows, and stoned; he obtained the reward of patience, and, having become
a herald of the word in the East and West, received the illustrious fame of
his faith. Instructing the world in righteousness, reaching the limits of the
West, and undergoing martyrdom under the emperors, he thus departed from the
world and went to the holy place, having become the highest example of
patience.” Who would believe that Clement would have omitted Peter’s journey
to the West, his stay at Rome, and his martyrdom there under the emperors,
which he said about Paul, if these things had been so? How could he have
enveloped in silence Peter’s far more illustrious Roman contests and a
punishment comparable to Christ’s—indeed, more grievous because his body was
inverted—while Rome looked on, after the consecration of his chair and the
designation of his successor, who, as they maintain, was Clement himself? Nor
should the various authors who repeatedly relate Peter’s coming to Rome and
his martyrdom there, such as Ignatius and Papias, be raised in objection,
because, as will be said later, either they are later than Clement or are
certainly of doubtful authority or opinion. The silence of writers of Roman
history, such as Tacitus and Suetonius, who handed down the events under
Claudius and Nero, can also be referred to this. They lacked neither occasion
nor cause to mention the Prince of the Apostles then dwelling at Rome,
especially in the affair of Simon Magus, who was very dear to Nero, as they
say: Peter’s conflict with him, his victory over him, Nero’s anger finally
kindled against the author of Simon’s calamity, and the monstrous punishment
inflicted upon the Prince of the Christians.

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions.]

XI. Although the Babylon about which John speaks in Revelation is none other
than Rome, because it is described by characteristics which can belong to no
other city—namely, that it was the city of seven hills and at that time held
dominion over the kings of the earth—it does not follow that the same city is
understood by Babylon in 1 Peter 5:13: “The church which is at Babylon greets
you.” For John was writing prophecy and therefore spoke mystically and
enigmatically, but Peter spoke historically and simply because he was writing
an epistle in which matters are related plainly and openly. Nor did he have
any more reason than Paul to conceal the city’s name; Paul nevertheless
openly displays it when he writes from Rome. And it is surprising that most
papists wish to take Babylon literally in Revelation, a mystical and
prophetic writing, yet wish to take that name mystically in a purely
historical writing which indicates the place from which the epistle was
written. Add that there is no reason why Peter would have designated Rome by
Babylon. Was it because idols were worshiped there? But this occurred
everywhere. Was it out of fear that his whereabouts would become known? But
why such timidity in him? Did not Paul write straightforwardly to the Romans
and write several epistles from Rome, neither suppressing nor changing its
name, but freely expressing it? Rome is called Babylon in Revelation chiefly
because of the spiritual bondage which the church of God was going to suffer
there, but at that time the church had not yet suffered any persecution. It
cannot be said that Rome was commonly called this, because John presents this
name as

[1847 vol. III printed p. 149 | IA leaf 183 | PDF p. 183]

*mysterious*, and thus as a secret. Nor does the testimony of Papias and those
who followed him persuade us of this, since their reliability is suspect, as
will be said later. Therefore, no place can be designated by Babylon more
appropriately and properly than the capital of the Chaldeans and Assyrians,
which was the citadel and seat of the empire and the metropolis of that
dispersion to which Peter writes, namely, of Pontus, Galatia, and so forth.
Babylon had its own *exilarch* and many from the circumcision, whose care was
common to Peter and John. How great a gathering of Jews there was is evident
even from the fact that the Jewish schools were eventually transferred from
Palestine to Babylonia, from which the Babylonian Talmud takes its name.
Finally, since no necessity compels us to depart from the proper sense of the
letter, there is no need to seek a mystical sense after abandoning the
literal one, as Bellarmine acknowledges in *On the Eucharist* 1.12.

XII. Although many Christians were already at Rome before Paul’s arrival, and
indeed the church there had already begun to be founded, it does not follow
that Peter founded it. It could have been gathered by others, for just as in
the Jerusalem dispersion the disciples scattered here and there preached the
gospel—Philip in Samaria and others elsewhere—why could some not also have
set out for Rome? It is evident that already in the time of Tiberius the name
of Christ and of Christians was not unknown, because he brought before the
senate the matter of enrolling Christ among the gods, and because the senate,
in refusing this, resolved that Christians were to be exterminated. If,
therefore, Christians already existed then, why not disciples also?

XIII. The agreement of very many ancients who consistently hand down that
Peter was at Rome and died there cannot command unconditional belief, because
this tradition takes its beginning from Papias, bishop of the church at
Hierapolis in Phrygia. According to the testimony of this same Eusebius,
Papias was not only “extremely small in mind”—“of very mediocre talent and
altogether rude and simple”—but also vain and fabulous, one “who handed down
many extraordinary and unwritten things, unbelievable and unwritten, and
certain things more mythical, more like fables than true histories” (Eusebius
3.39). He was also the author of the opinion of the chiliasts. He was the
first to write that Peter had been at Rome. Hegesippus, Irenaeus, Clement of
Alexandria, and others later followed him because of his antiquity, and thus
the opinion gained force. The same Eusebius testifies to this when he declares
that Papias “became the cause of a similar error” for very many ecclesiastical
writers after him, and specifically for Irenaeus himself. Therefore, since
the father of this tradition is of such suspect reliability in various
matters, how will it be able to obtain our assent when so many arguments drawn
both from Scripture and from reason prove the contrary? After Eusebius,
Jerome is the authority that this Papias was not a hearer of the apostle John,
but of a certain presbyter of the same name who was distinct from the apostle.
Baronius proves this at length under the year 118, numbers 3 and 4, and not
obscurely notes Papias’s doubtful reliability in number 5 after reporting
Eusebius’s words. “From these things you may easily understand,” he says,
“that discrimination must be exercised in traditions, so that as soon as
someone claims that he received something from the tradition of his
forebears, as happened with Papias, everyone does not immediately give him
credence.”

XIV. To the testimony of Irenaeus, who maintains that Peter and Paul
evangelized at Rome and founded the church (*Against Heresies* 3.1 and 3), we
say: (1) In excessive credulity he followed the testimony of Papias, or else
he attributed far too much to the tradition of the Roman church, which was
already exalting itself because of the city’s dominion and boasting of both
apostles as its founders. (2) The adversaries do not always believe Irenaeus
but often contradict him. (3) The words and intention of Irenaeus do not agree
with the pontifical hypothesis, because he attributes the foundation of the
Roman church equally to Paul and Peter, who administered it together and
finally handed the episcopate over to Linus with equal power (book 5, chapter
6).

[1847 vol. III printed p. 150 | IA leaf 184 | PDF p. 184]

XV. Since the story of Simon Magus defeated by Peter at Rome rests only upon
human testimony for which no eyewitnesses can be given, it cannot obtain
assent. It is established from elsewhere that his wickedness was exposed by
Peter in Samaria (Acts 8:23) before he came to Rome, and that he came to Rome
only after he had been defeated and had fled the apostle’s sight, as Clement
reports in *Constitutions* 6.6 and Eusebius in book 2, chapter 14. Hence it is
not at all probable that Peter had to betake himself to Rome to destroy him
again. Now the statue which Justin Martyr says was erected to Simon with this
inscription, “To Simon, the holy God,” is wrongly referred to him. Learned men
have long observed that ignorance of the Latin language misled the ancient
writer, so that he understood as referring to “holy Simon” what ought to have
been read as referring to “Semo Sancus.” The most illustrious Vossius shows
this in *On the Origin of Idolatry* 1.12: Semo Sancus was among the indigenous
gods of the Romans and was also called Fidius because faith was pledged by
swearing through him. A Roman inscription in Gruter reads, “Sacred to Semo
Sancus Dius Fidius,” and another, “Sacred to Sanctus Sancus Semo Dius
Fidius.” To this pertain these lines of Ovid, *Fasti* 6:

> I asked whether I should assign the Nones to Sanctus, Fidius,
> Or to you, Father Semo; then Sanctus said to me:
> “Whichever one of these you give them to, I shall have the honor;
> I bear three divine names; thus the people of Cures willed.”

Semo, Sanctus, and Fidius were the same. Hence arose the error of Justin
Martyr: because his fellow Samaritans regarded Simon Magus, mentioned in Acts,
as a holy god, he thought that a statue erected at Rome to the holy god Semo
had been erected to Simon Magus.

XVI. Not everyone who maintains that Peter was at Rome immediately asserts
that he was bishop of the church there, much less that he had a see there for
twenty-five years. It is one thing for Peter to have been at Rome and another
for him to have erected a monarchical government. Hence these testimonies do
nothing for the primacy of the pope, who, they maintain, succeeded Peter in
the ecumenical episcopate. Peter could have died at Rome although he was not
bishop there, just as Paul did. And since all these things rest upon merely
human testimony and Scripture is silent about them all, the primacy of the
pope cannot be asserted as an article which must be believed with divine
faith.

XVII. Although it were granted that Peter both lived and died at Rome, his
primacy would no more be established there than at Joppa or Antioch, where he
also lived. Rather, it would be claimed with far greater right for the church
of Jerusalem, where the Savior and the apostle James died, and from which the
light of the gospel was diffused into every corner of the earth. Nor can this
be proved from what is said among the ancients about the *See of Peter*, as if
it were a kind of royal throne and seat, since by it nothing else is
designated than the doctrine preached somewhere by Peter, according to
Matthew 23:2 and Acts 18:11.

XVIII. The true reason and origin of the primacy of the Roman church must be
derived not from the succession of Peter but from the dignity of the city
itself, because in the old Roman Empire the dignity of bishops was estimated
according to the political and civil dignity of the cities in which they sat,
as will be said later.

XIX. Although Peter was not bishop of Rome, it is not necessary for us to
show where he sat—that is, exercised the episcopate—from the time he left
Antioch until his final day, because it was no more necessary for Peter than
for Paul to occupy a see. It belonged to the apostles to travel through
various parts of the world, and this is evident concerning Peter himself from
the testimony of the ancients. But if *to sit* is taken for *to teach*, Peter
could have sat in various places—Jerusalem, Babylon, and the other churches
through which he traveled—although he was not and cannot be called bishop of
them.
