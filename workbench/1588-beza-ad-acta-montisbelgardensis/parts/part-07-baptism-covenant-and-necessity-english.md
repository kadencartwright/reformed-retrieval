## Part VII: The Covenant and the Necessity of Baptism

[Part II | PDF page 137 | logical source scan 136 | printed page 135 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

It remains for us now to discuss how far we are bound by the law of baptism and how far we are released from its necessity. For here two necessities confront us, corresponding to one another: one by which we are bound, the other by which we are freed. One who is not obligated labors in vain over liberation; conversely, one who remains bound by the law’s bond dreams in vain that he is free.

I say, therefore, that with respect to the obligation to fulfill the law itself, there is a great difference between the covenant of salvation itself and the seals appended to that covenant to seal its promise in believers’ souls with greater assurance of faith. We would not even need these if our faith were as great and firm as it ought to be. The covenant’s compact is such that it admits no exception whatever: without any distinction of persons, places, or times, one must either be included in it or perish. Outside Christ, who is as it were the substance of this covenant, there is no salvation at all. According to the covenant’s formula, however, Christ is applied only to believers. Christ’s saying therefore stands without admitting any excuse of necessity: “He who does not believe will be condemned.” We have said in its proper place how infants believe in their godly parents, or are reckoned faithful.

The sacraments, however, are appendices of that saving covenant. Properly speaking, God neither obligates himself by them to save us nor do we obtain by them our right to eternal life. Rather, they are aids—before us and before the church—to our antecedent faith: God has already obligated himself to that faith, and we in turn have received his grace, to be sealed more and more as faith thus grows in us. It follows that the use and reception of the sacraments is not commanded by God absolutely, as we said of the covenant itself, but insofar as God grants the opportunity and ability to obtain that aid lawfully, that is, according to his ordinance.

Thus in human contracts the obligation itself arises from the parties’ agreement, while the instrument of contract, the notary’s subscription, the witnesses’ names, and the affixing of the public seal are used only for confirmation. These are not so necessarily required that a contract cannot remain firm without them through the parties’ mutual consent, or be invalidated by one party’s dissent, provided the contract itself is established by some means other than those common and ordinary testimonies. This must be held also because the covenant itself was framed by God universally and absolutely, as we have said, while sacraments are commanded only with certain circumstances attached. If someone through no fault of his own is wholly prevented from observing those circumstances, he can rightly be judged guilty of violating the law neither before God nor before men, since God, as if by a clear voice, releases him from the law’s command—

—itself. Thus formerly circumcision never obligated females, while it commanded that males be circumcised only on the eighth day. Therefore it was never necessary for the salvation of females, and became necessary for males only on the eighth day. But what if, on the eighth day, an infant could not be circumcised because of disease or some similar circumstance without danger to life? Should he have been exposed to death rather than have circumcision deferred? Let Andreae, at once the Israelites’ gravest and gentlest accuser, answer as he pleases concerning their failure to circumcise their infants in the wilderness for so many years, while Moses and Aaron supposedly protested. For my part, I assert no less certainly, but I think more truly, that those who circumcised such an infant—likely to die from the wound—without regard to his health would have been guilty of homicide before God.

What Christ said concerning the Sabbath, and confirmed by his example, I do not hesitate to affirm of the whole ceremonial law and therefore of circumcision itself: the Sabbath was instituted for man, not man for the Sabbath—that is, the ceremony of Sabbath-keeping. Accordingly, the Lawgiver framed the threat against the uncircumcised in words sufficiently showing that the penalty bound not everyone who was not circumcised precisely on the eighth day, but indefinitely those who remained uncircumcised; and not all the uncircumcised generally, but finally those who as adults neglected circumcision. How many things, I ask, could have occurred on the eighth day or afterward which left no opportunity whatever for circumcision?

As for baptism, one might rightly say that negligence, or the fault of contempt which is not far from impiety, is less readily excused because its ceremony is less laborious and is circumscribed by neither a distinction of sex nor a fixed period. But I cannot see how the bond commanding the new sacraments can be regarded as stricter than that commanding the old. I rather hear elsewhere that through the gospel we are relieved of burdens of this sort. Cyprian certainly appears to reason by this one principle of equity when asked about sick adults who were not washed but had water poured over them, Book 4, Letter 7: *In the saving sacraments, when necessity compels and God grants his indulgence, divine abridgments confer the whole upon believers.* What he then attributed to that particular necessity among those called the *clinici* afterward gradually came to be treated as ordinary in the churches of the West: with account taken of infants’ tenderness in colder regions, pouring succeeded immersion.

And how many other things can happen which allow no possibility of baptizing an infant? From here came the Scholastics’ most empty disputes over what should be done if natural water were unavailable, and many others proposed no less foolishly than they were ridiculously debated and superstitiously concluded. Nevertheless, compelled by truth itself, they added baptism of desire or of the Spirit, and likewise baptism of blood, to ordinary baptism, which they call baptism of the river. But if the ancient—

—church had approved that rigid necessity of baptism, or had thought that faith and hope of salvation begin from baptism, is it credible that only those few days would have been appointed for administering baptism, to which an exception of necessity was finally added? Traces of these days still remain in the pseudo-Catholic Roman Church: the Sunday they call “in white” and the blessing of the fonts, as they call it, preceding the feast of Easter.

If so rigid a necessity of baptism had then prevailed, is it also probable that the most religious men of antiquity would have deferred baptism so long? Among them were the emperors Constantine the Great, baptized only shortly before death, and Valentinian, left unbaptized even through his own delay; and even bishops, such as Nazianzen’s father, baptized only in adolescence. I acknowledge that this delay was by no means to be approved and was rightly corrected afterward, since it arose from a contrary error: as Augustine testifies concerning himself in *Confessions* 1.11, the belief that after that washing guilt amid the defilements of sins would be greater and more dangerous. It is so difficult to preserve the mean in things, as the philosophers also rightly said concerning their virtues.

Yet it is enough to have shown that Christ’s words, “Unless one is born again of water and the Spirit,” were not understood so rigidly even concerning adults as Augustine supposed. Augustine nevertheless disagrees with himself somewhere and was also deceived concerning the necessity of the other sacrament by wrongly applying to infants Christ’s words in John 6:53, “Unless you eat the flesh of the Son,” etc. This was afterward corrected in the Council of Carthage by an argument drawn from the word “Take,” though it could have been drawn much more firmly from the Apostle’s words in 1 Corinthians 11:28: “Let each person examine himself.”

Therefore Bernard’s conclusion stands, concerning which he argues most fully and learnedly in Letter 77: not every deprivation of baptism is fatal, but only that which proceeds from contempt for it or gross negligence—and even this is afterward pardoned to one who truly repents.
