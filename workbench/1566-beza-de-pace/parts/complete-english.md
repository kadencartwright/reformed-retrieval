## To His Sacred Imperial Majesty and the Estates of the Roman Empire assembled at Augsburg

It seems the deed of an exceedingly bold man to address the most august and
ample assembly of the entire world. It seems the height of shamelessness to
give an unsolicited opinion on the most important matter of all—and to do so
while concealing one's name. Yet when a private building happens to catch fire,
the one chief concern is ordinarily that everyone run to extinguish the blaze;
at such a time little attention is paid to where a person comes from or who he
is. Much more, then, now that the Christian world is burning with internal
conflicts, I have thought that someone who comes to the aid of the afflicted
Christian commonwealth, even outside the ordinary course, ought not to be
rebuked. I have concealed my name for this one reason: that readers may consider
more attentively not who speaks, but what is said. If I have said anything
rightly and appropriately, let praise be given to God. If not, let me be seen
to have acted imprudently and foolishly; nevertheless, as I judge, this purpose
of mine cannot justly be condemned.

By command of His Sacred Imperial Majesty, a diet has been summoned at Augsburg
primarily to establish the Christian religion and to abolish the sects by which
it is shaken today. Who would not rightly congratulate His Sacred Imperial
Majesty and the most illustrious and powerful princes of Germany upon so holy,
useful, and altogether necessary an intention? It is nevertheless certain that
there are some who disapprove of this plan and strive with all their power to
hinder it, while others approve it only under a prescribed plan and form from
which not only would no benefit be received, but infinite evils must necessarily
arise. I think both groups can be answered as follows.

### The arguments of those who disapprove of princely deliberations for settling religion

Those who disapprove do so either because they expect no fruit from it, because
they hold that this question belongs to the Roman pontiff instead, or because
all these matters were settled long ago and have again been determined very
recently by decrees of the Council of Trent, so that they ought no longer be
examined.

#### Argument I: Synods have never eliminated sects

They say: “We know and confess that this matter is the gravest of all, and we
earnestly desire these great dissensions to be composed so that everyone may
hold one and the same belief. Yet the matter itself cries out that no place is
left here for human counsels. Although they have been employed so often since
the creation of the world, they have not healed this truly malignant ulcer;
rather, they have inflamed it most seriously, so that it now permits no remedy.
Who does not know that since the world's creation there have been almost as
many opinions about divine worship as there have been heads? How many and how
diverse were the factions even among the Jews? Those who had given their name
to Christ immediately began to quarrel among themselves over the chief articles
of our religion, and not even the apostles' authority restrained them. Who is
also ignorant of the longstanding controversy between the Turks and the
Persians over the law of Muhammad? These evils are owed to the weakness of
human nature, for which no one except God alone can furnish a remedy. The
unceasing experience of so many centuries therefore demonstrates that these
evils are driven away by prayers rather than by any decrees of synods or
princes, and by the passage of time itself rather than by human counsels.”

#### Argument II: No legitimate synod can now be held

“Indeed, to descend to our own times, what sort of consultation do we finally
expect this to be? From which faction will judges be chosen upon whom the
others agree? From where will the rule of judgment be sought? From the Word of
God alone? But the greater part rejects this. Even if it admitted that rule,
who would finally untie the inextricable knots of the disputants'
interpretations? Will the opinions of the ancients and the custom received
through many centuries prevail here? Those who command us to depend upon the
written Word of God alone will not bear this, and they rightly deny that a lie
is transformed into truth by any length of time.”

#### Argument III: No one will obey the synod's decrees

“Let us nevertheless grant that something can be decided in these matters by
the counsels and votes of so great an assembly. Who except the Germans alone
will accept these laws? The source of these evils will therefore remain in
other kingdoms, and from it the same streams, perhaps even larger ones, will
soon flow back. Indeed, who expects most people to be brought to the point of
preferring another person's judgment to their own conscience in religion? That
has not yet been extorted by flames, swords, or any other force. Hearts are
imbued with religions by persuasion, not by human force.”

#### The conclusion drawn by proponents of these arguments

We understand these to be their arguments. If they are asked what they think
ought to be done amid such evils, we can only suppose that they will give the
customary reply of those called political men and neutrals, so to speak: the
authors and followers of opinions that overthrow the public authority of
magistrates must not be tolerated; those who disturb the public peace for any
reason must be moderately restrained; but otherwise peaceful people should be

[Printed page 111; PDF page 3; OCR object 0115]

left to their own conscience until the Lord, in his goodness and mercy, looks
upon us.

### Reply to the first argument

Come, then, let us weigh the strength of these arguments. Granted that disputes
about true religion have always been perpetual and innumerable and will
continue to the end of the ages, and that no remedies have yet overcome what
is due partly to ignorance and partly to human wickedness: is that evil
therefore to be voluntarily permitted to grow stronger and stronger? Shall we
allow the disease itself to advance under its own momentum while attending only
to some very slight symptoms of it? Whatever does not pass beyond the bounds
of this age is exceedingly slight and indeed utterly empty in comparison with
the better life.

No laws have ever removed the audacity of criminals, not even those of Draco,
which are said to have been written in blood rather than ink because of their
immoderate severity. No precautions of jurists or penalties for rash litigants
have ever been able—or ever will be able—to extinguish zeal for litigation,
break the obstinacy of disputants, restrain completely the boldness of
slanderers, or abolish the corruption of judgments. Should laws therefore be
abolished, or proceedings against those who despise them be conducted so
lightly that, provided some public appearance of peace is maintained, everyone
is permitted to live as he pleases?

Or because no fewer people become ill than before medicine was discovered and
used, and because however much care you devote to preserving health everyone
must nevertheless die in his own time, shall we conclude that no effort should
be given to medicine? Shall its use be regulated by public laws only far enough
to prevent open murders, while everyone is permitted to practice medicine and
administer whatever drugs he pleases to whomever he pleases? Certainly not.
Everyone except the utterly insane confesses that removing laws dissolves the
bonds of human society, so that it would be better to be a beast than a human
being. Although the present life, brief and fleeting as it is, cannot be freed
from the necessity of death by the protection of medicine, it is established
that the properly applied aid of that art preserves it to some extent, so that
each person can perform his duty more vigorously and for longer. Thus the first
argument has no force unless someone happens to think religion either so
uncertain or so far beyond human comprehension that concern for it ought to be
entirely cast away by us or deferred to the next age. No wise person would dare
profess the former, nor any sufficiently godly person the latter.

They next charge these evils to human weakness. Although that is true to some
extent, it should not be granted without qualification. Who does not know that
the originators, embellishers, and defenders of the errors shaking the Christian
Church have been—and still are—crafty rather than ignorant? No one would deny
that such cunning in a private matter should be punished even by public courts.
By what right, then, do we think it should be concealed beneath profound silence
in the most important matter of all? Someone may object that such malice is
more easily detected in human controversies than in divine matters. Yet who
could rightly infer that contempt for divine things is so impossible to
ascertain that its punishment in no way belongs to human beings?

People devoted partly to their own vanity and partly incited by Satan may have
corrupted by various interpretations—or rather, ravings—the sacred laws of God
in which all religion is contained. Nevertheless, it seems to us that anyone
who thinks God has provided for humanity only so far as to illuminate those
matters that concern the justice, so to speak, of this life, but has so concealed
from every nation without exception the principle of heavenly justice that he
has mocked none more than those to whom he disclosed his counsels and even his
secrets in a peculiar and wonderful manner, does a grave injustice both to the
Deity himself and to humankind. Since this cannot be thought, much less spoken,
without a degree of impiety, it follows that those who charge all these evils
to human weakness do not think rightly—especially when they do so in such a
way that God is said to have furnished humanity no remedy in these matters.

Accordingly, I accept their claim that these evils are extinguished through
prayers and the passage of time itself only in this sense: I hold that prayers
must necessarily be joined with those means which, it is certain, have been
handed down to humanity both by God's Word and by the perpetual custom of the
ancient Church, to be used whenever necessary. Princes therefore have been
charged with ensuring that the churches in their dominions are administered
from God's Word. This can be confirmed by the examples of all the godly kings
of Judah and afterward of Christian emperors and kings. Churches cannot be
rightly administered by their pastors unless, besides sowing good seed, the
pastors also use God's Word like a sickle to cut down evil plants and, so far
as they can, root them out.

Because this generally cannot be accomplished by the authority of one or two
people, and perhaps ought not to be, the bishops of the provinces met whenever
need required from the very beginning of the constituted churches. They did so
following the apostles' example, lest anyone think this a human invention. The
synod of the Jerusalem and Antiochene church celebrated in Acts is well known.
Then, during the reign of Constantine the Great, came the first ecumenical
synod at Nicaea. There the ecclesiastical provinces were divided more carefully
than successfully, and it was also ordained that metropolitans convene two
provincial synods every year. Had that custom been diligently observed, it is
indeed likely that many of the Church's greatest calamities could have been
averted.

Some object that those synods more often kindled disagreements than extinguished
them, so much so that the famous bishop Nazianzen virtually condemned all such
assemblies in one of his statements. Against this opinion we do not hesitate
to set partly the apostolic example, which ought to serve us as a standard and
rule, and partly the history of events itself. The Nicene synod certainly did
not abolish the fury of Arius, nor did the several synods that followed. But
who

[Printed page 112; PDF page 4; OCR object 0116]

will therefore judge that it bore no fruit, when even at this very time we reap
that fruit most abundantly? Not even the apostolic synod completely restrained
Cerinthus and those obstinate defenders of circumcision. Yet who would deny on
that account that it was necessary for the Church? Everyone sees that
Nazianzen's statement has nothing to do with rightly administered synods,
unless perhaps we think he also meant to disparage the Nicene synod. That would
be utterly absurd, since it is sufficiently established how great a defender
of it he was.

Although the Arians did not cease raging throughout the world, how much more
would they have done the same if the authority of that holy synod, which agreed
with God's Word, had not been set against and restrained their repeatedly
renewed attempts? We wish the same to be said of the Macedonians, Nestorians,
Eutychians, and their offspring. The corresponding ecumenical synods defeated
them, if not with one wound, then with repeated blows from God's Word, and
indeed supply us with many weapons against the same errors when they are reborn
in our own age. In saying this, however, we do not hold that the Church rests
upon the authority of such assemblies, nor do we judge every little council,
whatever name it bears, to be regarded as a synod. We say only this: amid these
calamities of the Church we must ask God that we may use rightly and holily
these remedies too, which were delivered by God.

### Reply to the second argument

Who denies that the difficulties concerning the judges who will sit and the
very rule of judgment are extremely great? But must His Sacred Imperial Majesty
and the most illustrious and powerful princes of Germany therefore either not
dare what he who can do whatever he pleases has willed to lie within their
power, or neglect so great an evil, which carries with it the certain ruin of
all things? Anyone who thinks that kingdoms rise or fall by chance, by certain
conjunctions of the stars, by human choice, or by human industry is gravely
mistaken. We must ascend higher, all the way to God, who, as the prophet says,
girds the belt and removes the turban.

When Daniel, a witness beyond every exception, was inspired by the divine
Spirit and described the four monarchies—traces of one of which, Caesar, we
still behold in Your Sacred Majesty—so that he set their rise and fall before
our eyes, did he not provide every age with a perfectly clear mirror in which
all princes may behold themselves? This whole matter shines most brightly in
sacred history. There the Spirit of God, rising far above human prudence,
describes past and present in such a way as to teach us also to infer the future
from its true causes. Whoever reduces those causes to one head will find this
to be the sum of its teaching: whatever princes have either despised sincere
religion—that is, religion delivered in God's Word—or even opposed it, have
wretchedly ruined themselves and their posterity; but those who preferred to
all other things the worship of God established from his pure Word were not
only happy and blessed themselves, but in a certain manner also transmitted
the gracious favor of the most good God to their families.

This undertaking is therefore exceedingly difficult, but it is one upon which,
as upon a foundation, the throne of so many kingdoms is known to be built. It
thus seems that you should think about nothing more by night and day. As you
consider this matter, you will not lack God's admonitions to obey, ancestral
examples to follow safely, the counsels of wise people to weigh, or finally the
present aid of God himself in overcoming all these difficulties. They will,
however, be discussed individually in another part of this address.

### Reply to the third argument

Some object, “Germany alone will accept these laws.” As though it were not in
itself an excellent achievement to pacify Germany, that vast and flourishing
part of the Christian world! If Your Sacred Majesty accomplishes this, Caesar,
how much more justly, I ask, will you bear that illustrious title *Germanicus*
than those ancient emperors who were afterward defeated by the Germans? Nor
should we despair that a council of neighboring nations might also be lawfully
convened with the willing permission of their kings, princes, and peoples. If
that can be accomplished, who does not see that the benefit will be common?

“But persuasion, not coercion, is needed.” I grant this. I therefore judge that
the matter should not be conducted by naked authority or advanced by violence,
which is inhuman, much less fitting for Christians. But I say that the means
prescribed by God himself must be followed in this matter. A person who does
not assent to them should be tolerated only so far that—as the best and greatest
God is accustomed to deal with us—ignorance is borne so far as possible, while
wicked obstinacy is restrained according to the seriousness of the matter.
They are therefore right to judge intolerable those who either defend doctrines
that overthrow the magistrates' constitution or knowingly and deliberately
disturb public tranquility, provided that magistrates themselves define their
authority by the limits established by the Lord and that the public peace is
genuine, not a common conspiracy to oppress God's truth. Otherwise, no one
will be accused of sedition and contempt for princely dignity more readily than
the very people farthest removed from those crimes, as is seen throughout the
history of the Christian Church and especially in the history of Christ
himself.

### Reply to their conclusion

Finally, as for the last proposal—that the others should be left to their own
conscience, an opinion which I understand is held by some people who are by no
means wicked and are exceedingly prudent—I shall discuss in its place to what
extent it can be admitted.

### Those who think that a synod on religious controversies would merely reopen settled matters

I now turn to those who do not disapprove such an inquiry in itself, but for
certain reasons judge it partly superfluous and partly, if it has any place at
all, to belong to the Roman pontiff rather than to those whom they call laymen.
If I differ with them somewhat freely, I most humbly ask that in a matter of
such importance they consider within themselves not so much who says these
things, or against whom, as what is said.

They maintain that a disputation over matters settled so many times before is
superfluous. My first answer

[Printed page 113; PDF page 5; OCR object 0117]

is that, even if it were true that all these controversies had already long
ago been settled with sufficient clarity, nevertheless this is the custom of
the Christian Church received from Christ himself, which the apostles
afterward, and all true bishops, followed: not to weary of pressing even a
thousand times those things about which it is almost impious to entertain a
doubt. For we must ask not only whether the matter in question has been
explained before, but above all whether it has been clearly understood by
those for whose salvation we labor. For—to pass over Paul's saying that we all
know in part, and that consequently even the most expert have something left
to learn every day—who does not know that the pastors of the preceding age
were so negligent that very few of them took pains either to teach or to
understand the Christian religion? What wonder is it, then, that those coming
out of so dark a cave should be dazzled by the bright light, and that questions
should arise among Christians even about things handed down fully and clearly
so many centuries ago?

“But,” say the defenders of the Roman Church, “these men do not allow
themselves to be taught.” Others answer in turn: “Show us, then, precisely
which teachers we have rejected.” If the many thousands of books written
against us are produced, come, let our replies be compared with them, so that
the whole world may understand—if anyone still doubts it—whether we resisted
such teachers by reason or by obstinacy, with judgment or with rashness, and,
in short, rightly or wrongly. Let the proceedings of so many imperial diets be
examined, and let it be decided which party refused teaching from God's Word.
Let the confessions of so many martyrs, tortured by every kind of punishment,
be read, and let it be judged by what spirit they remained unconquered to the
very end: one of constancy or of obstinacy. But if that recent Council of Trent
is brought against us, no one is ignorant of the just reasons why all who
departed from the Roman pontiff rejected its jurisdiction; there appears,
therefore, to be no need of a lengthier reply.

Since these claims have thus been bandied back and forth by voice and in
writings for many years, everyone sees how little solidity there is in their
assertion that these matters should be investigated no further. But those who
have separated from the Roman Church go much farther still. “For,” they say,
“if this principle were valid—that there should be no inquiry into something
which has already been judged sufficiently and more than sufficiently—we
could hope for no argument by which we might more easily prevail before fair
judges. This is what we say, this is what we affirm, this is what we trust we
shall prove: nothing is now disputed between the Roman Church and us that has
not been decided by the plain, express, and clear teaching of the prophets and
apostles. Some object to us here that the written Word is ambiguous; others,
that disputes over the meaning of a few words are stirred up among ourselves.
For the present we answer them only this: we are prepared before suitable
judges to establish from God's Gospel, by which the world will be judged, the
truth of the doctrine we assert; and, besides, from the received decrees of
those ancient synods and from approved Greek and Latin writers alike, to prove
the Roman Church guilty of violating every human and divine law.”

Why, then, is their condition not accepted—whether what they say is true or
false—so that they may either be convicted of falsehood or all together submit
to the truth once it has been clearly perceived?

#### Those who refer this inquiry to the bishop of Rome

Those who refer this question to the Roman pontiff ought not to be heard, as is
shown by the following arguments, and in my judgment the strongest arguments
are offered by those who accuse that see of being Antichristian. “For,” they
say, “formerly, amid so great an ignorance of these matters as no one denies
existed among most people, it is no wonder that many followed the common error
and did not labor to investigate the truth. But now who is ignorant of the
beginnings and advances of that Roman power? In Italy it has left nothing to
Your Sacred Majesty, Caesar, except the empty name of the Roman Empire, and in
Rome itself nothing at all. Indeed, it has gone so far that the Roman pontiff
orders those by whose consent he once entered the episcopate to receive their
crown from him; he imposes whatever laws he pleases upon those to whom he was
subject after the example of Christ and Peter; he scarcely deigns to allow
those whom he once venerated as a suppliant, as was fitting, to kiss his feet.
Who, I ask, is ignorant of the beginnings and advances of this Roman power,
which has exalted itself to the point of saying that it is judged by God
alone?”

It is sufficiently established how much was once accorded to the Roman
bishops, both because of the excellence of the bishops and because of the
dignity of the city. But what has this to do with the power of which I am now
speaking? For if the ecumenical councils are in question, history makes clear
who convened those four most celebrated ones. We read that the Roman pontiff
was summoned to them by the imperial majesty just like the rest. Let him show,
if he can, that either the emperor or the other bishops asked his consent; that
he presided over those councils, or that any other bishop did so, except
perhaps in asking for and declaring opinions; or that the councils' decrees
were sanctioned by his authority. Let him explain this also: if the power of
the Roman pontiff was then what it is now, how did it happen that, while the
most religious Marcian was emperor and Leo occupied the episcopal see at Rome,
that most celebrated synod was held not in Italy, as Leo requested, but at
Chalcedon, although Leo resisted in vain with the humblest prayers—not with
threats or any command of the sort that Paul III long ago employed against
Emperor Charles, the uncle of Your Sacred Majesty, Caesar? Let him explain why,
when the holy Ambrose was bishop of Milan, a universal synod, so far as its
convocation was concerned, was held happily and holily at Aquileia without any
concern for the neighboring bishop of Rome, and without anyone's raising any
controversy about it. Let him explain why at the Nicene Synod the authority of
the Roman metropolitan was defined by the limits of the city of Rome and the
suburbicarian churches. Let him explain why two exceedingly ambitious Roman
bishops, Boniface and Celestine, in defending their jurisdiction over the
African bishops not from divine law—for had any such law existed, they surely
would never have kept silent about it at so critical a time—but from forged
decrees of the Nicene Synod, were condemned for manifest falsehood at the
African Council, at which Augustine himself was present; and why it was
expressly provided there that neither should the Roman bishop afterward send
legates into Africa by some right of his own, nor should anyone appeal from
Africa to that see. Let him explain by what right he invaded the jurisdictions
of other metropolitan bishops. Let him explain by what laws

[Printed page 114; PDF page 6; OCR object 0118]

either the emperors ceded to the bishop the capital of the Western Roman Empire
and that entire territory which they call, with a name no less ridiculous than
false, the Patrimony of Peter, or the bishop was permitted to take it from the
emperors. Let him explain why, not content even with this patrimony—which ought
to suffice to support all the pastors of Italy—he drains the ecclesiastical
benefices of every province. Let him explain what warrant for that most foul
marketplace appears either in God's Word or in the ancient canons. Let him
explain whether Gregory, himself both a Roman and a bishop, ought not to be
believed when he calls anyone who presents himself as universal bishop an
impious, wicked, and abominable forerunner of Antichrist.

Finally, even if the whole Roman Church had at some time granted some primatial
power to the Roman bishop, what has this to do with the Roman pontiffs? For we
deny that in them, such as they began to be long ago, even a single mark of a
legitimate episcopal calling can be shown; on the contrary, everything
condemned both by God's express Word and by the ancient canons is found in
them.

We affirm this further—and undertake to prove it from their own books—that
those pontiffs have conducted themselves, and still do conduct themselves, in
such a way that either they must be judged by everyone, rather than everyone
by them as they desire, or the Christian world must shortly be destroyed
utterly, which God forbid. For what thing, whether profane or sacred, has that
power not polluted by its touch? What kingdom, what territory, what city, what
family can be found which they have not afflicted with incredible losses? By
whose ruin have they not grown? By whose spoils have they not been enriched?
In whose plunder, finally, do they not appear adorned? We pass over those
ancient examples—the Ottos, Conradin, the Henrys, the Fredericks, illustrious
ornaments of Germany; Gaul, England, Italy, and indeed the whole world as if
drunk with Christian blood at the instigation and exultation of the Roman
pontiff. In our own memory, who so often set those most powerful princes
against one another? Who sounded the trumpet of civil war in Germany not so
long ago, and very recently in Gaul? Who strives by every means to disturb even
such peace as now exists?

But what if you, most powerful Emperor, or the other kings, or so many princes,
whether ecclesiastical or “lay,” as they call them, must seek from those
corpses of the city of Rome a prince to rule both your bodies and your souls?
And, I ask, what kind of prince—chosen by whom, in what order, and by what
votes? When a pontiff has died either naturally or by poison, a few men
assemble in conclave: Italians, most of them of low birth, who—as everyone
knows—have purchased the hat, as they call it, either at the most shameful
price, by favor, or by other similar means. There that Italian cohort, after
putting up for bidding among themselves for a long time your liberty, Caesar;
yours, most powerful kings; and yours, most illustrious princes, at last—as is
customary in public auctions—assigns your diadems, scepters, heads, fortunes,
honors, and finally your souls to whichever member of that assembly it pleases:
that is, if experience and the proverb bandied about by them themselves are to
be believed—“a good pope, a bad man”—to whichever is the worst. So very
recently it assigned them to that Dominican of Alessandria whom shortly before
they themselves mockingly called Brother Scarpone. You are immediately to
venerate his feet; he is to traffic even in your souls; at his nod, and indeed
at an agreed and fixed price, all sacred things are to be sold to anyone he
pleases; afterward it is no more permissible to investigate his words and
deeds than those of God himself; and if someone happens to wish to complain of
his crimes, the whole controversy must be referred to the decision of the very
man who ought to have stood trial and indeed paid just penalties to all.

“Is it so, then,” they say, “O eternal Jesus, Son of God, that you freed us
from Satan and eternal death by your own blood so that we might serve this
most wretched slavery of all? Did you take away Moses' yoke from us so that we
might become the slaves of men—and what sort of men? Was it for this that you
allowed mighty Germany to retain the name of empire: that your emperors might
serve, to be sure, this foreign servant of servants? Is it for this, Gaul,
Spain, England, and all other self-governing peoples, that you rest in the
divinely established authority of your kings and princes: that this most
unworthy man of all might be permitted to do anything whatsoever against you,
and indeed against your very kings?”

These are their arguments. They are indeed of such weight that I repeat: those
who refer this cause to the Roman pontiff and his clergy plainly appear to seek
not peace, but only the ruin of the other party.

#### It is unjust to demand that those who disagree with them about the Lord's Supper be condemned before they are heard in a lawful inquiry

It follows, therefore, that the form and method of this inquiry must be
discussed. First of all, however, I must refute the opinion of those who think
this the shortest road to concord: that, omitting the question of right, as
they call it, whoever disagrees with them should once for all be condemned on
the basis of their prejudgments.

For a report has spread, and indeed on no worthless authorities, that the name
of the sects for whose repression by public condemnation this diet has been
summoned includes all who either disapprove of the Roman pontiff's laws or have
not subscribed to the Augsburg Confession. Moreover, these things are said,
none too obscurely, against those whom some today call by new and odious names.
Yet if any are to be condemned rather than heard, either because of the
absurdity of their doctrines or because of their obstinate defenses, what
would prevent those whom they themselves call Lutherans from being condemned
first of all, if the followers of the Roman pontiff are to be believed? But if
it does not seem equitable to grant this to the latter, by what right, with
what appearance of fairness, or according to what custom will it be granted to
the clamors of certain men that so many thousands of innocent people, entirely
prepared to give a reason for their faith from God's Word, should be condemned
as heretics without their case being heard? They complain that these men
stubbornly resist the truth. What if the latter throw the same charge back at
them? Certainly no one has yet judged either for these against those, or for
those against these. Indeed, no synod has been summoned concerning these
matters; those whose condemnation they demand have been heard before no
judges.

##### Objection and reply

“But,” they say, “we maintain that it is not the people but the doctrines
themselves, not the churches but the impious dogmatists, that ought to be
condemned.” Yet what sort of procedure is this? Who will not consider himself
condemned in the doctrine that he defends? And when the doctrine of pastors
which all their flocks have received by common consent has been condemned for
heresy, how are the churches not condemned? No one has yet proved the doctrines
in question guilty of impiety. No one has refuted by public judgment either
the pastors themselves or those by whom they were instructed and taught. It
would certainly be no more equitable to condemn the doctrines themselves
without a lawful inquiry than to condemn the people. If the authority of
examples is required, what examples will be found for condemning anyone
without his case being heard? When the Jews once demanded this very thing
against Paul, Paul appealed from Festus to Caesar, and his appeal was accepted
by that man, though he was both a pagan and corrupt in mind.

##### Second argument: Acts 15

At the Jerusalem synod the apostles—or rather, the Holy Spirit himself—had
plainly and clearly decided that salvation was to be sought in Christ alone
and that the ceremonies of the law had been abrogated. That controversy was
of such importance that Paul wrote, “If you are circumcised, Christ will
profit you nothing.” Nevertheless, many members of that church could not be
drawn away from the use of the ceremonies many years afterward. Does James
wish them immediately to be condemned as heretics and members of Satan? On
the contrary, he advises Paul to join himself to certain Nazirites for their
sake, and Paul freely obeyed his advice. Here, however, it is by no means
requested that those to whom others are such obstinate heretics should grant
them something which they deny they can do with a safe conscience. It is
requested only that those who teach differently from them in certain matters
not be condemned unheard at their demand.

##### Third argument

Augustine, with whom not a few agree, does not wish any place to be left for
excommunication whenever the danger of schism threatens. But in the present
case everyone sees what dreadful ruin—not of one church, but of almost
countless churches—what destruction of kingdoms, how many civil wars, and what
slaughter would follow this condemnation. For it can be obscure to no one that
the English, Scottish, French, Flemish, Swiss, and Rhaetian churches which are
called Reformed, together with every one of their pastors, would be included
in this condemnation.

##### Fourth argument

If Germany itself is considered, no one is ignorant how many, even among those
who professedly embrace the Augsburg Confession, interpret it altogether
differently—and indeed relying upon the authority of the very man by whom it
was written—from those who are said to be pursuing this condemnation. If they
obtained it, it surely could not happen otherwise than that in this way Germany
too would be set against itself—a most wretched spectacle which may God avert.

##### Fifth argument

A doctrine has recently arisen which affirms what they call the omnipresence
of Christ not only according to his divinity but also according to his flesh.
Not only do the churches named shortly before utterly reject it, but, as I
hear, so too do almost all the German academies. Would its defenders, then,
wish this doctrine—which those who oppose it say is diametrically contrary
both to God's Word and to all the ancient synods—to be pronounced impious and
heretical before they themselves had been heard and convicted in a lawful
ecclesiastical judgment? Certainly not. For they too bring forward certain
statements drawn from God's Word and the Fathers which they affirm support
their position. Why, then, is it not equitable for both parties to enjoy the
same right? That unhappy Arius and his followers were heard, convicted, and
condemned at Nicaea in the most celebrated synod of all. When the Arians
continued to rage for so many years and almost centuries afterward, was the
matter not taken up with them again in repeated synods and assemblies? Are
these men worse than the Arians? Even if they are worse, nevertheless they
have thus far been summoned, convicted, or condemned by no one.

##### Seventh argument, showing the doctrine of those called Sacramentarians

To all these considerations, say those same people whom they call
Sacramentarians, this most important one is added: however much some endeavor
to magnify in speech and writing that unhappy disagreement concerning the
Lord's Supper, we nevertheless hope that all who wish to learn our opinion
from us rather than from them will not consider us at all such people as they
declaim that we are. Because we refuse to agree with them that Christ according
to the flesh is now anywhere other than in heaven, they infer from this that
we remove the true communion of Christ's flesh and blood in the Lord's sacred
Supper. Those who impute this sacrilege to us certainly inflict a great injury
upon us and upon all those churches named before, with which we confess
ourselves most closely joined. Would that they at last acknowledged it.

This, then, is our faith concerning the Lord's Supper. We hold that Christ,
according to one of his two natures—that is, according to the flesh—truly
departed from this world, to which he will not return until he comes to judge
the living and the dead. We therefore affirm that the expressions “the flesh
of Christ is in, or under, or with the bread” cannot admit the sense that
Christ's flesh is located where the bread is, since the bread is nowhere but
on earth and Christ's flesh is now nowhere but in heaven. Yet because Christ
did not take his flesh out of the world in order to desert us, and because he
has expressly testified that in his sacred Supper he gives us his body and
blood unto eternal life, we do not doubt that he truly performs what he
promises. That is, although according to the flesh he remains in heaven, he
gives his whole self to us who are on earth; and so great a distance between
places does not prevent us from truly becoming one with him, so that afterward
eternal life flows from this head into us as his members.

But if someone curiously asks how a body remaining in heaven can be united
with us who are on earth, we answer that even in natural affairs, for a gift
to be valid, it is not necessary that the giver, the gift, and the one called
the recipient be situated in one and the same place. Consequently, even in
human terms nothing prevents Christ from truly and most effectually giving to
us who now live on earth his body, situated nowhere but outside this world.
But because this gift has as its end that we become one with him who is given,
human reason dictates that this cannot be accomplished unless, where the one
is, there also is

[Printed page 116; PDF page 8; OCR object 0120]

the other. Therefore, with the Apostle, we say that the mystery of the union
of the Church and Christ is by far the greatest of all and indeed
comprehensible to faith alone. We oppose the promise of the almighty Son of
God—in which he testifies that he gives us his body delivered for us and his
blood poured out for us—to all the profane thoughts of our corrupted nature.
Consequently, we affirm that Christ's body and blood, and with them eternal
life, are apprehended not by bodily hands but by faith, not in a natural but in
a spiritual manner. What is universal in all spiritual promises, that is,
those pertaining to eternal life—namely, that they are apprehended by us
through no instrument other than faith—we maintain especially in this one,
since it is, as it were, the completion of the rest, because we are not blessed
in Christ unless we are his members.

From this we say it follows that Christ's flesh and blood are received by the
faithful alone. For although Christ offers himself to all who approach, both in
the daily preaching of the Word and in the sacraments, but especially in the
sacred Supper, nevertheless, because he is received by people through faith
alone, we must therefore maintain that he is accepted by the faithful alone.
The unbelievers' faithlessness detracts nothing from the word of Christ himself
as he offers himself and his gifts to everyone. When these things are rightly
understood, it can also be seen clearly by what right some charge us with
putting, as the reality of the sacrament, not Christ himself but only his
merits; with denying the presence of Christ's body and blood in the Supper;
with emptying the sacred signs; and thus, as they themselves say, with being
“sacrament-destroyers.”

From these things we also think it is clear in what sense that sacred bread is
called the body of the Lord, to be received and eaten, and likewise that
sacred drink the blood of the Lord, to be received and drunk: not because they
are transubstantiated into body and blood (for they would then cease to be
sacraments); nor because the body and blood are themselves there where that
bread and wine are—namely, in people's hands and mouths—which is contrary to
the reality of Christ's body and to his ascension; but because those bodily
signs (namely bread, wine, receiving, eating, and drinking) have the realities
themselves—that is, the Lord's body and blood, and their delivery—joined with
them sacramentally. This sacramental union, however, is not some simple
memorial by which the signified realities are recalled to our minds, but in
the minds of believers it is full of supreme power and efficacy. Indeed,
whenever, according to God's command and with faith looking to the promised
and signified realities, we naturally and bodily receive, eat, and drink those
signs, it is as certain that we become participants, in the spiritual manner
we have described and unto eternal life, in those realities—that is, in the
very body delivered for us and that blood poured out for us—as it is certain
that we see those signs with our eyes, receive them with our hands, eat and
drink them with our mouths, and finally digest them after they pass into the
stomach, so that by their substance we sustain the present life.

Thus, both so that we might distinguish the sacraments from common things and
so that we might not perhaps stop at the sacraments themselves—the two
precipices especially to be avoided in treating sacramental matters—the names
of the signified realities themselves have been applied to the signs by the
Holy Spirit's authorship. This is done not through some obscure figure of
speech, since these things ought most of all to be understood by all the godly,
but through a manner of speaking customary among every nation and language,
and especially with Moses and the prophets, as has been amply demonstrated by
our writers, or so it seems to us.

This, therefore, is their faith, and indeed that of all the churches which I
named before, so far as I have been permitted to gather it from the writings
and discourses of their teachers. Whoever weighs it diligently, even if he
finds that it differs from the opinion of those in the greater part of Germany
who interpret differently the expressions “to be in, under, and with the bread
and wine,” will nevertheless confess that it is neither impious nor heretical.
For if the signs themselves are in question, what more can be attributed to
them than these people attribute: namely, that whatever they signify to the
eyes by God's ordinance, God also truly offers to be received by faith? If the
signified reality is in question, what other can they establish than the very
body delivered for us and the blood poured out for us—that is, Christ himself
our Savior? If the chief benefit of the sacred Supper is in question, what
other can it be than communion in his very body and blood?

Therefore—to conclude this topic at last—we appeal to the conscience of all
who are experienced in these matters and are of a moderate spirit: is it fair
and just to number those who believe in this way among sectaries, and indeed
among those wicked and blasphemous people such as the Anabaptists, Davidists,
Mennonites, and finally the Tritheists now running rampant in Poland, and the
other monsters of that kind, worthy of execration rather than a hearing? So
they argue. If, moreover, there are other things in the confession of those
churches which are disapproved, equity itself certainly commands that they
first be pointed out to them and that their defenses be heard in a lawful
judgment before they are condemned.

#### Who should be selected as disputants at the synod

“But,” someone will say, “once it has been granted that they should be heard
in a lawful judgment, how, then, will it be constituted? Who will sit as
judges? What standard of judgment will be given? Who will induce the
neighboring kings and peoples to accept this condition?” As concerns the
judges, therefore, the objection made against those who follow the Roman
pontiff's party—namely, that it is unfair for the same people to be both judges
and defendants—could surely be turned back by them against the others with
good reason, if it were perhaps demanded that they submit to the judgment of
the other party, that is, of those who do not follow the Roman Church. In
either case, then, this appears unfair. Accordingly, as is customary in civil
controversies, it would seem best to have people attached to neither party
decide the disputes of both. But who, then, are such people? In our judgment,
there are none. For if there are any Epicureans, we number them not among human
beings but among beasts. Those who limp toward both sides—that is, those for
whom it is not yet clear on which side the truth stands—how could they render
judgment about matters concerning which they have settled nothing in their
own minds? There remain those who offer as religion a certain mixed medley
composed in a particular way from the opinions of both parties. If they were
perhaps to sit as judges of these controversies,

[Printed page 117; PDF page 9; OCR object 0121]

what would they do but gravely offend both parties? That this is so has been
proved more than sufficiently partly by what they call the *Interim*, and
partly by the writings of people of this kind. Those writings have been so
tempered that they can be approved by neither of the parties that seriously
follow one side or the other. For in establishing the doctrine of religion,
where it is not permissible to deviate even a finger's breadth from God's
commands, the matter cannot or ought not be handled as it can in civil
controversies. In the latter, everyone is permitted to yield something of his
own right, and, as the saying goes, the strictest right is often found to be
the greatest wrong. Thus this one remedy appears to remain: that from both
sides, and from the very people who disagree with one another, a set number of
judges distinguished by the greatest possible piety and prudence be chosen,
with all conditions equal. In making this selection, the following order
seems most suitable and indeed necessary.

First, those who follow the Roman pontiff's party should be ordered to agree
among themselves on all the articles to be disputed. For although they boast
of a catholic consensus, their controversies with one another over the most
important matters are clearly apparent not only from their older writers but
also from very recent ones, such as Cajetan, Catharinus, Soto, Torrensis,
Pighius, Tapper, and others. Some of these condemned others for the gravest
errors while the Roman pontiff readily looked the other way, since neither
side was undermining his authority. Furthermore, nowhere is there a simple
confession of their faith arranged under definite heads, but only a confused
chaos. Unless this is divided into several parts under some appearance of
order, every disputation undertaken by both sides will be fruitless.

On the other side, namely among those who do not follow the Roman Church,
although Satan has brought it about that there is also some confusion of
opinions, it nevertheless seems possible to remedy this difficulty chiefly in
the following way. Once all others whose errors are manifest and condemned by
both parties have been rejected—such as Anabaptists, Arians, Mennonites,
Schwenckfeldians, and others of that kind—let those finally be chosen who
embrace either the Augsburg or the Helvetic Confession, with the latter of
which the English, Scottish, and French confessions agree entirely in their
doctrinal heads. For although even these people do not sufficiently agree with
one another on some matters, nevertheless even in the article of the Lord's
Supper they oppose the Roman Church. Thus they will readily agree with one
another in opposing, as it were, a common enemy; the peace of the Christian
Church depends especially upon this, while all those sects will afterward
either vanish into smoke of their own accord or, as circumstances require, be
restrained by some suitable measures with God's favor.

When this has been done, we judge that neither too many should be selected—for
agreement would be more difficult among a larger number—nor too few, since it
is not probable that so many people would willingly follow the opinion of a
very small number. Above all, provision must also be made that those to whom so
great a matter is committed are, first, people of recognized integrity among
their own party; next, thoroughly instructed in the knowledge of all the
controversies; and finally, each chosen, so far as possible, by the will and
consent of his own party, with precisely equal conditions to obtain on both
sides.

#### Who ought to preside over the disputation

Yet because even in this way, perhaps, with feelings so inflamed, the harshness
of the disputes could not be avoided, it will belong to the wisdom of Your
Sacred Majesty, Caesar, and to yours, most illustrious and powerful princes,
not only to prescribe by definite laws the order of disputation, to which the
disputants on each side will individually consent by a solemn oath, but also
to select certain people from the entire number of princes and leading men of
both parties. These too should be of recognized integrity and modesty,
prudent, moderate, and sufficiently instructed in the subjects to be debated.
They are not to be mere spectators, as was customary in recent synods; they
are not to depend upon the disputants, but the disputants themselves upon them.
They should observe every point diligently, appropriately order the evasive to
answer, restrain the immoderate, collect the opinions, and ensure that the
articles concluded either by everyone or by the greater part are written down
plainly and clearly by both sides. Finally, throughout the entire proceeding
they should remember that they were appointed to this assembly not to favor
their own party or prejudgments brought from home, but, by showing themselves
impartial and true arbiters toward both sides, to moderate all these sacred and
momentous proceedings both devoutly and prudently. The acts of the ancient
ecumenical synods show that this was their practice.

#### Controversies are to be decided from the sacred Scriptures alone

When these things have been accomplished and the time and place have been
settled upon completely fair and equal conditions, it seems that inquiry
should be made among them first concerning doctrine, and afterward concerning
ecclesiastical polity. But what standard is there for judging doctrine other
than the writings of the prophets and apostles? For these are witnesses beyond
all exception. Those who do not rest in their testimony should not even be
counted as members of the true Church, much less as constituting the Church,
which Paul testifies is built upon the foundation of the prophets and
apostles. Two objections, however, are raised here. One is that not all
apostolic doctrine is contained in those writings. The other is that Scripture
is sometimes so ambiguous that the Church's interpretation is also necessarily
required.

Therefore, let this be the first head of the future disputation among them:
whether the complete doctrine of the Christian religion—we are speaking about
doctrines, not rites and ecclesiastical polity—is described in God's Word,
that is, in the books of both Testaments, or whether some unwritten traditions
must be added. In this disputation, those who rest in the written Word alone
seem likely to prevail. For Paul's statement stands impregnable: that all
Scripture is able to perfect the man of God—that is, the teacher of the
Church, much more the disciples. Since the writings of the evangelists, of
Paul himself, and of the other apostles were afterward added to it, in which
the fulfillment of the prophecies is related, what, then, shall we think ought
to be added to those books? But if some think that the authority of the Fathers
should rather be believed in this matter, let at least these two men of the
very greatest name be heard. One of them writes expressly as follows: “If
whatever is not from faith is sin, while faith is from hearing, and hearing
through God's Word, then whatever is not from God's Word is sin.” And lest
anyone think that he is speaking of some unwritten Word, he immediately adds,

[Printed page 118; PDF page 10; OCR object 0122]

“To reject anything among the things that are written, or to introduce
anything from what is not written, is a manifest departure from faith and a
crime of pride, since our Lord said of himself, ‘My sheep hear my voice.’” The
other has the following, than which nothing could be said more aptly for our
own times. “At this time,” he says, “since heresy has taken possession of the
churches, there can be no proof of true Christianity, nor can there be any
other refuge for Christians who wish to know the truth of the faith, except
divine Scripture. Previously it was shown in many ways which was the Church of
Christ and which was paganism; but now there is no way to recognize which is
the true Church of Christ except through the Scriptures alone.” And shortly
afterward: “The Lord, therefore, knowing that so great a confusion of affairs
would occur in the last days, commands for this reason that those who are
Christians, wishing to receive the solidity of the true faith, take refuge in
nothing except the Scriptures. Otherwise, if they look to other things, they
will stumble and perish, not understanding which is the true Church.”

#### The obscurity of the Scriptures, and the extent of the authority of synods and Fathers in establishing the Church's doctrine

Since this is so, one can easily see what must be answered to those who deny
that we should rest in Scripture because it is sometimes obscure and difficult
to understand. For if the matter were really so, the Lord would have mocked
his people, and what those two whom I cited shortly before affirm could not
stand; and it will be easy to prove that the rest agree with them too. “Will
there, then,” they ask, “be no authority among us belonging to the Church, to
synods, and finally to so many and such learned Fathers?” By no means. For by
God's grace I am not ignorant that the Church is the witness of truth, outside
of which there is no salvation; that it is right to esteem very highly the
consensus of the orthodox attested against heretics in synods; and that the
labors of the Fathers in interpreting the Scriptures, refuting errors, and
admonishing the peoples ought so far from being despised that they deservedly
hold the place second after the Scriptures.

But first of all I say that it is unjust for any crowd of people who agree
together to be foisted upon us as the Church. Since it is sufficiently
established that the true Church is that which bears witness to the truth,
while the truth is contained in God's written Word, it follows altogether that
the true Church must be distinguished from the adulterous one by God's Word.
Yet because each party boasts of God's Word, I think two things must be
observed here so that no one is deceived. First, interpretations of Scripture
must be drawn from nowhere other than the Scriptures themselves. Second, all
doctrines must be tested according to the analogy of faith, the articles of
which we possess written plainly and briefly. For it was shown shortly before
that the entire doctrine of the Christian religion is contained in the
prophetic and apostolic writings, and the Holy Spirit can in no way contradict
himself. Therefore, if these two principles are observed, I believe it will be
easy for people who are not utterly obstinate—such as I said should be admitted
to this disputation—to distinguish false interpretations from true, and thus
the true Church from the false.

Now, as regards synods and Fathers, I judge that somewhat more should be
accorded to them than some perhaps expect. For what if I were to say that the
Scriptures suffice for judging errors, however perplexing, and consequently
that neither good nor bad synods concern us at all? I would certainly be
following in Augustine's footsteps; when disputing against the Arians, he says
that neither were they bound by the Nicene Synod nor was he bound by that of
Ariminum. It is also clear from the histories of the ancient synods that
neither the authority of preceding synods nor the writings of the Fathers were
ever produced as though the foundation of truth lay in them. Rather, only
after the heretics had first been convicted from God's written Word were these
finally brought forward, so that every suspicion of novelty might be avoided.

For surely, if synods possessed the authority that some desire—namely, if they
were beyond every exception and infallible—what need would there have been of
synods other than the Nicene to defeat the Arians? What of the fact that
Charles, that truly Great and religious emperor, openly condemned the Second
Ecumenical Synod of Nicaea, by which alone the Roman pontiff today defends the
worship of images, as foolish, erroneous, and finally blasphemous? He did so
after holding a council at Mainz, in the very presence of the Roman bishop's
legates, and even after a book on the subject had been written in Latin in
which each of their arguments was answered from God's Word. If it is
permissible to judge synods, and indeed ecumenical synods, in this way, it is
much more apparent what we should think of private writings, whoever their
authors in the Church may have been—a point which Augustine too testified a
thousand times more than all others.

For the present, however, I would not think we should proceed so harshly. I
therefore think that this too should be granted to those who continually press
the writings of synods and Fathers. Let them unroll whatever synods they wish;
let them examine whatever ecclesiastical writers they wish, whether ancient or
modern. If they can draw from them any argument, any reason, or any
interpretation that rests upon God's Word, then by all means let them use them
all. Only two exceptions must be made. First, they must not fight by mere
authority or names. Second, we must be permitted to test what they produce
partly by the analogy of faith and partly by the standard and exact measure of
the same divine Word. Will they refuse even these conditions?

#### Ecclesiastical polity

There remains the other head, concerning ecclesiastical polity, under which
name I also include all rites and ceremonies. Here I am so far from rejecting
the ancient canons and the Church's custom, whether written or unwritten, that
on the contrary I think nothing should be urged more strongly than the
restoration of that ancient form of the Church. At the same time, however, I
would demand these conditions, which, unless I am mistaken, are most equitable
and indeed plainly necessary. First, if any things are discovered which are
either impious in themselves or superstitious—that is, which depart in any way
from God's Word—they should be abolished once for all. Second, useless things
should be removed; and because not everything suits every time and place,
whatever is retained should be adapted in the best faith to the circumstances
of times and places. Third, whether in abolishing or establishing such things,
no prescriptive force of time should prevail. Instead, the Church's edification
and thus the truth itself should be sought. That is, diligent attention should
be paid not to what has been practiced, by whom, or for how long, but by what
right and with what fruit it was practiced, and whether it can and ought to be
retained even now. I would hope that no lover of public peace and true religion
will reject these conditions, and that the Church's concord can therefore be
restored in this way without difficulty.

With these matters completed, it would remain for us to bring our address to
an end, were it not necessary to say something also

[Printed page 119; PDF page 11; OCR object 0123]

about how, finally, these things may be brought to completion, and by what
means, while they are being prepared and carried out, that deadly discord may
be restrained and the evil checked at least far enough that it does not spread
more widely.

#### How the obstinacy of certain people is to be met

We judge that two things in particular put a great delay in the way of so great
an undertaking: the obstinacy of certain people, and the present division of
the governance of the Christian world not only into many parts but even into
small fragments. Each difficulty is so great that it drives some people to
utter despair, as though God showed no remedy at all against these evils. For
it is obscure to no one that, by a certain most just judgment of God,
everything is now so confused and disordered that few can discern any traces
of order amid such disorder; and if any do discern them, they either waste
their labor by proposing remedies or even run into every kind of very great
danger. Nevertheless, there is nothing that all the godly ought not to place
after God's glory and public peace. I shall therefore bring forward openly and
freely whatever the Lord gives me.

#### How Catholics may be tolerated in possession of their wealth

There are indeed many reasons why no people today avoid more than those called
ecclesiastics the light of the Gospel, as though it were being born anew. Not
the least is that they see they must change their way of life if they receive
the Gospel. First, most of them see that, if they are required to discharge
their office, they can in no way do so, since they have plainly never yet
thought about it. Next, they understand well enough that those enormous riches
will thereafter be spent differently; and rather than lose them they resolve
that they must endure anything whatever. Hence comes the resistance of many,
although conscience pricks and bites them at every moment.

Yet a remedy for this evil can be found without great difficulty, namely, if
that supreme and plainly incompatible confusion of the two powers, civil and
ecclesiastical, is once removed. First of all, then, from those ample and
immoderate riches let as much as is sufficient be set aside to support
comfortably the parish ministers of each diocese, sustain academies and
schools, and nourish the poor. Let it be arranged in such a way that neither
the parish ministers nor those who teach the young administer anything other
than what belongs to their office: that is, let them serve the Lord for fixed
stipends and pensions, and let them by no means be obliged to cultivate lands,
collect revenues, much less possess those lordships. Let this be the concern
of others, who, in reaping spiritual things, should distribute those resources
to the laborers faithfully rather than lavishly.[^p119]

[^p119]: The controlling 1582 image clearly reads *qui spiritualia metendo*;
    the construction is difficult, and this working translation preserves it

Those who now possess the lordships and the things called secular possessions
may indeed retain them if they wish, since otherwise countless difficulties
would follow, but let them hold them as secular possessions. Let this moreover
be on the condition that, in proportion to each person's lordship or fiefs,
they be bound to public burdens just like the rest, even as they retain the
rank of nobility. Whether it is expedient for these things also to pass to
heirs, or rather—just as is still customary in some places with duchies,
counties, and some possessions of that kind—for them to be conferred upon
deserving people as the holders die; and under what conditions this ought to
be done, will be for the authority and prudence of His Sacred Imperial Majesty
and of the other estates of the Empire to consider.

Who, then, could justly interpose an objection to this decree? For if custom is
in question, who does not know that before the Church fell into ruin this
mingling of ecclesiastical and political power was unheard of? If I am not
believed, let Bernard at least be believed, who discusses this matter with the
greatest fullness and solidity in his work addressed to Pope Eugenius. But if
the question concerns right, by all means let the tables of divine law be
examined and judgment rendered according to them. Will any of those called
ecclesiastics say that he is wronged in this matter? Not at all, as we judge.
For besides losing nothing by this arrangement—the expenses for sustaining
ecclesiastical burdens will certainly be less than before once everything has
been distributed in order—they will also be relieved of a burden which they
confess, and demonstrate by the facts themselves, that they cannot sustain.

On the other hand, the best provision will be made at the least expense for
the churches themselves, even those called Catholic; and the strength of the
Empire will almost be doubled by the accession of people who, as they now are,
can neither make provision for their consciences, satisfy the burdens of their
churches, nor be useful to the commonwealth. Once these people have been
pacified in this way, Your Sacred Imperial Majesty need not be delayed by
those who, led either solely by shameful greed or by manifest wickedness,
contend that nothing in the present condition should be changed: that is, who
would rather perish themselves and destroy the rest along with them than be
saved. It would surely be no more just to comply with them than to make a
concession to robbers that—as they themselves would most desire—the laws and
the guardians of the laws should be abolished rather than that they should be
forbidden to commit robbery.

#### How a council can be convened

There remains that division, as it were, of the Christian world into so many
fragments, so that Your Sacred Majesty, Caesar, can no longer summon the whole
Christian world, even against its will, to a council by common consent, as the
ancient emperors once did, much less compel it to accept any laws. Yet I hope
that a remedy can be found for this difficulty too. For what prince, what
magistrates, or what peoples shall we suppose to neglect themselves and all
their affairs so completely that they will not voluntarily apply their effort
to calming these supreme disturbances once they perceive that remedies are
being sought sincerely and on fair conditions? Besides the fact that those
whom such miseries and calamities do not move must be the cruelest of all, it
is also certain that in the end either there will be kings without subjects or
subjects without kings, and therefore the human race will perish most
wretchedly unless some ready remedy for these evils is found.

Thus there is the best hope that what Your Sacred Majesty cannot accomplish by
command and imperial power may nevertheless be obtained without very great
difficulty, if not from all, then from most, by admonitions and gracious
appeals. In this way you may be regarded as the PEACEMAKER of the whole
Christian world—a title greater or holier than which nothing on earth can be
named. But even if you could not persuade many people of so holy and necessary
a matter, would you therefore cast away the care and concern of pacifying so
many provinces and cities which obey Your Majesty, instead of considering
what you owe to yourself and your people?

#### While the disputation is pending

Since, however, these things cannot be accomplished except over a very great
interval of time, it remains for us to consider how provision may meanwhile be
made for affairs

[Printed page 120; PDF page 12; OCR object 0124]

so that they do not turn for the worse.

Some people, then, maintain first that, just as is customary in civil matters,
so too in this dispute it is especially necessary that those who were
despoiled be restored in full and those in possession be protected in their
possession. Others, on the contrary, contend that everyone should be given the
power to worship God in his own way, provided the public peace is not violated.
I ask both groups to permit me to compare contrary reasons with them.

Certainly, as regards the former, if the restoration of property is in
question, it seems that an act in which the authority of at least the municipal
magistrates intervened cannot be called an act done by force. I pass over the
fact that those against whom restoration is demanded will say something rather
harsher—but what if they also speak truly?—namely, that a possessor in bad
faith cannot acquire title by prescription and that restitution is not granted
against people of this sort. But if the inquiry concerns not property but the
worship of God—for it is fair that pastors should be chiefly intent upon this
one thing—what sort of equity would it be to compel people who believe that
they cannot preserve a good conscience under the papacy to embrace it again,
without their case being heard, while the question whether the papacy is to be
approved or condemned is still being debated? Finally, since the matter has
come to such a point that this restoration cannot take place without an
extremely destructive upheaval, everyone sees how wholly foreign this opinion
is from the peace that we seek.

Therefore I say frankly—would that I might also say it without offending
anyone—that all who so press that restoration of property that they thereby
delay even in the smallest degree the common concord of Christians are
unworthy to be heard as ecclesiastics. The Son of God himself and head of the
Church placed his own life after the salvation of those Christians. Those who,
on the other hand, think everyone without exception should be left to his own
conscience because, as they say, persuasion and not compulsion is needed in
the cause of religion, are refuted first by God's Word itself, and then by the
clearest arguments. For it is well known from the writings of the prophets how
severely the Lord punished those kings and princes of his people who either
themselves polluted the purity of religion or permitted it to be polluted by
others. Is God not the same now, or is the office of holy kings now different
from what it was then? If everyone rightly judges that in the civil affairs of
this life the people of the same city must use some common law and that not
everything must be permitted to everyone, should there be less concern for
true and sincere religion?

Certainly those who wish liberty to be granted for every form of worship
confirm people who think wrongly in their errors, multiply sects, open the
road to impiety, and finally render incurable the very evil for which we seek
a remedy. Therefore, since just as there is one God, so too there is one faith,
I maintain that those judge rightly who think that magistrates must provide
for nothing so much as that God, the Best and Greatest, be worshiped by the one
true and sincere religion within their empire and dominion.

But here an immense difficulty arises, because there is no sect which does not
appear to itself alone to be wise. Some people, therefore, because they have
persuaded themselves that only what has prevailed among the majority should be
followed, think all the rest should once for all be restrained by the severity
of the laws or even, if necessary, by arms. This opinion is both cruel in
itself—for who does not see that in this way by far the greatest part of the
human race will be destroyed to the point of extermination?—and wholly useless
for removing this evil, as experience itself clearly demonstrates. Indeed, it
is also full of the greatest and most manifest injustice, since the facts
themselves testify that neither from numbers nor from custom, however ancient,
can or ought the sincerity of a religion be proved.

There are therefore some who mitigate that harshness in the following way.
They wish the things they call heresies to be borne, because the evil has
plainly grown to the point that there is now no place left for those harsh
remedies, but on the condition that those whom they call heretics in no way be
permitted to hold assemblies and worship God in their own manner. If it were
sufficiently established who the heretics are, this opinion might perhaps not
be rejected in the present state of affairs. But since those who call
themselves Catholics are said to err most of all, and the number of those who
oppose them is not much smaller, not even this opinion can appear suited to
calming these evils. What, then? I shall state frankly what I think.

I judge that princes, if only they wish the Christian commonwealth to be
preserved, must devote themselves above all to this, putting all other
considerations aside: as I said shortly before, all the controversies must be
decided at once from God's Word. In the meantime, however, the evil that cannot
be changed immediately—that is, some diversity of religions—must of necessity
be tolerated, though certainly not approved.

I add several exceptions. First, those who in the meantime violate the public
peace, from whatever party they may come, should be punished with the greatest
severity; but each person should worship according to his religion peacefully
and without disturbance until matters have been settled by that common and
equitable disputation. Second, where no sects appear publicly but the state of
religion is uniform, nothing should meanwhile be changed in the public
settlement. If there are any there who dissent from those called Catholics,
they should either remain in the condition in which they now are, excepting
persecutions, or, if they so please, move to a place where they are permitted
to profess their religion more freely.

Third, some distinction should be made among the sects themselves. For who
could rightfully allow either Anabaptists who oppose the magistracy and
introduce a community of all property, or plainly blasphemous Tritheists or
Ditheists, or Epicureans or Atheists, or other monsters of this kind, to hold
public assemblies, openly strengthen one another, and acquire disciples? To
settle this point once for all, I maintain that those who are manifestly
impious, such as Epicureans, must in no way be tolerated, but wherever they are
found must be restrained with the greatest severity as public enemies of the
human race. Anabaptists, enemies of the sacred Trinity, and all sectaries of
this kind—because it is probable that among so great a number many can be
healed—should be tolerated while the council is pending to this extent: no
inquiry should indeed be made against them, but they should be forbidden, on
pain of death—for what crime is more capital than manifest blasphemy?—to hold
any assemblies or spread their blasphemies. This law should also be added: all
must promise that, without any evasion, they will abide by the matters decided
in that common disputation, to which their leaders too will be summoned to be
heard and judged from God's Word.

[Printed page 121; PDF page 13; OCR object 0125]

If they do not do so, they are to be punished as seditious persons in
proportion to the magnitude of their crime. If this is done, however, there
will be no reason for some to complain of an infinite number of religions—a
thing horrifying in itself—since only two forms of religion will be tolerated,
and these agree in many heads: one, namely, belonging to those who are called
Catholics; the other belonging to those who have openly departed from the
pontiff. The latter, although they disagree to some degree in sacramental
matters, as I said before, nevertheless possess the same body of doctrine in
general, so that their religion neither can nor ought to be counted as two.

This, then—may it be happy and auspicious—is the sum of my desires; this is my
counsel, such as it is. If it shall appear both agreeable to God's will and
suited to healing so many and such great evils, then come, let it stand
approved. If not, let it be regarded as unwritten. And may our most merciful
God, having compassion upon the human race, grant that holier and more useful
plans come into the minds of others, and that all princes devote themselves
above all to the glory of him by whom they have been placed upon their thrones
and judgment seats.
