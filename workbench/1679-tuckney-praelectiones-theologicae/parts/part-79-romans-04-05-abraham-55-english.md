Fifth, to address our present subject more closely, this Osiandrian invention
overturns the entire nature and scriptural doctrine of justification.

1. First, as we noted before, it entirely confuses justification with
sanctification by locating it in inherent righteousness, and in this it
forms a syncretism with Roman theology. Osiander indeed speaks more
splendidly, as Pezel says, when he names God or God's essential righteousness
as the cause, while the papists name the new quality itself.

For they place justification in infused inherent grace, while Osiander
places it in God's essential righteousness itself. Yet both place it in a
righteousness that inheres and dwells within, not in one that is external
and received through imputation. They make a person righteous physically
through the infusion of righteousness, whatever that righteousness may be.
Melanchthon appropriately objects with Romans 3:21: the righteousness by
which we are justified, though witnessed by the law,

[1679 printed p. 309 | IA leaf 317]

is manifested without the law—that is, it is revealed not by the law but by
the gospel alone. The law itself requires a person to be righteous and to be
regarded as righteous by inherent righteousness, and so this is sufficiently
known from the law. But that a person is regarded as righteous through the
remission of sins and the imputation of another's righteousness becomes
known through the gospel alone. This alone, therefore, is Pauline—that is,
evangelical—justification.

2. The second point we may note is that justification, if it does not
consist in the remission of sins alone, at least consists in it to a great
degree. Forgiveness is not an appendix to justification or something it
presupposes, as Osiander raves, but belongs to its formal, essential account
and definition. The Holy Spirit himself thus defines this justification as
consisting in the pardon of sins, Acts 13:38–39; Romans 4:7–8; 2 Corinthians
5:19. But remission of sins is far removed from the communication and
transfusion of God's essential righteousness. The former is gracious and
privative, the latter a positive reality; the former occurs from without,
the latter within. Unrighteousness and guilt can in themselves be forgiven
to someone who does not immediately possess inherent righteousness or become
righteous, as frequently happens among human beings. And although in true
justification these are never separated, they are nevertheless not the same;
one follows from the other: infused righteousness follows the granted pardon
of sins. Osiander seems to have sufficiently perceived the force of this
argument against his cause. To evade it, he therefore placed the remission
of sins far away from justification, identified that remission with
redemption, and maintained that it was accomplished in Christ's passion
itself. But he says justification follows it much later in time, when God
applies this accomplished redemption to us and sanctifies us by infusing his
essential righteousness. He drags to this purpose the apostle's words:
“Christ was delivered for our sins”—that is, for their pardon—“and raised
for our justification,” by which true righteousness inheres in us from the
infusion of God's essential righteousness. Thus Osiander. But to the
contrary:

1. Although in Scripture redemption sometimes includes the entire
deliverance which Christ both merited and applies to us, together with all
its parts, insofar as it is distinguished from justification, adoption, and
sanctification it is restricted to Christ's passion and merit. It is
distinguished from their application in calling, justification, adoption,
and sanctification. Though all these were eternally predestined in divine
election and acquired in Christ's passion with respect to merit and payment
of the price, they are first applied and become ours from effectual calling
onward. Thus in Christ's passion the actual remission and justification of
our sins did not yet exist with respect to us as their terminus, since
actual faith is established as their means and instrument, Acts 10:43 and
13:38–39. At least in the order of nature, therefore, faith must antecedently
precede in justification so that actual remission of sins follows from it.
All the more abominable is Osiander's senseless position, which affirms that
Nero's sins were forgiven through Christ's death but that he was not
justified because he did not possess God's essential righteousness
inherently.

2. As to the passage from Romans 4 which Osiander adduces to establish his
position—“Christ was

[1679 printed p. 310 | IA leaf 318]

delivered for our sins but raised for our justification”—its sense is clear. Christ paid the
penalties of our sins by his death, but rose again, now the victor over
death and thereby made a suitable ground for our faith, in order to apply
this redemption actually to us by faith. He would thus provide us with what
he had merited, not only making us inherently righteous through
sanctification, but also actually and effectually pardoning our sins in
justification. Here we may note something that demonstrates the foolishness
of Osiander's interpretation and position. If, as he claims, remission of
sins does not occur in justification because Christ's passion long preceded
it, will justification not also be denied to the resurrection? Osiander
does not say this, but affirms the contrary, as the apostle himself states,
even though the resurrection, which followed Christ's passion on the third
day, also preceded our justification.

3. Third, Osiander's position also contradicts the doctrine of justification
because Holy Scripture everywhere attributes it to Christ's obedience,
death, and blood: “He was made under the law, that he might redeem those who
were under the law,” Galatians 4:4–5; “God set him forth as a propitiation
through faith in his blood,” Romans 3:24; “justified in his blood,” Romans
5:9; and “he made him sin for us”—that is, an offering for sin in his
death—“that we might become the righteousness of God in him,” 2 Corinthians
5:21. Just as “the faith of Christ” is not so called because Christ believes,
so “the righteousness of God” is “not that by which God is righteous, but
that with which he clothes a person when he justifies the ungodly,” as
Augustine most correctly says in *On the Spirit and the Letter*, chapter 9.
Nor does he justify the ungodly with the righteousness by which he himself
is righteous, “his eternal and unchangeable righteousness,” as Augustine
also says in Letter 120, chapter 30. Again, it is not called the
“righteousness of God” because God is righteous, but because a person has it
from God, book 3 *Against Two Letters of the Pelagians*. Christ's obedience,
blood, and death certainly are not God's very essential righteousness, for
they include Christ's humanity, which cannot be said of God's essential
righteousness. They designate not what God is in himself, but what Christ
became as man for us, and what he did and suffered. Osiander calls us away
from these things and blasphemously asserts that they contribute little to
our justification, since he says they operate only accidentally and not in
themselves.

4. Hence, fourth, our justification is most properly owed to Christ's
priesthood insofar as he offered himself to God as a victim to destroy our
sins, as is evident from the apostle's already cited words in 2 Corinthians
5:21. According to Osiander's position, this priesthood of Christ will have
nothing to do with our justification, since by his principles justification
must be attributed not to Christ's office but to his nature. Christ does not
justify us as priest, but only because he is God. For Osiander “vigorously
denies that Christ as priest is our righteousness by expiating sins and
reconciling the Father to us; rather, he is our righteousness as eternal God
and life.” Why, then, did Christ endure so much? Was it so that at such great
expense he might redeem nothing? Bellarmine argues this correctly.
