QUESTION IV.

THE UNITY OF THE MEDIATOR

*Is Christ alone our mediator before God? We affirm against the Roman
Catholics.*

I. Our dispute here is with the Roman Catholics. In order to establish more
firmly their invention concerning the invocation of saints, they contend that
departed saints discharge the office of mediators and intercessors before God
for the living, and that by not only their prayers but also their merits they
procure God’s favor for us.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The question does not concern simple prayer, or intercession improperly
so called, which consists in the mutual fellowship of prayer by the living for
one another. For we confess that believers can pray for one another and that
pastors especially ought to discharge this office before God for the people.
Rather, the question concerns mediation properly so called, by which someone
is considered to intercede by his own power, not only carrying prayers to God
but also commending the prayers offered to God and making them acceptable by
his merits.

III. The question is not whether the saints are our mediators before God in
an equal degree with Christ. The Roman Catholics disavow this, and therefore
distinguish a primary from a secondary mediator, and a mediator of redemption
from one of intercession. Rather, the question is whether the saints can in
any sense at least properly be called our mediators. They affirm this; we deny
it.

IV. The question comes down to this: Besides Christ, are the saints our
mediators before God, not merely interceding for us with their prayers, but
also procuring his favor for us by their patronage and merits? Or does Christ
alone discharge for us the task of mediator and advocate? We assert the
latter; the adversaries maintain the former.

V. Now two parts are customarily distinguished in Christ’s priestly office:
satisfaction and intercession. They are not ashamed to attribute both to the
saints. As for intercession, it is well known that they attribute it to the
saints, since they contend that their prayers are carried by the saints,
indeed they ask that pardon be given to them through the saints’ prayers and
that they themselves be heard. The Roman Missal says on the Feast of St.
Thomas, “O God, for whose church the glorious pontiff fell beneath the swords
of the ungodly; grant, we ask,

[Printed p. 341 | Internet Archive PDF 369 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

that all who implore his aid may obtain the saving effect of their petition.”
And in the same place for the Innocents: “Lord, may the pious prayer of your
saints not fail us, which may both commend our offerings and obtain your
indulgence for us.” On the Feast of All Saints: “We ask that through multiplied
intercessors you grant us the desired abundance of your propitiation.” As for
satisfaction, it is well known that good things are requested from God through
the merits of the saints. On the Feast of Bishop Nicholas they ask to be freed
from the fires of hell by his merits and prayers. On the feasts of Pope Leo,
Athanasius, and Basil, they say: “By his intervening merits absolve us from all
sins.” On the octave of Peter and Paul: “That by the merits of both we may
obtain the glory of eternity.” Bellarmine does not disagree in *On
Indulgences,* Book 1, chapter 4: “It is not absurd,” he says, “if holy men are
called our redeemers in some manner”; and he adds there that holy men can not
inappropriately be said in some manner to redeem our offenses by their
sufferings. Hence the falsity is exposed of what the same author says in *On
the Supreme Pontiff,* Book 3, chapter 21, where he denies that Roman Catholics
honor the saints as mediators and says that they only ask the saints to join
their prayers to ours.

[Marginal heading: The saints are proved not to be mediators: 1. From 1
Timothy 2:5.]

VI. We oppose the mediation of the saints. First, we do so by the explicit
words of Scripture, which attribute this task to Christ alone, 1 Timothy 2:5:
“One God, one mediator between God and men, Christ the man.” Here one mediator,
not several, is named—and specifically Christ, not any saint or angel—who gave
himself as a counter-ransom (ἀντίλυτρον) for us. It is objected in vain: 1.
That he is called *one,* not *the only one.* For the word *one* (εἷς) often
denotes not merely one but the only one, as in Homer: “The rule of many is not
good; let there be one ruler, one king” (οὐκ ἀγαθὸν πολυκοιρανίη, εἷς κοίρανος
ἔστω, εἷς βασιλεύς); and in Virgil: “The conquered have one deliverance,” that
is, the only one. So it is repeatedly in Scripture, Ephesians 4:6, Romans 3:10,
12, and 1 Timothy 3:2, 12, as Bellarmine does not deny in *On Christ,* Book 1,
chapter 3. Paul’s language also requires this meaning, because a mediator is
called “one” (εἷς μεσίτης) in the same way that God (θεὸς) is called “one”
(εἷς)—not merely one, but the only one, to the exclusion of anyone else.

VII. 2. It is objected that Paul is treating the mediator of redemption, who
is the only one, but not the mediator of intercession, who can admit
associates. We answer: 1. The distinction is unknown to Scripture, which
identifies one mediator, not several. 2. A mediator of redemption and one of
intercession are improperly distinguished, because no one can be a mediator
of intercession who is not also one of redemption. For these two functions,
propitiation (ἱλασμός) and appearance on our behalf (ἐμφανισμός), are two parts
of mediation which pertain to the same office and must never be separated, as
they are joined in Scripture, 1 John 2:1–2, Romans 8:34, and Hebrews 8:25
[sic]. Under the law it belonged to the high priest alone both to enter the
sanctuary and plead for the people and to offer sacrifice. 3. The Roman
Catholics themselves overthrow this distinction when they acknowledge the
saints as redeemers in a certain manner and wish them not only to pray that we
may be saved but also to obtain salvation for us by their merits. Hence they
ask from them the very same thing they ask from Christ the redeemer—the
remission of sins and eternal life—and ask it by virtue of their merits, as
was seen above. Everyone sees that this surely pertains to redemption. Nothing
is more frequent among them than to call the Blessed Virgin the redemptress
and liberator from sins. 4. Paul does not treat only the mediator of redemption
but also of intercession, because he treats the offering of prayers, which
ought to be made for all kinds of people and cannot rightly be made except in
the name of Christ alone, who is our one mediator.

VIII. 3. It is objected that Paul speaks of the primary, principal, and
immediate mediator, yet therefore does not exclude secondary, ministerial, and
mediate ones, just as although God alone is called good and Christ the one
master, this does not prevent others from being teachers and others good. We
answer: The distinction is unwritten (ἄγραφος), indeed contrary to Scripture
(ἀντίγραφος), for Scripture nowhere presents several mediators to us or
directs us to them in faith. Indeed, when it attributes this office to Christ
alone, by that very fact it teaches that it is impossible (ἀδύνατον) for
others. Nor does the comparison offered pertain to the matter. For God is
called good in such a way that this is proper to him alone preeminently

[Printed p. 342 | Internet Archive PDF 370 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

(κατ’ ἐξοχήν), and it can belong to another only analogically and in a far
dissimilar manner. And although Christ sends teachers to us, no one can or
ought to attribute to them the name *master* which belongs to Christ alone.

[Marginal heading: 2. From 1 John 2:1;]

IX. Second, from 1 John 2:1: “If we have sinned, we have an advocate with the
Father, Jesus Christ the righteous, who is the propitiation (ἱλασμός),” and so
forth. Three arguments are contained here: 1. Advocate (παράκλητος) is used in
the singular, which John would have said wrongly if there were several and not
only one. 2. This advocate is expressly called Christ Jesus the righteous, so
that no other may claim this task for himself. 3. The advocate is one who is
also the propitiation (ἱλασμός) for sins. But no one except Christ performed
this. It is vainly replied that Christ is indeed called the advocate, but not
the only advocate. For although the exclusive particle is not added expressly
(διαῤῥήδην), it is necessarily included in the added epithets, both *righteous*
and *propitiation* (ἱλασμοῦ). Moreover, if John acknowledged several
advocates, why does he name Christ alone and not add others to whom people can
flee? To this pertains the fact that he is called the way, the truth, and the
life, and that no one comes to the Father except through him, John 16:6 [sic],
and that there is salvation in no one else, Acts 4:12.

[Marginal heading: 3. Because this is injurious to Christ;]

X. Third, because the mediation of the saints tends to Christ’s dishonor, as
though he alone were not sufficient and could tolerate associates in this
office, which cannot be said without great blasphemy. It does not help to say
that no injury is done to Christ because the saints have from Christ the power
to intercede and obtain. For it is gratuitously supposed that Christ merited
for them and wills to confer upon them such power to intercede for us and
obtain salvation. Scripture has never said this, nor can it be proved by any
reason. Indeed, if Christ is the perfect mediator who can by himself completely
fulfill all the parts of this office, what need is there to invent others for
him? Nor should it be replied that no prejudice is thereby created against
Christ because the saints are mediators not so much between God and human
beings as between human beings and Christ, as Hesselius observes on 1 Timothy
2. For then a mediator would be needed to reach the mediator, although Christ
himself immediately (ἀμέσως) and most kindly calls us to himself and does not
send us away to others: “Come to me,” Matthew 11:28. Moreover, those mediators
to Christ either will or will not need another mediator to give them access to
him. If the former is asserted, it will again be asked whether yet another
mediator is needed to reach this third one, and so the matter would proceed to
infinity. If the latter, no reason can be given why those mediators should be
believed more accessible to us in approaching Christ than Christ himself,
since he pursues us with no less affection but with a far more fervent one and
was made like us in all things so that he could sympathize with our
infirmities, Hebrews 2:17.

[Marginal heading: 4. Because they cannot know our necessities;]

XI. Fourth, because the saints cannot know our necessities and search hearts
and reins so as to perceive each thought and desire distinctly and
individually. Without this they cannot discharge the task of mediators and
intercessors. What the adversaries object here concerning the saints’
knowledge was discussed above in Locus XI, on the law, Question 7.

[Marginal heading: 5. Because this doctrine savors of pagan superstition.]

XII. Fifth, because this doctrine savors of pagan superstition and was drawn
from the errors of the Gentiles, who distinguished their gods into primary
and secondary. Those of the inferior order, namely demons, were mediators
between the supreme gods and human beings. Hence Plato says in the
*Symposium:* “God is not approached by human beings, but all commerce and
communication between gods and human beings occurs through demons.” Hence
they distributed provinces, cities, arts, and diseases among demons and
assigned various tasks to various ones, just as

[Printed p. 343 | Internet Archive PDF 371 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

papists place saints over empires, cities, arts, and diseases and adopt them
as patrons and tutelary gods to whom they may repeatedly flee.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XIII. The consequence does not hold from the communion of saints to a
communication of offices among them. Saints admitted into heaven indeed have
communion with the living insofar as they are members of the same mystical
body. But a communication of offices is not therefore given, since they
neither attend to our necessities, nor can they hear our thoughts and prayers
and grant them, nor do we have any further commerce with them by whose benefit
we could implore their support.

XIV. Not everyone who prays to God for us is immediately a mediator before
God; otherwise we would have almost as many mediators as brothers. And although
Scripture very frequently mentions the mutual prayers of human beings, it
never teaches that human beings are mediators before God. Therefore a mediator
must be one who, first, prays not only in general but also for individuals;
and, second, prays by virtue of his own merits and obtains for us remission of
sins and salvation. This can be said of Christ alone.

XV. It is one thing for Moses and Samuel while living to pray for the people,
and another for the same men after death to discharge this office for them.
Jeremiah 15:1 does not appeal to the intercession of the dead, but
hypothetically shows the uselessness of their prayers if they were still alive
and prayed for the people, as they had done more than once: “If Moses and
Samuel stood before me and prayed for the people, my soul would not be toward
this people.” The sense is: If Moses and Samuel were still living and made
supplication for this people, they would accomplish nothing by their
intercession, though formerly they appeased God by their prayers, Exodus 32:14
and 1 Samuel 7:9. Thus it signifies that the salvation of this people is
hopeless and their destruction certain and determined. Compare Ezekiel
14:13–14: “Even if these three men were in its midst—Noah, Daniel, and Job—they
would deliver their own souls by their righteousness.” Thus the subject here
is not the prayers of the dead, but of the living, if they were to return to
the people.

XVI. What is said concerning the harps and golden bowls in the hands of the
twenty-four elders, Revelation 5:8–9, does not favor the intercession of the
saints. For if this is referred to departed saints, it will not signify
prayers which they offer for the living but their praise of God (τὴν εἰς θεὸν
δοξολογίαν), as Aretas and Andrew of Caesarea observe—that is, the praises of
God and incense of thanksgiving which they continually offer to God. This is
immediately explained in the next verse by the new song. Thus when those
golden bowls full of incense are said to be the prayers of the saints, the
words “which are the prayers of the saints” (αἵ εἰσιν αἱ προσευχαὶ τῶν ἁγίων)
mean, that is, symbolically designate, the prayers of the saints: either those
who possessed the harps and golden bowls, or indefinitely all saints whose
prayers are described by incense. But if it is referred to the praises and
prayers of living saints, as is more probable and Estius does not deny,
nothing will be elicited from it in favor of the prayers of the dead. Thus
evangelical worship in praises and prayers is described in words taken from
legal worship: as harps, instruments of praises, signify praises, so golden
bowls signify the prayers of believers offered in a heart purified by faith.
In this way they show themselves to be truly kings and priests, as they say
afterward, for harps befit kings, such as David, while bowls befit priests.

XVII. Although intermediaries and mediators are needed before earthly kings
for us to be admitted into their presence, the rationale is not the same for
the heavenly King, who calls us directly to himself and has never for that
reason sent believers to the saints. If an earthly king directly summoned his
subjects to himself, who would not approach him freely and with confidence?
Who would choose courtiers as patrons to give him access to the king?

XVIII. The passages Job 5:1, 19:21, and 33:23; Exodus 32:13; Zechariah 1:12;
Revelation 8:3; and Tobit 12, which are adduced here for the intercession of
the saints, were discussed in Locus V, on the angels, Question 9, and Locus II,
Question 7.

XIX. It is one thing to pray in general for the church and another for
individuals. Even if the saints were to pray in general for the church
militant, which they know is still oppressed by adversaries and exposed to
various contests, it would not immediately follow either that they pray for
individuals, whose necessities and prayers they do not know, or that they are
their mediators who obtain God’s grace for them by their prayers and merits.

XX. The passage 2 Maccabees 15:14, where Judas is said to have seen in a vision
the high priest Onias and Jeremiah, who had already died, praying for the
Jewish people, cannot help the adversaries. 1. Because this book is apocryphal
and full of fables, as the Roman Catholics themselves testify, specifically
Pope Gelasius in Distinction 15, canon *Sancta Romana,* where he admits only 1
Maccabees and rejects the second book. 2. The passage proves only that the
saints pray in general, not individually in the manner of mediators. 3. The
Roman Catholics themselves maintain that the fathers before Christ’s death
were still in limbo, excluded from the face of God, and therefore unfit to
pray for others.

XXI. The cry of the souls beneath the altar, Revelation 6:9–10, does not
pertain to intercession for others but to prayers for themselves. They do not
say, “Why do you not free our brothers?” but, “Why do you not avenge our
blood?” Nor should this be understood properly, since souls do not cry out,
but allegorically. It may denote the desire for the last judgment and
resurrection, as Ambrose and Bernard wished in *Discourse 3 on the Feast of
All Saints.* Or it may denote the manifestation of the wickedness of tyrants
who deserve punishment by God and of the innocence of the martyrs who deserve
reward, just as elsewhere the voice of Abel’s blood is said to cry from the
ground, Genesis 4. For the death and slaughter of the martyrs, always fresh
before God, demands the vengeance of divine justice, as Lyranus observes.

XXII. The consequence does not hold from the mutual prayers of the living to
the prayers of the dead for the living. Among the living there is a mutual
communion of offices and necessities; between the dead and living there is
none. The former prayers are commanded and commended in Scripture, but the
latter are not at all. Nor does the perfection of charity with which the
saints are endowed cause them to pray individually for us, because the
condition of that life does not admit it.
