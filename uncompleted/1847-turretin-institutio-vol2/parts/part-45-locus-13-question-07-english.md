QUESTION VII.

*Was the hypostatic union of the two natures in Christ made in such a way that
neither the Person was divided nor the natures confused? We affirm, against
Nestorius and Eutyches.*

[Marginal heading: Two rocks to be avoided concerning the hypostatic union:
Nestorianism and Eutychianism.]

I. Although the mode of the hypostatic union is positively ἄῤῥητος,
*ineffable,* it is nevertheless not badly described negatively, when it is
denied that it was effected either by a division of the Person or by a
confusion of the natures. These are the two rocks especially to be avoided
here: *Nestorianism* on the one hand, which divided the Person, and
*Eutychianism* on the other, which confused the natures.

II. To oppose this twofold error, the fathers assembled at the Synod of
Chalcedon in the year 451 said that the union was effected ἀδιαιρέτως,
*indivisibly,* and ἀχωρίστως, *inseparably,* against Nestorius, and ἀτρέπτως,
*without change,* and ἀσυγχύτως, *without confusion,* against Eutyches. For
the two natures were so united in one Person that neither was converted into
the other, nor were they confused so that some third nature, compounded from
the two, emerged from them. Rather, they remained unconfused, each with its
own properties.

[Marginal heading: The opinion of Nestorius is examined.]

III. The first error is that of Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople, who
fashioned two persons from Christ’s two natures. Therefore he denied that the
blessed Virgin could be called θεοτόκον, *God-bearer,* or *Deipara, mother of
God,* allowing only χριστοτόκον, *Christ-bearer.* And he said that Christ was
not θεὸν, *God,* but only ἄνθρωπον θεοφόρον, *a God-bearing man,* a man who
bears God. Thus he made two Christs, one of whom was crucified by the Jews,
while the other was not at all. Hence he repeatedly cried, *Why do you glory,
O Jew? You crucified a man, not God.* Nor did he acknowledge any other
ἕνωσιν, *union,* of the natures than an accidental one: first, *κατὰ
παράστασιν, according to presence,* and a simple indwelling, insofar as the
Word dwelt in the man Christ as in his own temple; then *κατὰ χάριν* and
*εὐδοκίαν, according to grace and good pleasure,* insofar as God especially
rested and delighted in this man; then *κατ᾽ ἐνέργειαν, according to
operation,* insofar as he was God’s instrument in those marvelous works which
the Son of God performed; then *κατὰ ταὐτοβουλίαν, according to identity of
will,* and affection, with respect to the agreement between the will of the
man and the will of God in Christ; and then *κατ᾽ ἀξίαν* and *ἰσοτιμίαν,
according to worth and equality of honor,* with respect to the dignity
conferred upon the assumed flesh.

IV. There are indeed some who are more favorable to Nestorius and more
hostile to Cyril. They have ventured to proclaim the former orthodox, as one
who distinguished the natures in Christ but did not separate them and who was
therefore unjustly condemned at the Council of Ephesus, while proclaiming the
latter a heretic. Thus they have not hesitated to oppose the doctrine hitherto
constant in the Reformed churches. Yet we do not think that this novel
invention and that *κυρίαν δόξαν, authoritative opinion,* can be demonstrated
by any solid argument. Indeed, there are many things which prove its vanity.
1. No one can readily presume that Nestorius did not really hold the view
unanimously charged against him by the three ecumenical councils of Ephesus,
Chalcedon, and Constantinople; by writers of ecclesiastical history and
heresiologists; and even by the imperial laws themselves, in the title *On
the Supreme Trinity,* laws 5, 6, and 7. These laws order the books of Nestorius
to be burned and consigned to utter destruction, and threaten the last
punishment against those who dare to possess or read such writings or books.
2. The twelve chapters or anathemas of Cyril approved by the Synod of Ephesus,
reported by Forbes in book 2, chapter 6, clearly prove this. 3. Nestorius
denied that Mary could be called θεοτόκον for no other reason than that he
denied the two natures had coalesced in one Person. Vincent of Lérins,

his contemporary and an impartial witness, testifies to this in his
*Commonitorium,* book 1, chapter 17, which he says he wrote three years after
the Council of Ephesus: “Nestorius, afflicted by a disease contrary to that
of Apollinaris, while pretending to distinguish two substances in Christ,
suddenly introduces two persons. In an unheard-of crime he wishes there to be
two Sons of God, two Christs, the one God and the other man,” and so forth.
Even if Nestorius used ambiguous and evasive phrases to conceal his error,
phrases which might somehow admit a sound sense, it does not immediately
follow that he was orthodox or that the fathers, who knew his mind more
deeply, fastened upon him a doctrine different from the one he cherished. Nor
if Cyril sometimes used harsher phrases which seemed to approach
Eutychianism when refuting Nestorius—as not infrequently happens when those
who set out to attack one error seem to slip into another—can it be inferred
that he understood this mystery less correctly and favored Eutyches. Thus,
when he sometimes says that there is *one nature of the incarnate Λόγου,
Word,* he does not mean that the humanity and deity coalesced into one nature,
since in the same place he declares that the union was effected ἀσυγχύτως,
*without confusion.* Rather, he says this in opposition to Nestorius, because
there is one ὑφιστάμενον, *subsisting subject,* by which *the Word is united
to flesh according to hypostasis,* as stated in Anathema 2. Whatever the case,
the question is historical, a question *of fact,* not *of right.* This does
not prevent the error attributed to Nestorius from being rejected as
fundamental, as it is rejected by all the orthodox, who on various grounds
maintain that the hypostatic union was effected with a distinction of the
natures but without a division of the Person.

[Marginal heading: And it is rejected.]

V. First, this is proved by passages showing that the Son of God was born of
the Virgin. Since this cannot be understood with respect to the divine
nature, which is uncreated and eternal, but must be understood of the human
nature, which had a beginning in its own time, it is rightly concluded that
the divine and human natures constitute only one person in Christ. Whatever
belongs to the divine and human natures is rightly predicated of that person:
“The holy one who will be born from you will be called the Son of God” (Luke
1:35); “God sent his Son, made of a woman” (Gal. 4:4), and so forth.

VI. Second are the passages in which one person consisting of two natures is
described. In Romans 1:3 Christ is said to be “from the seed of David
according to the flesh, and the Son of God according to the Spirit of
sanctification”; in Romans 9:5, “God blessed forever, and a man sprung from
the fathers”; in 1 Timothy 3:16, “God manifested in the flesh”; and in
Philippians 2:6–7, “He who was in the form of God emptied himself, having
assumed the form of a servant.”

VII. Third are passages which attribute diverse properties and operations to
the one Christ: the Son of Man, who descended from heaven, is nevertheless in
heaven (John 3:13); the Lord of glory was crucified (1 Cor. 2:8); God acquired
the church with his own blood (Acts 20:28); and all the fullness of deity
dwells in him (Col. 2:9). These things could not be predicated of him unless
the two natures had coalesced in one person.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VIII. Where there are two complete and incommunicable natures, complete both
in substantial being and in subsistence, there are two persons. But in Christ
there are not two natures complete and incommunicable with respect to
subsistence. Nor does it follow that because two individual natures in Peter
and Paul constitute two persons, the same must hold in Christ. In human beings
the two natures have incommunicability together with singularity, but not in
Christ, because the human nature was destined for assumption by the Word, to
whom it is personally united. Nor does it follow from this that the word
*man* is predicated equivocally of Christ and of us. Rather, the word *man* is
taken *specifically* for the human nature when predicated of Christ, but
*suppositally* for the person when attributed to us.

IX. Actions and passions belong denominatively to supposita. Yet this does not
prevent them from being attributed formally to one nature or the other. Thus
suffering and death belong properly and formally to the human nature, but
denominatively to the Person according to one of his natures.

X. A temple taken physically and materially cannot be identical with the one
who dwells in it, but this is denied when it is taken metaphorically and
mystically. Thus the soul is said to be in its body as in a temple or
tabernacle, although it constitutes the same person with the body. Christ’s
humanity is called the temple of the deity in this latter sense, not the
former. This therefore properly marks a distinction of the natures, but not a
division of the person.

XI. Mary is rightly called θεοτόκος, *God-bearer,* or *Deipara, mother of
God,* concretely and specifically, because she bore one who is also God, but
not abstractly and reduplicatively insofar as he is God. Although Scripture
does not say this expressly, it nevertheless sufficiently intimates it when
she is called the mother of the Lord (Luke 1) and the mother of Immanuel.
Though the blessed Virgin contributed nothing to the Person of the Λόγου,
*Word,* considered precisely in himself, she can nevertheless be said to have
contributed something to the Person of the incarnate Word considered in the
economy, insofar as she supplied the human nature which he assumed into the
unity of the Person.

XII. If the title *Deipara* bestowed upon the Virgin was seized by
superstitious people as an occasion for idolatry, as Paolo Sarpi observes in
book 2 of his *History of the Council of Trent,* then “from the time the
impiety of Nestorius divided Christ, constituting two sons and denying that
the one born of the Virgin Mary was God, the church, in order to implant the
Catholic truth in the minds of the faithful, thought these words should be
inculcated more frequently in the churches of both East and West: *Μαρία
θεοτόκος,* ‘Mary, Mother of God.’ This was indeed instituted solely in honor
of Christ, but gradually it began to be shared with the Mother and finally
was referred altogether to her alone,” and so forth. Although, I say, this
most egregious error either arose or increased on this occasion, that takes
nothing from the truth, because the abuse and error of the Roman Catholics
ought not to abolish the legitimate use of this name.

[Marginal heading: Eutychianism.]

XIII. The second rock to be avoided here is *Eutychianism,* the opposite of
the first. It is named after Eutyches, the archimandrite of Constantinople,
who, while too vehemently opposing Nestorius as he divided the Person of
Christ in two, confused the two natures into one. He was condemned by the
Council of Chalcedon. The two adverbs ἀτρέπτως καὶ ἀσυγχύτως, *without
conversion and without confusion,* bear on this error. They intimate that
through the hypostatic union one nature is neither changed nor converted into
the other, and that the natures are not so confused or mixed with each other
that each does not retain its own properties and conditions.

XIV. The reasons are: 1. Scripture frequently sets the two natures in Christ
in opposition (Rom. 1:3; 1 Pet. 3:18; Heb. 9:14; John 1:14; Phil. 2:6–7, 11).
2. Two wills are attributed to him: “Not as I will, but as you will” (Luke
22:42). It does not therefore follow that there are two who will, because will
belongs to the nature, while the one willing belongs to the person. It is
clear that personality does not immediately follow will, because there are
three Persons in God but only one will. 3. Contraries are attributed to
Christ which could have no place in him without two natures: he would depart
from the world yet remain with us forever; the one born an infant is the
Father of eternity; he was put to death and made alive; and so forth, in the
form of God and in the form of a servant, and so forth.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XV. Although the *principium quod,* the acting subject, of Christ’s operations
is one, the *principium quo,* the principle by which he acts, is twofold: the
deity and the humanity. There is one ἐνέργημα, *work effected,* in which each
principle by which he acts exerts its power, but there is a twofold ἐνέργεια,
*operation.*

XVI. Things which coalesce into one nature are not to be distinguished, but
this does not apply to things which coalesce into one suppositum. In the
latter way the two natures in Christ coalesce in the same suppositum, but not
in the same nature constituted from the two, as soul and body do. Nor, if the
fathers sometimes employ this likeness to describe the hypostatic union, does
it follow that the account is the same in both cases. They agree in this: as
body and soul come together in one person, so do deity and humanity; and as
the soul operates through the body as an organ substantially united to it, so
the deity operates through the humanity. But they differ in this: body and
soul come together in a person as parts and incomplete natures, whereas the
deity is neither properly a part nor an incomplete nature. Moreover, a
certain third nature arises from soul and body, but not from deity and
humanity.

XVII. It is one thing to speak of *Toto Christo,* the whole Christ, and
another to speak of *Toto Christi,* the whole of Christ. The whole Christ is
God and man, but the whole of Christ is not. *Totus* denotes the Person in the
concrete, but *Totum* denotes the nature in the abstract. Thus it is rightly
said that *Totum Christum,* the whole Christ, is God or man, because this
denotes the Person; but not *Totum Christi,* the whole of Christ, because this
denotes both natures which are in him.
