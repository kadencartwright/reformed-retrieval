# John Calvin to Laelius Socinus

Letter 1323, 7 December 1549

This is a fresh English translation of the complete Latin letter printed in
*Ioannis Calvini opera quae supersunt omnia*, vol. XIII (Corpus Reformatorum
XLI), pp. 484–487. It was translated from the normalized Latin and checked
against the page images. It is not copied from Beaty and Farley or from any
other modern translation.

The CO editors' heading, synopsis, witness description, and notes are not
translated here because they are editorial apparatus rather than Calvin's
letter. Square brackets mark words supplied for intelligible English. The p.
485 marker falls within a sentence, while the p. 487 marker is necessarily
approximate in English because the printed page turn divides the Latin word
*integro*.

## Translation

### [CO XIII, p. 484]

I am replying later than you wished because, to confess the truth, I am
reluctant to be drawn where your letter calls me. I see that you are

### [CO XIII, p. 485]

by no means satisfied concerning the resurrection of the flesh. But if you
demand more from me, I do not even seek to know beyond what I have taught. If
I were to plead that no more has been given to me by the Lord, it
would not be fair to press me any further. For we must hold to the rule: “I
believed; therefore I speak.” But I say that I rest so content in this faith,
which I professed to you, that I do not think it lawful for me to reach any
farther. I hear what you say in reply: since this is a chief article of our
faith, and since the sum of our blessedness is placed in it, investigation of
it does not appear superfluous. Those are indeed attractive enticements. But
when the Spirit of God admonishes us through the mouth of John that [what we
shall be] has not yet appeared, and that knowledge is deferred until the last
day, I feel that a limit is being prescribed for me. What I wrote to you is so
firmly settled in my mind and so confirmed by the word of God that no doubt
troubles me. These things are, moreover, sufficient for me to look down on
death without fear by trusting in them. If you desire more, it must be sought
elsewhere, for you will never prevail upon me, out of eagerness to oblige you,
to leap beyond the bounds set by the Lord.

Forgive me if I am more sparing than you wish on the other questions too. If
someone asks my advice about taking a wife, I will advise nothing other than
that he marry one who is ready to follow Christ with her husband. She should
profess this not only with her mouth but demonstrate it in reality. For
whoever marries a woman who, although otherwise thinking rightly, nevertheless
refuses to withdraw from a profession of impiety will put the worst sort of
snare upon himself. I have seen far too often, in many people, how far those
who entangle themselves in marriages of this sort fall away from Christ.

But to answer you point by point: you ask whether one must abstain from a
papist marriage just as from a Turkish one. For my part, I would not venture
to place on the same level as Turks those who thoroughly retain the
superstitions of the papacy in which they were raised, for they come somewhat
nearer to us. Leaving that comparison aside, however, I deny that it is lawful
for a Christian man to join himself to a wife who is estranged from Christ.
We know that all papists fall into this category. A man who thinks it lawful
to take a woman who is still entangled in many errors, provided only that she
seeks her righteousness in Christ, does not realize how many matters will
later be sources of disagreement between him and her. Therefore no one, with
me as his adviser, will run this risk. Rather, my judgment is that a man who
chooses as his companion anyone other than a woman who has first divorced the
pope contracts marriage neither piously nor in the Lord. I know that a person
is not always given the choice he would wish, but difficulty does not remove
guilt. If a solemn renunciation of the papacy is added, it will certainly be
commendable; but I would not dare to require it as though it were necessary.
Nor would I define a fixed measure of how far a prospective wife must have
advanced in the doctrine of piety. I would require only that she have forsaken
Christ's enemy, bid manifest impiety farewell, and given her name to Christ.

### [CO XIII, p. 486]

As for your asking whether the church ought to regard a marriage of this sort
as valid or invalid, I do not sufficiently grasp where the question is meant
to lead. For just as the mutual consent of both parties is required for a
marriage to be duly solemnized in God's name and under his auspices, so, if
one party falls away from duty, the marriage bond by which each is bound to
the other does not cease to be legitimate. [The church] will indeed rightly
condemn the fickleness, but it does not dissolve the pledge already given.

For my part, if the lesser of two evils must be chosen, I think that someone
living under the papacy cannot do otherwise than present his children for
baptism, however corrupted it may be. If he repudiates the corruptions that
conflict with God's command, he will act piously and perform the duty of a
Christian man. There is, to be sure, an immediate danger of death. But his
steadfastness will deserve correspondingly greater praise because, whoever he
may be, no fear will break him or prevent him from making a confession worthy
of his piety. If someone deprives his children of baptism, he will produce
more than twice the occasion for stumbling. Therefore I would not advise
that.

Now, since weakness of spirit prevents almost all who live under Antichrist's
tyranny from testifying freely that they have nothing to do with the papists'
defilements, they should be admonished from this—as I myself admonish
them—how wretched they are and how deeply they are sunk in a labyrinth: they
cannot avoid one offense against God except by another offense. This truly is,
as they say, to be pressed between the sacrifice and the stone: they cannot
offer their children to Christ without exposing them to Antichrist's
defilements, yet they cannot omit the use of baptism without taking upon
themselves the stigma of contempt for Christianity. I apply this goad to those
who flatter themselves there, so that they may recognize their own misery in
their children too.

When I distinguish papist baptism from a baptism performed in play, you invent
an answer for yourself and attack it as though I had given it. You will not
find in my letter, I am sure, [the claim] that its force depends upon the
intention of the one consecrating. All I said, unless I am mistaken, was that
it is efficacious insofar as it is administered for this end: that it may graft us
into Christ's body, or that it may be a symbol of our renewal. Now it makes no
difference to me whether the person baptizing is a Lucianist—an irreligious
scoffer—or the devil. For regard must be had only to the institution and to
the continuous use that flowed from Christ's command as from a fountain.
Surely the rite of baptizing did not first begin yesterday. Neither the papists nor other idolaters
invented it. You therefore appraise it wrongly unless you refer it back to its
origin. However much it may have been corrupted by many depraved additions,
this nevertheless always remained fixed: that baptism is performed at
Christ's command, in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, so
that it may be a testimony of regeneration. This is the end that I teach must
be considered. Nor was there any other reason why God nevertheless regarded
circumcision as valid of old, amid the profanation of every sacred thing,
except that it was directed toward that original end, so that it might be the
seal of the divine covenant: “I will be your God, and [the God] of your
offspring after you.” Therefore, when the people under Josiah

### [CO XIII, p. 487]

renewed the covenant and performed solemn repentance for their defection,
circumcision was nevertheless not repeated, although it had been polluted not
only by unfaithfulness but by many impious superstitions. For God's holy
institution is of such weight that, amid the manifold corruptions of human
beings, it prevails so as to retain its own force. The counterargument you
want to turn against me amounts to nothing. For it was not a mock imitation of
baptism, as you suppose, that grafted [them] into the people of God, but
the observance of an institution handed down by God. And yet they were
degenerate children who had violated the covenant made with their fathers.

When I say that remnants of the church remain in the papacy, I do not restrict
this to the elect who are scattered there; I mean that the ruins of the
dispersed church exist there. And so that I need not argue with lengthy
reasons, it is fitting for us to be content with Paul's authority, for he
declares that Antichrist will sit in the temple of God (2 Thessalonians 2:4).
For that matter, I also think that I have proved with sufficiently strong
reasons that some church remains in the papacy, albeit half-shattered—or
indeed, if you prefer, shattered and deformed.

Yet I do not permit baptism to be administered purely according to Christ's
rule in the private home of a godly man. The answer is easy. For I would more
readily tolerate baptism of that sort—which is not permitted among us and is
indeed rightly forbidden—than papal baptism infected with so much filth. But
the issue here is not what I approve. As for your supposing that I would count
as nothing a baptism which perhaps one of my brothers administered within
private walls, I call God and human beings to witness that this never entered
my mind. For prescribing what is right and pure is one thing; determining what
remains from God's institutions amid foreign additions is quite another.

If a Turk is received into the church by a solemn profession of faith, I have
no doubt that his children too should be baptized. For that promise remains
in force: “I will be your God and [the God] of those who are yours.” It is not
for me to
condemn outright the timidity that leads some people to live too cautiously
to come under the tyrants' suspicion. For there is one lawgiver and judge, by
whose mouth I do not see it condemned. But as for those who think that their
cunning dissimulation in attending masses is a useful aid for advancing the
glory of God—would that they considered that God is not so destitute that he
needs our lies. It is certainly true that some people are occasionally won
for Christ on this occasion. Whenever that happens, I rejoice after Paul's
example. For in whatever way the kingdom of God is propagated, we ought to
count it as gain. But the question now under dispute is whether service of
this sort is approved by God—and he himself denies that it is.

7 December 1549.

## Translator's notes

1. The printed witness begins without an address or greeting and ends directly
   after the date. The CO editors state that the manuscript was not extant and
   note that an earlier editor apparently cut off the close of the letter.
   Nothing has been supplied to reconstruct either end.
2. “What we shall be” is supplied from the apparent allusion to 1 John 3:2;
   Calvin's Latin says only that it “has not yet appeared.”
3. The edition reads *Ego, qui superstitiones ... retinent, non ausim*, a
   syntactically compressed or possibly defective sentence. “For my part”
   renders *ego*, while “those who” supplies the otherwise missing object of
   *locare*. “Turkish” and “Turk” translate *turcicus* and *Turca* and
   reproduce Calvin's sixteenth-century comparison, not editorial language.
4. “[The church]” supplies the most likely subject of *improbabit* and
   *solvit* from Calvin's immediately preceding question. The subject is not
   repeated in the Latin sentence.
5. “More than twice the occasion for stumbling” renders *plus duplo ...
   offensionis*. *Offensio* can denote religious stumbling or scandal, not
   merely hurt feelings.
6. *Inter sacrum et saxum* is a Latin proverb, translated here by its ritual
   image, “between the sacrifice and the stone.” Its practical sense is “caught
   between two inescapable dangers.”
7. *Homo Lucianicus* evokes a follower or type of Lucian of Samosata—an
   irreligious or atheistic scoffer. “Lucianist” preserves the historical
   label while the gloss identifies its force; it does not mean a generic
   heretic or a follower of Lucian of Antioch.
8. “Counterargument” renders the Greek-derived *antistrophon*, literally a
   rhetorical turning-back or counter-turn.
9. The bracketed “the God” supplies English required by the ellipsis in the
   two covenant formulas.
