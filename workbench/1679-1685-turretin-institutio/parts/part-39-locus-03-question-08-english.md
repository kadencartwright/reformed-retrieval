QUESTION VIII

ON THE INFINITY OF GOD

Is God infinite with respect to essence? Affirmative against Socinus and
Vorstius.

I. God's simplicity is followed by his infinity, which extends equally
through his other attributes. By this infinity we conceive the divine nature
as lacking every limit and imperfection: with respect to essence through
incomprehensibility, with respect to duration through eternity, and with
respect to circumscription in place through immensity. Here we treat the
first, not the others, which will be treated later.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The subject here is not infinity improperly so called and with respect to
us, as the number of the stars or of the sand of the sea is called infinite
because we cannot attain it, although it must necessarily be

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 176 | IA leaf 230 | PDF p. 230]

finite in itself, since nothing except God is actually and absolutely
infinite. Rather, the subject is infinity proper and in itself.

III. The subject is not syncategorematic infinity and infinity in a certain
respect, of which it is said, “there are not so many that there cannot be
more,” as quantity is said to be divisible to infinity. Rather, it is
categorematic and absolute infinity. Nor is the subject potential infinity,
in the sense in which matter's appetite with respect to forms is said to be
infinite, but an infinity that is actual, according to which the essence is
not only indeterminate (ἀόριστος) but truly infinite (ἄπειρος), not only
actually infinite in the oblique case, but also infinite act in the direct
case.

IV. The Socinians oppose this infinity, as does Vorstius, who plows with their
heifer. Just as they do not acknowledge God's immensity with respect to
essence, so they refuse to hold that God is actually and absolutely infinite,
although he is often called so with respect to us. The Remonstrants, though
indirectly, agree with them when they ascribe to God infinity of power and
operation but either take away infinity of essence or call it into doubt.

[Marginal heading: That God is proved to be infinite in essence. From Psalm
145, etc.]

V. The orthodox attribute absolute infinity to God with respect to essence.
First, they do so from Scripture, which predicates it when it says in Psalm
145:3 that God is great and exceedingly praiseworthy and that there is no end
to his greatness. There the psalmist does not speak of greatness of mass,
which is far removed from the most simple God, but of the greatness—or rather,
the infinity—of essence and power. So Zophar says in Job 11:7: “Will you find
the depth of God or reach the perfection of the Almighty? It is higher than
the highest heavens—what can you do? It is deeper than hell—what can you know?
His measure is longer than the earth and broader than the sea.” Nor should it
be said that these words properly concern only the perfection of wisdom, as
Crellius maintains in book 1 of *On Religion*, chapter 27. For besides the fact
that the passage testifies that God's incomprehensibility itself is under
discussion, if it referred only to wisdom, the argument would press no less,
because the property of an infinite perfection cannot belong to a finite
nature. The same is the point of Isaiah 40:12: “Who has measured the waters in
his fist? Who has measured the heavens with his span?” and so forth; and
verses 15 and 17: “The nations before him are like a drop from a bucket, and
before him as nothing,” and so forth.

[Marginal heading: From God's perfection.]

VI. Second, this is proved from God's supreme perfection. For since God has
every perfection that can exist and be possessed, it is evident that nothing
better or more perfect than he can exist or be conceived. Therefore he must
be infinite, because an infinite good is better than a finite good. Moreover,
the perfections that inhere in created things are enclosed within fixed limits
beyond which they do not extend, and all their activity has a fixed sphere
which it cannot surpass. But the matter is otherwise in God. For besides
containing every perfection of every being, he has it in the most eminent
mode, that is, a mode which embraces every degree of every perfection without
any limitation.

[Marginal heading: And from power.]

VII. This is further confirmed by considering God's power. For since it, like
the other attributes, is identified with the essence, the essence must have
the same character as the power and thus be infinite just as it is. That the
power is infinite is evident from the fact that nothing is impossible for
God, for this could not be said of a finite power that has some limit.
Finally, if God's essence is finite, it must be limited by some cause. This
cannot be said of him who has no higher cause but is the cause of all things.
We must therefore come at last to say with the ancients that “the divine is
infinite and incomprehensible, and this alone about it is comprehensible: its
infinity and incomprehensibility” (ἄπειρον ἐστὶ καὶ ἀκατάληπτον τὸ θεῖον. καὶ
τοῦτο μόνον αὐτοῦ καταληπτὸν ἡ ἀπειρία, καὶ ἡ ἀκαταληψία)—namely, that God is
infinite and incomprehensible, and that his infinity and incomprehensibility
are the one thing about him that can be comprehended.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 177 | IA leaf 231 | PDF p. 231]

VIII. God is called infinite with respect to essence in three ways: 1.
originally, because he is absolutely independent and has, and can have,
nothing prior or superior to himself; 2. formally, because he has an
absolutely infinite essence (ἄπειρον); and 3. virtually, because he has no
finite sphere of activity and needs no concurrence of any cause in acting,
but can do whatever he wills.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

IX. Although God cannot produce an infinite effect, because such an effect is
not producible, he nevertheless does not cease to be of infinite power,
because he acts in an infinite mode. The infinity that cannot be found in the
terminus because of a contradiction is found in the very mode of acting.

X. *Comprehension* denotes either the attainment and full enjoyment of the
thing desired, by relation (σχέσιν) to those who reach the finish in a race.
In this sense the blessed can be called comprehensors, Phil. 3:12; 1 Cor.
9:24, in distinction from the faithful who are still on the way. Or it denotes
an accurate and in every respect complete knowledge of God which is wholly
adequate to its object. In this sense no one, neither humans nor angels, can
be called comprehensors, because the finite is not capable of the infinite.

XI. Although God knows himself perfectly and adequately, it does not follow
that his essence is finite, because God's very knowledge and comprehension of
himself is infinite. Thus, less properly, God's essence is said to be bounded
by God—not because God judges his essence to be finite, but because his
knowledge is adequate to it.

XII. It is indeed contradictory for something to be indefinite and to be
actually this particular thing. But it is not contradictory for something to
be actually infinite without any limits of essence and to be actually this
particular thing, that is, utterly singular, undivided in itself, and divided
from every other thing.

XIII. Although all creatures are infinitely distant from uncreated perfection
and there is no proportion of equality and commensuration between them and
God, it does not follow that all creatures are equally perfect. For the
measure of creatures' perfection is taken not from their distance from God's
perfection, but from the greater or fewer degrees of perfection which each
thing participates from God.

XIV. All perfections belong to God either formally, as perfections simply so
called—that is, those which, speaking absolutely, it is better to have than
not to have and than which no greater perfection can be conceived—or
eminently, as perfections in a certain respect. These latter indeed express
some perfection in their own genus, but one that is necessarily joined with
some imperfection. Because God cannot have them properly and formally, he is
nevertheless said to possess them virtually and eminently, either because he
can produce that perfection or because without it he can accomplish whatever
it accomplishes, with every imperfection excluded.

XV. That which is this thing in such a way that it is not another, either
formally or eminently, is finite with respect to substance. But this cannot be
said of God, who is this particular thing in such a way that he is nevertheless
all things eminently, by containing in himself eminently the perfections of
all things.

XVI. Although infinity with respect to place requires the formal existence of
a thing in all places, infinity of essence does not require that a thing
formally be every substance, but only that it contain the perfections of every
substance, if not formally, at least eminently.
