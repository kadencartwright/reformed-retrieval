### QUESTION XXIV

#### How Isaiah makes sixty-five years until Samaria’s ruin, when there were only twenty-two

Question twenty-four. How is Isaiah 7:8 to be understood: “And within
sixty-five years Ephraim shall be crushed so that it is not a people”? This
prophecy was delivered, as verse 1 states, in the time of Ahaz; yet from the
first year of Ahaz to the destruction of Ephraim and the captivity of the ten
tribes (in Hezekiah’s sixth) no more than twenty-two years are counted. For
Ahaz reigned no more than sixteen; add Hezekiah’s six, and they make only
twenty-two. Answer: The solution Jerome brings to the passage from the Jews
pleases most Roman Catholics and Protestants alike: the beginning of those
years is to be taken not from the time when Isaiah prophesied these things,
but from an earlier point—namely the prophecy of Amos, who in his fifth
chapter, verse 27, foretold Samaria’s destruction and the captivity of the ten
tribes before Isaiah. From the time of this prediction, in the days of Uzziah
and Jeroboam II, sixty-five years can readily be counted to Samaria’s
destruction.

Salian rejects this answer, but offers no other at all himself, and attacks
the common solution with only a slight objection. Ahijah the Shilonite, he
says, foretold the ruin of the Ephraimite kingdom long before; why, then, did
Isaiah take his reckoning from Amos’s prophecy rather than Ahijah’s? Cornelius
a Lapide answers that Amos’s prophecy about the Israelites’ ruin was more
familiar and widely known than Ahijah’s.

The latest English Annotators also reject this common solution, but the one
they offer from Junius’s notes is no better. They wish the head of this
reckoning to be taken from the time when Isaiah spoke these things, and thus
must put its end around the middle of Manasseh’s reign, more than forty years
after Samaria was overthrown. But it is asserted without proof that the
kingdom of Israel stood so long, or that any form of public polity among that
people endured so long. When Esarhaddon son of Sennacherib carried Manasseh
captive to Babylon not long after the wretched beginnings of his reign, we do
not read that he carried away any remnants of the Israelite kingdom with him;
a fabrication of this kind is a human addition to the divine Scriptures.

### QUESTION XXV

#### How Tyre’s captivity could be perpetual, yet last seventy years and only one king’s lifetime

Question twenty-five. How are those seventy years of Tyre’s captivity in
Isaiah 23:15 to be understood? First, what is their beginning and what their
end? 2. How could they be so many, since it is said there that Tyre’s
captivity would last no longer than one king’s rule, while no single king’s
reign reached seventy years? 3. How can they be so few, when that captivity
appears perpetual in Ezekiel 26:14, “You shall not be built again”? Answer to
the first: the seventy years of Tyre’s captivity did not have the same limits
as the equal number of years of the Jewish captivity. It is certain that
Tyre’s captivity did not begin before the eleventh year after Jeconiah was
carried away, for in that year Ezekiel speaks of Tyre’s destruction as future
in chapter 26; nor was it delayed beyond the twenty-seventh year after
Jeconiah’s removal, for in that year Ezekiel 29:18 speaks of it as already
accomplished. Indeed Tyre appears to have been captured in that very
twenty-seventh year of Jeconiah’s removal, for immediately upon the end of
that most arduous labor of Tyre’s thirteen-year siege, the spoils of Egypt
were granted to the Chaldeans as wages. Therefore, if the beginning of Tyre’s
captivity followed the beginnings of the Jews’ and Jeconiah’s captivity by
twenty-seven years, its end had likewise to follow the end of the other by the
same number of years. Its beginning should therefore be set, with Cornelius a
Lapide, in Nebuchadnezzar’s thirty-fifth, and its end in the first of Darius
Hystaspes. Henry Philip’s objections carry little weight.

Answer to the second: Calvin rightly rejects as trifling the Jewish fiction
that “one king’s time” signifies the lifetime of David, a king known to the
Tyrians and to King Hiram, who lived seventy years. It is more satisfactory
to signify the lifetime of any man—even one living amid royal luxuries—as
expressed by Moses in seventy years in Psalm 90. More perplexing is what the
English Annotators assert after Junius and others: by one king is meant

[Printed p. 87 | IA leaf 117 | access PDF 117 | OCR object 116]

the stock and family of one king, namely Nebuchadnezzar, who sat on the
Babylonian throne with his descendants for approximately seventy years until
the time of the Persians.

Answer to the third: We are not compelled here to interpret the scriptural
phrase “no longer” of merely a long time rather than perpetuity. Nothing
prevents us from affirming that ancient Tyre lies perpetually buried in its
ruins, and from locating the Tyre later restored near the site of the ancient
city, but some distance away. What Cornelius a Lapide advances to the contrary
from Josephus has no force.

### QUESTION XXVI

#### How long Isaiah’s ministry lasted

Question twenty-six. Did Isaiah prophesy beyond Hezekiah’s days? Answer: With
great agreement the Greek as well as Latin Fathers, and after them not only
Roman Catholics but very many Protestants, extend Isaiah’s life into the time
of Manasseh, by whom they say the Prophet was sawn apart. We do not love to
reject pious traditions of the ancients, received by most learned men, with
proud insolence, but prefer either to admit them with modest gentleness or to
pass them by silently and leave them untouched. Yet we judge that Isaiah’s
sawing—which the Roman Catholics wish to thrust upon the whole Church as
undisputed and solemnly impress upon the people every year through a reading
in their Martyrology—is rightly and deservedly denied by Abulensis. Would
sacred history have been silent about so great an event? Indeed, would
Josephus, who customarily pursues much smaller circumstances with precision?
As for the Fathers’ tradition, Jerome admits that its source is the Jews,
namely the Babylonian Talmud, that most filthy fountain of foul fables. The
tale before us actually seems to refute itself. Roman tradition says the
executioner first applied his wooden saw to the Prophet’s crown, then cut
through the middle of his head, neck, breast, and down to the hip. But the
Talmudic tradition does not agree with this, for it tells its tale as follows:
as the Prophet fled cruel Manasseh’s hands, a cedar opened itself by a divine
miracle and, when it received the trembling man, immediately closed its sides;
yet Manasseh, not at all deterred from his crime by the present miracle,
ordered a carpenter to cut the cedar with his saw together with the Prophet
enclosed within it. This cutting through the middle of the Prophet enclosed
in the cedar does not agree with the different manner of cutting related by
the Roman Catholics. But arguments against this fable need not be gathered
from anywhere beyond the sacred chronology before us itself. Isaiah 1:1
restricts the Prophet’s ministry to the days of four kings—Uzziah, Jotham,
Ahaz, and Hezekiah. What boldness it would be, contrary to explicit Scripture,
to extend it even into the time of Manasseh, the fifth king. By this reckoning
we must extend Isaiah’s life beyond the ordinary limits of human duration in
those centuries, to at least 124 years. Suppose Isaiah entered the prophetic
office at age twenty, itself a premature entrance upon any sacred office.
Today’s fanatical sectarians introduce thirteen-year-old boys into pulpits to
preach, just as recently English actors used to bring chatty young boys onto
the stage in women’s clothing to play the parts of whores; but this practice
and shameless impudence of profane men is far removed from the rules of divine
wisdom and the Church’s custom in appointing the ages of sacred ministers
from the world’s beginning to this very day. In the New Testament νεόφυτοι,
novices, are forbidden to administer sacred things, and the devil’s snare is
declared for those who hurry to burst into God’s sanctuaries, 1 Timothy 3:6.
Even in the Old Testament the Levites did not handle sacred things before age
twenty-five. But grant that, out of the ordinary course, Isaiah acted as a
twenty-year-old prophet in Uzziah’s fifteenth year, according to Jerome and
the other followers of the tradition we refute. There remain thirty-seven
years in Uzziah’s reign, sixteen in Jotham’s, as many in Ahaz’s, twenty-nine in
Hezekiah’s, and it is reasonable to grant another four or six years after
Manasseh entered his reign in his twelfth year, before he could mature to that
height of impiety at which he took Isaiah’s life—a man of the highest
authority among the people, his father-in-law as well, according to the
traditionalists, and closely related by blood to the kings, since he was the
son of Amoz, Amaziah’s brother and Uzziah’s uncle. The sum of these years
makes 124, an age to which human life in Hezekiah’s days did not customarily
extend, as Moses teaches in Psalm 90. We ought not to extend the Prophet’s
life so far without a greater necessity than a Jewish tradition from the
Talmud can provide.

### QUESTION XXVII

#### How Jeconiah began his reign both in his eighth and his eighteenth year

Question twenty-seven. How does verse 9 of 2 Chronicles 36 agree with verse 8
of 2 Kings 24? In the former Jeconiah comes to the kingdom at eight years of
age, but in the latter not before eighteen. Answer 1: We must reject

[Printed p. 88 | IA leaf 118 | access PDF 118 | OCR object 117]

Cajetan’s reconciliation and that of his followers, who apply a wedge far
harder than the hard knot: they say that the text is corrupt in Chronicles,
and that “eighteen” ought to be read for “eight.” But it is an excessively
rash venture of human ignorance to seek a remedy nowhere except through
violation of the divine Scriptures, against the harmonious witness of all
sacred manuscripts in every language. Torniellus and other Roman Catholics
themselves, after Abulensis, blunt this unhealthy boldness.

Second, the solution of the very learned Junius also seems unacceptable,
although both the earlier and later English Annotators embrace it as sound.
Junius translates the passage in Chronicles thus: “Jehoiakim was eight years
old when he began to reign,” and adds a marginal note asserting that the reign
said to begin here was not Jehoiachin’s or Jeconiah’s, but his father
Jehoiakim’s—an exceedingly subtle and violent interpretation. The Hebrew has
[*be molco*, according to Montanus’s interlinear, “in the reigning of him”].
The pronoun “him” can refer only to the nearest person, and Junius himself
explains the phrase that way in both the preceding and following verses,
indeed many times elsewhere as well, never otherwise except in a single
passage which he advances as the sole argument for the present interpretation,
2 Kings 16:2. There, because of a similar difficulty in Ahaz’s reign, he
invents a similar interpretation; certainly something obscure is proved by
something equally obscure, and here the great man assumes the very point in
question. We deny that the Hebrew *be molco* at 2 Kings 16:2 can be translated
“when he began to reign” more correctly than in the controversial passage
before us. In neither place should the pronoun be rendered with the relative
“he,” referring to another person, but in both with the possessive “his,”
referring to the immediately preceding person, as everywhere else according
to Junius himself. Let Deodati’s solution therefore prevail, which I see
approved by Torniellus, Henry Philip, and other Roman Catholics: Jeconiah had
two beginnings to his reign, as was said above of other Jewish kings. The
first came at age eight, when in his father’s second year he was taken into
association in the throne, so that amid those turbulent times the scepter
might be more firmly secured in the royal family. The other came when his
father died, in his own eighteenth year, after which he sat on the Jerusalem
throne for only three months and was soon carried to Babylon by Nebuchadnezzar
with his wives because he had administered the kingdom wickedly in the Lord’s
sight. An eight-year-old boy certainly did not have wives, nor at such a
tender age could he be tried in either the divine or human court as guilty of
wickedly administering the kingdom.

### QUESTION XXVIII

#### How Jeconiah was both Josiah’s son and grandson, and how he had both several brothers and only one

The question is how 1 Chronicles 3:15 agrees with Matthew 1:17. Here Jeconiah
is represented as Josiah’s son together with his brothers; there Jeconiah is
Josiah’s grandson and has only one younger brother, Zedekiah, while four
brothers begotten by Josiah are counted: Johanan, Joakim the father of
Jeconiah, the elder Zedekiah, and Shallum. Answer: This difficulty was once
habitually pressed against the truth of sacred Scripture by Porphyry and other
apostates. Various answers have been offered by various theologians, ancient
and recent, as may be seen in Epiphanius, Augustine, Ambrose, and other
ancients, and in Calvin, Beza, Gomarus, Maldonatus, Jansen, Spanheim, and other
more recent writers. Their substance comes to this. First, according to some,
Matthew’s text is corrupt in most copies, contrary to the testimony of various
manuscripts. Robert Stephanus had one, Faber Stapulensis another, and others
had several other manuscripts that added these words to the common texts of
Matthew: [“and Josiah begot Joakim, and Joakim begot Jeconiah”]. The context
of the passage seems to favor this correction, for Matthew expressly asserts
three *tessera-kai-decads*, sets of fourteen, in Christ’s genealogy, while in
the second series only thirteen generations, not fourteen, will be found
unless the aforesaid supplement is added.

Second, those who scruple to disturb anything in the common reading of the
sacred manuscripts on the testimony of a few copies answer differently as to
the method, although in substance the same as the preceding writers: there
were two Jeconiahs. This does not merely mean one man under two conditions—a
king before the removal and a private person afterward, though at the end of
his life a king again by favor of Evilmerodach, the Babylonian monarch—but
actually two men of the same name. They say many kings of Judah had two
names: Uzziah was called Azariah; Solomon, Jedidiah; Zedekiah, Mattaniah;
Jehoiachin, Jeconiah; and Joakim, Eliakim. It is therefore no wonder that
Jehoiakim son of Josiah was also called Jeconiah. It is true that in the
letters there is somewhat greater affinity between Jehoiachin and Jeconiah.
In the Hebrew name Jehoiachin there are *caph* and *nun*, the consonants
expressed in Jeconiah; but the final syllable in Joakim consists of *koph* and
*mem*, which do not

[Printed p. 89 | IA leaf 119 | access PDF 119 | OCR object 118]

appear in Jeconiah. In the reality and meaning, however, there is no
difference among all four names—Joakim, Jehoiachin, Eliakim, and Jeconiah—for
they all signify God establishing or confirming. In three the name Jehovah
is contracted; in one is *El*, also a name of God. In the two Jehoiachin and
Jeconiah is the verb *kun*, to establish or confirm; in the other two, Eliakim
and Joakim, is the verb *kum*, to rise, in the Hiphil to raise or establish,
the same as the former.

However this literary matter may stand, they say that Jeconiah, named twice
here, is really two men with one name. The former was Josiah’s son, otherwise
named Joakim and Eliakim, whose brothers really were Johanan, Zedekiah, and
Shallum; the latter was Josiah’s grandson and the son of the former Jeconiah.
That this former Jeconiah is different from the latter is, they say, evident
from the text: brothers and Josiah as father are ascribed to the former, both
of which are false of the latter, while the son Salathiel is given to the
latter, which is false of the former. But it may seem strange and contrary to
the rest of the series to interrupt the order, since the one first named as a
son is afterward said to be a begetter—not the same man, but another—and one
not previously called a son. The very learned Gomarus offers three examples
in the genealogies described in 1 Chronicles 3. In the passage of Matthew
before us, more suitably than in any of those passages, the continuity of the
series is interrupted because a new epoch of the last set of fourteen is
inserted between these two Jeconiahs.

### QUESTION XXIX

#### How Jeconiah was without children yet father of eight children

The following question, 29, is no less difficult than the preceding. The
question is how Jeremiah 22:30 is reconciled with 1 Chronicles 3:17. In the
former Jeconiah is written childless, ערירי, without any children; in the
latter many children are ascribed to Jeconiah, Salathiel with seven brothers,
or at least six. Answer: This question has troubled ancient and recent
theologians and chronologists alike, as may be seen in Gomarus on Christ’s
genealogy and Spanheim in the first part of his *Gospel Difficulties*. After
much rather perplexed disputation, most finally arrive at one of two
solutions; each has many great authorities. Some explain Jeremiah’s passage
not of simple childlessness but of the misery of Jeconiah’s seed and children.
They say the Hebrew edition says nothing of children, but that ערירי signifies
naked, wretched, empty, and is not correctly rendered “empty of children,”
since in the preceding verse 28 express mention is made of Jeconiah’s seed,
which was cast into exile together with its father; and in the cited passage
of Chronicles seven of his children are counted, while in Matthew a son,
Salathiel, is not merely ascribed to him but he is said in the most explicit
words to have begotten Shealtiel.

Others take a different course, contending that Jeremiah’s words speak of
childlessness and insisting that ערירי can here be understood of no emptiness
other than that of children. Pagninus’s interlinear, Junius’s Latin, the old
and new English, and the French and Italian Bibles all read the passage so.
They press this interpretation of Jeremiah all the more strongly because Luke
the Evangelist at 3:27 makes Salathiel the son not of Jeconiah but of Neri,
and traces Salathiel’s ancestors in a line altogether different from
Jeconiah’s, through David’s son Nathan rather than Solomon, and describes his
descendants too in another line, which terminates not, as in Matthew, in Eli
and Joseph, but in Jacob and Mary or Christ. They also affirm that Solomon’s
line ended in Jeconiah, and that those said to have been his descendants were
not his natural but his civil sons, by reason of kinship and succession to the
ducal dignity, which was the condition of David’s descendants for some
centuries after the Jewish kingdom was overthrown.

I would gladly incline to the former opinion if its champions, Spanheim and
Gomarus, did not admit that in this genealogy they must establish two
Salathiels and as many Zorobabels, and must make the Salathiel begotten by
Jeconiah and the Zorobabel begotten by Salathiel private and obscure persons,
far different from the other Salathiel and Zorobabel who, when Jeconiah died,
administered the leadership and government of the Jews. This difficulty is so
hard for us that it frightens us away from the former view and drives us to
the latter, which is indeed more common though intricate.

To the objections against this latter view we answer that the word “seed” in
Jeremiah 22:28 presses us no more than “sons” in 1 Chronicles 3. Both may be
explained of legal, not natural, seed and sons. This most ancient distinction
between natural and legal children—which Africanus professes to have learned
from the relatives of the Lord Jesus commonly called δεσπόσυνοι—is maintained
in the thesis by all Christian teachers, ancient and modern. We are compelled
to apply it to the present hypothesis not only because Jeremiah attests
Jeconiah’s ἀτεκνία, childlessness, but chiefly because Luke names Neri as
Salathiel’s father and makes the whole line of descent different from
Jeconiah’s, and finally because the former view assumes two Salathiels and two
Zorobabels without Scripture.

[Printed p. 90 | IA leaf 120 | access PDF 120 | OCR object 119]

As for what they repeat to the point of nausea about the meaning of the word
ἐγέννησε, “begot,” as though it must be taken of natural children alone, the
answer is that this is asserted without proof. We acknowledge no greater
emphasis in one conjugate than in another. They concede that “begotten,”
“offspring,” “son,” and even “begetter” and “father” can and often must be
taken of civil relations no less than natural ones; after conceding this in
good faith concerning the related nouns, they vainly deny the same concerning
the related verb “to beget.”

### QUESTION XXX

#### How Joahaz was the first of Josiah’s sons yet younger than Joakim, and how Joahaz, Johanan, and Shallum were the same man, yet there were two Shallums

Question thirty is the reconciliation of three passages with one another:
Jeremiah 22:11, 1 Chronicles 3:15, and 2 Kings 23:4. In the first place it is
affirmed that Shallum, who reigned for his father, was taken into Egypt
without returning; in the second, that Shallum was the fourth of Josiah’s sons
and apparently never reigned, since only three of Josiah’s sons reigned; but
in the third, the man taken to Egypt by Pharaoh Necho was not Shallum, the
fourth of Josiah’s sons, but Joahaz, the first, who also does not appear to
have been Josiah’s first son, for the first was named Johanan, not Joahaz, and
the second, Joakim, was two years older than Joahaz, who died a captive in
Egypt.

Different people answer these things differently; to us the following appear
most probable. First, we concede that Joahaz, who is named first among
Josiah’s children, was actually younger than the second, Joakim, but was named
first because he was the first of all the brothers to reign, before even his
elder brother Joakim. For when their father Josiah died, the people placed his
son Joahaz on the paternal throne in the twenty-second year of his age; but
after three months Joahaz was taken into Egypt, and his elder brother Joakim,
aged twenty-five, was commanded by Pharaoh to reign. Second, the son of Josiah
taken into Egypt appears to have had three names: Johanan, Joahaz, and
Shallum. Third, two of Josiah’s four sons appear to have been called Shallum:
the first, who died a captive in Egypt, and the fourth, who died before his
father and never sat on the throne, of whom there is no mention elsewhere
than in the aforesaid passage of Chronicles. Since no interpreter offers
demonstrably certain conclusions in questions of this sort, one must rest in
what comes nearest—in the most probable conclusions.

### QUESTION XXXI

#### How the Babylonian captivity began in Nebuchadnezzar’s first, eighth, and nineteenth years

Question 31. How is Daniel 1:2 reconciled with Jeremiah 25:1 and Ezekiel
40:1, and also with 2 Chronicles 36:21? Daniel seems to say that the
Babylonian removal and captivity began in Nebuchadnezzar’s first and Joakim’s
third; Jeremiah in Joakim’s fourth; Ezekiel in Jeconiah’s first, which was
Nebuchadnezzar’s eighth and Joakim’s eleventh and last; while the author of
Chronicles begins the Babylonian captivity at the capture of Jerusalem,
which occurred in Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth and Zedekiah’s eleventh. Answer:
There were four partial Babylonian removals of the Jews, which constitute one
complete removal from Jerusalem’s first harassment by Nebuchadnezzar to the
final extermination of the region and city by Nebuzaradan. The first
harassment occurred as Joakim’s third year was passing, as Daniel has it, and
his fourth beginning, as Jeremiah has it, when King Joakim was put in chains
and Daniel, with part of the people and part of the temple vessels, was
carried to Babylon in Nebuchadnezzar’s first; hence Jeremiah and Daniel make
their calculations. The second removal occurred in Nebuchadnezzar’s eighth,
when Joakim had been killed in the eleventh year of his reign and his son
Jeconiah—who had returned from Babylonian chains to his Jerusalem throne four
years earlier and reigned only a few months in his father’s place—was taken to
Babylon with seven thousand Jerusalemites and ten thousand from the whole
nation, Ezekiel among them; hence Ezekiel customarily derives his numbers.
The third removal came eleven years after the second, when in
Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth and Zedekiah’s eleventh and last, Jerusalem was
captured, the temple burned, and almost the whole people were carried to
Babylon with the blinded Zedekiah. From this point the author of Kings and
Chronicles and the generality of writers begin the Babylonian captivity. The
fourth removal came four years after the third, in Nebuchadnezzar’s
twenty-third, as we have in Jeremiah 52:30, when after Gedaliah’s murder
Nebuzaradan—who had previously been commissioned by Nebuchadnezzar for the
service of burning the temple and destroying the city—carried the few remains
of the people to Babylon. No one customarily counts the years of the removal
from this transport of a few people.

### QUESTION XXXII

#### Whether there were several Babylonian captivities

Question 32. How does verse 12 of Jeremiah 52, where Jerusalem is destroyed
in Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth year, agree with verse 28 of the same chapter,
where in Nebuchadnezzar’s seventh the people are led to Babylon, and with
verse 29, where in Nebuchadnezzar’s eighteenth Zedekiah is said to migrate
with other captives? Answer: Verse 28 speaks not of Zedekiah’s captivity but
Jeconiah’s, which occurred as Nebuchadnezzar’s eighth began and his seventh
ended, namely in Joakim’s eleventh; while verses 12 and 29 speak of
Jerusalem’s destruction and Zedekiah’s captivity, which occurred as
Nebuchadnezzar’s eighteenth year ended and his nineteenth was passing, namely
during Zedekiah’s eleventh.

If you object that Jeremiah 52:28 cannot speak of Zedekiah’s captivity,
because ten thousand were then carried to Babylon, as 2 Kings 29:14 has it,
whereas only three thousand are named here, the answer is that in the cited
passage of Kings those ten thousand are divided into seven thousand and three
thousand. The former appear to comprise only Jerusalemites or soldiers of the
tribe of Judah; the latter, the remainder of Jeconiah’s army from the other
tribes. The one thousand craftsmen in that passage of Kings are included in
the seven thousand.

### QUESTION XXXIII

#### How so few—scarcely four thousand—were carried to Babylon when Jerusalem was destroyed

The question is why so few Jews underwent the Babylonian captivity, since
when the last chapter of Jeremiah enumerates by head those who came to Babylon,
whether with Jeconiah beyond the aforesaid seven thousand, or with Zedekiah,
or sent in that final captivity by Nebuzaradan, they total no more than 4,600.
Indeed, that number is round rather than exact, for if you compute the
individual totals accurately, you have only 4,570. Answer: The small number
of captives carried to Babylon arose from various causes. Many among the
people, foreseeing disaster, fled before the storm into the neighboring
regions of the Ammonites, Moabites, Idumaeans, Egyptians, and others, as may
be seen in Jeremiah 40 and following. Then divine vengeance horribly ravaged
that numerous people by sword, plague, and famine before Jerusalem was
captured, so that the small number of surviving captives is not so wonderful
as the multitude returning from Babylon some years later, begotten from those
few captives by divine blessing.

### QUESTION XXXIV

#### Whether the seventy years of captivity begin nineteen years before the temple’s destruction

The question is where the seventy years of Babylonian captivity take their
beginning. Answer: From Jeremiah 25:10–12 it appears that their beginning must
be taken from Nebuchadnezzar’s first year—whether completed or passing makes
no difference—namely nineteen years before the temple was destroyed and the
epoch before us closed. Thus after the city’s and temple’s destruction only
fifty-one of those seventy years remained, so that many could readily have
seen both temples: Solomon’s thrown down and Zorobabel’s foundations laid.

### QUESTION XXXV

#### Whether Zedekiah went to Babylon twice

Since Zedekiah was not carried to Babylon before his eleventh year, Jeremiah
52:6, how is he nevertheless said at 51:59 to have gone to Babylon in his
fourth year? Answer: Although both the old Latin and both English versions
translate the former passage of Jeremiah “with Zedekiah,” Arias Montanus in
the margin, Tremellius, and the Italian and French translators more correctly,
as it seems, render it “from Zedekiah.” For just as in chapter 29 Zedekiah sent
messengers to Nebuchadnezzar in his first year, so in the fourth year of his
reign he seems to have sent others with Serajah, the head of that embassy, on
the kingdom’s business. The English Annotators bring to the passage various
places of Scripture where the Hebrew preposition מן must be taken as “from”
or “out of,” and by no means as “with.” If these things are less pleasing, and
you judge it necessary that מן here be taken in the common way as “with,” then
we shall grant that Serajah accompanied Zedekiah toward Babylon. Nothing more
will be extracted from this, however, than that Zedekiah went to Babylon on
business of state in his fourth year, never that he was carried there captive
so early, which happened only in his eleventh.

### QUESTION XXXVI

#### Whether the same year was both the fifth and the thirtieth of the captivity

The question is how Ezekiel 1:1 agrees with verse 2 of the same chapter. The
former verse mentions the thirtieth year, and the second the fifth year of
the captivity: could one and

[Printed p. 92 | IA leaf 122 | access PDF 122 | OCR object 121]

the same year be both the thirtieth and the fifth year of the captivity?
Answer: In the second verse the captivity is explicitly mentioned; in the
first there is no word of captivity. There is therefore no doubt that these
two years are reckoned on different bases: the fifth is counted from the
first year of Jeconiah’s captivity, which coincides with the same year of
Zedekiah’s reign, just as almost all the other years mentioned by Ezekiel are
computed from this same point. But this thirtieth must plainly be referred to
another beginning, and the whole difficulty lies in finding the true starting
point of this thirtieth. There is great disagreement about it among ancient
and recent authors. Many think this thirtieth refers to the reign of
Nabopolassar, father of Nebuchadnezzar, monarch of Babylon. Others prefer to
take account of the Jewish Jubilee, as though the fifth year of the captivity
coincided with the thirtieth of the Jubilee. Others wish this thirtieth to be
understood of the Prophet’s own life, as though in the fifth year of the
captivity Ezekiel was in his thirtieth year. Finally, others—and this seems
most probable to us—wish this thirtieth to be reckoned from Josiah’s
eighteenth, in which the Book of the Law was found and the most celebrated
Passover was observed by Josiah. The memory of that solemnity had been so
memorable among the Jews that thereafter they counted their years from it.
Certainly exactly thirty years are found from that notable time to Zedekiah’s
fifth. For if you take fourteen from Josiah’s thirty-one and add Joakim’s
eleven and Zedekiah’s five, they make exactly thirty.

### QUESTION XXXVII

#### What the limits are of Ezekiel’s 390 and forty years

The question concerns the meaning of verses 4 and 6 of Ezekiel 4: what are
the limits of those 390 years during which God bore Israel’s iniquity, and of
the forty years during which God bore Judah’s iniquity? Answer: Whoever wishes
may see the great diversity of ancient and recent opinions in Henry Philip.
The solution in which most rest is this: whether the Prophet in revelation
and ecstasy lay on one side for 390 days, or actually remained in the people’s
sight in a single bodily position throughout that whole span of time and was
strengthened by divine power to endure the discomfort of this portent, he
displayed to the spectators a sign of God’s miraculous patience, by which,
pressed for so many years by his people’s sins, he finally blazed against the
sinners. “Israelites” here must be taken not restrictively of the ten tribes
alone, but extensively of the whole Hebrew nation, the twelve tribes
descended from Israel. Their greatest provocation was Jeroboam’s idolatry, by
which the ten tribes, withdrawing from David’s house, erected public idolatry
at Dan and Bethel. The earlier limit of these years must therefore be fixed in
the first year of Rehoboam and the same year of Jeroboam, and the last in
Zedekiah’s eleventh, when at Jerusalem’s destruction all the remaining
descendants of Jacob were cast out of the promised land. Exactly 390 years
elapsed between these two limits, as may be seen in the chronological tables
of Ubbo Emmius, Calvisius, and Cappellus, whom we customarily follow in our
computations; Henry Philip falls ten whole years short and Ussher four.

As for the latter total of forty years, its final limit is the same as the
last limit of the former sum, but its first limit is variously fixed. Many put
it in Josiah’s eighteenth year, when the covenant was solemnly renewed and
from which time the hypocrisy, idolatry, and other iniquity of the covenanted
people were observed more closely by the Lord. More place that limit in
Josiah’s thirteenth, when Jeremiah’s ministry began, whose light and
convictions added much weight to the people’s sins. Certainly from Josiah’s
thirteenth to Zedekiah’s eleventh exactly forty years are counted in the
reckoning of the three named above.

### QUESTION XXXVIII

#### What twenty-third year falls in Joakim’s fourth

The question concerns reconciling Jeremiah 25:1 with verse 3 of the same
chapter: namely, what is that twenty-third year which falls in Joakim’s
fourth? The answer is easy and ready. This twenty-third year is the
twenty-third of the Prophet Jeremiah’s ministry, which, beginning in Josiah’s
thirteenth, had continued for twenty-three years by Joakim’s fourth. For
Josiah reigned thirty-one years and Joahaz some months, so that before Joakim
Jeremiah had ministered in the prophetic office for nineteen years; if you
add Joakim’s fourth, the present twenty-third year of Jeremiah results.

### QUESTION XXXIX

#### Whether the revelation about putting on the yoke was given to Jeremiah in Joakim’s first, but not executed until eleven years later in Zedekiah’s fourth

The following thirty-ninth question is not as easy to solve. It concerns the
agreement of

[Printed p. 93 | IA leaf 123 | access PDF 123 | OCR object 122]

the first verse of Jeremiah 27 with verse 3. In the former passage Jeremiah is
commanded to put on the yoke at the beginning of Joakim’s reign; in the latter
Zedekiah is reigning, when the envoys of neighboring princes came to him
fourteen, or at least eleven, years after the point just mentioned. Authors
answer differently here, as may be seen in Torniellus and Henry Philip, and
especially in the later English Annotators, who report many opinions of many
people without indicating whether any one of them pleases them. Salian thinks
the name Joakim crept into the place of Zedekiah through the carelessness of
copyists of sacred Scripture. This is to cut the Gordian knot, not untie it,
and to plunge into a far greater difficulty in avoiding one. Others wish
Joakim to be a name common to all Josiah’s sons, and thus to Zedekiah as well;
since it is supported by no Scripture, it falls as an empty invention. We
would gladly admit what Torniellus and many others after Jerome do when they
refer verse 1 back to the preceding chapter, so that it has no connection
with the prophecy about the yoke and bonds. For through the inadvertence of
those who divided sacred texts and chapters into verses, what is placed at
the beginning of various chapters not seldom clearly belongs to the end of
the preceding chapters. But we are compelled to reject this solution too,
because the last word of the first verse, “saying,” necessarily imports a
connection with what follows. We therefore embrace the answer of others, who
place this revelation about the yoke and bonds at the beginning of Joakim’s
reign, but refer its execution to Zedekiah’s fourth, when the Prophet sent
yokes to the neighboring princes through their envoys, whom they had then
sent to Jerusalem and Zedekiah. It does not follow from this that the Prophet
walked yoked and in bonds for all eleven or fourteen years from Joakim’s first
to Zedekiah’s first or fourth—though even in that there would be no intolerable
absurdity. For although we grant that Jeremiah’s prophecy was revealed at the
beginning of Joakim’s reign, we do not admit that it was put into execution
before the envoys came to Jerusalem after Zedekiah began his reign.

### QUESTION XL

#### How the fourth year was the beginning of Zedekiah’s reign

The fortieth question is supplied by Jeremiah 28:1, where Hananiah the false
prophet’s outrage is said to have happened both at the beginning of Zedekiah’s
reign and in the fourth year. Could the fourth year be the beginning of
Zedekiah’s reign? Answer: There are quite a few opinions of interpreters and
chronologists on this matter. Two, however, seem principal, neither
improbable, and either may be accepted without great inconvenience. The first
asserts that the fourth year agrees well enough with the beginning of
Zedekiah’s reign, because his reign lasted eleven years, and when a period of
eleven is divided in the customary way into beginning, middle, and end, the
fourth year will belong to the beginning rather than the middle or end of that
number. If this view does not please, embrace the second: the fourth year is
referred not to Zedekiah’s reign but to Jubilees and sabbatical years, so that
Zedekiah’s first year was the fourth from a sabbatical year, and Zedekiah’s
fourth was sabbatical, just as Zedekiah’s eleventh was another sabbatical year
in which destroyed Jerusalem began to enjoy its pure and unpolluted Sabbaths.
Some, seeing that neither method of calculation is used in the Scriptures,
forge a third view: Zedekiah’s fourth is called the beginning or first of his
reign because in that year he went to Babylon to Nebuchadnezzar and obtained
a new confirmation in his kingdom and a new ἐπιστασίαν, supervision and
superintendence, over the neighboring princes. But we shall not linger to
show the fragile props supporting this third opinion.

### QUESTION XLI

#### How Jerusalem’s destruction was not heard in Babylon until seventeen months later

The question is how 2 Kings 25:6 agrees with Ezekiel 33:21. In the former
passage the city is taken in Zedekiah’s eleventh year, the fifth month, on the
seventh day of the month; in the latter the messenger concerning the captured
city comes to Ezekiel in Babylon only in the twelfth year, the tenth month,
on the fifth day of the month. How could the destruction of Jerusalem have
been hidden from the Jews in Babylon for seventeen whole months? Surely a
journey from Jerusalem to Babylon took a swift traveler only a few weeks; and
indeed the report of a far smaller calamity at Jerusalem would customarily
outrun the swiftest messengers. Nor does Nebuchadnezzar appear to have wished
to conceal the good news of his triumphs over Jerusalem from his friends in
Babylon for so long. Answer: Jerome and others after him judge that the news
of Jerusalem’s devastation was not heard in Babylon before the aforesaid
seventeen months; but this seems harsh to most interpreters. Some of these,
with Torniellus, Salian, and Henry Philip, attempt to solve the knot by a
distinction between

[Printed p. 94 | IA leaf 124 | access PDF 124 | OCR object 123]

sacred and civil years, as though what happened in the twelfth sacred year
could have occurred in the eleventh civil year. But the reasons for such a
distinction neither resolve enough nor prove anything. We therefore rest in
another answer with the recent English notes: although the Jewish disaster
reached the ears of Ezekiel and the Babylonians early, nevertheless a
distinct account of the total destruction of the city and nation—even after
Gedaliah’s murder—was not presented by an eyewitness until the stated time.
For long after events have occurred and confused reports have spread, precise
knowledge of the circumstances is customarily communicated by someone who
was present to nations far away.

### QUESTION XLII

#### How Nebuchadnezzar saw the statue in both his second and fourth year

The question is how Daniel 1:5 is reconciled with 2:1. In the latter passage
Nebuchadnezzar saw the statue in his second year; from the former it appears
that this dream came to Nebuchadnezzar only after the fourth. For Daniel did
not stand among Babylon’s wise men in the king’s sight before he had devoted
three full years after Nebuchadnezzar’s first—when he was taken captive from
Judea to Babylon in Joakim’s third or fourth—to private study of Chaldean
learning. Answer: After Jerome, the chief Roman Catholic chronologists explain
verse 1 of chapter 2 not of Nebuchadnezzar’s reign but of his monarchy. They
say chapter 1 speaks of the reign in which Nebuchadnezzar sat on the
Babylonian throne after his father in Joakim’s third, but suppose that he
began the Babylonian monarchy many years after this beginning of his reign,
namely after subduing Egypt and all neighboring regions. Torniellus thinks
this happened no earlier than the twenty-third year of his reign, Henry
Philip no earlier than the thirty-fifth; in any case they wish the second
year mentioned by Daniel to be taken as the second of the monarchy, not of
the reign. I would rest in this view were it not inconvenient to make Daniel
an obscure man unknown to Nebuchadnezzar for thirty whole years, or at least
twenty, after his captivity, as this view necessarily does. For in the year
they call the second of the monarchy and the twenty-third or thirty-fifth of
the reign, when the king saw the statue in his dream, Daniel was scarcely
known to him even by name. Furthermore, if the Chaldean monarchy and the
growth of its capital did not begin before Nebuchadnezzar’s thirty-fifth, this
monarchy lasted so short a span that it had almost no proportion to the
durations of the following three, Persian, Greek, and Roman; yet the generality
of writers represents the Chaldean monarchy, the first of the others, as by
far the longest of all. Pressed by these difficulties, we incline to Junius’s
second view, followed by Deodati and both sets of English Annotators: the
second year before us is to be referred neither to Nebuchadnezzar’s reign nor
monarchy, but to another beginning, namely the time when, after three years
of private study, Daniel began to stand with his companions in the king’s
sight, which fell in Nebuchadnezzar’s fifth and Joakim’s ninth. A single
objection presses this view: the sacred text explicitly calls the year in
question the second year of Nebuchadnezzar’s reign. Answer: The Latin, French,
and both old and new English translators do indeed render the passage so; but
the Hebrew source quite conveniently bears Junius’s and Deodati’s
interpretation, namely “in the second year,” not “of the reign” but “while he
reigned” or “in the kingdom,” *Lemalcuth*. This exposition easily destroys
the objection without evasion.

### QUESTION XLIII

#### Whether there was only one Nebuchadnezzar, son of Nabopolassar, not Nabonassar

The question is who that Nebuchadnezzar so often mentioned by the Prophets
was, and how he is to be distinguished from Nabonassar and Nabopolassar, about
whom profane authors have so much to say. Answer: Some ancient as well as
recent writers make two Nebuchadnezzars, father and son bearing the same name;
but this multiplication, supported by no necessary foundation, is rejected
by most of the most learned and judicious chronologists and interpreters of
Scripture. They attribute to Nabopolassar, Nebuchadnezzar’s father, what is
ascribed to the first Nebuchadnezzar. Sacred Scripture says nothing of
Nabopolassar, but profane authors say many things that fit none other than
Nebuchadnezzar’s father.

Nabonassar’s is a most celebrated name in foreign history, not because
anything is read there concerning his deeds, but because Ptolemy—easily the
chief chronologist and mathematician in the days of the Emperor Antoninus—
constructed the Canon he called Mathematical by most accurately coordinating
the histories and chronological calculations of the Chaldeans, Egyptians,
Greeks, and Romans; its head he placed in

[Printed p. 95 | IA leaf 125 | access PDF 125 | OCR object 124]

Nabonassar’s first year. This man was king of Babylon in Hezekiah’s days,
according to some Merodach, according to others Baladan, either the very man
or his father who congratulated Hezekiah on the recovery of his health. Before
this time Ptolemy, a most diligent investigator of antiquity, found nothing
certain in ancient monuments on which to set his foot. He either did not know
the Jews’ sacred books or, as a pagan, despised them. But from Nabonassar to
his own times he composed a table of times which all chronologists adore as a
most precious *κειμήλιον*, treasure, almost as an aegis fallen from heaven
from Pallas; of this, more in its proper place. In this table he arranges the
sequence and times of all the rulers among the Chaldeans, Persians, Greeks,
and Romans from Nabonassar to Antoninus, far more accurately than any
chronologist before or after him, until one reaches our own age, when the
incomparable excellence of many in every science has pierced the eyes of the
crows, thrown all sixty-year-olds from the bridge, and long since obscured
what were once the greatest lights with its new and wonderful brilliance.
This Nabonassar, the first of the Chaldean monarchs at the head of Ptolemy’s
Canon, set a hundred and some years before Nabopolassar and his son
Nebuchadnezzar, became a highly celebrated epochal marker.

### QUESTION XLIV

#### What the limits of Isaiah’s prophecy are

The question is: what was the Prophet Isaiah’s period? Answer: The limits of
either this Prophet’s life or his prophesying are not certain. We refuted
above the fact that many extend not only his life but his prophecy more than
130 years, from the beginnings of Uzziah to the middle of Manasseh. It is
certain that both this Prophet’s life and his ministry were long. If we grant
the strictest limits that can be conceived for his ministry, it will occupy
more than forty-five years. For he served God and the Church in the days of
Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah. Grant that he began no earlier than
Uzziah’s last, and continued no later than Hezekiah’s fifteenth, when he
entreated God against Sennacherib; these limits certainly exceed forty-five
years. For the rest, it is not known how long before Uzziah’s last year,
mentioned in chapter 6, and Hezekiah’s fifteenth, mentioned in chapter 36 and
following, he acted as Prophet; nor should anyone here, relying on conjectures,
thrust his own pronouncements upon others.

### QUESTION XLV

#### The periods of Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Daniel

The question is: what were the periods of the next three major Prophets,
Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Daniel? Answer: Jeremiah began to discharge his office
a full century after Isaiah, in Josiah’s thirteenth, as he himself teaches in
his chapter 25. From that time he continued more than forty years in ministry,
for forty years flowed from Josiah’s thirteenth to the end of the epoch and
the temple’s destruction. After that time Jeremiah remained for a while in
Judea with Gedaliah, and later was dragged by Johanan into Egypt with the
remnants of the people. There too he acted as Prophet for a time, but the
sacred writings do not make clear for how many years. We cannot admit without
any proof what Epiphanius has, and after him many Roman Catholics, concerning
Jeremiah prophesying in Egypt for three, four, or more years, and finally
sealing his testimony and martyrdom in blood when a furious people, raging in
its lust for idols, buried him beneath stones; we do not dwell on traditions,
whether Jewish or Roman Catholic.

As for Ezekiel, he began his prophetic ministry in the fifth year after he
was deported to Babylon with King Jeconiah, as we have in chapter 1:2,
thirty-five years after Jeremiah began to prophesy and six years before
Jerusalem was overthrown. Thus he was Jeremiah’s σύγχρονος, contemporary, for
seven or eight years, preaching in Babylon the same divine truth that
Jeremiah preached in Jerusalem and Egypt. He continued in his ministry until
the twenty-seventh year of Jeconiah’s removal, as we read in chapter 29:17.
How long he lived or prophesied afterward is not established from Scripture.
It does appear from certain points that, as he began thirty-five years after
Jeremiah and served the Jewish Church with him for six or seven years, so he
continued for thirteen or fourteen years after him, completing a course of at
least twenty-two years in his office.

As for Daniel, he was taken to Babylon in Joakim’s third or fourth,
Nebuchadnezzar’s first, as appears from Daniel 1:2 compared with Jeremiah 25.
He continued in the prophetic office until Cyrus’s third year, as we read in
chapter 10:1. At least seventy-four years passed between Nebuchadnezzar’s
first and Cyrus’s third. Thus he was taken to Babylon thirteen years before
Ezekiel and acted as Prophet for many years after him. How long he extended
his life after

[Printed p. 96 | IA leaf 126 | access PDF 126 | OCR object 125]

Cyrus’s third year Scripture is silent; only this is certain: just as he
served the Church for some years with Jeremiah, so he did for more with
Ezekiel.

### QUESTION XLVI

#### Whether sacred chronology refutes the history of Susanna

The question is whether the apocryphal story of Susanna transmitted in Daniel
13 can agree with sacred chronology. Answer: Not at all. It is important for
liars and tellers of tales to be very mindful, for to the perceptive a tiny
crack is often a broad window for exposing and exploding the whole falsehood
which they thought they had woven for themselves so excellently. Besides
other arguments for convicting the tale before us of falsehood, this single
chronological demonstration is enough. Verses 45 and 65 of this chapter 13,
when compared, make Daniel a boy when Cyrus began to reign. But the true
chronology from various canonical Scriptures, as appears from the preceding,
does not allow Daniel to have been younger than eighty in even Cyrus’s first
year; these things cannot stand together. Bellarmine and other Roman Catholics,
in order to reconcile this harsh ἐναντιοφάνειαν, apparent contradiction, are
forced to invent two Daniels: one who had lived with Nebuchadnezzar and by
Cyrus’s beginning had reached an advanced old age; and another who only
entered early adolescence in Cyrus’s reign and about whom the story of
Susanna is told. But Torniellus and most other Roman Catholics dislike this
multiplication of Daniel, since it is supported by the testimony of no
ancient author, sacred or profane. They therefore shore up this apocryphal
building with another prop: they say chapter 13 is placed outside its proper
location by a *hysteron proteron* and ought to stand before chapter 1 in the
true history, for the story of Susanna occurred before Daniel came into
Nebuchadnezzar’s sight. As for our reading in chapter 13 that Daniel did these
things as a young man after Astyages’s death, when Cyrus began his reign, they
say this belongs not to chapter 13 but is the beginning of chapter 14. We
reply: grant that by a *hysteron proteron* chapter 13 is placed before the
first, and admit this ἀναβολὴν, transposition, in the sacred writer without any
reason. Furthermore, let us admit that those dividing this book do so ἀκριβῶς,
accurately, when they separate verse 63 of chapter 13 from chapter 14, whose
beginning it
should have been. So let us grant all this at the mere pleasure of the
patrons of apocryphal Scripture. But we want them to answer how that verse 63
of chapter 13 could be read together with the first of chapter 14 without the
crudest tautology. What writer in his right mind, without any προφάσει,
pretext, would repeat the same thing by the same words at the same moment? Why
was it
necessary to narrate in the same breath and the same words that when Astyages
died Cyrus occupied the throne? Therefore, having dismissed this sophism too,
let us say openly that the story of Susanna, falsely titled as Daniel’s, is
fabulous, trifling, and invented by the Jews—people most vain whenever they
depart from Scripture.

### QUESTION XLVII

#### Whether chronology refutes the history of Bel and the Dragon

The question is whether the history of Bel and the Dragon attached to Daniel
as chapter 14 agrees with chronological reasoning. Answer: Sacred chronology
also demonstrates these supplements to be apocryphal. Besides the fact that
the Prophet Habakkuk, who issued his prophecies before the temple was
overthrown, could not by age have been fit in the time of Cyrus the Persian
for rustic ministry to reapers—so that the patrons of the Apocrypha must
invent, as another Daniel, so another Habakkuk different from the Prophet—
Torniellus after others admits that this Cyrus, whom that other Daniel is said
to have turned from the worship of Bel and the Dragon, was not the Persian
but a Babylonian, Evilmerodach, son of Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar's maternal
forebear.
For the Persian kings worshiped the Sun and stars but are said to have
abhorred images and idols, especially the Chaldeans’ Bel. However these things
stand, it seems the harshest difficulty, without any sacred or profane
authority, to transubstantiate Cyrus the Persian into Evilmerodach the
Chaldean. And if anyone happens to possess the stomach of an ostrich, which
easily digests iron absurdities, which Daniel is it that is nevertheless cast
into the lions’ den a second or even third time, and so often miraculously
rescued from it? Are miracles so readily to be multiplied at mortals’ whim?
The true Daniel in chapter 6 relates that he was cast into the lions’ den by
Darius the Mede, lest the decrees of the Medes be violated; but the
apocryphal Daniel in chapter 14 declares that after Darius the Mede, or
Astyages, died, Cyrus cast him in. Torniellus and others, however, in order to
conceal the Apocrypha’s falsehoods, pretend he was cast in by Evilmerodach,
Belshazzar’s father, long before either Darius the Mede’s or Cyrus the
Persian’s time, stuffing one absurdity, as often happens, with another greater
absurdity. Thus either one Daniel or three Daniels were cast three times into
the lions’ den by Evilmerodach, Darius, and Cyrus at different intervals—
something

[Printed p. 97 | IA leaf 127 | access PDF 127 | OCR object 126]

no one would readily admit to sustain the apocryphal writer’s authority.

### QUESTION XLVIII

#### Whether chronology can convict the book of Tobit of being apocryphal

Question 48. Can the book of Tobit also be convicted from chronology of being
apocryphal? Answer: So indeed it appears, if you compare chapter 1, verses 2
and 11, with chapter 14, verse 15. From these joined together it follows that
Tobias the Younger was not merely born but had advanced to an age at which he
could be carried from Israel to Nineveh in the days of Shalmaneser. It is
therefore necessary to suppose that Tobias the Younger was two years old in
the final disaster with which Shalmaneser afflicted the Israelites, namely in
Hezekiah’s sixth, when Samaria was destroyed, and that with the ten tribes
Tobias the Elder, his wife, and son were carried captive. From that time he
lived until Nebuchadnezzar destroyed Nineveh, which you may suppose occurred
in Nebuchadnezzar’s first or Joakim’s third. Tobias the Younger thus lived
from Hezekiah’s fourth to Joakim’s fourth, that is, somewhat more than one
hundred years, to an age no mortal in that century customarily reached.
Hezekiah reigned twenty-five years after his fourth; Manasseh fifty-five;
Amon two; Josiah thirty-one; Joakim four: joined, these make 117. Many Roman
Catholics add still more. This longevity without an extraordinary cause,
together with the many other arguments customarily offered here, demonstrates
that the book of Tobit is apocryphal.

### QUESTION XLIX

#### Whether Judith’s history is refuted by chronology

The question is whether anything can be produced from chronology to convict
the book of Judith of error. Answer: It matters little to us to what period
the apocryphal tale of Judith is assigned. We are only confirmed in rejecting
such trifles when we see the most learned Roman Catholics sweat in vain to
reconcile this tale with sacred chronology. They have two principal opinions.
The first, that of Salian, Bellarmine, and others, holds that the
Nebuchadnezzar mentioned in Judith, who killed Arphaxad king of the Medes and
administered the Assyrian kingdom in the city of Nineveh, was Merodach king
of Babylon, who lived in the days of Hezekiah and Manasseh, kings of Judah.
According to them Judith’s history occurred before the Babylonian captivity.
But Torniellus, Henry Philip, and others place Judith after the return from
that captivity, and contend that Nebuchadnezzar was either Xerxes himself or
some Assyrian prince in Xerxes’s days. The authors of these two views rightly
refute one another’s positions but are utterly unable to establish their own.
The latter rightly demonstrate that no Nebuchadnezzar appears in either
sacred or profane history in Hezekiah’s time; that the temple had not then
been overthrown nor the Jews carried away, much less returned from captivity;
and that the Jews had not enjoyed the profound peace of more than one hundred
years since Manasseh’s time—all things the compiler of the apocryphal tale
babbles. The former no less forcefully destroy the latter’s view, asserting
most truly that Nineveh was destroyed long before Xerxes the Persian; that in
Xerxes’s time there was no Nebuchadnezzar and then no prince in Assyria who
could have led so great an army into the dominions of the Persian monarch;
and that no Arphaxad ever reigned among the Medes. That exceedingly ignorant
trifler, ignorant of all sacred and profane history and chronology alike,
cannot be defended from his very many rotten errors.

### QUESTION L

#### The periods of Hosea, Joel, Amos, and Obadiah

Question fifty. How are the periods of the first four of the twelve minor
Prophets—Hosea, Joel, Amos, and Obadiah—to be distributed? Answer: As for
Hosea, we dare not affirm with some ancient and recent writers from verse 3
of chapter 1 that the word of the Lord came to him at the beginning of all the
other Prophets who recorded their prophecies, and thus that he preceded—even
if not Elijah, Elisha, Gad, and others who wrote no books—nevertheless Isaiah
and the other writers of prophecies. This seems to us too great a twisting of
the passage, which means nothing more than that what follows in that chapter
was the first of the prophecies God’s Spirit revealed to this Prophet. We do,
however, readily admit from verse 2 of the same chapter that Isaiah was
altogether his contemporary, since he lived under the same kings, Uzziah,
Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah. But in which year of Uzziah he began and in which
of Hezekiah he ended is no more established for Hosea than for Isaiah. Only
this is clear concerning Hosea: he began before the end of Jeroboam II,
chapter 1:2. Jeroboam’s son Zechariah began to reign in Uzziah’s thirty-eighth
year, 2 Kings 15:8, that is, fourteen years before Uzziah’s death; indeed, if
with most chronologists we place an interregnum of twelve years between
Jeroboam and

[Printed p. 98 | IA leaf 128 | access PDF 128 | OCR object 127]

his son Zechariah, so that Uzziah lived twenty-six years after Jeroboam—which
a comparison of 2 Kings 14:23 with 15:1 and 8 seems to compel—then Hosea
began to prophesy at least twenty-six years before Uzziah’s death. So early a
beginning of Isaiah’s prophecies does not seem likely. No one has precisely
stated when Hosea ended his ministry; nevertheless it appears that he endured
in his office more than fifty years, or at least more than forty-three.

We have nothing explicit about Joel’s period. Jerome’s rule is at least not
improbable here: every Prophet whose times are not expressed should be
referred to the limits of the preceding Prophet whose years are expressed.
Certainly most interpreters make Joel σύγχρονον, contemporary, with Hosea and
Isaiah, nor does the subject of the prophecy appear to resist this. Augustine’s
view, though it has some followers, does not please us: that Joel was Elisha’s
contemporary because he foretold the famine Elisha saw in his days at Samaria.
There is no necessity to confound Joel’s famine with Elisha’s.

Concerning Amos it is clear that he was Isaiah’s and Hosea’s contemporary,
since chapter 1, verse 1 explicitly says that he prophesied in the days of
Uzziah and Jeroboam, two years before the earthquake. But in what year that
earthquake occurred—whether, as Augustine and many others say, in Uzziah’s
twenty-seventh, at the moment the king was struck with leprosy in the temple—
we dare not affirm without Scripture.

Nothing is explicitly stated in sacred Scripture about Obadiah’s period. Most,
following Jerome’s rule, make him σύγχρονον, contemporary, with Amos and thus
with Joel, Hosea, and Isaiah. Some wish him to be the same as Obadiah, Ahab’s
servant, who spoke with Elijah. But the disaster inflicted on the Idumaeans,
which is the subject of this prophecy, does not allow this seer to be so
ancient. Why otherwise would he have been silent about the Jews’ other
disasters, which occurred several times after Ahab but before the Chaldean
arms? Junius’s reckoning seems to us most true, referring Obadiah to the time
of Jeremiah and Ezekiel, because the destruction he denounces against the
Idumaeans is on account of their service hired by the Chaldeans in Zion’s
overthrow. Obadiah does not so much prophesy about this as speak historically
of an event already done, for which he threatens the Idumaeans with
extermination.

### QUESTION LI

#### What should be held concerning the periods of Jonah and Nahum and the destruction of Nineveh

Answer: We have joined these three in one question because the Prophets Jonah
and Nahum have Nineveh’s overthrow as their subject. Nineveh, the capital of
the Assyrian monarchy, was in earlier centuries by far the largest and most
fortified of cities on earth; but it was so horribly overthrown by divine
vengeance that for a long time even learned men have had scarcely anything
certain about its location and site. Bochart indeed attempts to demonstrate
very learnedly, as he does everything, in his *Phaleg*, book 4, chapter 20,
that it was situated on the eastern side of the Tigris below the Lycus,
opposite modern Mosul. This city was captured and severely punished more than
once, but finally by Cyaxares the Mede and his son-in-law Nebuchadnezzar in
the latter’s first year, and thus Joakim’s fourth, when after the Assyrians
were subdued Ecbatana of the Medes and Babylon of the Chaldeans became
capitals of empire. The Prophet Jonah foretold Nineveh’s ruin long before
Nahum. For Jonah was prophesying either before or at the beginning of
Jeroboam II, as we have explicitly in 2 Kings 14:25, where Jonah son of
Amittai is reported to have foretold Jeroboam’s victories over the Syrians.
He must therefore have lived either before or at the beginning of Jeroboam’s
reign, whose last years coincided with Uzziah king of Judah’s first, when
neither Isaiah nor Hosea was yet acting as a Prophet. It follows that the
Prophet Jonah was older than these and all their συγχρόνοις, contemporaries.
We can by no means concede with Epiphanius, however, that this Jonah was the
son of the woman of Zarephath whom Elijah raised from the dead. Besides the
fact that the periods do not agree, that widow of Zarephath was a Sidonian
from the Gentiles, while Jonah son of Amittai explicitly professes in his
second chapter that he is Hebrew in nation and lineage.

As for the Prophet Nahum, no circumstance of time is recorded in his book;
but from the prophecy’s subject we gather that he lived after the ten tribes’
deportation and before Nineveh’s overthrow. For he speaks of Nineveh as to be
overthrown, not yet overthrown, and that on account of the Assyrians’ cruelty
to the deported Israelites. He must therefore be considered Jeremiah’s
contemporary and later than Isaiah.

### QUESTION LII

#### How the periods of Micah, Habakkuk, and Zephaniah are to be distributed

Answer: The matter concerning Micah is clear. Although the precise year in
which he began or ceased to prophesy is not established, nevertheless he
confines the limits of his ministry to the reigns of Jotham,

Ahaz, and Hezekiah. Thus he was a contemporary of Isaiah, Hosea, and the
others who served under the same kings, and therefore he could not have been
that Micaiah who in the time of Ahab and Jehoshaphat, against all the false
prophets, announced the king’s death at Ramoth-gilead. Granted that the names
are the same, the interval between the periods is nevertheless so great that
the same man could not have acted as Prophet in the days of Jehoshaphat and
Hezekiah.

As for Habakkuk, although no indication of time is expressed in his writing,
its subject shows that he poured out his prophecies not only before the
Chaldean empire was overthrown by the Persians, but also before Jerusalem was
destroyed, for he speaks of both events as future, not past. He therefore
seems Jeremiah’s contemporary. Nor, as the Roman Catholics themselves admit,
could he have been that Habakkuk who, more than seventy years after Jerusalem
was overthrown, while carrying stew to the reapers in the strength of his
prime, was transported by an angel to feed Daniel in the lions’ pit, as the
Apocrypha tells the tale.

Zephaniah professes that he prophesied in Josiah’s days, and so he was
Jeremiah’s contemporary and older than Ezekiel and Daniel.
