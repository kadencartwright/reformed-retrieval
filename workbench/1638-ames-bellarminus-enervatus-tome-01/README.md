# William Ames, *Bellarminus enervatus*, Tome I (1638)

> **Working edition in progress:** This directory contains an AI-assisted,
> diplomatic-normalized Latin transcription and independent close English
> translation. Both are unreviewed working drafts, not a critical edition or
> publication-ready text. A qualified human editor must compare both files
> with the facsimile before quotation or publication.

## Work and edition

- **Author:** William Ames (1576–1633)
- **Title:** *Bellarminus enervatus*, divided into four tomes
- **Edition:** Amsterdam, Johannes Janssonius, 1638
- **Edition statement:** *Editio ab auctore novissime ante obitum recognita &
  aucta*—the edition most recently revised and enlarged by the author before
  his death
- **Tome I:** Book I, *De verbo Dei*; Book II, *De Christo*; Book III, *De
  Pontifice Romano*

The text alternates abbreviated reports of Robert Bellarmine's claims (`Bell.`)
with Ames's Protestant replies (`Prot.`). Those speaker labels and Bellarmine's
quoted or closely reported matter are authorially integral to Ames's argument
and are retained.

## Editorial method and status

historical spelling, authorial numbering, headings, citations, and Greek are
retained. Printer abbreviations are expanded silently when unambiguous.
Page-crossing words are joined and placed after the new page marker. The
edition's erroneous point numbering on printed p. 10 (`6` before `5`) is

74 have likewise been checked against both copies.

### CAPUT III.

##### De Monarchia Pontificis Romani, Lib. 2.

Quaestio est, an Romanus Pontifex, tanquam Petri successor, supremam vel
Monarchicam, in Ecclesia universa, potestatem habeat, jure divino? Bell.
affirmat, nos negamus.

##### THESIS I.

##### Argumenta Bellarmini

Bellarm. *Fundatur jus successionis Pontificum Romanorum, in eo, quod Petrus
Roma suam sedem, jubente Domino, collocaverit, atque ibidem usque ad mortem
sederit.* Prot. Fundatur igitur, 1. Non in jure, sed in facto. 2. In tali facto
Petri, quod in scripturis non narratur, neque ex illis potest ullo modo probari.
3. In tali facto quod non constituit jus humanum, nedum divinum. 4. In tali
facto, quale vim hujusmodi juris communicandi habere negant doctissimi
Pontificiorum. Nam Scotus in 4. dist. 24. Cordubensis, lib. 4. q. 1. Cajetanus,
de primat. Papae, cap. 23. Bannes in 2. 2. q. 1. a. 10. contendunt per accidens
tantum conjungi Episcopatum orbis & urbis, non jure divino, aut imperio
Christi, ita ut jure divino Episcopus Romanus non sit Petri successor, sed
possit eligi in successorem Petri, Episcopus Parisiensis, aut quilibet alius.
Hanc etiam sententiam errorem esse in fide, aut cum fide pugnare, negat
Azorius, Jesuita, Inst. mor. p. 2. l. 4. c. 6. 12. Jus igitur Pontificum
Romanorum non est de fide, neque nititur jure divino, tanquam fundamento: sed
facto omni ex parte saltem incerto. Incertum est enim 1. An Petrus unquam
fuerit Roma? 2. Incertum etiam an sit mortuus Romae. 3. Certum est, eum non
fuisse, propriè loquendo, Episcopum Romae. 4. Illud etiam

certissimum eum Pontificem Romanum non constituisse sibi in eodem munere quo
ille functus est, & cum parili authoritate successorem.

2. Bellarm. *Petrum fuisse Romae, apparet, 1. Ex eo quod fuit in Babylone. 1.
Pet. 5.13. Roma autem, in Apocalypsi passim vocatur Babylon. 2. Quod Romae fuit
Ecclesia collecta, antequam Paulus eo veniret: quis autem illam Ecclesiam
collegit: si Petrus non fuit Romae? 3. Ex historia de Euangelio Marci, qui
Romae scripsit, quod Petrus ibi praedicavit. 4. Ex historia de Simone Mago.*
Prot. Utrum Petrus fuerit Romae necne, non magnopere contendimus, aut
laboramus: sed incertum hoc dicimus in Scripturis relinqui, neque istis
argumentis evinci. Nam 1. Babylon quamvis in typicis visionibus Apocalypticis,
Romam designet, non sequitur tamen inde quod in simplici salutatione
epistolica, ubi neque ratio, neque exemplum est istiusmodi tropi, eo sensu
capiatur. Paulus & Lucas passim loquuntur de Roma, & nusquam tamen Babylonem
appellant. 2. Ecclesia potuit ibi colligi, antequam vel Paulus, vel Petrus eo
venerint eodem modo quo fuit collecta Samaria, atque etiam Antiochiae, quem
alterum faciunt Episcopatum Petri. Illud certè testatum habemus, Actor. 8.4.5.

##### Conjectura facientes adversus Petri sedem Romae collocatam

5. Protest. Authores hujus narrationis non consentiunt inter se de tempore quo
Petrus eo venerit, & ibi manserit, neque de successoribus ejus. Bellarm. 1.
*Discordia de tempore alicujus rei gestae, non infirmat ipsius rei veritatem:
sicut apparet in varietate sententiarum, de tempore mortis Christi, & de

redditur incerta: sed cum est incerta, incertior per eam vel improbabilior
evadit. Primi authores historiae Christi, Matt. Marcus, Lucas, Johannes, de
tempore mortis ejus non dissentiunt: sed primi authores hujus narrationis, qui
saltem nobis noti sunt, & variant inter se, & à Scripturis discedunt. Bell. 2.
*Nulla est inter bonos authores*

Claudii, & ibi sedisse annis 25. ac obiisse ann. 14. Neronis.* Prot. 1. Non ita

Aquin. in Gal. 2. Neque ipse quidem Damasus in Pontificali: ergo non omnes
authores boni. 2. Qui ita statuunt, dissentiunt à Scriptura Sacra, ut postea

praecesserit in Episcopatu Romano.* Quia autem iidem testes de quorundam
successione, & Petri praecessione simul testantur, ex infirmatione unius
partis, totum testimonium infirmatur. Bell. 4. *Tota res ita componitur: Petrus
sedem Episcopalem reliquit Clementi: Clemens autem, Petro mortuo, ex humilitate

alium successorem? 7. Tota res melius & certius componitur ex Scriptura: quod

scilicet quemadmodum Paulus Ecclesiae Ephesinae curam non uni alicui Domino
Episcopo, sed variis Presbyteris qui & Episcopi fuerunt, reliquit, Act.

9. Prot. Paulus cum Româ scribebat varias Epistolas, etiam Nerone imperante
(quando Petrum eo rediisse dicunt) & quamvis aliorum, inferioris notae

creditur tribus testibus affirmantibus, quam mille nihil dicentibus.* Prot. 1.
Argumentum hoc non tantum petitur ab auctoritate negativè, sed & à minore ad
majus. Mentionem fecit Paulus aliorum quos minus attinebat commemorari: ergo de

locis citatis ex Phil. Col. & Tim. 3. Testimonium negativum est vel merè
negativum, vel etiam privativum, id est, non affirmat ubi debet affirmare, si
res ita se haberet. *Hoc genus argumenti efficacissimum est* (ait Medina in
Thom. p. 3. q. 1. a. 3.) *Nam hoc genere argumentationis colligitur, tantum
unum mundum esse factum & solum filium assumpsisse carnem humanam: licet non
dicatur in Sacra Scriptura, alios mundos non fuisse factos à Deo, neque vero
quod alia persona non assumpserit carnem humanam.* 4. Testes affirmantes, ut
majorem fidem mereantur quam nihil dicentes, debent esse similis fidei &
authoritatis cum

Hactenus fundamentum vidimus Monarchiae Papalis: jam videndum est, quomodo

10. Bell. *Oportet Petro aliquem succedere in Pontificatu Ecclesiae universae,
ex divino jure.* Prot. 1. Sicut Petrus non alium habuit Pontificatum Ecclesiae
universae quam Paulus; sic neque magis quam Paulus successorem in illo debet
habere. 2. Si successio concedatur, non est igitur necessarium, ut unus aliquis
in solidum, quasi haeres ejus ex asse, sit Petri successor. Imperatori in
initio uni succedebant postea duo, unus in Oriente, & alter in Occidente.
Fuerunt etiam inter Scholasticos, qui docuerunt Ecclesiam posse simul duos
summos Pontifices creare, & habere, ut Armacanus de quaestio. Arm. lib. 7. c.
17. & Occham in Dialog. part. 3. ad finem. Si autem duos, cur non & quot sunt
Archiepiscopi, vel Episcopi Dioecesani? 3. Jus divinum est quod à Deo
promulgatur. Thom. 2.2. q. 57. art. 2. ad 3. Sed ut unus aliquis succederet
Petro in Ecclesiae Pontificatu, nullibi à Deo promulgatur. Haec igitur
successio non nititur jure divino. 4. Rationes quibus haec propositio à Bellar.
probatur, sunt quisquiliae nugarum antea satis explosarum: 1. *Pontifex est
propter Ecclesiam: Ecclesia autem non minus nunc eget uno Pastore quam olim.*
Prophetae etiam fuerunt propter Ecclesiam: & Ecclesia (si hominum judicio
standum sit) non minus nunc eget variis Prophetis quam olim. Prophetia tamen
non est per successionem continuata. 2. *Ecclesia est una: non debet igitur
mutari forma regiminis.* Ecclesia non est una externa aliqua combinatione in
unam aliquam singularem societatem. Sic enim Scriptura ubique loquitur de
*Ecclesiis*. 3. *Dominus dixit Petro, pasce oves meas: officium autem pastoris,
est ordinarium & perpetuum, dum manet ovile.* Dixit etiam omnibus Apostolis
praedicate & baptizate: sed officium praedicandi & baptizandi est ordinarium &
perpetuum, dum manet Ecclesia. 4. *Dominus omnes oves Petro commisit, non solum
ratione loci, sed etiam ratione temporis.* Omnes etiam

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 181 | Augsburg canvas 195 | printed p. 171 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

gentes omnibus Apostolis commisit, usque ad consummationem saeculi, Mat.
28.19.20. 5. *Ecclesia habet caput praeter Christum.* 1. Cor. 12.21. Eadem
etiam ratione, ex eodem loco probatur, Ecclesiam habere unam *manum*, & duos
*pedes*. 6. *Sacerdotium veteris Testamenti figura fuit sacerdotii novi
Testamenti, & successio illius, successionis hujus figura.* Fuit figura Christi
unici Sacerdotis nostri, qui nullum habet successorem. Successio igitur non
fuit figura successionis, sed continuationis aeternae.

ipsius dignitate, & potestate. Nullus enim fuit unquam qui se Petri successorem
ullo modo asseruerit, vel pro tali sit habitus, praeter Romanum & Antiochenum
Episcopum: sed Antiochenus non successit Petro in Pontificatu Ecclesiae totius:
ergo Romanus.* Prot. 1. Si Pontifex Romanus successit Petro, in tota ipsius
dignitate & potestate, tum Linus, Cletus, Clemens, vel quicunque primus fuit
Episcopus Romanus, dignitate & potestate, supra Johannem & reliquos Apostolos
qui Petro supervivebant, tanquam eorum Pastor & caput fuit constitutus. 2.
Petrus potestatem habuit ejiciendi daemonia, linguis loquendi novis, serpentes
tollendi, lethalia bibendi sine noxa & aegrotos sanandi, Matt. 10.1. Luc. 9.1.
Mar. 16.17. Habuit etiam illam dignitatem, ut ejus Epistolae partes sint
Canonicae Scripturae: nunquid ista conveniunt Pontifici Romano? 3. Si nemo
praeter Papam, asseruerit se Petri successorem, eo modo quo ille; hoc inde
fuit, quod haec impudentia propria debet esse Meretricis illius magnae. Cum
tamen alii fuerunt qui dictitabant se Apostolos esse, Apoc. 2.2. & Montanus
etiam se Paracletum esse à Christo promissum jactitabat, Pontifici Romano ne
istam quidem gloriam reliquerunt, ut solus sibi summam potestatem in Ecclesia
vindicaret. 4. Non satis est, ut hoc asserat Pontifex Romanus, aut talis etiam
à quibusdam habeatur, nisi possit ostendere, hoc sibi deberi jure divino.

### Chapter III

##### On the Monarchy of the Roman Pontiff, Book 2

The question is whether the Roman Pontiff, as Peter's successor, possesses by
divine right supreme or monarchical power in the universal Church. Bellarmine
affirms it; we deny it.

##### Thesis I

##### Bellarmine's arguments

Bell.: *The right of succession of the Roman Pontiffs rests upon the fact that
Peter, at the Lord's command, established his see at Rome and remained there
until his death.* Prot. It therefore rests: 1. Not upon a right, but upon a
deed. 2. Upon such a deed of Peter as is not related in the Scriptures and
cannot in any way be proved from them. 3. Upon a deed which establishes no
human right, much less a divine one. 4. Upon a deed which the most learned of
the Papists deny has the power to confer a right of this kind. For Scotus on
book 4, distinction 24; Cordubensis, book 4, question 1; Cajetan, *On the
Primacy of the Pope*, chapter 23; and Báñez on 2a2ae, question 1, article 10,
contend that the episcopate of the world and that of the city are joined only
incidentally, not by divine right or Christ's command. Thus, by divine right,
the Roman Bishop is not Peter's successor; the Bishop of Paris, or any other
bishop, could instead be chosen as Peter's successor. Azor the Jesuit also
denies that this opinion is an error in the faith or conflicts with the faith,
*Moral Institutes*, part 2, book 4, chapter 6, section 12. The right of the
Roman Pontiffs is therefore not an article of faith, nor does it rest upon
divine right as its foundation, but upon a deed that is, at the very least,
uncertain in every respect. For it is uncertain: 1. Whether Peter was ever at
Rome. 2. Whether he
also died at Rome. 3. It is certain that, properly speaking, he was not Bishop
of Rome. 4. It is also

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 171 | Augsburg canvas 185 | printed p. 161 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

absolutely certain that he did not appoint a Roman Pontiff to succeed him in
the same office which he discharged and with equal authority.

2. Bell.: *That Peter was at Rome is evident: 1. From the fact that he was in
Babylon, 1 Peter 5:13, while Rome is everywhere called Babylon in Revelation.
2. From the fact that there was a gathered church at Rome before Paul came
there. Who, then, gathered that church if Peter was not at Rome? 3. From the
account of Mark's Gospel, which Mark wrote at Rome from what Peter preached
there. 4. From the account of Simon Magus.* Prot. We neither contend nor labor
greatly over whether Peter was at Rome or not. We say, however, that Scripture
leaves this uncertain and that these arguments do not establish it. For: 1.
Although Babylon designates Rome in Revelation's typological visions, it does
not therefore follow that the word bears that sense in a simple epistolary
greeting, where there is neither reason nor precedent for a trope of this
kind. Paul and Luke speak of Rome throughout their writings and yet nowhere
call it Babylon. 2. A church could have been gathered there before either Paul
or Peter came, in the same way as the church in Samaria and also the church at
Antioch—which they make Peter's other episcopate—were gathered. We certainly
have testimony in Acts 8:4–5 and 11:18–19 that the doctrine of the Gospel had
previously been preached at Jerusalem to many Romans, Acts 2:10. Paul also
shows in Romans 16:7 that Andronicus and Junia, who were living at Rome, were
in Christ, or were Christians, before he was—that is, five or six years before
Peter is imagined to have been at Rome. Moreover, they were *prominent among
the Apostles*. Why, then, might that church not have been gathered, at least
in some measure, through their labor as well? Finally, Scripture does not
relate which of the Apostles gathered many other churches, yet those churches
cannot therefore be confidently ascribed to Peter. 3. Those accounts of Mark
and Simon Magus rest only upon human testimony; nor can any eyewitnesses to
them be produced.

3. Bell.: *That Peter died at Rome is attested by his tomb, pilgrimages, and
the ancient writers.* Prot. In matters of faith an argument drawn from tombs
is cadaverous, and one that rests upon

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 172 | Augsburg canvas 186 | printed p. 162 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

pilgrimage is wandering. We, however, require greater certainty even in human
testimony, especially
from those relating things heard and previously done, in a matter upon which
the Church's salvation depends. The testimonies of the ancient writers about
Peter's residence at Rome also derive all their force from the first authors
of that account. Chief among them seems to have been Papias, Eusebius, book 2,
chapter 15, who is also the first cited by Bellarmine in chapter 2 as the
leader and chief of the rest. Papias, however, was a man devoted to unwritten
traditions; from them he imbibed many errors and became the source of error to
those who followed. He used the Gospel called *According to the Hebrews*.
Eusebius testifies to these things in book 3, chapter 39, thereby rendering
weak the testimonies of all who spoke from Papias's mouth.

4. Bell.: *Peter was Bishop at Rome until his death. For: 1. The Roman Church
has always been regarded as foremost of all because of Peter, its Bishop. 2.
No other place can be shown where Peter had his see from the time he left
Antioch. 3. All the ancients affirm this with the highest agreement.* Prot. 1.
The foundation Bellarmine laid above was that Peter established his see at
Rome *at the Lord's command*. Yet when he comes to his proofs here, he seems
entirely to have forgotten the clause *at the Lord's command* and attempts to
show only the deed, even though the whole force of this see for establishing
the Roman chair depends upon that command. To fair judges of the facts, such a
deed of an Apostle would indicate God's command as its antecedent cause. But
who could bear Bellarmine, who does not shrink from denying that Peter, Paul,
or any other Apostle wrote the sacred books at God's command—*On the Word of
God*, book 4, chapter 4—and yet dares to thrust upon us, without any proof, the
Lord's command that Peter's see be established at Rome? Let him therefore
answer his own argument contained in that place, with a few alterations: *If
Christ and Peter had intended to confine and restrict the authority of the
Church's head to the Roman see, Christ would first have commanded a matter of
such great importance, and Peter would somewhere have testified that he
established his see there at the Lord's command; but we nowhere read this.* 2.
Here Bellarmine assumes as granted that Peter was made the proper

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 173 | Augsburg canvas 187 | printed p. 163 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Bishop of a particular church, which we deny outright. For this would
have diminished Peter's own status: he would have been reduced from the higher
rank of the Apostles to the order of Bishops. If a prince staying in a
city does the things which that city's prefect or praetor customarily does, he
does not thereby become the proper Prefect or Praetor of that city. Such was
the manner of the Apostles' residence in this or that church: they did
everything Bishops do, but, properly speaking, they were not Bishops. 2. The
Roman Church was regarded as one of the foremost churches by the Apostle Paul,
not because Peter was their Bishop, but because their faith was proclaimed
throughout the whole world, Romans 1:8. It later became renowned partly
because of the majesty of the city together with the excellence of the
citizens who were Christians, and partly because of Paul and Peter, who were
regarded not as its Bishops but as its founders. The most ancient writers
almost always join Paul with Peter in the establishment of that church and
distinguish them as founders from its Bishops. The others who call either
Peter or some other Apostle a Bishop must be understood in that broader sense
in which Peter also calls himself a *presbyter*, 1 Peter 5:1.

##### Arguments against the establishment of Peter's see at Rome

5. Prot. The authors of this account do not agree among themselves about the
time when Peter came there and remained there, nor about his successors.

the event itself, as is evident in the diversity of opinions about the time of
Christ's death and about Daniel's weeks.* Prot. 1. Where a fact is certain,
disagreement of this kind does not make it uncertain; but when it is
uncertain, the disagreement makes it still more uncertain or improbable. The
first authors of the history of Christ—Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John—do not
disagree about the time of his death. But the first authors of this account,
at least those known to us, both vary among themselves and depart from the
Scriptures. Bell. 2. *There is no disagreement among reputable authors*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 174 | Augsburg canvas 188 | printed p. 164 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Claudius, sat there for twenty-five years, and died in Nero's fourteenth year.*
Prot. 1. Origen on Genesis, Bede on Acts 15, Marianus Scotus in his *Chronicle*,
and Thomas Aquinas on Galatians 2 do not hold this. Nor, indeed, does Damasus
himself in the *Pontifical*: therefore, not all these authors are
"reputable." 2. Those who do hold it dissent from Holy Scripture, as we shall
teach below; therefore,
insofar as they hold it, they are neither reputable nor to be approved. Bell.
3. *Although we did not know who succeeded Peter immediately, it ought not
therefore be called into doubt whether anyone succeeded him.* Prot. Nor is the
present doubt *whether anyone succeeded him*, but *whether Peter preceded
anyone in the Roman episcopate*. Because the same witnesses testify both to
the succession of certain men and to Peter's preceding them, the weakening of
one part weakens their entire testimony. Bell. 4. *The whole matter is arranged
as follows: Peter left the episcopal see to Clement. After Peter's death,
however, Clement in humility was unwilling to occupy it while Linus and Cletus

Linus succeeded Peter, Cletus succeeded Linus, and Clement succeeded Cletus.*
Prot. 1. If Peter left or appointed a successor for himself, it seems more
consistent that every Pontiff should also designate his successors than that
so great a matter should be left hanging upon the Cardinals' election. 2. If
Peter had a just reason for choosing Clement, Clement had no just reason for
rejecting the office entrusted to him. 3. If Peter could readily govern all
the churches, what need did he have of Linus and Cletus as assistants in
governing the Roman Church? 4. If Clement seemed worthy to Peter to succeed
him, why was he not at least equally appointed as an assistant beforehand? 5.
If Linus and Cletus had been worthy of that honor, they would surely have
shown their humility no less by revering Peter's last will than Clement showed
his by pressing so stubbornly his predecessor's judgment or deed. 6. Why did
Clement not declare this humility of his while Peter was alive, so that Peter
might leave another successor? 7. The whole matter is arranged better and
more certainly from Scripture:

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 175 | Augsburg canvas 189 | printed p. 165 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

namely, just as Paul left the care of the Ephesian Church not to some one Lord
Bishop,
but to various Presbyters who were also Bishops, Acts 20:17, 28, so Paul
likewise, together with Peter (if Peter was then at Rome), left the care of the
Roman Church to Clement, Linus, Cletus, and others who, even while Peter was
alive, were together Presbyters or Bishops of that church. Because all these
men became very famous, the continuance of that church is consequently
demonstrated by the ancient writers under now the name of one, now of another.

6. Prot. That account of Peter—that in the fifth year after the Lord's Passion

seventeenth after Paul's conversion. Bell.: *Peter remained in Judea for about

through the neighboring Provinces. In the seventh year of his Antiochene

to Rome and established his see there for twenty-five years. After seven
years, however, he returned to Jerusalem, having been expelled from the city,

returned once more to Rome and ended his life there.* Prot. 1. Scripture does
not contain even the slightest indication that Peter did not remain in Judea

Paul sought him there after an interval of fourteen years, Galatians 2:1; he

9. Prot. Paul wrote various Epistles from Rome, even during Nero's reign (when

regarded equally as a fable and as history.* Jerome, *Catalogue of
Ecclesiastical Writers*, in the life of Luke, and on Matthew 13.

Bell. 2. *In Colossians 4:11 Paul speaks only of his household attendants and
those ministering to him. In 2 Timothy 4:16 he speaks only of those who could
have helped him before Caesar.* Prot. 1. There is no evidence, nor is it even
probable, that Aristarchus, Mark, and Justus were members of Paul's household.
Yet he speaks of them in Colossians 4:11 and also speaks of all his fellow
workers in the kingdom of God. He therefore ought not to have omitted Peter.
2. In 2 Timothy 4:16 he speaks of those who ought to have strengthened him, as
is clear from verse 17: *But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me.* Peter
would not have failed him in this duty. We never read that Paul desired
intercessors with Nero. Bell. 3. *At the time when Paul reached Rome and wrote
his Epistles from there, Peter was perhaps not at Rome. For although he had
established his see there, he was very frequently absent.* Prot. Peter was
therefore Bishop of Rome, but he was never at Rome whenever Scripture mentions
the Roman Church. Paul was never able to find him at Rome, although Dionysius,
whom Bellarmine commends in chapter 3, affirms that he and Paul were crowned
with martyrdom at Rome together and at the same time.

Thus far we have seen the foundation of the Papal Monarchy; we must now see
how it is built upon.

10. Bell.: *By divine right someone must succeed Peter in the pontificate of
the universal Church.* Prot. 1. Just as Peter had no other pontificate of the
universal Church than Paul had, so no more than Paul ought he to have a
successor in it. 2. If succession is granted, it is still not necessary that
some one man succeed Peter to the whole, as though he were sole heir to his
estate. At first two later succeeded one Emperor, one in the East and the
other in the West. There were also Scholastics who taught that the Church
could create and have two Supreme Pontiffs at the same time, such as
Armagh in *The Questions of the Armenians*, book 7, chapter 17, and Ockham in
the *Dialogue*, part 3, at the end. But if two, why not as many as there are
Archbishops or diocesan Bishops? 3. Divine right is what God promulgates,
Thomas, 2a2ae, question 57, article 2, reply 3. But God nowhere promulgates
that some one person should succeed Peter in the Church's pontificate. This
succession therefore does not rest upon divine right. 4. The reasons by which
Bellarmine proves this proposition are the scraps of trifles already
sufficiently exploded: 1. *The Pontiff exists for the Church, while the Church
needs one Pastor no less now than formerly.* Prophets also existed for the
Church, and (if the matter is to be determined by human judgment) the Church
needs various Prophets no less now than formerly. Yet prophecy was not
continued by succession. 2. *The Church is one; therefore the form of
government ought not to be changed.* The Church is not one through any
external combination into some one particular society. For Scripture
everywhere speaks in this way of *churches*. 3. *The Lord said to Peter, Feed
my sheep; but the office of a pastor is ordinary and perpetual while the fold
remains.* He also told all the Apostles to preach and baptize; but the office
of preaching and baptizing is ordinary and perpetual while the Church
remains. 4. *The Lord entrusted all the sheep to Peter, not only with respect
to place, but also with respect to time.* He also entrusted all

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 181 | Augsburg canvas 195 | printed p. 171 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

nations to all the Apostles until the end of the age, Matthew 28:19–20. 5.
*The Church has a head besides Christ*, 1 Corinthians 12:21. By the same
reasoning and from the same passage, it is also proved that the Church has one
*hand* and two *feet*. 6. *The priesthood of the Old Testament was a figure of
the priesthood of the New Testament, and its succession a figure of the
succession of this one.* It was a figure of Christ, our one Priest, who has no
successor. That succession, therefore, was not a figure of succession, but of
eternal continuance.

11. Bell.: *The Roman Pontiff succeeded Peter (when Peter died in the City) in
all his dignity and power. For there was never anyone, except the Roman and
Antiochene Bishops, who in any way declared himself Peter's successor or was
regarded as such. But the Antiochene did not succeed Peter in the pontificate
of the whole Church; therefore, the Roman did.* Prot. 1. If the Roman Pontiff
succeeded Peter in all his dignity and power, then Linus, Cletus, Clement, or
whoever was the first Roman Bishop, was established in dignity and power above
John and the other Apostles who outlived Peter, as their Pastor and head. 2.
Peter had power to cast out demons, speak in new tongues, take up serpents,
drink deadly things without harm, and heal the sick, Matthew 10:1; Luke 9:1;
Mark 16:17. He also had the dignity that his Epistles are parts of Canonical
Scripture. Do these things belong to the Roman Pontiff? 3. If no one besides
the Pope declared himself Peter's successor in the way he did, this was
because such shamelessness ought to belong especially to that great Whore. Yet
others said repeatedly that they were Apostles, Revelation 2:2, and Montanus
even boasted that he was the Paraclete promised by Christ. Thus they did not
leave even this glory to the Roman Pontiff, that he alone claimed supreme
power in the Church for himself. 4. It is not enough that the Roman Pontiff
assert this or that certain people even regard him as such, unless he can show
that it is owed him by divine right.

12. Bell.: *Succession itself is by Christ's institution and*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 182 | Augsburg canvas 196 | printed p. 172 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*divine right; but the manner of succession by which the Roman Bishop is Peter's
successor arose from Peter's deed (that he established his see at Rome and
retained it until death), not from Christ's original institution. It must
nevertheless be asserted absolutely that by divine right the Roman Pontiff is
Pastor and head of the whole Church. Even if the Roman Pontiff's succession is
perhaps not of divine right, it nevertheless belongs to the Catholic faith,
like Paul's cloak, because it is Peter's tradition. Nor are there two
episcopates, Roman and universal, except potentially; there is only one. From
this it follows that whoever is chosen Roman Bishop by that very fact becomes
Supreme Pontiff of the Church, even if perhaps the electors do not express
it.* Prot. 1. If succession itself were by Christ's institution and divine
right (since knowledge of it is not only useful but also necessary for
everyone), it would undoubtedly have been declared in the Scriptures. For
Bellarmine concedes in *On the Word of God*, book 4, chapter 11, that *all
necessary and useful things were written by the Apostles.* But Bellarmine
could produce no passage of Scripture in which this succession is declared.
When he twists the three words *Feed my sheep* to this end, he raves without
any reason. 2. From where comes this hesitation and tangled manner of speaking
about the succession of the Roman Pontiff as such: *it is not from Christ's
original institution; perhaps it is not of divine right?* Is there a second
and third institution of Christ concerning Peter's successors? Do the Jesuits
themselves doubt by what Right the Roman Pontiff is Peter's successor and
Christ's vicar? 3. If the succession of the Roman Bishop in Peter's
pontificate arose from Peter's deed, then it did not arise from his institution
or from any right of his, for his deed does not establish divine or human
right. 4. What is absolutely false ought not to be affirmed absolutely. But
even in the opinion of Scotus, Cordubensis, Báñez, Ockham, and the like, from
whom Bellarmine himself does not wholly dissent, it is no less false that by
divine right the Roman Pontiff is head of the Church than that some Spanish or
French Bishop, of this or that title, is by divine right his Cardinal. 5. If
the succession of the Roman Pontiff belongs to the faith, then either his
right or only the deed is an article of faith. Not the right, since on
Bellarmine's confession it is not certainly divine; not the deed, because
before Peter's

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 183 | Augsburg canvas 197 | printed p. 173 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

death (which introduced no change in the faith) it was not an article of faith,
again by Bellarmine's confession. 6. Paul's cloak is only incidentally an
article of faith because it is mentioned in Scripture, which is the rule of
faith, 2 Timothy 4:13. But the succession of the Roman Bishop does not have
even the slightest testimony in Holy Scripture. Nor can it be Peter's
Apostolic tradition, because, as Bellarmine admits, it arose from his death;
and immediately before his death Peter wished to depart from Rome (if the
account of his death proved by Bellarmine in book 1, chapter 23, at the end,
is true), unless the Lord had called him back—ignorant, that is, both of the
place of his death and of the power his death possessed to establish a
Monarchy there. 7. Bellarmine's perversity should be observed. When he disputes against
Scripture, he contends that there are many things in Scripture which *do not
in themselves belong to the faith*, a class in which he wishes to place all
the histories of the Old Testament and many of the New, *On the Word of God*,
book 4, chapter 12. Yet here, in pleading the Roman Pontiff's case, he wishes
without any distinction that Paul's cloak, as the Roman Bishop's pallium, be
an article of faith, so that he can reduce to an article of faith the
succession of a pallium-wearing Bishop, although it is written nowhere. This
succession therefore holds a more important place in the Roman faith than all
the histories of the Old Testament and many of the New. 8. That last point is
a device of Babylonian logic: to confound into one the episcopate of the world
and that of the city. True logic teaches that offices whose objects are
distinct are distinct; but the world and the city are not one and the same.
The connection which they are now forced to have in the election of the Roman
Pontiff is no greater than the conjunction of the episcopate with the secular
Principate both of the city and of the rest of the Roman territory. For the
man who is chosen Bishop thereby becomes Prince.

13. Bell.: *The primacy of the Roman Pontiff is proved from the Councils, from
the testimonies of the Pontiffs, and from the agreement of the Greek and Latin
Fathers.* Prot. 1. The question here is not any sort of primacy, but the
primacy of monarchical power. 2. The question is not whether the Roman Pontiff
possessed such power for some time, but whether he possessed it in this way
from the beginning. 3. Whenever he first began to act as Monarch,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 184 | Augsburg canvas 198 | printed p. 174 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

it remains to be investigated by what right he did so—divine, human, or
entirely his own, that is, no right
at all. No ancient Council and no one among the ancient Fathers ever affirmed,
or even dreamed, that *the Roman Pontiff possessed the primacy of monarchical
power by divine right from the beginning*.

14. Bell.: *The same is proved from the origin and antiquity of the primacy;
from the authority the Roman Pontiff always exercised over other Bishops; and
from his laws, dispensations, and censures.* Prot. 1. The very origin and
antiquity must first be proved, and that from divine right, before anything
else can be proved. 2. There is no valid inference from the exercise of power
to a right to that power. Nor, in any case, is what is related about its
exercise true. 3. Monarchical laws, dispensations, and censures in the Church
of God proceeding from a man identify the man of sin sitting in the temple of
God as though he were God.

15. Bell.: *It is also proved from the Pope's vicars; from the right of
appeals; from the fact that the Supreme Pontiff is judged by no one; and from
the names customarily attributed to the Roman Pontiff.* Prot. 1. If Christ's
vicar himself is such by no divine right, as was proved above, by what right
will he appoint Vicars? 2. Ecclesiastical appeals from one church to another
are not appeals to a higher jurisdiction, but recourse to communion and the
sharing of brotherly assistance. In fact, appeals of this kind were not in
use among the first Christians. Socrates in the *Tripartite History*, book 5,
chapter 34: *Cyril was the one and the first who caused appellate petitions to
be used as in public courts.* 3. In wishing to be judged by no one, the Pope
exalts himself above everything called God or a deity. 4. There is no name of
dignity, much less of power, which either in the Scriptures or among the
ancient Fathers is attributed to the Roman Bishop alone.

16. Bellarmine opposes nothing to our principal arguments except a begging of
one and the same principle, repeated to the point of nausea. There is
therefore no need to defend them one by one. Indeed, this monstrous claim to
universal Monarchy ought to be refuted in no other way than by breaking the
tablets produced as its proof.
