But let us deal more generously with Osiander.

First, let us set out what we freely concede to him in this matter.

Second, let us examine the points on which, as is right, we recoil from him.

We therefore concede, indeed openly and freely confess, and even contend:

1. When Osiander objects that we are justified by the one God alone, we
readily acknowledge that God alone is the fountain and cause of all
righteousness within us—its efficient cause, that is, but not its formal
cause. Our righteousness is not God's own essential righteousness and thus
the divine essence itself, “by which God, pouring himself into us, makes us
as it were part of himself.”

2. We freely confess that our redemption and justification necessarily
require Christ to be the God-man, God as well as man. But Osiander's desired
conclusion does not follow: that Christ therefore justifies us by his
essential righteousness only insofar as he is God. Our justification
required satisfaction, and satisfaction required suffering. The deity gave
value and worth to this suffering and satisfaction, while the humanity
endured it; both natures, hypostatically united, completed the whole work.

3. We gratefully acknowledge, to gather many things into one bundle, that
Christ becomes ours by faith; that we are united to Christ as members to
their mystical head, and through Christ to God; that God according to his
essence is present and exists in us, as in all other created things; and
that the Father and Son dwell and make their abode in the saints through the
special, intimate, gracious presence and efficacy of the Holy Spirit, John
14:23 and Ephesians 3:17. But this is not:

As the Father is essentially in the Son;

Nor as the Son, the second person of the Godhead, is hypostatically and
personally in Christ's humanity, as though we had one and the same
personality with Christ or one essence with the Father. For it is peculiar
to Christ that “the whole fullness of deity dwells in him.” And although he
said, “I in them and you in me,” John 17:23, and “as I live through the
Father, so whoever eats me

[1679 printed p. 306 | IA leaf 314]

will live through me,” John 6:57, the word *as* indicates only similarity
and proportion, not equality or essential and specific identity. God's
essence, or Christ's essential righteousness as God, is not thereby mixed
with ours. The Father and Spirit indeed are in Christ, and, as Calvin
correctly says, “just as the fullness of deity dwells in him, so in him we
possess the whole God.” Yet each is present in his own proper manner: the
Father is essentially in the Son; the Son is personally in Christ's human
nature; and the Father, Christ, and the Holy Spirit are mystically in the
saints through union by faith and the efficacy of grace.

4. We believe that Christ makes us saved and righteous not only through his
merit and satisfaction imputed to us, but also through communicating himself
to us, by the infusion, inception, and increase of inherent grace and
righteousness, until it finally matures and is perfected in glory. “Those
whom he called, he justified; those whom he justified, he glorified,” Romans
8:30; and “But you were sanctified, but you were justified in the name of
the Lord Jesus and by the Spirit of our God,” 1 Corinthians 6:11. But,

First, we attribute this communication of inherent righteousness to
sanctification, which is always joined to justification but distinct from
it. With the apostle in Romans 4 and throughout that entire discussion, we
place justification in Christ's whole obedience performed in our place and
imputed to us, and in the pardon of sins granted from it.

Second, we place this communication of inherent righteousness in
sanctification in the infusion of created grace by the Holy Spirit, not in
the blasphemous participation in God's essential righteousness, and thus in
the divine essence itself, which Osiander invents. We are indeed said to be
“made partakers of the divine nature,” 2 Peter 1:4. But the following
discussion will, I hope, sufficiently establish that this passage does
nothing to support enthusiasm of this kind. Let us therefore at last prepare
ourselves for that discussion.

Insofar as Osiander's position forms a syncretism with Roman theology on
justification—namely, by denying that the term *justification* is taken in a
forensic sense in this controversy, hissing away justification by the
imputation of Christ's righteousness, and contending that it lies in
inherent righteousness—we need not overturn it here. We have already dealt
fully with the first two points, and perhaps the third can be attacked more
appropriately in its own place.

It therefore remains for us at present to investigate the dogma in which he
far surpasses the Roman theologians, as I have said, and joins his vote with
the ancient Manichaeans and Gnostics and our more recent fanatics and
enthusiasts.

I freely admit that pursuing this at greater length will carry us somewhat
away from the path of our discussion of justification. Yet it will not be
altogether irrelevant; indeed, it will relieve the weariness of the longer
journey if, as we have sometimes done before, we make a brief excursion into
side matters. This is especially so because the enthusiasts of our age—men
of turbulent temperament, like Osiander, and thoroughly ready to disturb
both civil and ecclesiastical peace—leave almost nothing unattempted by
which they clearly demonstrate that they are not God-bearers, as they
advertise themselves, but demon-bearers. They are not partakers of the
divine nature, which is gentle and kindly, but, if we may infer anything
from their hostilities, insults, and savage deeds, full of Satan and the
Devil, who is called Abaddon and Apollyon and was a murderer from the
beginning.
