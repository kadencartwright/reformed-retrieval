Verse 13. Do not love sleep, lest you become poor; open your eyes, and be satisfied with bread.

In פקח עיניו (*aperi oculos*, open your eyes) there is a synecdoche of the part for vigilant zeal in carrying out affairs. For someone can have his eyes open and nevertheless remain confined to bed. There is the same synecdoche in לחם (*pane*, bread).

The purpose is to exhort people to business by deterring them from idleness.

Just as we use food and drink so that, strengthened in our powers, we may be fit and suitable for undertaking business, so we use sleep that, refreshed by it, we may become more eager to undertake labours. But those who indulge in sleep not only cut something away from the time appointed for labours, but moreover render their bodies less fit for enduring labours. Indulging in sleep, or loving sleep, is prohibited just as loving other perishable things—food, drink, and money—is prohibited, 1 Tim. 6.10. Rather, let us remember the statement at 1 Cor. 7.29–31.

Indeed, the time appointed for labour is excellently described at Psalm 104.22–23. Here caution is needed lest we measure the time of our labour strictly and crudely by that time. For in that case we would not take the proper sleep owed to nature in the middle of summer, and in the middle of winter we would be overwhelmed by sleep. For Judea is so situated that nearly all the days equal the nights; but the span granted for labours must be adjusted according to the sun’s rising and setting among us.

That the powers of the body are enervated by excessive sleep

[1632 witness | PDF p. 438 | printed columns 850-851]

is evident, since from excessive sleep there is engendered an abundance of harmful and redundant humours which renders the body languid and diseased.

Next, since this perishable life is like not only sleep but a dream, which is shorter and more confined than sleep, and since nearly half of it is necessarily expended upon sleep and eating, care must be taken that no part of the time for doing work should fall away from us which necessity itself does not take away from us.

What in this place seems to be proposed under the name of a punishment as something accidental and uncertain is said at Prov. 6 to rush in so violently as an armed man upon one naked and unarmed.

Just as the person who gives to sleep the time which ought to be devoted to doing work is diminished and becomes poor, so the person who on the Lord’s Day—consecrated to amassing the treasures of eternal life—indulges in sleep or spends it on games, jests, and drinking parties must necessarily be oppressed by spiritual poverty, which is the most wretched of all. There are very many who on working days rise early to labour but on the Lord’s Day sleep far into the day. Since by this they sufficiently declare how dissolute they are in seeking God’s Kingdom, it is not surprising if they prove themselves needy and exhausted in piety. By contrast, at Matt. 6 and Psalm 92 they are taught that the Kingdom of heaven must be sought first and before all things. The Prophet invites us to rise early so that we may duly celebrate the Sabbath day.

The former half is proved from the contrary which is placed in the latter. For since by vigilance and working at night we grow rich in such a way that we possess abundantly that by which we may acquire food and clothing, it follows that, having been made poor through excessive devotion to sleep, we are left destitute of food. (For it is clear that something must be understood in each half.) This is confirmed at Prov. 10.4, where the hand of the diligent is said to enrich people. Likewise at 12.24, the hand of the diligent is said to rule. This is taught by common experience. See Psalm 128.

But when he speaks in the imperative, “(and be satisfied),” he seems thereby to intimate not only the certainty of this promise, but moreover that all things which pertain to sustenance are prepared without the care and anxiety by which people are generally accustomed to wear themselves out. See Matt. 6.

This, however, must not be understood in such a way that, placing excessive confidence in diligence and vigilance, we neglectfully pass over the invocation of divine aid and blessing. For although we rise early and return to bed late, nevertheless all this diligence is vain unless God’s blessing is added, Psalm 127 and Hag. 1.

Next, it must also be noted that these promises which pertain to this life are fulfilled ἐπὶ τὸ πολὺ, for the most part, insofar as it seems expedient to God, the most wise Father.

Verse 14. “Bad, bad,” says the buyer; but when he has gone away, he praises himself to himself.

Verse 15. There is gold and an abundance of carbuncles; but knowledge of the lips is an instrument of great price.

The purpose is to display, so that vanity in commerce may be avoided, the value of truthfulness over the small gain which is acquired by lying.

The mutual exchange of useful things and trade are an extraordinary benefit of God toward mortals. Yet this benefit is either utterly overthrown or at least very greatly undermined by human perversity, when this exchange is turned into robbery, those who practise trade into robbers, and the marketplace or forum is made not so much a den of thieves as a fortress, under whose protection and shadow people may practise their frauds and thefts safely and securely. This human malice is vividly ζωγραφεῖται (*depicted*) by Solomon. For he introduces a buyer acting his

[1632 witness | PDF p. 439 | printed columns 852-853]

part deceitfully and fraudulently. In order to carry the merchandise away at the lowest possible price, though it is sound and uncorrupted he loads it with many faults and defects. It is vanity not to recognize the goodness of merchandise when it is good; it is a certain greater vanity to call corrupt and adulterated those things which are legitimate: but woe to those who call good evil, and so forth, Isa. 5. He surpasses this vanity still further by affirming that those things are utterly bad—for that is what the repetition signifies—which are free from defect, or which certainly possess that goodness with which they are offered by the seller. But it is the vainest vanity to turn one’s tongue about and immediately proclaim as the best what one had called the worst. Thus it happens, as James 3 affirms, that sweet and bitter water flows from the same fountain. Thus it happens that you speak with a heart and a heart, Psalm 15, so that you are double-tongued like a prodigy and pronounce a sentence of condemnation upon yourself. Nor is it merely vanity but consummate wickedness, not only to commit what is evil but to glory in wickedness, of which it is fitting for you to be ashamed.

All these things, with a slight inflection of the words, apply equally to a seller who, in order to increase the price, will proclaim as the best those things which are thoroughly corrupt; and who, as soon as the buyer has turned his back, will congratulate himself, acknowledge the fraud among his own people, and mock the buyer’s inexperience. And as if it were too little to lie and heap lie upon lie, when he asserts that the thing is worth so much, cost him so much, and could have been sold for so much; when the buyer refuses to give so much as he has fixed, the seller will assert that no one will thereafter buy it at so low a price. He will confirm the false matter by an oath and bind himself with many curses and imprecations. Nor will he do this alone, but he will also teach his servants to do it and impose upon them the necessity of lying, so that he will dismiss them from his service unless they are willing to be Ministers of his shameful gain. Thus to deliver a son or daughter to a merchant or craftsman for instruction or servitude is to thrust that child into the very gullet of the devil. It is not difficult to discern how far this is from the simplicity which Christ commands at Matt. 5: [let your speech be yes, yes; no, no], and so forth.

Even if human beings do not punish these things, God is nevertheless the stern avenger of them all, as the Apostle expressly confirms at Eph. 4.

In the other Proverb a tongue rightly instructed and conformed to that which is true is compared with gold and many carbuncles. Above it was compared with perennial water, or a river in the midst of a city, and likewise with the tree of life. But in this place, looking to the present subject, he compares it most aptly with gold and an abundance of gems. What the Apostle admonishes—that we are members of one another—ought to move us very greatly to speak truthfully. For what benefit is it for the stomach to retain all the juice of food enclosed within itself and not communicate it to the other parts of the body by which they may be sustained? By this very thing it constructs destruction for itself. Or what benefit would it be to the hand if it drew to itself the nourishment which ought to sustain the arm? But if we are not led by care for others, nor is our own salvation dear to us, let us nevertheless consider what unprofitable people and what inept traders we are, in that for a single small coin or a little gain we exchange a most precious thing and the rarest gems. For the tongue which speaks true things to its neighbour, being more excellent than all gold and precious stone, is changed by these lies into iron or flint for a copper coin, for one small coin or another. Thus in your chest and coffer you have gold, but in your mouth copper or iron or, if there is something still more abject and more suited to engendering not so much tonsillitis or quinsy in the throat as in the soul itself, that thing.

The profit, moreover, which is received from this, whether by the buyer or by the seller, is cast into a pierced coffer or chest, and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 440 | printed columns 854-855]

is like a certain machine by which thieves open locks and break bolts, and a spark by which their houses and the things which are in them will be consumed by fire.

Let them remember what we read about David at the final chapter of 2 Samuel: he was unwilling to drink water brought at the peril of his servants’ lives, although he desired it greatly and laboured from longing for it as though from a case of pica. What, therefore, will become of those who day after day fearlessly eat and drink the blood not of the perishable life but of the eternal life of their servants?
