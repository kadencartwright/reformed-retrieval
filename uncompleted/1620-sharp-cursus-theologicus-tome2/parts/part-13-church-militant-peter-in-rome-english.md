QUESTION III

Whether Peter was at Rome

Bellarmine, book 2, *On the Roman Pontiff*, chapter 2, asserts that Peter was at
Rome; chapter 3, that he died at Rome; and chapter 4, that he was bishop at Rome
until his death and, once having undertaken the episcopate, never changed it.

We say that Peter was never a bishop in the sense in which the Papists intend;
and therefore he could not have changed his episcopate. We prove by these
arguments that Bellarmine’s assertions are false.

Argument 1. What cannot be taught from Scripture is not to be received as an
article of faith. But that Peter was at Rome and exercised the episcopate there
until his death cannot be taught from Scripture. Therefore.

Argument 2. That concerning which nothing certain is established, not even
among secular and ecclesiastical writers, is not to be received as an
indubitable article of faith. But nothing certain about Peter’s coming to Rome
and death is established among the writers. Nothing is certain about his
coming, because Orosius says that he came at the beginning of Claudius’s reign;
Jerome, in its second year; the *Fasciculus temporum*, in its fourth year; and
Pas., *On the Lives of the Saints*, in the thirteenth year of Claudius. Nor is
anything certain about his death, because, as some say that he died at Rome, so
many say that he died at Jerusalem, such as Chrysostom on Matthew 23:24 and
Jerome on the same passage: *Of these*, he says, *Stephen was stoned,*

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 68 | printed col. 135 | bsb10175568]

*Paul was slain, and Peter was crucified.* Nicholas of Lyra on the same passage
says: *Of them you will slay some, such as James the brother of John*, Acts 12,
*and Stephen*, Acts 7, *and many others; and some will be crucified, such as Peter
and Andrew.* Bellarmine responds only to this last testimony, saying that Lyra
spoke with too little caution.

Argument 3. If what the Papists say about Peter’s tenure in the Roman episcopate
conflicts with sacred Scripture and true history, then it is not to be received.
But the former is true. Therefore. The assumption is proved. They concede that
Christ died in the reign of Tiberius and Peter in the reign of Nero. Yet,
according to Tertullian and certain others, Christ died in the fifteenth year of
Tiberius; but according to Onuphrius, Mercator, and others, he died in the
eighteenth year of Tiberius. Tiberius, moreover, reigned for twenty-three years;
Nero died in the thirty-seventh year after Christ’s death; and between the
reigns of Tiberius and Nero came Caligula, who reigned for four years, and
Claudius, who reigned for fourteen. But it is evident from Scripture that Peter
was not at Rome before the eighteenth year after Christ, because Paul was
converted near the beginning of the second year after Christ’s death and, after
three years, visited Peter at Jerusalem, Galatians 1:18; and after fourteen
years he again visited Peter at Jerusalem, Galatians 2:1. After that time Peter
went to Antioch, Galatians 2:11, and the Papists maintain that Peter sat there
for seven years. He therefore could not have come to Rome before the
twenty-fifth year after Christ’s death. They also maintain that Peter sat at
Rome for twenty-five years. It will therefore follow that Peter died fifty
years after Christ’s death, namely, fifteen years after Nero’s death—that is,
after the times of Galba, Otho, and Vitellius, namely, in the time of Vespasian,
which no one has ever asserted. Therefore.

Argument 4. If Peter had been bishop of Rome, it certainly ought to be possible
to establish

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 68 | printed col. 136 | bsb10175568]

who succeeded him in the episcopate. But this is not established. Therefore.
The assumption is proved. Tertullian, *On Prescription*, and Jerome on Isaiah
52 place Clement after Peter. Optatus, book 2, *Against Parmenian*, and Augustine,
Epistle 165, place Linus after Peter, Cletus after Linus, Anacletus after Cletus,
and Clement after Anacletus. Others, finally, take Cletus and Anacletus for the
same man. But Bellarmine’s claim that, although we do not know who succeeded,
someone certainly did succeed, is futile.

1. Because the question is whether the apostles had successors.

Further, since it is an article of faith, as they maintain, if they had
successors, at least the first should be established.

Further, his claim that Peter assigned the see to Clement, but that two others
occupied the see before him because they were Peter’s assistants in the
episcopal office, is ridiculous. For it would then follow that Peter’s first
successors lacked a calling and, indeed, that while the successor nominated by
Peter was alive they usurped the see.

Finally, certain writers assert that Linus died before Peter.

Argument 5. Paul writes the Epistle to the Romans while on the journey to
Jerusalem with the alms that Peter had asked him to collect, Galatians 2:10;
Romans 15:25. But at that time Peter was not at Rome, because Paul greeted
various believers at Rome, but not Peter, whom he would not have omitted if
Peter had been bishop of Rome at Rome.

Argument 6. When Paul came to Rome, he was greeted by various believers, Acts,
last chapter, and from Rome he wrote various epistles, in which he greeted many
by name; yet he makes no mention of Peter. Therefore, it is not probable
that Peter was at Rome at that time. Bellarmine’s claim that negative arguments
do not conclude is false when the matter concerns

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 69 | printed col. 139 | bsb10175568]

dogmas of faith.

Argument 7. If Peter and Paul, around the eighteenth year after Christ’s death,
entered into an agreement under the direction of the Holy Spirit that Peter
would preach to the Jews and Paul to the Gentiles, then Peter was not bishop at
Rome, but remained by agreement within the prescribed bounds. He is therefore
called the apostle of the circumcision, Galatians 2:8. But the former is true,
Galatians 2:7.

Argument 8. When Paul came to Rome, the Jews who had been entrusted to Peter by
the agreement seem still to have heard nothing about Christ, Acts 28:22, 24.
Those whom Paul greets, however, were for the most part Gentiles, or Jews who
had heard the gospel among the Gentiles through the teaching of Paul and
others, such as Aquila and Priscilla, Acts 18. Therefore.

Argument 9. In Philippians 2:20 Paul complains of all who were then at Rome that they
sought their own interests, and in Colossians 4:11 he names a few whom alone he
affirms to have been fellow workers in God’s kingdom; and in 2 Timothy 4:16 he
says that he was deserted by everyone at his first defense. But if Peter had
been at Rome, he would not have said this of everyone, for the apostle uses
universal terms. Therefore.

Argument 10. When Paul wrote from Rome to Philemon, he does not number Peter
among his fellow workers in the gospel, but names others, verses 23–24.
Therefore, Peter was either not at Rome then or was not Paul’s fellow worker in
the gospel.

Argument 11. Peter did not write from Rome to other churches, but from Babylon
in Assyria, 1 Peter 5:13. Nor, after he had departed from Rome, as they maintain,
did he write to the Romans, but only to the dispersed Jews. Therefore, it is not
probable that he was bishop of Rome, because Paul, who was neither bishop of
Rome nor head of the Church, did both.

Argument 12. Eusebius, book 4, *Ecclesiastical History*, gathers from Peter’s
epistles where he preached the gospel: *In which provinces*, he says, *Peter
evangelized Christ to those who were of*

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 69 | printed col. 140 | bsb10175568]

*the circumcision and delivered the doctrine of the New Testament is always
made manifest by his own words and by the epistle which he wrote to the Hebrews
settled in the dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, and Asia.*

Argument 13. No apostle is a bishop—unless you say that one man has two offices,
or that these are one office, which cannot be done if the name *bishop* is taken
properly and strictly. But Peter was an apostle. Therefore. The proposition is
proved: apostles are called immediately by Christ and were commanded to preach
to every nation, namely, in an extraordinary office; but bishops are chosen by
men and have a fixed see and church in an ordinary office, 1 Timothy 3:1; Titus
1:5; 1 Peter 5:2. Therefore. It is proved: if he desires to be a bishop, he
ceases to be an apostle and thus abandons the charge laid upon him by Christ.

Argument 14. If Peter was a bishop, then either he was ordained by Christ, or by
men after Christ’s ascension, or Peter appointed himself bishop. But none of
these can be said. Therefore. The assumption is proved. Not the first, because
if he was appointed bishop by Christ, then Christ put him in charge of a fixed
church. But this is false, because Christ ordered him to preach to every nation
with the others.

Further, there is no trace of this institution in Scripture. Finally, if he was
appointed bishop of Rome, how could he desert the episcopate for so long and
have other co-bishops? Romans 1:6. Not the second, because then he stripped
himself of the apostleship that he received from Christ and allowed himself to
be deprived of authority by men in order to discharge an office of less worth
and narrower scope; and it is asked by what authority he did so. Finally, it
will follow from this that Peter was inferior to the other apostles, who were
not ordained by men and through men, Galatians 1. Not the third, because no one

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 70 | printed col. 141 | bsb10175568]

can take that honor to himself, Hebrews 5:4. Therefore.

Argument 15. Nothing is established about the place and time of Peter’s death,
for Ambrose, Sermon 67, says, *On one day, in one place, they endured the
sentence of one tyrant*, speaking of Peter and Paul. But Linus, in the passions
of Peter and Paul, says that they suffered neither at the same time nor in the
same place nor at the command of the same tyrant.

Argument 16. In Nero’s time Josephus wrote at Rome about the Jewish War and
mentioned the deaths of John the Baptist, Christ, and many other Christians
slain by Nero. But he makes no mention of Peter, whom he would not have omitted
if Peter had been head of the Christian Church and had died at Rome.

Argument 17. Those who trace the origin of the succession of the popes number
not Peter alone but Paul with him, and yet no one ever said that Paul was bishop
of Rome. Irenaeus says, *When the blessed apostles had founded and instructed
the Church, they handed over to Linus the episcopate for administering the
Church.* Epiphanius says, *The succession of bishops at Rome had this sequence:
Peter, Paul, Linus, Cletus*, and so forth. Eusebius, book 3 of the
*Ecclesiastical History*, chapter 2, says: *After the martyrdom of Peter and
Paul, Linus was the first to receive by lot the episcopate of the Roman Church.*
Gregory of Tours, book 1, chapter 27, says that Clement was the third bishop,
where he numbers Linus first and Anacletus second. Finally, Irenaeus, book 3,
chapter 3, says that Linus was the first bishop and Anacletus succeeded him.

OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. Peter wrote his first epistle from Babylon, 1 Peter 5:13, which is
figuratively Rome according to Eusebius and others, and Rome is commonly called
Babylon in Revelation. But if Rome is Babylon in Scripture, and Peter wrote
from Babylon, then he wrote from Rome and thus was at Rome.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 70 | printed col. 142 | bsb10175568]

Response. It is indeed true that in Scripture *Babylon* is sometimes taken
figuratively, as in Revelation, and sometimes properly, namely, for Babylon in
Egypt or Assyria. But the question is whether it is taken figuratively in this
passage, for which they present no argument. It is evident, however, that it is
not taken figuratively, because if Peter wrote from Rome, his own see, to what
end would he have suppressed its name? Further, this Babylon in Assyria was not
far from the Jews; by the agreement with Paul, Peter had been appointed apostle
of the circumcised, Galatians 2.

2. We concede that in Revelation Babylon signifies Rome, because John foretold
that it would be the seat of that harlot and of Antichrist.

Instance. In Revelation *Babylon* is not spoken of the Roman Church, but of old
Rome as it was in John’s time.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because from Revelation 4 onward John
teaches not what the Church’s present state was, but what its future state
would be, Revelation 4. Further, almost all interpreters teach that that harlot
is Antichrist, who was going to sit in the temple of God, that is, in the
Church, 2 Thessalonians 2, as we shall say afterward.

Objection 2. From the last chapters of Acts and Romans it is evident that there
were many Christians and a large church at Rome before Paul came there. But no
one made them Christians except Peter, whom the ancients testify to have been
the first to preach to the Romans.

Response. The assumption is denied, because many apostles and evangelists
besides Paul and Peter had been sent to the Gentiles, and it is probable that
one or more of those whom Paul names in Romans 16 first preached there. 2. Many
ancients testify that Barnabas was the first to preach at Rome, namely, in
Tiberius’s time, whereas Paul came there only when brought there in Claudius’s
time, such as Clement, book 1, *Order of the Recognitions*; book 7 of the
*History of Platina, in the Life of Christ*; and Dorotheus of Tyre, in his
*Synopsis*.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 71 | printed col. 143 | bsb10175568]

3. Bellarmine’s assertion that Peter was the first to preach to the Gentiles is
false, as was proved above. Likewise false is the assertion that Barnabas
preached nowhere except with Paul, because he was sent to Antioch alone, Acts
11:19.

Objection 3. Historians write that Mark wrote his Gospel at Rome, according as
he had heard Peter preaching. Therefore.

Response. The writers intend only that Mark wrote at Peter’s dictation or with
his approval; therefore he wrote at Rome—this does not follow. Further, they
testify that Mark the evangelist was bishop of Alexandria. It is therefore not
probable that he remained at Rome. There was indeed another Mark, who was
called John Mark, the cousin of Barnabas and disciple of Paul, who was at Rome,
as in Colossians 4:14 and 2 Timothy 4:11, and concerning whom also Acts 12 and
15 speak. Yet, as the historians maintain, this man did not write the Gospel.

Objection 4. History recounts that Simon Magus was defeated by Peter at Rome.
Therefore.

Response. The history Bellarmine cites is fabulous and corrupt, for Augustine,
discussing superstitions in Epistle 2 *to Januarius*, says that a custom arose
at Rome from a rashly conceived opinion that they should not fast on the day on
which Peter gained that victory.

Objection 5. Peter died at Rome, as his tomb and the pilgrimages made to it
teach. Therefore.

Response. It does not follow: he was buried there, therefore he died there,
because even they concede that the bodies of many saints were transferred. 2.
We taught above in Argument 2, according to Jerome and others, that Peter and
Paul died at Jerusalem. 3. What they say about Peter’s body is no more to be
believed than what they say about the head of John the Baptist, Mary’s milk,
Christ’s cross, the limbs of the apostles, and the head of Dionysius. Finally,
it does not follow: no one can say in what other place Peter’s body is,
therefore it is at Rome. Similarly, no one could say where the body

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 71 | printed col. 144 | bsb10175568]

of Moses was buried; therefore it was not buried.

Objection 6. The Roman Church has always been regarded as the chief of all the
others. But no reason for this excellence can be given except that the prince
of the apostles was its own pastor and bishop. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, because Theodoret, in his *Epistle to Pope
Leo*, when he gathers the causes of the Roman Church’s preeminence, says
nothing about Peter’s episcopate, but speaks of the city’s size, beauty,
multitude of inhabitants, and faith, concerning which the apostle speaks in
Romans 1:8. Finally, it does not follow: the cause of its dignity was in Peter;
therefore it was because he was its own bishop of Rome. For it could have
proceeded from the fact that that Church was founded by an apostle, just as for
this reason other churches were called apostolic.

Objection 7. If Peter was not bishop of Rome until his death, let them teach
where he sat after he left Antioch, because he did not always remain there. But
they cannot do this. Therefore.

Response. Peter sat neither at Antioch nor anywhere else as bishop any more
than Paul—because he remained at Rome—was bishop of Rome. Bellarmine therefore
falsely asserts that we say Peter was bishop of a particular church. For he was
an apostle and therefore was not bound to a particular church. Thus Epiphanius,
*Heresy* 27, says that the apostles were often sent away to other lands and that
Rome could not be without a bishop; therefore Linus and Cletus were appointed
bishops at Rome by Paul and Peter. Finally, Tertullian assigns Paul a see among
the Corinthians, yet Paul was not bishop there, because he did not lay down the
apostleship.

Objection 8. All the ancients testify that Peter was bishop at Rome. Therefore.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because some maintain that Peter was the
first bishop; others do not number Peter among the bishops, but name Linus as
the first bishop; and others differ among themselves, because some maintain
that Linus, others Cletus, and others Clement was the first of the

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 72 | printed col. 145 | bsb10175568]

bishops, as was shown above. Therefore.

Objection 9. Certain Fathers call the Roman Church Peter’s see and chair.
Therefore.

Response. The inference “therefore he was bishop at Rome” is denied, because
they say it is called his chair because they think it was founded by Peter, as
Tertullian teaches in *On Prescription*, chapter 36. Thus Paul is said to have a
chair at Corinth. Indeed, Theodoret, in his *Epistle to Pope Leo*, places Peter
and Paul in the Roman see because they had founded it.
