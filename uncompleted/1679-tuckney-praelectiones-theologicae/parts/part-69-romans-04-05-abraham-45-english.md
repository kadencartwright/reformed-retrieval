In the fourth place, Bellarmine concedes that “our position would be
correct if we meant only that Christ's merits are imputed to us because
they have been given to us, and that we can offer them to God the Father for
our sins, since Christ took upon himself the burden of making satisfaction
for us and reconciling us to God the Father. But that Christ's righteousness
is imputed to us in such a way that through it we are formally called and
made righteous,” he contends, “conflicts with right reason. For although a
designation is sometimes derived from an extrinsic form, in the way a thing
is called known through the knowledge that is in the mind, and so forth,
nevertheless, when two contrary forms are present in something, one
inherent and the other extrinsic, the absolute designation is undoubtedly
taken from the inherent form rather than the extrinsic. If someone dressed
an Ethiopian in a white garment, he would not correctly say, ‘This
Ethiopian is white,’ but would correctly say instead, ‘This Ethiopian is
black,’ because his own inherent blackness belongs to him more than the
acquired external whiteness. So if you clothe a person who is truly ungodly
and unrighteous through inherent unrighteousness with Christ's righteousness
through apprehension and thought, you will not correctly pronounce him
righteous, but will most correctly call him ungodly and unrighteous.”

Many things must be answered.

1. First, Bellarmine hands us the token of surrender when he concedes that
our position is correct if by justification from the imputation of Christ's
righteousness we mean only that Christ's merits are imputed to us because
they have been so given to us for the satisfaction of divine justice and
our reconciliation with God that we can offer them to God for our sins.
This is precisely what we mean; indeed, it is all we mean: namely, that from
Christ's righteousness, obedience, or merits im-

puted to us we receive deliverance from guilt and punishment, forgiveness
of sins, and acceptance into God's favor and eternal life. We maintain and
assert that the true and proper nature of justification consists in these
things.

But Bellarmine and the other Roman theologians wish justification to
consist in the infusion of inherent righteousness. When they assume this
silently and then prove against us that justification does not consist in
imputation, they beat the air in an inept war of words. They invent an
adversary who does not exist among us. Who among our writers was ever so
insane as to say that really inherent righteousness is imputed? None of us
denies that the form by which someone is intrinsically righteous, and is so
called, is an inherent habit or quality of righteousness. But we deny—and
have sufficiently disproved above—that the word “justification” is used in
this sense in Paul's dispute against Jews and Gentiles in Romans, against
false brothers in Galatians, or even in James. Arminius is correct on this
point.

In this meaning of the word “justification,” we do indeed oppose them and
their position, which adds their own merits to Christ's merits imputed to
us. But if they ask who affirms that the inherent righteousness in which
they falsely place the form of justification is imputed to us for
righteousness, they will find not us at all but rather themselves, with
whom they may fight if they please.
