### Chapter II

#### First argument

Page 117.

In this argument the first question is proposed in a discrete axiom:
“Although Christ is said to have died for all with respect to the sufficiency
of the price—and on that basis he is also offered to people generally—yet
with respect to real efficacy he did not die for all and each.” The reason is
then appended: because Scripture, when it says that he died for all, for his
own sheep, for his church, and for those whom

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 114 | printed p. 104]

the Father gave him, and so forth, explains itself elsewhere by teaching that
by “many” it means believers (John 3:16; Acts 10:43; Rom. 3:22; 19:4), those
who obey God (Heb. 5:9), or those who are sanctified (Heb. 10:14). But it
nowhere says that reconciliation and remission of sins were obtained through
Christ for all and each.

The Remonstrants bring forward many things here which might perhaps appear
difficult, but will not trouble someone who looks at them rightly. 1. “When
Scripture says that Christ died for all, it does not add that limitation,
‘with respect to sufficient power’; this gloss therefore goes outside the
text.” As though the passages which they interpret with the aid of
“procurement,” an “opened door,” or similar formulas devised by the
Remonstrants made express mention of them; or as though an interpreter did
not discharge his trust just as well when he derives the meaning of a passage
from the analogy of faith and comparison with other passages. 2. They carve
out a contradiction from these two statements brought into collision: “Christ
died for all,” and, “It was not the Father’s design that he should die for
all.” But this apparent opposition is not found within the terms of this
argument; and if it occurs elsewhere, they still ought to have mentioned it
anywhere else rather than here. For these expressions can very easily be
explained here so that no appearance of contradiction remains. Namely,
“Christ died for all” means that Christ died in such a way that the dignity
and worth of his death must be said to be in itself sufficient for all. “It
was not the Father’s design that he should die for all” means—to retain the
argument’s words—that he should actually obtain remission of sins and
reconciliation with God for all and each person and restore them to the state
of grace. The contradiction, therefore, is invented. They say that they will
show the absurdity of this exposition by a comparison which is no less
absurd. They institute a comparison of this kind: just as a physician or a
prince who advertised that he possessed medi—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 115 | printed p. 105]

—cine or money sufficient for all the sick and all captives, while meanwhile
they did not intend to free everyone, could not justly boast of it, so it
cannot stand that Christ’s death should be called sufficient for all unless
the Father’s will was that it be expended upon all. But 1. what proportion is
there here between the boasting of vain people and the agreement of two
axioms which, at least as they are proposed here, do not have even the
slightest appearance of any boast? 2. If a responsible physician commended a
medicine under this description—that it sufficed to heal any disease—and did
so to spur and inflame certain beloved people to ask for it, take it, and even
receive it freely, although he did not offer that medicine to all and each,
having just grounds for what he did, then I say that the commendation would
be both true and just. The same must be thought of a king and prince. 3. I
affirm that it is absurd to suppose that the sufficiency of any price or
medicine arises from the will of the person paying or giving it. For
sufficiency is the intrinsic power and fitness of a thing; it does not flow
from the extrinsic act of someone ordaining or directing it. 4. Since they
want to conduct the matter with comparisons and examples, come then: let them
tell me whether God does not have sufficient power to create more worlds, to
bring this one world to ruin more often or sooner, and, finally, to form more
creatures. They will not deny it, I hope; in any event the prophet affirms it
in Malachi 2:15. Nevertheless, it is not God’s will that his power should be
expended in effecting these things. Was not Adam’s fall also sufficient to
destroy perpetually not only those descendants who have already arisen or
will arise from him, but also very many others, if only they were born from
him? Nevertheless, neither God nor Adam himself willed that it should happen
in that way. These two statements, therefore, agree perfectly with each other
and abide in one seat without any conflict.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 116 | printed p. 106]

They hurl at the truth a fiery and, as they think, inextinguishable dart from
hell: “If Christ can be said to have died for all because his passion was
sufficient to take away the sins of all people, then he can also be said to
have died for the devils, for the brethren will not dare to deny that his
passion was sufficient even for the devils.” They will not dare? What, I ask,
would they fear? What is not *hikanon*—fit and suitable—is not sufficient. A
large part of Christ’s fitness to take away human sins was that he assumed
the likeness of sinful flesh (Rom. 8:3). Since, therefore, he did not assume
the nature of angels (Heb. 2:16), he neither had a suitable condition nor a
passion sufficient to take away the sins of evil angels. “If that is true,”
they say, “what a disgrace to Christ!” None at all—unless they say that it
tends to diminish the guilt and disgrace of the first Adam that his fall was
not sufficient to condemn the devils. “How, then, is his passion said to
suffice for many thousands of worlds?” In exactly the same way that Adam’s
fall can truly be said to suffice to condemn very many worlds: worlds not of
devils, of course, but of human beings, if they were members from his members
and flesh from his flesh.

So much for the question as it was proposed in this argument. The argument
itself follows, where they first propose a common reply and afterward apply
the same reply to the individual phrases and passages contained in the
argument.

That common reply is this: when Christ is said to have died “for all,” this is
understood of the procurement of reconciliation; but when he is said to have
died “for many,” “for the sheep,” “for the church,” and so forth, then
participation in it, or enjoyment of it, must be understood. But 1. it was
proved above, when the state of this question was discussed, that this
distinction is worthless. 2. If participation and application contain in
themselves a just ground for the phrase

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 117 | printed p. 107]

in question, so that it can properly be said, “He died only for certain
people,” then sufficiency by equal right also contains in itself a just ground
for the same manner of speech, and it is permissible to affirm, “Christ died
for all insofar as his death is sufficient for all.” For no reason can be
given why power should not be said to be “for” those to whom it can extend,
just as an effect or act is “for” those whom it reaches. Indeed, this
particle, “for,” much more properly designates power than act, for by its own
nature and in common usage it indicates a relation to a possible future. 3.
If Christ died in a special and proper manner for some rather than others,
then God, in appointing him to death, and Christ, in undertaking it, had a
special regard for some rather than others. This can in no way agree with the
order of decrees which Arminius invents, in which Christ was appointed to
death in so indeterminate a manner that no one could be regarded in that act
as elect and to be saved. Nor can it agree with Nicolaas’s procurement, which
he contends is separated from every consideration and intention of
application; nor, finally, with that vague intention which the Remonstrants
attribute to Christ in such a way that, when he was now laying down his life,
they teach that he regarded the salvation of any disciple or of the blessed
Mary no more than that of Pharaoh or of anyone long since condemned.

Among their replies to the particular testimonies, I find only three that are
pertinent. 1. “In Scripture ‘many’ sometimes denotes all; the reasoning,
therefore, ‘he died for many, therefore not for all,’ is weak.” I indeed would
not dispute this if that bare word constituted the whole argument. But since
those “many” are described elsewhere—as is also added in the argument—by
marks which are not common to all and each person, the most suitable
interpretation appears to be the one which Jer—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 118 | printed p. 108]

—ome approves on Matthew 20: “He did not say ‘for all,’ but ‘for many,’ that
is, for those who will be willing to believe.” 2. “Almost all the other
passages treat of real application and are therefore cited beside the point.”
I do not deny that real application is treated in those passages. But it is
also clear that they denote a particular intention of that application and
that this intention was present before God as he ordained and before Christ
as he died; otherwise this application would not occur according to the
intention of God and Christ in that work, but apart from or contrary to it.
There is also a certain real application made before God in the propitiation
itself. For just as God really applied Christ’s mediation, not yet actually
consummated, to those who believed in him as one who was to come, so he has,
in a similar real manner, applied that same mediation, now actually
consummated, to those who did not yet actually believe. Nor is this
application before actual faith imperfect because of any imperfection in God
who applies it, but because of a deficiency in the person who receives it.
The sun, actually shining, illuminates a closed house just as much as an open
one, but the house does not receive that light unless it has been opened. No
greater difference is granted between that application which was being
perfected in Christ’s sacrifice and its correlative which occurs in a person
actually believing. 3. “Our sanctification is the end of redemption which
Christ expects as the fruit of his passion (2 Cor. 5:15), but,” they say,
“Christ does not always attain it.” Yet if our sanctification is the proper
end of this death, then procurement alone is not; application is then the
proper aim of that procurement, and consequently, in the intention of God and
Christ, the work of redemption did not pertain equally to all. Then also
God’s proper will for our salvation and sanctification was prior in order
to Christ’s satisfaction. For properly to intend is to will to arrive at one
thing through another. These are points which the Remonstrants are neither
accustomed to acknowledge nor able to concede without the ruin of their whole
cause. As for the further claim that God’s intention is sometimes not
fulfilled

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 119 | printed p. 109]

but that the contrary often occurs: if they mean his proper intention, this
is more than a monster and portent in theology. Even the pagan Varro was so
religious concerning the divine intention that he once wrote that portents
themselves do not occur contrary to nature, because they occur by the divine
will, since the will of the creator of each created thing is its nature. From
this Isidore also concludes at 12.3 that a portent does not occur contrary to
nature, but contrary to nature as it is known. But if nothing even among
portents occurs contrary to nature’s intention, faith and piety certainly do
not permit us to think that anything in the sphere of grace occurs contrary
to God’s proper intention. Indeed, God’s intention must no more be thought to
go astray than to be deceived. Finally they add that those words, “those who
live,” can be taken of all living people, as was stated a little earlier when
it was said that he died for all. Here no other refutation is needed than the
one contained in the context at verse 15: “he who died for them and rose
again.” This expression cannot be extended to any others than those for whom
Christ rose; and the Remonstrants themselves concede and believe that the
resurrection pertains only to believers, on pages 188–89.
