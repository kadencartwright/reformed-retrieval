CHAPTER IV. On the distinction between a bishop and a presbyter.

1. Bellarmine. *The question arises whether a presbyter is altogether the same
as a bishop, especially by divine law.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n304 | printed p. 87]

Protestants. I. We institute this question not *especially*, but only,
*concerning divine law*. Human law ought to keep silent in theology where
divine truth and will are under consideration. This question must be understood
of presbyters *who labor in word and doctrine*, 1 Timothy 5:17, just as bishops
are also described in Eusebius, book 7, chapter 13, οἱ τῶν λόγων προεστῶτες,
“those who preside over the word.” For Origen testifies that in the primitive
Church there was also another kind of presbyters who attended only to
government, in book 3 *Against Celsus*, as it is found in his *Philocalia*,
chapter 18. There, when Celsus reproaches Christian teachers as though they
sought foolish hearers, Origen replies, among other things, that Christian
teachers first discern and examine their hearers, προβασανίσαντες, and
from among the approved establish two orders, one of beginners (who are called
catechumens by others), the other of the perfected. And among the more perfect,
he says, there are τινὲς τεταγμένοι πρὸς τὸ φιλοπευστεῖν τοὺς βίους, ἢ τὰς
ἀγωγὰς τῶν προσιόντων, and so forth; that is, in Tarinus's translation: *Some
are set over them to inquire into the life and conduct of those who are
admitted, so that they may prohibit from the common assembly those who commit
shameful things, but may heartily embrace those who shrink from them and make
them better every day.*

2. Bellarmine. *It was the ancient heresy of Aërius that there is no difference
between a bishop and a presbyter.*

Protestants. I. No mention of a heresy of this kind is found in the councils or
even in ecclesiastical history. II. On Epiphanius's authority Augustine assigns
this title to this opinion among others, but in the preface he admits that it is
uncertain what the *regular definition of a heretic* might be. Now in
Epiphanius's time Aërius was called a heretic not because of that opinion, but
because of the separation which he made together with it. For thus the Fathers
of the First Council of Constantinople say in the *sixth canon*, which is canon
169 in the code of canons: *By heretics we mean those who were formerly cast
off by the Church and those who were afterward anathematized by us; besides
these, also those who, προσποιούμενοι, pretend to profess the sound faith, but
separate themselves and hold congregations against our canonical bishops.*
Aërius was
perhaps among the last of these with respect to this opinion and his manner of
defending it. But Bellarmine himself admits and proves in chapter 15 that the
defense which Epiphanius opposed to Aërius's opinion and arguments was
incompetent. His authority therefore has little force in this matter. III. If
this opinion is numbered among heresies by the Pontificians, this is not to be
wondered at. For in the papacy a heretic is defined as *one who does not
believe all those things which are delivered as true in the Roman Church*,
distinction 19, chapter *Nulli*. It was once heresy if anyone affirmed that
there were antipodes. Aventinus, *Annals*, book 4.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n305 | printed p. 88]

3. Bellarmine. *The Catholic Church teaches that by divine law the episcopate
is greater than the presbyterate, both in the power of order and also in
jurisdiction. Council of Trent, session 23; the theologians on book 4 of the
Sentences, distinction 24; Thomas, II-II, question 184, article 6.*

Protestants. I. It is remarkable that this is a doctrine of the Catholic
Church, since it cannot be declared from any council except the Council of
Trent, composed of the Pope's sworn slaves, nor from any theologians except the
Schoolmen. II. The Council of Trent itself did not define that bishops are
superior to presbyters *by divine law*, although the Spaniards strove with all
their might to have a canon framed with those words. They do indeed say that
the hierarchy was instituted *by divine ordination*, but the legates
fraudulently inserted that phrase at the suggestion of Laínez, general of the
Jesuits, who devised a distinction between *divine law* and *divine
ordination*. For he said that those things are of divine law which depend upon
God alone, universally as well as particularly and at every time; but that
those things are of divine ordination which come from God alone universally,
while men execute the particulars. *History of the Council of Trent*, book 7.
III. The Schoolmen say absolutely nothing about *divine law* with respect to
the power proper to bishops. They do indeed customarily ascribe a certain
dignity and office to the bishop above the presbyter, just as they do also to
the archpresbyter, archbishop, metropolitan, primate, and patriarch. Yet among
all the older Schoolmen not even one is found who teaches that those differences
are derived from *divine law*. Durandus, on book 4, distinction 24, question 7,
indicates clearly enough that in his judgment the bishop's superiority depends
upon ecclesiastical ordination, not divine institution; and in question 6 he
expressly teaches that a bishop is nothing other than *a perfected or complete
presbyter*. Yet imperfect pastors are not such by divine law, for it requires
of them that they all *fulfill their ministry*, Colossians 4:17. This whole
mystery is explained very well by Cardinal Cusanus, *On Catholic Concord*, book
2, chapter 13: *All bishops, and perhaps presbyters also, are equal in power as
to jurisdiction, though not as to its execution. The exercise of execution is
confined and restricted under certain positive enactments, and so forth. Hence,
when the cause of that statute ceases, those positive laws then cease.*

Bellarmine's arguments.

4. Bellarmine. *A bishop is greater than a presbyter in the power of order, and
that by divine law.*

Protestants. Bellarmine does not dare say that a bishop is superior with
respect to order, because all the Schoolmen have taught the contrary. He
therefore changes the phrase and

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n306 | printed p. 89]

wants him to be superior *with respect to the power of order*. But one who is
not superior in order cannot be superior in the power of order, because by the
nature of the thing and by divine law, order and the power of order advance
with equal step or rank, so that order is defined by its right and power. And
certainly, since every power of order is the power of the keys, and the keys
are delivered to bishops in no higher degree than to presbyters, it cannot be
that the former possess a greater power of order by divine law.

5. Bellarmine. *Only a bishop can ordain presbyters, as witnessed by
Epiphanius, Damasus, the Councils of Ancyra and Antioch, Chrysostom, Theodoret,
Theophylact, Oecumenius, and Jerome.*

Protestants. I. Divine law is what is promulgated by God. But God did not first
promulgate his law through these witnesses; therefore their testimony is not a
sufficient proof unless they speak from the Scriptures. II. They testify
nothing about divine law. III. They do not even testify to the practice except
long after the Church's first and divinely established form. IV. The Council of
Ancyra testifies to the contrary: presbyters can ordain, but *not in another's
parish without the bishop's letters*. Speaking of his own time, Jerome makes a
presbyter equal to a bishop in all things except ordination. But in the
judgment of Marsilius of Padua, *Defender of the Peace*, discourse 2, chapter
15, *ordination does not signify the conferral of sacred orders, but the
administrative power of regulating or directing rites*. The remaining
authorities either have nothing pertinent to the matter or speak from the
prejudice and custom of their own times.

6. Bellarmine. *That it is by divine law that only bishops ordain is inferred
from the fact that what those who were not true bishops did in this matter was
held to be invalid. Gregory III and Damasus.*

Protestants. I. An inference does not follow from what *was held* by certain
persons, and those not the most ancient, to what *is by divine law*. II.
Ordinations *without a title* were also held invalid, Council of Chalcedon,
canon 6, and distinction 70, chapter *Sanctorum*; so were those performed
*without three bishops together with the metropolitan, through vicars of
bishops, or, finally, by bishops themselves outside their own dioceses without
permission*. Yet the Pontificians will not pronounce such ordinations invalid
by divine law.

7. Bellarmine. *Only bishops, as the ordinary ministers, can confirm the
baptized, as well as consecrate temples and altars. Acts 8.*

Protestants. I. We are not concerned about the consecration of temples and
altars. Whitewashed walls can be the sole consecrators of walls and stones. But
confirmation of the baptized, insofar as it contains anything of divine law
and is considered on that basis, belongs at least equally to presbyters and
bishops. This can be proved sufficiently from the canon law itself,

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n307 | printed p. 90]

distinction 95, and is demonstrated by Jerome's reasoning: *What is greater
than Christ? Or what can be preferred to his body and blood?* II. The apostles
in Acts 8 were not bishops, nor were they sent to perform ordinary
confirmation.

8. Bellarmine. *Bishops are in the Church what the high priests were in the Old
Testament; presbyters among us are what the lesser priests were there. But the
high priests there were greater than the priests in jurisdiction; therefore
bishops too are greater than presbyters.*

Protestants. I. Earlier, in his work on the Pontiff, Bellarmine sought to prove
the singular power of his Pope from the figure and example of the Jewish high
priest, but here in place of the one Pope he substitutes an infinite number of
bishops. He therefore himself destroys all the force both of that figure and of
his own argument. II. The Jewish high priest was a figure of Christ, not
of the Pope or of bishops. III. If a bishop of the same kind as the Jewish high
priest had been instituted in the New Testament, then all the things which
pertain singularly to him would have been delivered to us in the New Testament
just as clearly and distinctly as everything concerning the Jewish high priest
was delivered in the Old Testament. But no sane person will affirm this.

Bellarmine. *No reasoning allows the hierarchy to have been more distinct in
the Old Testament than in the New.*

Protestants. I. The ministry is more distinctly divided into pastors, doctors,
elders, and deacons than it would be if the addition of a papal bishop made all
the rest his servants. II. The same reasoning allows the difference of
administrations in the New Testament to be different from that in the Old,
which willed that different sacred things be administered, that there be a
different outward form of the Church and different laws, saying nothing about
a similar Jewish distinction. III. But what Bellarmine intimates by this
argument, that a bishop is distinguished from a presbyter by the power of
jurisdiction, is entirely contrary to the facts. For 1. jurisdiction among the
Jews did not rest with one high priest. 2. *Titular, or ringed, bishops* are
created by the Pope who have the power of episcopal order without any
jurisdiction and carry out within other bishops' dioceses those things which
belong to order. Azor, part 2, book 3, chapter 29. Jurisdiction therefore is not
essential to a bishop. 3. Johannes Filesacus, a Parisian theologian, clearly
proves from the common canon law itself that presbyters—parish priests or
plebans—have jurisdiction and the power of excommunication, in his commentary
on the authority of bishops, from the title *On the Office of the Ordinary
Judge*, book 1 of the *Decretals*, chapter 3, section 1. 4. Nicholas Cusanus
formerly taught that presbyters are distinguished from bishops not by power,
but by the execution of power, and that by ecclesiastical law alone, *On
Concord*, book 2, chapter 13. In brief: all

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n308 | printed p. 91]

bishops, and perhaps presbyters also, *are of equal power as to jurisdiction,
though not as to its execution; the exercise of execution is restricted under
certain positive enactments*, and so forth. *Hence, when the cause of that
statute ceases, those positive laws then cease.*

9. Bellarmine. *By divine law the apostles were greater than the seventy
disciples; but bishops succeed the apostles, and presbyters the seventy
disciples. Therefore bishops are greater than presbyters by divine law.*

Protestants. I. Unless in the minor premise bishops are said to succeed the
apostles in such a way that they also are apostles, or obtain the same rank as
they, and that by divine law, the syllogism is formally false and concludes
nothing. II. But if the minor premise is understood in this way, it is denied
as manifestly false. Bellarmine himself has these words in *On the Pontiff*,
book 4, chapter 25: *Bishops do not properly succeed the apostles, since the
apostles were not ordinary but extraordinary pastors, like legates, and such
persons have no successors. Bishops possess no part of true apostolic
authority. Bishops succeed the apostles in the same manner as presbyters
succeed the seventy-two disciples. But it is evident that presbyters do not
properly succeed them, but only by way of likeness. For those seventy-two
disciples were not presbyters and received no order or jurisdiction from
Christ.* IV. Just as the apostles had no successors in apostolic authority, by
Bellarmine's admission, neither could they have any successors in episcopal
order, because the apostles never had any such order. *The apostles and bishops
existed in the Church at the same time*, Bellarmine says in the same place,
*but one properly succeeds only a predecessor.* Yet in that part of their work
which is always necessary for the Church, presbyters are in their own way the
successors of the apostles. Bishops as they exist nowadays are in no way such.
If some of the Fathers play with comparisons of this kind while looking to the
usage of later times, this does not touch divine law. For, as Bellarmine admits
in chapter 15, the Fathers sometimes say that presbyters occupy *the place of
the apostles*, indeed, of *Christ himself*.

10. Bellarmine. *Matthew 24:25, “The faithful and prudent servant,” and so
forth: Hilary and the other Fathers understand these words to have been spoken
of bishops.*

Protestants. I. In *On the Pontiff*, book 1, chapter 9, Bellarmine contends that
these same words are to be understood *of Peter alone*, to the exclusion of all
others, even the apostles themselves. How then can they be extended to all
bishops? II. If some Fathers apply these words to bishops, it does not follow
that they properly pertain to them. Origen and Remigius apply them to all good
preachers; Chrysostom even to secular powers; and Lyranus, finally, to all.
III. If they properly pertained to bishops, nevertheless nothing relevant to
the question would follow from them.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n309 | printed p. 92]

11. Bellarmine. *Acts 20:21: “The Holy Spirit constituted you bishops to govern
the Church of God.”*

Protestants. These were bishops of the kind of whom the one Ephesian church
then had many, that is, presbyters, as they are also expressly called in verse
17: *he summoned the presbyters of the Church*. By this one passage the entire
episcopal cause is slain, as the ordinary gloss itself observes on these words
from Jerome.

12. Bellarmine. *If bishops presided over presbyters by human law, surely some
beginning of this institution after apostolic times would be found.*

Protestants. I. The consequence would have to be denied, even if it were
understood of Tertullian's century, for he makes the first mention of a supreme
priest. Tertullian himself could not discover the beginning of various rites
whose *origin he claims to be tradition and whose confirmation custom*, *On the
Soldier's Crown*, chapters 3 and 4. Yet the Pontificians themselves will not
affirm that those rites are of divine law. But to demand that we, who are
further removed from the beginning by so many centuries and destitute of the
annals of those times, should either discover the certain beginning of every
ancient custom or acknowledge those same customs as divine institutions is
most unjust. The Pontificians do not define the beginning of archbishops,
primates, patriarchs, and so forth, yet they will not therefore say that these
preside over bishops by divine law. 2. To deprive this or that custom of the
protection of divine law, it is enough that its beginning is not delivered in
the Scriptures, which are the complete corpus of divine law.

13. Bellarmine. *In 1 Timothy 5:19 the bishop is made the judge of presbyters.*

Protestants. I. No bishop is mentioned there, but Timothy the evangelist. II.
Nothing is said about a judge armed with ordinary jurisdiction, for an
accusation is received by persons other than a judge. III. Presbyters were to
be judged by presbyters, just as prophets by prophets, 1 Corinthians 14:32,
with Timothy directing the whole proceeding while he was present.

14. Bellarmine. *Ignatius, Clement, Dionysius, Irenaeus, and Tertullian testify
to the same thing concerning their own times.*

Protestants. I. Bellarmine himself admits in his little book *On Ecclesiastical
Writers* that Clement was corrupted by more recent writers and that the
writings of Dionysius were unknown until the time of Gregory. Ignatius is also
of doubtful credit, yet by *Pontiff* he understands Christ. Irenaeus makes no
comparison between bishops and presbyters, but by bishops he understands the
more distinguished presbyters. Tertullian assigns the bishop's presidency *to
the honor of the Church*.

15. Bellarmine. *We read that all councils, even the most ancient, were
celebrated by bishops alone.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n310 | printed p. 93]

Protestants. This is to conquer by impudence, not by arguments. Eusebius
himself, whom Bellarmine cites here, testifies in express words that
*presbyters and deacons* were in the Roman synod, book 6, chapter 43; that
*presbyters and doctors of the brethren in the villages* were present in
another synod with others from among the *brethren*, book 6, chapter 24; and
that in another there were *countless presbyters and deacons*, book 7, chapter
28.

16. Bellarmine. *In many places we read that presbyters were excommunicated by
bishops, but nowhere that bishops were excommunicated by presbyters.*

Protestants. I. Nowhere do we read in the Scriptures that the right to
excommunicate belongs to one pastor, but we read that it belongs to the
assembled congregation, 1 Corinthians 5. II. After bishops had been introduced,
acts of this kind were customarily attributed to them because they presided in
them, not because they alone performed them.

17. Bellarmine. *Only bishops have a seat or throne in the Church, which is a
mark of power.*

Protestants. I. Christian pastors existed for a long time before they had
thrones as though they were insignia of power. II. Aërius objects that
presbyters also had their own seat on the throne, and Epiphanius does not deny
it, *Heresy* 75: *The bishop sits on the throne, and the presbyter also sits.*
Eusebius likewise testifies in book 10, chapter 4, that there were several
thrones in one temple, εἰς τὴν τῶν προέδρων τιμήν, “for the honor of those who
preside.” There were therefore several in one church sitting on thrones and
sharing the same honor and power. The Council of Laodicea, canon 56, also
testifies that presbyters sat with the bishop ἐν τῷ βήματι, that is, as some
translate it, on the tribunals.

Our arguments.

Protestants. Philippians 1:1. The ministers of one church are divided into
*bishops and deacons*.

Bellarmine. *In the time of the apostles, those names, bishop and presbyter,
were common to all priests, although the realities themselves and the powers
were distinct.*

Protestants. I. In the previous chapter, when Bellarmine had to prove a
distinction by divine law between bishop and presbyter, he did not produce even
one passage of Holy Scripture in which those realities and powers are
distinguished. But now he concedes that these names were common. From his own
concessions, therefore, we may conclude that absolutely no distinction either
of names or of powers between bishop and presbyter is delivered in Holy
Scripture. II. If the realities and powers had then been as distinct as they
are now, it certainly would have been just as ridiculous to employ the names
indiscriminately as if someone today were to write to the *Roman bishops and
deacons*. III. If those names had been common, although in reality, according
to Bellarmine's opinion,

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n311 | printed p. 94]

there was only one bishop and several presbyters, it would have been thoroughly
backward to pass over the name and plural number of the presbyters and name the
bishops in the plural. IV. Not a few of the Pontificians themselves acknowledge
the evidence of this testimony to be so great that they are compelled to admit
that the realities themselves, not only the names, were then common. Thus
Cardinal Contarini on that passage: *At that time those who were presbyters were
also bishops; both orders were conferred upon a presbyter.* Dionysius the
Carthusian: *By bishops presbyters are understood, as Haymo asserts. Hence it is
customarily said that in the primitive Church bishops were not distinguished
from priests.* The Jesuit Azor, part 2, book 3, chapter 16: *In the times of the
apostles presbyters who were constituted throughout the cities were at the same
time also bishops.*

19. Protestants. Titus 1:5, 7. Presbyters are bishops.

Bellarmine. *The names were common at that time.*

Protestants. Although a less discerning person who interpreted the preceding
salutation in this way from prejudice could be excused, here, where the reality
itself, the power, and the properties of a bishop are ascribed to the presbyter
in common just as much as the name is, such an evasion is intolerable. II. If
only the names are shared in this place, Bellarmine ought to have told us to
which order the realities found in verses 6, 7, 8, and 9 properly belong.

20. Protestants. 1 Timothy 1:3. The apostle delivers precepts concerning only
bishops and deacons. The bishops were therefore presbyters, or else presbyters
would not have been omitted.

Bellarmine. *Presbyters are also comprehended under the name of bishops, for
Paul wished to instruct the entire clergy generally.*

Protestants. I. The same fiction concerning the name is thrust upon us to the
point of nausea. But grant the sharing of the name in all the passages, and
this clearly implies the sharing of the realities also. II. If only the clergy
in general is instructed here, we demand to be shown the passage where the
bishop as distinct from the presbyter is instructed in particular. III. We
demand a reason why, if the distinction of offices was similar, a bishop is not
distinguished from a presbyter in the New Testament both in name and in
institution just as clearly as a high priest from a lesser priest in the Old.

21. Protestants. 1 Timothy 4:14. The hands of the *presbytery* were laid upon
Timothy. Therefore at that time there were no other bishops greater than
presbyters.

Bellarmine. *By the name presbytery is understood the company or assembly of
presbyters, that is, of bishops, who laid hands upon the new bishop.*

Protestants. I. In conceding that it was an assembly of presbyters, that is,
bishops, Bellarmine, if he dealt candidly,

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n312 | printed p. 95]

would concede the whole argument. II. But if he means that the assembly of
bishops which laid hands upon Timothy consisted of men such as now lay hands
upon a new Pontifician bishop, he does violence both to the word and to the
apostle's meaning. Since Bellarmine produces no example of bishops of this kind
from Scripture, from where does he infer an assembly of such men? Since,
moreover, prophets and doctors, not bishops, laid hands even upon the Apostle
Paul, Acts 13:1–3, nothing is more fitting than that similar prophets and
doctors, together with Paul, blessed Timothy *through prophecy with the laying
on of hands*.

22. Protestants. Acts 20:17, 28 and 1 Peter 5:2. Presbyters and bishops are the
same.

Bellarmine. *The names were common at that time, and therefore in these passages
true bishops are called presbyters.*

Protestants. I. If all those addressed in these passages were true bishops,
then there were many bishops of one and the same city. II. If there was one
person greater than the rest, to whom the rest were *porters*, as Thomas speaks,
or *servants*, as Bellarmine's phrase has it in the preface, then an injury was
certainly done to that very man because he was treated in the same manner as
his servants, without distinction. And the rest were also summoned from
Ephesus to Miletus to no purpose in order to receive commands which properly
pertained to the bishop and not to them, except by the bishop's command and
consent. They could more conveniently have heard the same things from him, and
indeed ought to have done so before they could put any of them into execution.
III. If only the names were common, then certainly neither Paul nor Peter
rightly dispensed the precepts which they delivered to them, for in treating
such distinct offices they commanded nothing which is not common to the lower
order. Thus they gave that greater bishop no instruction peculiar to him and
did not intimate to the inferiors by a single word what obedience they owed
those superiors.

23. Protestants. On Titus 1 Jerome testifies that *bishops are greater than
presbyters more by custom than by the truth of the Lord's appointment, and that
they ought to govern the Church in common*. And in the epistle to Evagrius he
says that *the apostle clearly teaches that those who are presbyters are the
same as those who are bishops*, and that from his words *it is most manifestly
proved that a bishop and a presbyter are the same*.

Bellarmine. 1. *Some piously endeavor to interpret these words of Jerome as
concerning the names bishop and presbyter, not the reality itself. But surely
the words, “The churches were governed by the common counsel of the
presbyters,” and those other words, “Bishops are greater than presbyters more
by custom than by the truth of the Lord's appointment,” and so forth, signify
something other than only a sharing of names.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n313 | printed p. 96]

Protestants. I. This is the new piety, or rather the pious fraud, of the
Pontificians, whereby *with a fiction devised*, as they expressly admit in the
Belgian *Expurgatory Index*, they fasten upon the testimonies of the ancients
*a more convenient sense*, convenient not for the authors themselves but for
the Roman Curia. II. Since Bellarmine admits here that Jerome's testimony does
not permit that fictitious interpretation concerning the *sharing of names*,
he tacitly acknowledges that Jerome is his adversary, along with the
innumerable interpreters who followed Jerome, and indeed the canon law itself,
distinction 95, in that Catholic evasion by which he attempts to mock all the
testimonies of Scripture produced by us in this cause: *The names were common
at that time.* For Jerome adduces all those same testimonies, each and every
one, in his epistle to Evagrius to confirm his own opinion. Therefore, if Jerome
means something other than a sharing of names, it is also necessary that in his
judgment Scripture itself means something else as well.

24. Bellarmine. 2. *The opinion which teaches that Jerome means that all
presbyters were bishops, because those two dignities were bestowed upon all
priests in that first age, does not fully satisfy.*

Protestants. It does not satisfy in reality if episcopacy and presbyterate are
taken as two offices conferred upon one man—which is a second plank for the
shipwrecked hierarchy. Yet it sufficiently indicates that the more moderate
Pontificians acknowledge that the two offices now held separately were joined
in one and the same person in the apostles' time and in his one calling.

25. Bellarmine. 3. *Michael Medina affirms that Jerome held the same opinion as
Aërius and that Ambrose, Augustine, Sedulius, Primasius, Chrysostom, Theodoret,
Oecumenius, and Theophylact were in the same heresy. But this opinion is very
ill-considered.*

Protestants. This confession of Medina is simple and candid as to the substance
of the matter, and Bellarmine refutes it by no sound argument.

Bellarmine. 1. *Jerome everywhere acknowledges that a bishop is greater than a
presbyter with respect to the power of order.*

Protestants. He nowhere acknowledges this concerning divine law or apostolic
times. Nor does he in fact place a bishop above a presbyter in the power of
order, but only in the direction of ordination and similar rites as to their
exercise.

Bellarmine. 2. *Although Jerome does not seem to acknowledge by divine law a
difference between the jurisdiction of a bishop and that of a presbyter, he
nevertheless acknowledges that this difference was introduced from the time
when it was said, “I am of Paul, I of Apollos, and I of Cephas,” or when the
First Epistle to the Corinthians was written, chapter 4, and that it was
introduced by the apostles themselves.*

Protestants. This evasion is far more futile and shameful

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n314 | printed p. 97]

than the first two which Bellarmine himself rejected. For I. Jerome says that
this difference was introduced after people began to say *among the
congregations*, not after it was said *at Corinth*, *I am of Paul, I of
Apollos*, and so forth. He uses the apostle's words as a fitting formula to
denote schism. II. Jerome proves from other epistles, written a long interval
after the first to the Corinthians, that presbyters are equal to bishops, as
Bellarmine observes. III. If this difference had been introduced by the
apostles on the occasion of the schism arising among the Corinthians and as a
remedy for it, Paul would have made some mention of this change, whether to be
made or already made. Yet nowhere does it appear to have come into his mind.
IV. Jerome expressly says that this change was made *by custom*, and in his
epistle to Evagrius he shows that at Alexandria this difference was not
introduced until the time of Heraclas and Dionysius, that is, in the second
century after the Church was gathered and constituted there. He also shows
that even in his own time there was no difference except in *ordination*.

Bellarmine. 4. *Jerome does not appear entirely consistent in that opinion, so
it is very probable that he was not altogether certain.*

Protestants. His words in the epistle to Evagrius are: *The apostle clearly
teaches; it is most manifestly proved; do the testimonies of so many great men
seem insignificant to you?* These are not the words of one in doubt. The things
which Bellarmine ingeniously musters to fasten a suspicion of uncertainty upon
Jerome have no force. Yet if Jerome had been uncertain, of his two opinions the
one which he confirms from the Scriptures ought rather to be embraced, while
that which he admits without the Scriptures should be neglected.

26. Protestants. On Ephesians 4 Ambrose teaches that the chief presbyter was
once called the bishop, and on 1 Timothy 3 that bishops and presbyters have one
ordination.

Bellarmine. 1. *He speaks only of the manner in which bishops succeed.*

Protestants. Then he would have had no cause to say, *The apostle's writings do
not agree in every respect with the ordination which is now in the Church.* He
would also without any reason have produced an example of an episcopal
privilege shared also by presbyters: *Among the Egyptians the presbyters
confirm if the bishop is not present.*

Bellarmine. 2. *On 1 Timothy 3 Ambrose teaches that a bishop is greater than a
presbyter, and that therefore a bishop cannot be ordained by a presbyter,
because a presbyter does not have episcopal ordination.*

Protestants. He teaches none of these things concerning divine law or the
purest and earliest times of the Church, but plainly teaches the contrary.

27. Protestants. In homily 11 on 1 Timothy 3 Chrysostom says that *there is
almost no difference between a bishop and a presbyter*, and so forth. The same
is also said by

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n315 | printed p. 98]

Primasius, Theophylact, and Oecumenius on the same passage.

Bellarmine. 1. *They also testify that bishops can ordain and presbyters cannot.*

Protestants. I. Perhaps by custom and human institution, as Jerome interprets,
not by divine law. II. Chrysostom does not even testify that ordination belongs
to bishops in such a way that presbyters cannot ordain, but that *in ordination
bishops are superior*, that is, have the principal part. III. Whatever is
ascribed to them in ordination above presbyters is, in Chrysostom's judgment,
*almost nothing, and in jurisdiction they are equal*.

Bellarmine. 2. *These authors speak of those things which in no way belong to
presbyters, and there is nothing of this kind except ordination, especially of
priests.*

Protestants. I. They speak generally: *Almost nothing; ordination alone.* II.
Not even of ordination can it be said that it in no way belongs to presbyters,
as Bellarmine appears to have understood well enough from his addition,
*especially of priests*. But in fact this is not added. For, as Durandus
testifies in the *Rationale*, book 2, chapter on the priest: *According to
canonical tradition, when a presbyter is ordained and the bishop blesses him
and holds his blessing hand over his head, all the presbyters who are present
should also hold their hands over his head beside the bishop's hands, invoking
the Holy Spirit.*

28. Protestants. On Philippians 1 Theodoret repeatedly says that those whom
Paul calls bishops were presbyters.

Bellarmine. 1. *He indeed teaches that they had the name of bishops, but that at
that time true bishops were customarily called by the name apostles.*

Protestants. He teaches the first point in such a way that anyone with
understanding can see that he teaches from the Scriptures concerning not only
the name, but also the reality itself. But he delivers the latter point in such
a way that Bellarmine himself did not dare to take so futile an observation
from him and urge it among his own arguments.

Bellarmine. 2. *The same author teaches on 1 Timothy 3 that bishops are those
who seek the greater dignity.*

Protestants. Not by divine law.

29. Protestants. Augustine says thus to Jerome in epistle 19: *Although in
accordance with the titles of honor which the usage of the Church has now
obtained, the episcopate is greater than the presbyterate, nevertheless in many
things Augustine is inferior to Jerome.*

Bellarmine. *The sense is this: before Christian times those names, bishop and
presbyter, were not titles of honor, but now they are. According to these names
Augustine is greater than Jerome, however much in many other respects Augustine
may be inferior to Jerome.*

Protestants. I. Bellarmine makes Augustine idly and ineptly

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n316 | printed p. 99]

philosophize about the use of names which existed before all Christian times.
II. There was no such use of these names before Christian times without a
signification of honor. III. He makes Augustine's proposed aim to have been to
proclaim himself greater than Jerome, which is plainly contrary to his
intention. In sum, Bellarmine trifles in such a way as to make Augustine mad.
