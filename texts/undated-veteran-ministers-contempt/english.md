# When Disturbances Had Unfortunately Arisen in the Church from Contempt for a Veteran Minister

*A fresh translation of the complete surviving Latin presentation at CO X/1
218–220. The long title and page notices are editorial. Although the requested
directory slug says “undated,” the body is internally dated Geneva,
22 December 1551.*

[CO X/1, p. 218]

It grieves me greatly, dearest brothers, that I am not instead offered an
occasion to congratulate you on the flourishing condition of your church, but
must tell you how painfully I have borne the sad reports about you that have
reached me. The news of the death of that excellent brother N. was already
bitter. In him the Lord had given you an exceedingly suitable and truly
faithful pastor—a thing rarer especially in these times, when few are found
who devote themselves sincerely to the Lord's work. I do not doubt that by
now you have learned by experience both how useful his service was and how
uprightly he conducted himself among you.

Nevertheless, I hope that the man you have now elected will likewise render
faithful service, according to the grace of God bestowed upon him, so that you
will not be left destitute. Only see to it that others are not meanwhile left
bereft. I fully hope you will do so, since I think these matters are being
handled by common consent between you and those brothers, and their straits
appeal to your faithfulness.

What has impelled me to write, however, is the distress of mind that your
contentions and quarrels cause me, for I see no end to them. I thought
everything had been settled and had given thanks to God on that account,
especially when I had learned from that excellent brother N., a competent
witness to these matters, that this was how things stood. What gives that
wound of mine its bitterness is that I now hear an evil to be thriving which
I had heard was extinguished.

Previously the issue concerned certain things you found lacking in brother N.
A remedy had been applied to them which, in my judgment, ought to have
satisfied you adequately, especially since he had personally submitted to
censure. Now I hear that a new dispute has arisen: most refuse to regard him
as pastor unless he is ordered to withdraw from the ministry and submit to a
new election. I cannot keep silent. In my view, those who cut everything so
close to the quick, in a way that contributes nothing to the church's
edification, are the authors of bad counsel.

I do not in fact know how he was first installed as pastor there and began to
exercise the ministry. If an election had taken place in its proper form,
there would today be no ground for dispute. But let us grant that the matter
was not free from some defect. Consider meanwhile: when someone comes forward
in a foreign country and asks to be given a place and opportunity to gather
the Lord's flock, should not those who are there and first consent to his
ministry be said to have elected him by the fact itself, even if no rites
accompany it at the time? It seems to me full of ingratitude if, with the
passage of time, the man through whose service a church was established is
rejected, and now

[CO X/1, p. 219]

no consideration is shown for the man who himself opened the way and means by
which the church was first gathered in the Lord through his ministry.

I acknowledge that once an order has been established, it must be maintained
as fixed and unaltered. But the case is entirely different where not even the
slightest beginnings of a church exist. For what shall we say about all those
churches established for the Lord throughout Germany? Were not those who first
devoted their labor to preaching the gospel to them admitted as true pastors
without any rite? I am certainly not thinking of subjecting you to the
authority of men. I offer that example because it serves to confirm the point
I have advanced: an election is not required to take place in the same form
when affairs have not yet been constituted as it is where a church has already
adopted a definite form and order.

Therefore, I can find no sound basis for those difficulties, except that I
judge pretexts are being fetched from far away. Consider, moreover, how deeply
you plunge yourselves if this point is once carried through. For what
judgment will have to be made about all the baptisms of the intervening time?
What about the Supper administered to you in the same way? I shall add no
more, since even these points are more than sufficient to put a bridle on
unrestrained contentions.

But if not even these things satisfy the brothers who, though led by zeal, are
too attached to their own opinions, I ask them by the Lord to consider again
and again into what danger they are bringing the church. You live there as if
on sufferance. When it is noticed that you are so difficult in many matters,
will not your captiousness bring some weariness and estrangement of mind to
those good and godly senators by whom you were received so kindly?

Then, is it not clear to you that all of us are attacked at once in the person
of the man in question by those whose single aim is to crush all of you? It is
surely astonishing that the Guelphs and Ghibellines can bring themselves to
unite against a new enemy who emerges, while you are not led by their example,
although you have been called into one confession of the truth of the gospel.
Those who oppose you on a particular article are already beginning to attack
you so that they may overthrow you at last. That brother stands ready to repel
them and withstands their first assaults. How unworthy it is, then, that he
should moreover be harassed by you too.

Indeed, even the affliction by which he is constrained ought to have been
sufficient to soften the hearts of those who were most bitterly incensed
against him. This is especially so when you consider both the plague sent upon
you by God and the fact that one of your pastors has already been taken away,
as though the Lord now threatens to withdraw your spiritual feeding entirely
when you admit no means by which you may be satisfied. I write these things to
you to extinguish the fire that has been kindled. Yet if anyone still cannot
acquiesce,

[CO X/1, p. 220]

at least I ask you by the Lord to be willing, without any disturbance or
contention, simply to lend an ear to right counsels and then calmly yield to them.
I do indeed hope that the admonition you have here from me will satisfy you;
nevertheless, I did not wish to bar you from that final remedy.

Yet I would much rather wish that, as soon as possible, whatever is wrong be
corrected without delay, so that everyone may grow together into true concord.
If this happens, I tell you now, you will bring great joy to very many of your
brothers. Your welfare and tranquility are so dear to them that, if there is
need, you will find them entirely ready to secure those ends for you.

Farewell, dearest brothers. May the Lord guide you by his Spirit with true
prudence, gentleness, and constancy. My colleagues greet you from the heart.
Geneva, 22 December 1551.

## Translator's notes

1. In the editorial heading, *turbae* (“disturbances”) is the feminine plural
   subject of *subortae essent*, while *ministri veterani* is genitive singular,
   “of a veteran minister.” The English title reflects that syntax.
2. CO says that the French archetype is lost. This translation therefore
   proceeds from the surviving Latin presentation and does not claim to
   reproduce lost French phrasing.
3. “Others ... left bereft” (*alii ... exuti*) most likely concerns a related
   community's loss of pastoral service, but the immediate object is unstated
   and the English preserves that restraint.
4. “Cut everything so close to the quick” renders *omnia ita ad vivum
   resecant*, an image of applying a principle with injurious rigor rather than
   contributing to edification.
5. The writer's sacramental questions are an argument from the consequences
   of denying the veteran minister's original standing; they do not say that
   baptism or the Supper derives its efficacy from a flawless ordination rite.
6. “On sufferance” renders *ut precario*: the congregation's civic welcome is
   revocable, which makes internal quarrels especially dangerous.
7. The exact referent of *illud ultimum remedium* (“that final remedy”) is not
   supplied in this surviving text and is not silently resolved.
8. The English was translated afresh from `latin.txt` and checked against the
   facsimile. The Beaty–Farley translation was not consulted or used for
   wording.
