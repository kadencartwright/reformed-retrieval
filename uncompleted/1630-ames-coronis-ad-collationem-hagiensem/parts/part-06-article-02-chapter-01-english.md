ARTICLE TWO

ON UNIVERSAL REDEMPTION

### Chapter I

#### The state of the question is elicited.

The Remonstrants express this thesis in the following words: “Accordingly,
Jesus Christ died for all and each; and this in such a way that through the
death of the cross he obtained reconciliation and remission of sins for all,
yet on this condition: that no one enjoys that remission of sins except the
person who believes” (John 2:16 [sic; compare 3:16]; 1 John 2:2).

The first and principal statement in this thesis, that Christ died for all
and each, can and customarily is taken in one of two ways. It may denote the
power and worth of this death, namely that it is sufficient and suitable for
saving all and each; in this sense it is acknowledged by our side. Or it may
denote its act and some efficacy regarding all and each indiscriminately,
partly caused by Christ’s death and partly causing the death itself. This is
what the Remonstrants understand here and our side attacks. For between

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 104 | printed p. 94]

sufficient worth and actual efficacy no middle is given. This act and efficacy
must be thought to be produced either in all and each of the people themselves
or in God alone. In people it is application unto the reception of
reconciliation and salvation; in God it is either some procurement—I do not
know what—or an intention of it and of application. Which of these is what the
Remonstrants extend by the force of Christ’s death to all and each—although
they say that they agree well among themselves about this—they either have not
yet explained sufficiently in their writings, or at least, I hope, they will
pardon our slowness when they have heard the ground for our hesitation.

They will not affirm that salvation itself, or eternal life, is actually given
to all and each. But 1. there are some among them who teach that the whole
human race has been received into the grace of reconciliation. These are the
words of Borreus in Arminius’s *Response to the Thirty-one Articles*, articles
13–14. In his reply to Bogerman’s notes, part 2, page 233, Johannes Arnoldi
tries to apply a remedy to them, but a thoroughly miserable one. “The
meaning,” he says, “is that all have been received by God into grace to this
extent: that he entered into a covenant with them by which he offered grace
under the condition of faith to those whom he could justly condemn by a
definitive sentence.” But
what Borreus teaches there is altogether different. He openly denotes that
reception of everyone into grace which requires nothing other than mere
continuance for consummated salvation, or the remission of all sins; that
reception which is of itself effectual and sufficient for the salvation of
all and each of the infants. For this is Borreus’s conclusion: all infants of
all nations who die without actual sins are saved. His ground is that God has
received the whole human race into the grace of reconciliation and entered
into a covenant of grace with Adam and all his posterity on that basis, in
which he promises remission of all sins to all who perse—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 105 | printed p. 95]

—vere in that covenant and do not transgress against it. Nor can the
Remonstrants avoid this in any way when they place all and each of the infants
in such a state that those who depart this life before attaining the use of
reason have certain salvation, unless they either establish that they are
free from every sin and are saved without Christ, or say that Christ is
applied to them all, whence, once the guilt of sin is removed, they are
received into grace which is both actual and saving. They also seem to teach
very clearly that there is some universal application of Christ to all and
each of mortals by teaching that some sufficient grace is communicated to all
and each as universal. For they must either say that this grace is separated
from Christ, which Christian ears will not tolerate, or that Christ together
with grace is communicated in some manner and degree to all and each.

As for the divine acts: 1. the Remonstrants not only deny that an absolute
intention of Christ to apply salvation either to all and each or to certain
people arises from Christ’s death; they also say that it no more agrees with
the conditional nature of application than fire agrees with water. 2. They
establish that this conditional intention to save neither precedes Christ’s
appointment to death nor is the proper end or direct effect of the death
itself. Indeed, they say that once Christ had died, God had attained his end
and that the action was complete and whole in itself even if it were afterward
applied to no one. For they say that once Christ had died it remained entirely
open to God either to apply it or not to apply it. All these things are
contained clearly enough in the order of decrees which Arminius establishes,
in which he has Christ appointed to the cross before God had determined
anything at all about those to be saved through him, whether generally or
particularly, absolutely or conditionally. But they are set forth more clearly

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 106 | printed p. 96]

and expressly by Nicolaas Grevinckhoven on pages 8, 9, 12, and 13. He goes so
far as to make God uncertain, when he was appointing his Son to death, whether
any person would be saved through him or not; and he contends that even after
Christ had died, all mortals could nevertheless have perished. 3. Now only
procurement remains, and that a solitary one, separated not only from
application but also from the intention of application, whether absolute or
conditional. What this procurement was will be especially worth hearing.
“When we say,” the Remonstrants state on page 172, “that Christ obtained
remission of sins for all, our position is that Christ, by satisfying God’s
justice, brought it about that God, without injury to his justice, reopened to
the sinful person the door of his grace, although no one has entered into the
communion of that grace except through faith.” But 1. this phrase, “to open
the door of grace,” appears no less doubtful than the thing itself whose
nature it ought to explain to us. For it is not yet clear in what vestibule
the Remonstrants place this door of grace: whether in some act of God’s will
or in God’s bare power. Nicolaas himself leaves it no place in the divine will
when he says that, after the procurement had been accomplished, the will
remained intact and entirely free either to will or not to will the application
of grace, and under whatever condition he might wish. Christ’s satisfaction,
therefore, does not bring it about that this door of divine benevolence is
opened to us. Power remains, and Nicolaas Grevinckhoven, the best interpreter
of his own and his companions’ mind, does not allow us to remain suspended
there: “The end of procurement here was that God could save the sinner without
prejudice to his justice, and that the sinner could be saved notwithstanding
sin.” They therefore make this door of grace, the sole effect of procurement
and of Christ’s death, consist in the most remote power by which God can, if
he wills, restore us to the state of grace. On their position, then, Christ
must no more be said to have obtained salvation for believers

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 107 | printed p. 97]

than for workers, for, in Nicolaas Grevinckhoven’s judgment on pages 4–5, God
could prescribe this condition to those who are to be saved just as well as
the other. God no more opened the door of grace on account of Christ than he
created many worlds on account of himself, for he is able to do this too. Do
these people not have an admirable view of the effect of Christ’s death and
of our reconciliation with God, whose dignity they complain our side has
diminished? 2. From this it must further be observed that, on the Remonstrants’
principles, the thing obtained through Christ’s death is properly neither our
salvation nor grace that saves us, but God’s freedom and power. It is not so
much that God was reconciled to us by this means as that the freedom and power
were procured for God by which he could now will and effect what previously
he could not even will. These are Nicolaas’s words on page 9. Indeed, neither
the meaning of the term nor the nature of an action of this kind permits
something to be said to have been obtained for us through Christ’s death when,
once that death had been accomplished, it was in no way ours. What is obtained
by merits is immediately owed to that person, or belongs by right and by the
relation it bears to the person for whom it is prepared through that merit
(Rom. 4:4). What is obtained by prayers immediately pertains to those whose
cause the prayers were pleading (1 John 3:22). Whether it is awarded from merit
or from grace, whatever is obtained is immediately granted and accrues to the
benefit of the person whose case is being conducted. Yet no salvation and no
grace was created for any person through Christ’s death—if the Remonstrants
must be heard—so that immediately afterward and by its force he could count it
among his goods or say, “Salvation is mine; the Lord has now returned into
favor with me.” For when Christ’s offering was now whole and complete,
Nicolaas teaches that it was possible that neither salvation nor grace should
ever be awarded to anyone, and that they were never awarded, even
conditionally, except by a new and free decree after

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 108 | printed p. 67 (sic; intended p. 98)]

Christ’s death which did not follow necessarily. Thus they say that these
goods were procured for us while denying that they were accomplished for us.
They have invented a procurement without accomplishment or grant, although in
law a rescript is called “obtained” when the petitioner has secured it: the
act of granting is referred to the prince and obtaining to the party, but
there is no obtaining without a grant and no relative without a correlative.
Indeed, to pursue the metaphor they themselves have chosen, attainment of the
enjoyment of God is called the door of heaven by a likeness to the function of
a door—which is either to impede or to render ready access into the interior.
Thus, to open the door of heaven is to render the attainment of eternal
blessedness accessible, that is, to remove sin with respect to guilt and every
punishment. For the closing of the kingdom of heaven occurs in no other way
than through the barrier and bolt of sin by which we are kept from heaven.
Consequently, the door is not opened unless sin has been removed—namely, from
God’s sight—not with respect to his power but with respect to his will, now
appeased in Christ, and to such an application as can consist in God himself
while not yet actually communicated to us.

Since they teach that this procurement was made through Christ’s death for
all and each, it is remarkable that they take it so badly on pages 170–71 that
the impious, unbelieving, and impenitent are numbered with equal right among
those for whom, they teach, remission of sins was obtained through Christ.
Does not the greater contain the lesser? Is anything predicated of a genus
which does not also agree with each of its species or with any part of it? Or
will they grant that no impious or unbelieving people are found among “all
and each”? They plead none of these things. Nevertheless, they allege three or
four grounds by which they would prove that the brethren’s error did them an
injury when the brethren charged them with what was not theirs. 1. “The thesis
makes no mention of unbelievers and the impenitent.” As if nothing were im—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 109 | printed p. 99]

—puted to our side by them unless it were expressly posited in our theses. In
this very reply they say it is absurdly imputed to them that they say Christ
died for believers and unbelievers “as such,” although that qualification,
“as such,” was never mentioned in our side’s proposition—as if nothing could
be contained in a thesis unless it were mentioned by name. 2. “The thesis
makes no comparison between believers and unbelievers, as though their rights
were equal with respect to redemption through Christ.” But the rule of law is
well known: no distinction is to be made where the law makes none. Since,
therefore, they teach in the thesis that this procurement pertains
indifferently and indiscriminately to all and each, who would not say that all
are established as equals and possess equal right? 3. “Since the passion and
death precede faith and unbelief themselves in order, they think it absurd to
say that Christ died for believers or unbelievers as such.” “As such” is
inserted ambiguously and ineptly. If the rest is absurd, what will become of
Scripture, which expressly says that Christ lays down his life for his sheep
(John 10:11), that is, as the Remonstrants interpret it on page 93, for
believers? Nor does the fact that Christ’s death precedes faith in order pose
any obstacle. For he is said to die for believers, that is, for those who will
be believers, as is openly clear from Ephesians 5:25, where Christ is said to
have given himself for the church, though the church comes to exist by the
power of that death. So too in Hebrews 1:14 angels are said to be sent for the
sake of those who will be heirs of salvation. Nor does piety permit us to
believe that the redemption accomplished by Christ has no efficacy at all
either for removing human unbelief or for abolishing the sins of believers.
Yet it seems that both must be conceded if it is admitted without
qualification that Christ died neither for believers nor for unbelievers.
Moreover, since they teach that Christ’s counsel and intention as he was now
laying down his life was to redeem Pharaoh himself and those long—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 110 | printed p. 100]

—since condemned to hell equally with the Apostles then living—as Nicolaas
does quite openly on pages 46–47—it seems necessary for them to extend this
benefit to unbelievers as well. But grant that this comparison is somewhat
absurd and that the reply “Christ died neither for believers nor for
unbelievers” is true. Nevertheless, what we just saw rejected will follow from
it: that this offering was made equally for unbelievers and believers, for
among nonentities there are no unequal degrees of quantity.

That circumlocution of words which they first placed in the article is
deceptive and captious: “yet on this condition, that no one actually enjoys
that remission of sins except the person who believes.” They repeat it again
in their description of procurement on page 172: “although no one will enter
into the communion of that grace except through faith.” For 1. nothing is
found in the decree of redemption which savors of a condition of this kind.
This decree is absolute, as even Arminius testifies, and there can be no place
in it for faith or a demanded condition, since, on Arminius’s position, faith
was not prescribed or connected with salvation before that second moment in
which he says God decreed to save believers, when he had earlier in order
appointed his Son as Mediator and Redeemer, which belongs to the first decree.
That reconciliation, therefore, is absolute, not conditional, because the
condition is added from outside and not from the decree of procurement. 2. If
these words are joined with those preceding, they openly convey the position
which the Remonstrants had just repudiated as though it were not their own:
“Christ obtained reconciliation and remission for all, but on this
condition,” and so forth. Namely, all and each have been restored to the state
of grace on God’s part by an active donation or institution, although not by
actual possession or passive reception. Thus the difference between
redemption and justification and salvation is like that between an inher—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 111 | printed p. 101]

—itance and being put into possession of or entry upon an inheritance, or
between an inheritance devolved and one entered upon. Someone not yet
born—perhaps a posthumous son or a grandson—is instituted as heir, yet he
cannot enter upon or take possession of the inheritance or intermingle
himself with it until
after he has been born. Thus long ago God instituted the children of Abraham,
or of the promise, as his heirs (Gen. 17:8); yet they cannot take possession
of the inheritance unless actually believing. If the Remonstrants affirm this
of all and each of mortals, as they do clearly enough, then they must concede
that all and each have not merely been received into grace once on God’s part,
but also at last enjoy his benefit and remain in it perpetually, unless they
suppose that the testament of Christ Jesus is sometimes annulled or revoked.
For that rescript concerning reconciliation, once it has been duly obtained,
can never be rejected or disallowed, because it rests upon truth in every
respect and is liable to no exceptions for obreption or subreption—for false
allegation or concealment. And indeed, I do not see how this difficulty can be
avoided by those who contend that reconciliation and remission of sins were
properly obtained for all and each. For if they were obtained for all, they
must also have been granted and given in some manner. A bestowal or grant not
only always accompanies a procurement of this kind but also, by a certain
force of relation, constitutes and perfects it even before mutual acceptance
or *lēpsodosia*, a transaction of receiving and giving. The right and
ownership of a gift results from the donor’s beneficent bestowal; otherwise
every recipient, when he took possession of the thing given, would receive
what was not his. Moreover, there was also someone who accepted in our name
the reconciliation that had been given, namely Christ our advocate. Nor does
it help to say that this gift is made with a certain manner, law, and
condition. For—not to repeat here that the decree of redemption is absolute,
not conditional—even in a modal gift the condition or cause always

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 112 | printed p. 102]

must be understood as subsequent, not preceding, because then it would be a
reward rather than a gift. A gift always takes effect in the present,
although its effect or possession of the gift is sometimes customarily
suspended upon a condition. In this way,
therefore, all and each have both grace and salvation given to them, although
they do not have possession of the thing given before faith. They teach that
a gift of the heavenly homeland was made to all and each like the earthly one
which Abraham’s seed once had. Genesis 1:18 [sic; compare 15:18]: “To your seed
I have given this land.” There we see the manner and law annexed: “They will
be sojourners in a foreign land for four hundred years.” Nevertheless, the
gift was absolute and made in the present, although its execution was delayed
until the four-hundredth year, and they did not enter or enjoy that land
except through faith (Hebrews 3–4). This very thing is implied in the
Remonstrants’ words when they teach that nothing further is needed for the
enjoyment and communion of the grace or salvation procured except entrance
through faith into the door previously opened. There is therefore no need for
God to open any further door through a new reconciliation on his part. This
was once obtained for everyone through Christ. Only let those who will enter,
and they will enjoy it immediately—or, if that seems good, they can also
reject it.

The sum of this controversy, therefore, finally comes down to this: 1. whether
in Christ’s death there is such a procurement as has no application or
proposed intention to apply as its proper end; 2. whether, with respect to
God’s counsel and Christ’s purpose, this procurement pertains equally and
indiscriminately to all and each of mortals; and 3. whether all those for whom
Christ’s death was intended have not had it applied before God, or on God’s
part and by his eternal donation or appointment, so that actual application
will certainly follow from it in its own time. The Remonstrants say: 1. that
God appointed his Son to death before he had appointed anyone to salvation,
and thus while he was still

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 113 | printed p. 103]

uncertain whether anyone would be saved through him or not; 2. that they want
this grace of redemption in itself and by its own nature to be simply
indeterminate, as though it were scattered into the air so that whoever wills
may seize it for himself. And so they want Christ’s death alone, and in
isolation, to pertain to all, but not also his resurrection and intercession;
they admit that these are restricted to believers alone. We say: 1. that
death, resurrection, ascension, session, and intercession must be joined
equally in end and intention, just as they were joined in fact in Christ’s
person; 2. that we believe the fountain of application to consist in the
Father’s eternal decree concerning those for whom he willed to offer Christ,
and then in the will of the Mediator obedient to that decree; 3. that not all
and each are regarded in that decree and intention, but certain elect people;
and 4. that in redemption itself there was a most firm application before
God, and that it is most certainly made in the elect themselves when, by its
power, the teaching of the gospel is sent to them with efficacy, so that by
the Spirit’s operation and by their own faith they now actually enjoy the
reconciliation prepared for them.
