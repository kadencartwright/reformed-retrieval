## Part XIX: Reprobation and the Relation of Contraries

[Part II | PDF page 167 | logical source scan 166 | printed page 165 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

By the same relation of contraries I necessarily proved that the decree of reprobation depends upon God’s will alone, no less and no differently than election.

I said, and affirm again, that a necessary conclusion follows from that most certain axiom, established by the agreement of all sane people: the relation of contraries is the same—that is, if one prefers, their proportion or, as commonly said, correspondence. Since all human beings are enclosed under sin, foreseen faith and good works are not the cause why God determined to have mercy upon some; likewise, foreseen lack of faith and its fruits cannot be the cause why he determined not to have mercy upon some. And since even my adversary confesses that the former has no other cause than God’s will, precisely the same must—

[Part II | PDF page 168 | logical source scan 167 | printed page 166 | ends at a clean internal boundary]

—be determined concerning the latter.

Opposing this—that is, expressly contradicting the Apostle—Andreae says: “The relation is not the same here. For the one and only cause of the ungodly’s destruction is the devil and their own evil will, but the cause of the godly’s eternal salvation is God’s will revealed in Christ.” Certainly true. I do not wish to press that word *will*, by which all who perish outside Christ before mature age could nevertheless appear to be excepted. But what does this answer address? What is this—sophistical malice or ignorance of the art of disputation?

First, as I said shortly before and now repeat, our question is neither about the intermediate causes through which God mercifully saves in Christ those whom he sees fit, nor about their salvation; nor is it about the means through which the rest, whom God sees fit to leave in themselves, go to their own place, nor about their destruction. All sound-minded people versed in God’s Word agree on these matters perfectly and without controversy. We dispute the eternal decree which precedes every subordinate cause on both sides. What, then, does our adversary’s answer address? If this is not *metabainein eis allo genos*—passing into another kind—that is, answering about one thing when asked about another, what is?

Second, how widely different are these two procedures: comparing the contraries themselves and their nature and powers insofar as they oppose each other, which Andreae does in his answer; and examining the contraries insofar as they are divided from one and the same source, which afterward pours itself into opposite streams, and as they flow back together into one stream—finally, considering that, as common sense no less than philosophy dictates, they have the same knowledge and capacity, which is what I affirmed here. In the former consideration, positing one removes the other, and conversely: if someone is elect, he is not reprobate; if the elect are saved through mercy, the justly condemned perish through judgment; if eternal life is given to the elect, eternal death is given to the reprobate.

But in the latter consideration, positing one posits the other: if God has mercy only upon some, he does not have mercy upon others; if God destines some to election and salvation, he destines others to rejection and death; if the decree of election which precedes all causes of salvation is eternal, so is the decree of reprobation. If foreseen faith is not the cause of the decree of election, foreseen lack of faith is not the cause of the decree of reprobation. Rather, just as the former follows the decree of election in the order of causes, the latter follows the decree of reprobation; and just as the former serves the execution of the divine and eternal decree in its time, so does the latter and in the same order. What kind of disputation is it to deny that the relation of contraries is the same where the proportion of contrariety itself is the same—so that positing one posits the other—by taking an argument from the opposition of contraries, by which positing one removes the other? But why say more about this?
