It is asked whether the Remonstrants rightly declaim in this way against what,
according to us, is the necessary perseverance of believers. In article 5,
pp. 276–77, they say: “All you who believe in my Son, and who consequently,
with the undoubted certainty of faith, because I so will and command and
indeed seal your minds to this very thing, are persuaded that you cannot fall
totally and finally from faith through any crimes, even the most grievous,
that I will produce perseverance in you by irresistible grace, and that
nothing is required of you except thanksgiving: I nevertheless admonish,
adjure, and beseech you to persevere in faith, lest there ever be in you a
wicked and unbelieving heart that falls away from the living God. If you fall
away, you will die eternally; if you persevere, you will be saved.”

Response: There is nothing here except empty rhetoric. 1. Throughout this
ridiculous speech God the Father could address his Son as follows: “Persuade
yourself, my Son, that it cannot fail to happen that, after about thirty-three
years, the preaching of the Gospel, and the glorious miracles done by you,
you will undergo the ignominious death of the cross for the salvation of the
human race at the hour appointed and fixed by my eternal decree. This is as
necessary as it is certain that my decree cannot be rescinded and my knowledge
cannot be deceived. Nevertheless,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 587 | PDF p. 636]

my Son, take care meanwhile to flee the violent hands of the Jews who attempt
to stone you and throw you headlong from the mountain, lest perhaps you not be
crucified at that appointed hour but perish before that time, crushed by
stones.” For just as an absolute decree and certain knowledge concerning such
a future outcome can coexist with wary fear, lawful care, and caution lest
that outcome by no means occur, so God can threaten punishment to those who do
not persevere even though they have been foreordained by God to persevere
through his irresistible grace. In the same way Christ's cautious flight and
avoidance of the violent hands of the Jews who attempted to crush him with
stones coexisted perfectly with the certainty of faith in Christ by which he
knew he would not be killed before the hour appointed by God the Father. These
passages prove it: John 11:53–54; Luke 4:29–30; John 10:39–40; Mark 1:45,
compared with John 7:30; 8:20; 13:1; Matthew 26:45; and Mark 14:41.

2. We teach no such thing as that God instills in us the persuasion that no
crimes can cause us to fall totally and finally from faith. For to possess
faith and to commit the most atrocious crimes with the whole force and effort
of the will—which the Remonstrants maintain can occur in true believers—are
truly *asystata*, incompatible and mutually repugnant things.

3. The force of this argument rests on the claim that a threat has no force or
efficacy when it threatens an evil which we are persuaded by the certainty of
faith will never actually be inflicted upon us. This

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 588 | PDF p. 637 | split word joined: certitudine]

is a weak argument. 1. The regenerate are not always actually and fully
persuaded, without any intervening doubt of conscience, that this evil will
never actually be inflicted upon them. In that case the threat is useful in
deterring them from the sin which would demeritoriously procure that evil. 2.
Indeed, a threat of eternal death would have no force for those persuaded with
the certainty of faith that they will escape eternal death unless God had
willed fear—or rather horror—of eternal death to be a means of avoiding the
sin that brings eternal death.

4. They locate the command's supposed uselessness in the fact that God
commands something as free obedience—namely, perseverance—which he
nevertheless produces in us by irresistible power. The Pelagians formerly
raised this objection because they could not bear Augustine's saying, “Give,
Lord, what you command, and command what you will.” Yet they themselves cannot
deny that God commands the inward illumination of the mind, from Psalm 32:9:
“Do not be like the horse and mule, which have no understanding,” while their
doctrine simultaneously holds that God illuminates the mind by irresistible
power. The carnal Jews once raised this same objection in Romans 3: it is
impossible for human beings to be justified through obedience to the law, yet
Paul exhorts them to perfect obedience to the law under penalty of eternal
death. Meanwhile he denies that he makes the law void and instead establishes
it.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 589 | PDF p. 638 | split word joined: Nihil]

5. The claim that our doctrine requires nothing from us except thanksgiving
is a diabolical slander. We teach that those who persevere are required to
render universal obedience to every command of God without exception—not
under the title and designation of legal obedience, but as evangelical
gratitude toward our Redeemer.

6. They deny that the command or threats are means and instruments of our
perseverance unless God produces perseverance only in a persuasive and wholly
resistible manner, through the Word's merely persuasive, contingent,
changeable, and uncertain action. This is the same as denying that a pen is an
instrument of skillful writing because the pen in itself is endowed with no
art. Certainly the pen does not form characters artfully by itself, but it
does so quite well when it is raised above itself by the hand of a skilled
writer. In the same way, although the action of the Word as means and
instrument is only persuasive and resistible in itself, when it is elevated
by the Holy Spirit it has an action that is more than persuasive—indeed, a
necessary and irresistible action.

It is therefore vain for the Remonstrant Synodists to say in article 5, p.
279: “If God produces perseverance irresistibly through exhortations, then
the exhortations are not means of perseverance; rather, a certain irresistible
power produced through the exhortations will be the means.” This is the same
as if they said, “If a writer writes artfully by means of a brutish pen devoid
of cognition,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 590 | PDF p. 639 | split word joined: artificiosè]

then the pen is not the means or instrument of writing artfully; rather, a
certain artistic power produced through the pen is.” The facts that God
irresistibly produces perseverance through the Word, while the Word in itself
and without elevation by the Holy Spirit produces perseverance only
resistibly, conflict no more than the facts that the pen in itself writes
neither necessarily nor artfully, while the pen in the hand of a skilled
writer writes both necessarily and artfully. It is well known that an
instrumental cause shares with the principal cause in a more eminent and
noble action when it is raised into exercise by the principal agent—an action
which it cannot perform by itself and unelevated. The Word of God in itself is
indeed a dead letter, operating only persuasively and resistibly, and it cannot
of itself illuminate the blind and convert the soul. But the Word through
God—that is, elevated above itself by the Spirit of God—is powerful to
demolish strongholds in the soul, 2 Corinthians 10:4, and is “the power of God
unto salvation to everyone who believes,” Romans 1:16. It is not called power
simply, but the power of God who uses it.

It is also false for them to say that the Word has no efficacy in itself
toward perseverance if God irresistibly produces perseverance. In itself it
has moral efficacy for producing perseverance and, when elevated by God, an
efficacy that is more than moral. In itself it has a changeable, contingent,
and uncertain efficacy, but when

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 591 | PDF p. 640 | split word joined: elevatum]

elevated by God and animated by the grace of the Holy Spirit it has an
unchangeable, necessary, and certain efficacy. Otherwise the Remonstrants must
acknowledge here no action of God for securing perseverance more noble than a
moral action. That is, they must teach with the Pelagians that all the grace
required for the saints' perseverance is the letter of the law and Gospel—a
grace which a pastor, a mere human being, can provide by himself.

7. Although no one is certain with the certainty of faith that he will
persevere unless he obeys the exhortations, and although obedience considered
in itself is a contingent and changeable condition, obedience to the
exhortations, insofar as it is subject to God's decree of election and God's
predetermining grace, is not that changeable and contingent “condition of the
jurists” which is indifferent to being or not being, as though a purely
Pelagian cause—that is, bare free will—operated here. The obedience rendered
to the exhortations is itself perseverance, unchangeably produced by God
through grace, believed with the certainty of faith, and at the same time
commanded by God. These things do not conflict with one another.

It is asked whether those who are certain of eternal life as their own cannot
be moved to obedience by the promise of eternal life. In article 5, p. 297,
the Remonstrants deny that people who already possess that good can be moved
by its promise. We affirm that they can.

1. A person who knows for certain that he will snatch the prize before all his
fellow competitors in the race is moved to run more

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 592 | PDF p. 641 | split word joined: currendum]

swiftly because he knows that he will obtain the prize only by running. In the
same way, one who is assured of eternal life is moved to run because the
knowledge that eternal life is attained only by running is essentially
included in that assurance. But if someone were certain that he would obtain
eternal life whether or not he obeyed and whether or not he ran, the promise
of eternal life could not move him morally to obey, run, and persevere. The
faithful have not been taught this way by Christ, and the Arminians vainly
attribute such a ridiculous and empty absolute certainty to us.

2. Christ was certain of his future glory, yet because of the glory set before
him as the end, he endured the cross and despised the shame, Hebrews 12:2 and
Psalm 16:9–11. Christ says in John 15, “I chose you,” yet in the same place he
says, “Bear fruit and abide in me.” Because David knew that he would become
king of Israel, could he not accept from the promise of the kingdom and hope
of it the throne offered to him by the tribes of Israel? That would be a
strange conclusion.

3. Nor do the elect possess eternal life in actuality and possession. Rather,
we are saved by hope. It follows that this hope is vain if it does not rest
upon sincere obedience as a secondary support, 1 John 3:3 and 2 Corinthians
5:7. Indeed, this hope produces the fear of religious caution by which we
avoid sins—although not the despairing fear concerning the outcome—and the
fear of filial

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 593 | PDF p. 642 | split word joined: filialis]

reverence, whose object is God's goodness, Hosea 3:5 and Psalm 130:4. By this
fear we do not fear something as a penal evil, 2 Corinthians 7:15; still less
does it produce the terror and trembling of servile fear.

It is asked whether, because believers sin in such a way that they are men
worthy of death, 2 Samuel 12:7, and incur the guilt of eternal death unless
they repent, they therefore fall totally from the grace of God. The Arminians
affirm it in article 5, p. 303. We deny it.

1. For the Lord has promised that he will raise up the righteous person as
often as he falls, Psalm 37:24.

2. They incur the guilt of eternal death demeritoriously not only on account
of atrocious sins but also on account of lighter ones, even an idle word, if
God were to enter into judgment with his servants. But the adversaries will
not say that people fall totally and finally from grace on account of the
lightest sins.

3. God is angry with believers' sins but not with their persons, Psalm
89:30–34. Nor do the more atrocious sins of the regenerate change God—with
whom there is no variation or shadow of turning, James 1:17—from one who
elects to eternal life into one who reprobates.
