### QUESTION V

#### What Are the Limits of the 1,290 and 1,335 Days Mentioned in Daniel 12:11–12?

This question is grave and exceedingly difficult amid such great disagreement
among learned men; it is fitting for people like us to be modest. Our concern
here will be to set out the principal opinions that please us less, together
with the reasons why they displease us; to establish our own with several
arguments; and to defend it against the obvious objections.

> Marginalia, printed p. 256: *The question’s difficulty*.

The first of the other opinions is that of the rabbis. As may be seen in
Calvin on the passage and in Jacchiades as presented by L’Empereur, they not
only extend the days into years, but combine both numbers, so that the 1,290
and 1,335 days make 2,600 years for them. They begin these at the moment of
this prophecy in the days of Cyrus and Daniel and close them at the end of the
world, approximately the six-thousandth year from creation. Much is wanting in
this opinion; for the present we shall indicate only four points. First. They
hold without any necessity that the days here are prophetic and must be
converted into years. Second. They separate these two numbers so badly that
each must be calculated distinctly, although each is one and the same number,
except that the latter adds forty-five days to the former. Third. Contrary to
express Scripture, they establish their beginning in the days of Daniel and
Cyrus, for at that time the continual sacrifice was not abolished but
restored. The Jews’ solemn sacrifices were indeed abolished first in the days
of Nebuchadnezzar, then of Antiochus, and finally of Vespasian, but by no means
in the times of Daniel and Cyrus. Fourth. They determine the end of the world
far too precisely as the six-thousandth year from creation, although the
angels in heaven are entirely ignorant of its hour, day, or year.

> Marginalia, printed p. 256: *Arguments against the rabbis’ opinion*.

The second opinion is that of John Calvin, though I do not know whether anyone
follows him here. He holds that these numbers signify nothing other than a
long time, a longer time, and a short one. It is as if, in the Church’s
calamities, the godly in their weakness reckon one day of the cross as an
entire year, indeed as several years, yet God’s goodness shortens that time,
however long, to what is almost half a year. If even this half-year sometimes
appears to be prolonged further, nevertheless the Lord does not permit that
time to become excessively long, but in his fatherly mercy confines it to a
few days, namely forty-five. Those who descend to a more distinct and
particular calculation of these numbers he considers Pythagoreans, trifling
with numbers. He also interprets the abolition of the continual sacrifice
mentioned by Daniel as the final devastation of the temple by Titus.

> Marginalia, printed p. 256: *Why Calvin’s opinion is displeasing*.

This judgment of that very great man does not satisfy. Granted that those who
determine the revolutions of the ages in minute detail from these numbers,
with excessive curiosity and boldness and without Scripture or any probable
reason, are Pythagoreans and trifle; nevertheless, it is altogether too meager
to interpret Daniel’s mysteries—repeated so often in numbers, sometimes the
same and sometimes different—as nothing other

[Printed p. 257 | IA leaf 287 | access PDF 287 | OCR object 286]

than a general doctrine, always known to all the godly, about the shortening
of the cross, in which no mystery lies.

Furthermore, Calvin lays two foundations beneath his opinion, neither of
which is firm. The former is that the desolation of the temple noted here by
Daniel was the final one, after which it was never repaired. The latter is
that the abomination of desolation in chapter 12 is the same as that which we
read of in chapter 9 and Matthew 24:15. Neither may be granted. Not the former,
for it is evident that the temple’s final devastation is not indicated in
12:11–12, but one which was soon to be repaired and from whose repair the Jews
were to rejoice with great joy—abundantly indeed after 1,290 days, but most
abundantly after 1,355.

As for the latter, we deny that the eleventh verse of chapter 12 is parallel
to the twenty-seventh verse of chapter 9. Chapter 9 speaks of the devastation
by the Romans, but chapter 12 of the calamity inflicted by Antiochus. When
Christ speaks in Matthew 24:15 of the Roman devastation, he refers to chapter
9, not chapter 12, as may be seen in Pareus, Diodati, the first and second
English Annotators, and other interpreters. The last English Annotators indeed
wish to refer Matthew 24 to the passages from Daniel 12 now before us, but,
contrary to Calvin, they extend the desolating abomination to the times of
Hadrian, beyond Vespasian. Indeed, as often elsewhere, they contradict
themselves disagreeably here. For at Matthew 24:15 they teach that the
desolation noted there was that of the Romans and the same as the one Daniel
indicates in both chapters 9 and 12. Nevertheless, at Daniel 12:11 they
expressly and correctly observe—though cutting down their own vineyards—that
the abomination expressed in chapter 12 differs from the one in chapter 9,
since the latter was brought about by the Romans and Titus but the former by
the Greeks and Antiochus.

The third opinion is Bullinger’s. With Calvin he interprets Daniel 9 and 12 and
Matthew 24 as concerning one and the same devastation of the Jews by Titus,
but adds that Daniel’s numbers must be understood as astronomical days. He
places the beginning of the first number of 1,290 days on May 17 in Nero’s
thirteenth year, when Vespasian began the war against the Jews, and its end on
September 8 in Vespasian’s second year. In the whole interval he counts a
little more than three and a half years. He takes the addition of forty-five
days in the latter number as the days during which, after the city’s capture,
Titus dragged the Jews from their hiding places—some to slaughter, some to
slavery, and some to his triumph.

> Marginalia, printed p. 257: *What I find wanting in Bullinger’s opinion*.

The following points are particularly displeasing in this opinion. First.
Although he attempts learnedly and accurately to coordinate Daniel’s days with
Roman history, nevertheless, in calculating the first number, where he
professes to count more than three and a half years, he does not complete
three years and four months in his computation. Moreover, without the
testimony of any author, he ascribes the forty-five days of the latter number
to Titus’s stay at Jerusalem after the devastation.

Second. Daniel begins his days after the continual sacrifice was abolished,
but that sacrifice was not abolished three and a half years before the
temple’s burning, for the continual sacrifice persisted until the last day the
temple stood.

Third. At the end of those days the Jews experienced great joy and happiness.
Yet Bullinger admits that, according to his opinion, the Romans’ cruelty
toward the Jews persisted at the end of the latter number, while at the end of
the former number no joy or happiness arose for the Jews other than what could
befall that most wretched people from the dreadful devastation of the city,
the temple, and the entire land. As for the Christian Church, although its
members adored God’s just judgments upon the obstinate Jews, they could not
but feel great grief at such a slaughter of God’s people and of their own
kinsmen, in which so many myriads of souls were hurled into hell together with
their bodies.

The fourth opinion is that of nearly all the Roman Catholics. They interpret
this passage as concerning their Antichrist, who reigns for 1,290 days after
the mass—their solemn sacrifice—has been removed from the Church. Once
Antichrist has been killed, forty-five days then pass in which Elijah preaches
the gospel, so that those who fell away under Antichrist may be called back to
repentance. We refuted this papist tale above. We add here that the Roman
Catholics boast of the Fathers’ authority as the principal foundation of
their opinion, especially the commentaries of Jerome and Theodoret on Daniel
12. To be sure, the Fathers’ authority carries very little weight with Roman
Catholics when it contradicts their own dogmas. In the present case Theodoret
holds that Elijah will preach the gospel during the forty-five days until the
Lord’s advent. Pererius, a Lapide, and the whole crowd of Jesuits make nothing
of Theodoret here and hold that Elijah, once killed by Antichrist, will not
rise before the day of the Lord.

> Marginalia, printed p. 257: *What is displeasing in the Roman Catholics’
> opinion*.

Furthermore, in this very argument the Roman Catholics reject both Jerome and
Theodoret when they count precisely forty-five days from Antichrist’s death to
the Lord’s

[Printed p. 258 | IA leaf 288 | access PDF 288 | OCR object 287]

advent. The Jesuits are certainly right that the Lord’s advent is not
determinately known by any mortal, but they wrongly use Ezekiel 39 to define
the time from Antichrist’s death to the end of the world as seven years. For
what do the prophets’ Gog and Magog have to do with Antichrist? Cornelius a
Lapide absurdly uses the Sibyl’s oracles to make a certain widow rule the
world during those seven years; she will gather all the gold, silver, bronze,
and iron from the whole earth and cast it into the depths of the sea. Indeed,
the recent Jesuits are so various and uncertain here that a Lapide sometimes
converts those forty-five days into seven years, sometimes into forty-five
years, and sometimes into an entirely indefinite period.

Third. Both Theodoret and Jerome build their opinions upon an exceedingly
frivolous foundation. Theodoret holds that Daniel does not speak of Antiochus
because the temple’s profanation perpetrated by him lasted 2,300 days in
chapter 8, whereas the profanation in chapter 11 lasted only 1,290 days. This
argument proves nothing, for, as was said above, Daniel speaks in chapter 8
not only of the temple’s profanation, as he does in chapter 12, but also of the
trampling of the people, which preceded by three whole years the placing of
the abomination of desolation in the temple. Jerome admits that Porphyry’s
opinion is true—an opinion a Lapide acknowledges that some Catholics also
hold, ascribing the 1,290 days not to Antichrist but to Antiochus—if the times
did not disagree. He proves three times over from this one reason that the
times do disagree: both the books of Maccabees and Josephus say that
Antiochus’s profanation lasted exactly three years, whereas the profanation
Daniel mentions in chapter 12 remained for three and a half years. The Jesuits
themselves—a Lapide, Salianus, and others—will solve this knot without
difficulty by affirming with Josephus that the temple may rightly be said to
have been polluted by Antiochus not only for three years but also for three
and a half. For six whole months before the idol of Jupiter was placed upon
God’s altar, Antiochus, through his legate Apollonius, had filled the city and
temple with slaughter and disturbed the sacrifices. They also concede that
Jerome wrongly fitted 1,290 days into three and a half years, for at least
twelve days remain. Indeed, if equal months each number thirty days, 1,290
days will make not only three and one-half years, but more than three and
seven-twelfths, so that Daniel’s calculation differs by a full month from the
time the Fathers or the Roman Catholics attribute to Antichrist.

Fourth. The Roman Catholics count only three and a half years from
Antichrist’s first rise to his end. But Daniel assigns this time to the tyrant
he names, whoever that may ultimately be, not from his first rise, but from
his *akmē*, the height and culmination of his power. For the abolition of the
continual sacrifice was an act of the highest tyranny and the fullest
dominion.

The fifth opinion is that of the English—not, I would say, chiliasts, but
certainly men who chiliast far too much. Their leader is Thomas Brightman;
Ephraim Huit follows him step by step. They and their followers profess to
interpret the 1,290 days prophetically, after the rabbis, as years, so that
these days stand for as many years and the latter number of 1,335 days stands
for forty-five years beyond the former sum. All the chiliasts agree on this,
but there is much disagreement among them about the limits and distinguishing
marks of these years. Brightman and Huit begin them in the year of Christ 360,
when Julian the Apostate ruled, and close them in 1650, when the converted
Jews, having returned to Canaan, began to overthrow the Turkish Empire with
their own arms, Rome then having been burned, and, in the forty-fifth year
after their resurrection from unbelief, utterly abolished the Turks.

> Marginalia, printed p. 258: *How Brightman, Mede, and Parker agree and differ
> among themselves*.

But Joseph Mede establishes very different limits for these years and
different distinguishing marks. He begins with Antiochus Epiphanes’s pollution
of the temple 167 years before Christ and closes with the revelation of
Antichrist and the Waldensians’ secession from the Roman Church in the year of
Christ 1123.

Robert Parker follows Mede in the distinguishing marks, supposing Antichrist
to mean the Turk, but follows Brightman in the limits, beginning in the year
of Christ 360 and closing in 1650. Lest an excessively hasty conclusion be
caught in manifest error even by his contemporaries—as all who end this epoch
in 1650 were caught long ago—by peculiar devices he provides the option not
only of establishing this epoch’s end either in 1650 or in 1859, at each
person’s choice, but also of placing its beginning either in the year of
Christ 360, 390, 1160, 1075, or 1260. The intervals are vast and the subtlety
more precise than that on whose refutation

[Printed p. 259 | IA leaf 289 | access PDF 289 | OCR object 288]

any sober person would deign to spend time.

We argue as follows against the principal foundations of this fifth opinion.
First. This opinion is a novelty openly set against all the orthodox, ancient
and modern. Brightman invented it and Mede polished it, although it still lies
quite unpolished in its crudeness. Mede indeed attempts to embellish it with
every testimony of the Fathers he can, but with testimonies of a kind that
openly patronize the chiliasts’ heresy contrary to the mind of the orthodox
Fathers.

Second. These new shreds of chiliasm, raised from the dead by English hands,
are expressed so variously by their interpolators that they clash with one
another head-on. On many matters of great importance Mede differs from
Brightman, Parker from both, and scarcely one of them appears sufficiently
consistent with himself in this chiliastic dream.

Third. Against his own associates, Mede agrees with us in establishing the
beginning of the 1,290 days in the temple’s pollution by Antiochus Epiphanes.
We embrace this, although we do not approve the indifference by which Mede
thinks it all the same from which year of the six-year persecution—or at least
of the three-year pollution—we count. For where the Prophet counts the individual
days with the greatest precision, mortals ought not to assume a license to run
at their pleasure through at least five, or even two, whole years.

> Marginalia, printed p. 259: *Arguments against Brightman*.

But what especially displeases us in Mede is his belief that the end of these
days must be placed not at the true end of any mystery, action, or event, but
only at a certain revelation of the end of those mysteries, as though the
Prophet aimed only at the year of Antichrist’s revelation and not at his true
rise, progress, or end. Our particular objection to Mede is not that he
fluctuates somewhat in establishing this very end of his, whether of the 1,290
or the 1,335 days. He sometimes places the former’s end in 1106, sometimes in
1120, and sometimes in 1123; he places the latter’s in any year from 1160 to
1170. We readily pardon these oversights of lesser importance. But I judge
Mede’s chief error here to be his peculiar invention, in which he glories as
though in new light from the Holy Spirit: that Daniel’s angel speaks not of
any end, but of the revelation of the mystery of the end. This paradox from a
most learned man is certainly absurd enough.

> Marginalia, printed p. 259: *Against Mede*.

First, he supports this new and peculiar commentary of his with no authority
and no reason.

Second. Daniel speaks of the end no fewer than six times in this very chapter,
namely in verses 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 13; he also does so as many times in the
preceding chapter and very often elsewhere. Yet it can nowhere be interpreted
as a revelation of the end, but always as the end itself, or the true outcome
of some thing. Mede’s sense here, therefore, is not only apart from but
contrary to all the Scriptures that speak of the end.

Third. Granted that Antichrist was revealed at the times Mede names, nothing
whatever cited by Mede speaks of Antichrist’s end. His end can be utterly
hidden from those who see Antichrist’s beginnings and progress clearly. But
Daniel speaks not of just any mystery, but of the mystery of the end, not of
the beginning or progress.

Fourth. The revelation of Antichrist in 1120, fifty years before the
Waldensians, was so slight and made to so few people, while the darkness of
Antichrist and his tyranny remained so thick for several centuries after
those days, that this revelation appears to deserve little weight.

Finally, at the end of the latter number of 1,335 days the godly experienced
great peace and joy and blessedness beyond former times. But Mede admits that
the end of his computation, 1160 and 1170, was so mournful and unhappy that
the godly never experienced worse times in the Church on earth. Certainly at
that time more than a million Christians were cruelly killed.

In Mede’s opinion the end was displeasing; in Parker’s and the others’, both
the beginning and the end. There is nothing for us to say about the end, as
though in 1650 Rome was to be overthrown, the Jews converted, the Turks
exterminated, and the kingdom of the world delivered into the godly’s hands.
The days and the eyes of those still awake refuted these dreams long ago. It
is true that in 1639, and especially in 1643 and 1645, the arms of the Scots,
flourishing wonderfully against the Malignants’ party throughout Britain,
aroused in many minds on both sides of the sea great hopes of Antichrist’s
imminent fall and of many prophecies’ fulfillment already drawing near. But in
1650, when our arms were seen to be broken and our kingdom and Church ground
beneath the feet of a new faction, then among all the orthodox those hopes
were beheaded and for a time grew entirely cold.

As for the beginning of their epoch in the year of Christ 360, in the days of
Julian, it does not at all fit the angel’s words in Daniel 12. Brightman wants
three distinguishing marks for this beginning. The first, from verse 7, is the
completion of the dispersion of the

> Marginalia, printed p. 259: *Against the beginning of their epoch*.

[Printed p. 260 | IA leaf 290 | access PDF 290 | OCR object 289]

holy people; the second, from verse 11, the abolition of the continual
sacrifice; the third, from the same verse, the erection of the desolating
abomination.
Yet none of the three fits this beginning.

As for the first, did the Jews deserve to be called God’s holy people in the
year of Christ 360? Repudiated by God, they had long since cast away every
profession of true faith. Openly displaying bitter hostility toward Christ,
they no more possessed a place in the Church and the people of God than they
do today. Furthermore, was their dispersion completed in Julian’s days?
Julian indeed granted the Jews many favors because of his impiety, but he
certainly added nothing to their former servitude and dispersion. If not in
Titus’s days, then certainly in Hadrian’s, many centuries before Julian, the
Jews’ dispersion was brought to its highest culmination.

As for the second distinguishing mark, the continual sacrifice was not
abolished in Julian’s days. For after Titus burned the temple, neither a
continual nor any other sacrifice has existed among the Jews to this day. The
impediment miraculously placed by God against restoring the temple and the
solemn sacrifices in it is wrongly called the abolition of the temple and
sacrifices, which at that time existed nowhere in fact, although they did in
the desire and hope of Julian the Apostate and the blasphemous Jews.

The third distinguishing mark, the erection of the desolating abomination,
also has nothing to do with this beginning. Brightman engages in much
Hebraizing, distinguishing between an abomination that desolates and one that
has been desolated, as though משומם were an active form and שמם passive,
contrary, however, to grammatical reasoning, the usage of Scripture, the best
interpreters, and even sound reason. For the *benoni qal* is an active form,
not passive. Even if you take שומם not as a participle but as a *nomen toar*,
or verbal noun, nevertheless, by Brightman’s own admission, it is used
actively in Daniel 8, and all the interpreters here do indeed understand it
actively. Not one of Brightman’s followers keeps his master here by
interpreting the abomination of desolation as the abomination of the desolated
people, broken by calamities. Instead, all interpret it as the abomination
that causes desolation among the people. “The abomination of the desolated” is
an entirely new interpretation, founded on no reason, example, or witness. In
any case, the erection of an abomination—whether desolating or desolated—does
not fit Julian’s days. Whatever may be made either of Julian’s or of the Jews’
intentions and desires to erect every abomination, they were able to erect
nothing abominable. The Prophet speaks of an abomination actually erected,
not of an intention to erect one that was never put into effect.

Finally, although the ceremonial law after the temple’s destruction was not
only dead but deadly, nevertheless that law is nowhere in sacred Scripture
marked with the dreadful title “the desolating abomination.” This infamy is
usually applied to the crassest pagan idolatry. We defer the rest of this
disputation to the questions on Revelation, if God grants that we continue
this little work that far.

The sixth and final opinion is that of those who hold these to be astronomical
days. They place the beginning when, at Antiochus’s command, the statue of
Jupiter Olympius was set above Jehovah’s altar on the twenty-fifth day of the
month Cisleu in the Seleucids’ year 145, and the end on the 1,335th
astronomical day after that beginning. The opinion’s authors agree about the
kind and limits of these days, but not about the distinguishing marks. All
indeed place the beginning in Antiochus’s pollution of the temple, and they
also locate the end of the second number and its days at Epiphanes’s death.
They differ, however, about the end of the former number and therefore the
beginning of the latter. Junius, and after him Piscator, Polanus, Rollock,
Brighton, Diodati, Grotius, and others, place the end of the former number,
namely the 1,290 days, at the peace of Antiochus Eupator in 2 Maccabees 11:33,
when the Jews were granted *autonomia*, or full liberty. But the middle
English Annotators, and the last ones more fully and distinctly, in my
judgment rightly pass over this distinguishing mark. They place the end of the
former number and the beginning of the latter at some illustrious act of God’s
mercy toward the Jews concerning which Scripture is silent, as it also is
concerning the whole remaining history of these ages. The books of Maccabees
and Josephus also either remain silent or neglect to ascribe the time named to
this distinguishing mark of the Englishmen.

> Marginalia, printed p. 260: *Junius’s sixth opinion*.

Although the authors named follow Junius’s opinion precisely, it appears to be
set out somewhat carelessly. In any case, we find the following five points
wanting in it.

> Marginalia, printed p. 260: *Points requiring correction*.

First. He proves his distinguishing mark concerning Antiochus Eupator from 2
Maccabees 11:33. For if that stands as the foundation, this part of Junius’s
opinion must be false. In the passage cited, Antiochus Epiphanes is proclaimed
dead in his son Eupator’s letter before the end of the first number and the
beginning of the second. Nevertheless, Junius places the end of the second
number at Epiphanes’s death. According to Junius, therefore, Antiochus will die
both before the number’s beginning and after its end, which is impossible.

Second. Junius approves the chronology of 2 Maccabees 11, according to which
he holds that

[Printed p. 261 | IA leaf 291 | access PDF 291 | OCR object 290]

Epiphanes died in the 148th year of the Greeks, contrary to the sixth chapter
of the first book and to Junius’s own opinion that Epiphanes died in the 149th
year. This contradiction cannot be reconciled.

Third. In verse 7 of chapter 12 Junius counts only three years and ten days,
but the original text expressly adds to the three years half a year, not a
part. The last English Annotators should not have followed Junius here,
especially since the middle ones abandoned Junius here and followed the
original text.

Fourth. From the 1,290 days Junius constructs three years, seven months, and
thirteen days. Both groups of Englishmen correctly amend Junius here by
placing only six months.

Fifth. Junius also appears to err about the months here. He holds that
Xanthicus is the penultimate, that is, the eleventh month, yet in his notes on
2 Maccabees 11 he makes Xanthicus the fifth month. Learned men indeed dispute
which was first, Hyperberetaeus or Xanthicus, yet Xanthicus can never be either
the fifth or the penultimate month. For if you begin the Macedonian year with
Hyperberetaeus in autumn, corresponding to our October, then Xanthicus,
corresponding to our March, will be the sixth. But if you count from spring,
Xanthicus will be the first.

Finally, Junius holds that Jupiter of Corinth is the seventh month suited to
sowing. In Macedonia our Junius, answering Jupiter of Corinth, certainly
appears unfit for sowing. Moreover, this *Dioscorium* must have been an
intercalary, not an ordinary, month. Indeed, no such name for any month was
ever heard among the Greeks, and it appears to have been invented by the
author of Maccabees. And if you trust Scaliger, the Macedonians’ intercalary
month preceded, rather than followed, Xanthicus.

How easy it is for the most learned and acute men to err in details of this
kind when, in their haste, they are unable or do not take care to subject many
things to examination. In any case, we embrace the sum and substance of
Junius’s opinion and shall endeavor to defend it against the weapons of its
opponents.

> Marginalia, printed p. 261: *Junius defended against Brightman’s objections*.

Brightman objects as follows on page 92 of his work on Daniel. From the
temple’s pollution to the decree concerning the Jews’ *autonomia* on the
fifteenth of Xanthicus, there cannot be counted 1,290 days. For, he says, from
the temple’s pollution on the twenty-fifth of Cisleu in the Greeks’ year 145
to its cleansing, exactly three years or 1,080 days elapsed. From then to the
fifteenth of Xanthicus there are 108 days. These joined together do not reach
the sum of 1,290, for seventy-seven days are wanting. But if, in accordance
with Josephus’s false tale, you count the three and a half years in verse 7,
they indeed make 1,272 days. Yet even so, eighteen are lacking from the sum of
1,290. If you add the 108 days that elapsed from the twenty-fifth of Cisleu,
or Apellaeus, to the fifteenth of Xanthicus, then the sum will exceed our
number by ninety days.

Answer. First. There appear to be various oversights in this arithmetic of
Brightman’s, but we pass over them. Thus even granting that the entire
calculation is correct, this argument has nothing to do with us, since we do
not make Eupator’s decree on the fifteenth of Xanthicus the end of the former
number or beginning of the latter. Consequently, what he adduces about his
three years and ten days, and the remainder that follow from the temple’s
cleansing to Eupator’s peace—or from the twenty-fifth of Cisleu to the
fifteenth of Xanthicus—does not touch us.

Second. This reasoning falls of its own accord if three and a half years are
counted in verse 7. Although Junius denies this, the text affirms it, where
*half*, not *part*, is expressly read. This is not Josephus’s tale. For besides
other Protestants and Roman Catholics, the middle English Annotators, and even
Huit, the leading man among Brightman’s own followers, embrace the meaning of
the seventh verse.

As for Brightman’s objection that eighteen days are still wanting to complete
the number, we readily answer that this arises from Brightman’s erroneous
calculation. He counts only 360 days in his year from twelve equal months of
thirty days. He forgets that the five supplementary days in every year, and
the six hours, rise to nineteen days in three and a half years. Those who used
equal months and years always intercalated these at fixed cycles.

His insistence that ninety days must be added to the former sum does not
concern us, since we reject the distinguishing mark of Eupator. Nor does it
concern Junius, who always reads *a part of a year*, namely ten days, in place
of half a year.

Huit makes further objections, of which these are the principal ones. First.
Wherever Scripture speaks of Antiochus’s actions, it always adds circumstances
that mark their foulness. But in Daniel 12:11 no foul circumstance is
expressed. Answer. There is no necessity in the major premise; in any case,
the minor is false. Daniel 12:11 expresses actions that were exceedingly evil
in their own nature and singularly befitted a most foul man: namely the
suppression, through savage

[Printed p. 262 | IA leaf 292 | access PDF 292 | OCR object 291]

persecution, of divine worship and, through the most dreadful idolatry, the
erection of the desolating abomination.

Second. The interval between the end of the former number and that of the
latter was so great that those alive at the one would have difficulty
surviving to the other. But it was very easy to endure for forty-five days.
Answer to the major premise: the Prophet says absolutely nothing that lessens
the difficulty of persevering from the end of the former number to the end of
the latter. Huit assumes this without any proof.

Third. At the end of the latter number, great and unexpected happiness is
promised to the Jews. But when Antiochus Epiphanes died, the Jews experienced
no happiness. Answer. Epiphanes’s death—namely, the death of the greatest and
cruelest tyrant who had harassed the Jews down to that day—was the occasion of
the greatest benefit and signal joy for the Jews. His armies had indeed
already been broken by Maccabaeus, but if his life and health had remained,
the tyrant could readily have restored them in his vast empire. The Jews also
experienced many calamities after those times, but for several centuries
nothing befell them comparable to Epiphanes’s persecution.

But Mede rises with the greatest force of all against this opinion of ours.
He argues with great confidence as follows. From the temple’s cleansing on
the twenty-fifth of Casleu in the Seleucids’ year 148 to Antiochus’s death
before the fifteenth of Xanthicus in the year 149, there were not four full
months. When these are added to the temple’s three years of pollution, they do
not make more than 1,200 days. Thus Daniel’s numbers can in no way be fitted
to the history of Epiphanes. He proclaims that he demonstrates this visibly in
figures and tables of the Macedonian months and of Antiochus’s deeds fitted to
the days of those months. Hence he professes his considerable amazement that
the opinion of Junius could have found so many supporters, and those most
learned men, who erred so markedly and so manifestly perverted the historical
record. Answer. Mede ought not to have used this boastful speech against so
many distinguished doctors of the Reformed Church, especially in an argument
where he openly professes to follow the most insolent Jesuit Petavius, and in
a dispute against the Reformed Church. Our men prove that the books of
Maccabees are apocryphal from, among other indications, the fact that they
openly err in chronological matters. They adduce as an instance the death of
Antiochus Epiphanes, which the first book reports occurred in the Greeks’
149th year, but the second in the 148th. Various Roman Catholics have invented
various reconciliations of this chronological contradiction. But when all of
them were driven off by the force of our arguments, the Jesuit Petavius
hammered out a new one, trusting in which he most impudently derides from on
high the ignorance of all our men.

His “wise remedy” is this: there were two beginnings of the Greeks’ year,
either from autumn or from spring—from our October, the Macedonians’
Hyperberetaeus, or from our April, the Macedonians’ Xanthicus. The author of 1
Maccabees, being a Palestinian Jew, began his year from spring, while the
author of the latter book, an Alexandrian Jew, began the Alexandrian year from
autumn, according to the Macedonians’ new custom. After defeating Darius at
Arbela on October 8, they thereafter began their year with this month and day
in Alexander’s honor. The Alexandrians, like the rest of the Macedonian
people, followed this change, but the Syrians and Palestinians did not. In
this way the difference between the books of Maccabees is readily reconciled.
When Antiochus died at the beginning of Xanthicus, he can correctly be said,
with the first book, which begins the year from Xanthicus, to have died in the
149th year. He can no less correctly be said with the second book to have died
in the 148th, because that author begins his Alexandrian year from
Hyperberetaeus in autumn, so that the following Xanthicus will be his seventh
month of the 148th year, although it is the other author’s first month of the
149th.

Petavius boasts greatly of this shield and marvels at Scaliger’s childish
ignorance and inattentive reading of the books of Maccabees, and even at his
laughable hallucination.

I would more readily allow this most insolent vanity to Petavius, as something
domestic and proper to Jesuits, than to Mede, who ought not to have turned the
Jesuits’ abuse against our men.

However that may be, no cause at all appears for such boasting by Mede or his
authority Petavius. First, the Jesuit borrows the invention itself and all its
foundations from our Scaliger. In the later edition of his *Emendation of
Times*, Scaliger fished out these different beginnings of the Greeks’ year,
attempting with this scoop to draw the authors of Maccabees out of the abyss
of chronological contradiction. Yet for this great benefit to the Roman
Catholics, the only thanks they return him is false laughter and mockery.

Second. Granted that this invention of Scaliger’s is true and sound, two
points nevertheless still remain to be proved. One is that the author of 2
Maccabees begins his year in autumn. Scaliger, and Petavius after him,
sufficiently proved that the author of the first book begins in spring with
Xanthicus, but neither

proves what especially required proof: that the author of the second book
begins in autumn with Hyperberetaeus. Even if this is granted, that the second
begins its year in autumn, a doubt still remains whether that autumn follows
or precedes Xanthicus. There is certainly such perplexity and confusion in the
Greeks’ reckoning of these years that the very acute Scaliger himself admits
it, while both Serrarius and Torniellus profess that in their reckonings the
second author differs from the first by a full two years, although each of
these Jesuits is severely beaten by his brother Petavius here for his labors.

Third. Although we were to grant every postulate of Petavius and Mede, this
medicine of theirs nevertheless does not cure the disease. See the straits
into which they cast themselves. Mede admits that Epiphanes must have died at
the beginning of Xanthicus, neither later nor earlier—no earlier because the
149th year had not begun before Xanthicus, and no later because the second
book pronounces him dead by the fifteenth of Xanthicus in the 148th year. Let
us add from Scaliger that the Greeks’ autumnal year began not on the first of
Hyperberetaeus but on the eighth, and therefore their spring year began not on
the first but on the eighth of Xanthicus. Antiochus must therefore have died
after the eighth of Xanthicus, and his son Eupator must have written to the
Jews about his death before the fifteenth. The journey from Elymais in the
farthest part of Persia, or even from the mountains of Media where Antiochus
is said to have died, to Antioch in Syria, Eupator’s seat, is far more than
seven days. The news of his father’s death therefore could not have reached
Eupator in so little time. Even granting that he died on the very first day of
the 149th year, namely the eighth of Xanthicus, could Eupator before the
fifteenth not only receive certain news of it, but also be solicited by Lysias
by letter concerning peace with the Jews, deliberate and decide with his
counselors about the terms of this peace, and write to the Jews what he had
decreed? Who will have persuaded either Mede or Petavius of this?

Fourth. Antiochus’s death could not have occurred earlier than the first day,
or the first month, of the 149th year. For Judas cleansed the temple on the
twenty-fifth day of the month Casleu or Apellaeus—our December—in the 148th
year. Then, after the solemn festival of eight days, with great labor he
surrounded Mount Zion with walls and fortifications. Afterward he fought many
battles against Edomites, Ammonites, Arabs, Philistines, Syrians, and Greeks,
and captured many citadels and fortified cities, as may be seen in 1 Maccabees
5. News of these events reached Antiochus Epiphanes at Ecbatana in Media,
several months’ journey from Judea. At that sad news Antiochus took to his bed
for many days and finally died. Not a rumor, but certain news of his death was
carried to his son in Syria. Then, after envoys traveled back and forth
between Judas and Lysias, peace was concluded and established between the
then-king Eupator and the Jews. Could so many and such great events have been
accomplished in three winter months, namely after the beginning of January and
before the middle of April?

But breaking off these apocryphal trifles, we demonstrate irrefutably as
follows the certain falsehood of Petavius’s invention, in which Mede takes
such great pleasure. If the author of 2 Maccabees begins his years in autumn,
so that Hyperberetaeus is his first month and Xanthicus, or Nisan, his seventh,
then the month *Dystros*, the Hebrews’ Adar, which immediately precedes Nisan,
will be his sixth month. Just as in the first book, which makes Nisan and
Xanthicus the first month, Adar and *Dystros* are the twelfth and last. But for
the author of 2 Maccabees, *Dystros* and Adar are not the sixth month, but the
twelfth and last, as we expressly read in 15:37. It is therefore clear and
certain that the author of 2 Maccabees numbers his years and months in the same
way as the author of the first, and that for each author the beginning and end
of the years are the same. For since Adar is the twelfth month for each, Nisan
or Xanthicus must also be the first for each.

When one notices in a Jesuit such a laughable hallucination and childish
inattention—as he is pleased to triumph over Scaliger—Mede would certainly
have done better to join arms with his countryman Rainolds against the
fraudulent Apocrypha, which Rainolds excellently demonstrates to be ignorant
of all the liberal arts, and especially chronology. In this field, I say, Mede
ought to have fought with his companions against the Church’s enemies, rather
than borrowing from them arguments against his brothers which, when brought
back to the anvil, are plainly glass and easily shattered.

In any case, rejecting the author of 2 Maccabees and interpreting Daniel from
the first book, we see how Daniel’s final number of 1,335 days can be preserved
from the temple’s pollution in the Greeks’ 145th year to Antiochus’s death in
the 149th year—not on its first day, but on any day of that year, even its last,
for neither the day nor the month of that year is stated.
